# Marking guide: first-class standard for UK law answers

This guide tells an automated grader how to mark the bot's answers against the standard of real first-class (70+) undergraduate law work in England and Wales. It covers **essays**, **problem questions** and **everyday or practical questions**, and is written from an experienced examiner's point of view. UK law schools mark against four criteria: **knowledge**, **analysis** (including problem solving), **communication** and **research**. Use all four.

Calibration: most competent answers land between 60 and 69. A 70+ is earned by doing something extra (a defended thesis, sharp issue-spotting, real engagement with scholarship), not by the absence of errors. Accurate and tidy is not enough for a 5.

---

## 1. Bands

### 1A. Essays (critically discuss / evaluate / "to what extent")

| Band | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **70+** | The introduction states a thesis that answers the exact question, and the essay defends it throughout. The answer cites leading authority precisely: sections and subsections, and cases for what they actually decide, noting the court and whether a point is ratio, obiter or dissent. It engages named academics in dialogue rather than name-dropping them. It answers the strongest counter-argument. It often has an original frame (an asymmetry, a tension, a benchmark regime) or a reasoned reform proposal. The conclusion follows from the analysis and commits to a view. The prose is continuous, signposted and within length. |
| **60–69** | Accurate, organised, with a recognisable argument, but uneven. Some sections describe rather than evaluate. Scholarship is thin or decorative. Counter-arguments are noted but not answered. The thesis blurs mid-essay, and the conclusion partly restates. Upper 60s: strong on law and structure, but lacking scholarly depth or criticism of leading appellate decisions. |
| **50–59** | Mostly a description of "what the law is". Drifts into a general survey of the topic. Uses only the obvious cases and few academic sources, makes unsupported assertions, and ends with a hedged or disconnected conclusion. |
| **Below 50** | Significant legal errors, invented or misattributed authority, an answer to a different question, no argument, or seriously wrong length. |

### 1B. Problem questions ("Advise X")

| Band | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **70+** | Frames who is advised and what each issue is. Spots every issue, including the less obvious ones, in a logical order. For each issue it gives the rule and its exact authority, applies it closely to the facts (dates, ages, figures, wording of documents), and reaches a reasoned mini-conclusion. It flags **missing or ambiguous facts** and gives alternative outcomes. It commits to the most likely result, advises every named party, gets calculations and thresholds right with the working shown, and ends with practical next steps. |
| **60–69** | Main issues spotted and competently applied, but some application is generic. A secondary issue or a named party gets thin treatment. It fails to commit on a balanced point. Missing facts are not flagged. Citations are not specific enough (a whole Act cited where a section was needed). |
| **50–59** | Misses a significant issue or exception. States the rules at length and applies them in a sentence. No clear advice. Mechanical errors (wrong calculation, misread date, an amendment treated as ineffective). |
| **Below 50** | Wrong legal framework (for example pre-Brexit rules for new proceedings), major issues missed, no application, or advice that would mislead a client. |

### 1C. Everyday or practical questions

The standard here is **a clear, correct and useful answer**, not academic depth. Never penalise the absence of scholarship.

| Band | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **70+** | Answers the question in the first one or two sentences. States the correct current law for the right UK jurisdiction and names the key statute or rule. Covers the main exception or condition. Gives practical next steps (time limits, who to contact, evidence to keep) and says when professional advice is needed. Concise and in plain English. |
| **60–69** | Correct and helpful, but the answer is buried, a key caveat or deadline is missed, or it is longer or more hedged than needed. |
| **50–59** | Broadly right but vague ("it depends" without saying on what), generic, or slightly inaccurate. |
| **Below 50** | Wrong or outdated law, wrong jurisdiction, invented sources, or advice that could cause harm. |

## 2. What drags a 70+ answer down

1. **Description instead of argument.** Case summaries with no link to the thesis. A good test: delete the summaries, and see whether any argument is left.
2. **Thin or decorative scholarship.** Relying almost wholly on cases and legislation. Even strong legal advice stalls in the high 50s or low 60s without secondary literature. Citing a textbook generically, with no pinpoint, is weak.
3. **Unsupported assertions.** Claims about regulatory failure, cost, feasibility or market behaviour with no source. Footnoting a whole statute for a specific proposition counts as unsupported.
4. **Uncritical treatment of appellate decisions.** Treating a dictum as settled law when it was obiter, came from a narrow majority, or is contested by a dissent or by later commentary.
5. **Missing leading authority.** Omitting the provision or case that frames the question, or a dissent that directly challenges the thesis.
6. **Misstated law.** Getting the mechanism wrong (which provision did what, from when, to whom), misreading an amendment or transitional rule, or wrong arithmetic.
7. **Not answering the question.** Writing on the topic rather than the question, skipping named parties, or ignoring an express instruction such as "consider justification".
8. **Hedging without committing.** Setting out both sides and not deciding.
9. **Fragmentation.** Many very short sections, or bullet lists in an essay body that read like a briefing note. Links between sections that are asserted rather than shown. Fewer points in depth are better.
10. **Over-breadth.** Too many regimes or jurisdictions, each with a paragraph. Ambition without depth tops out in the high 60s.
11. **Poor issue-spotting.** Missing the less obvious issue, or failing to notice a missing fact (whether a document was witnessed, consent given, a scheme registered, or a survey covered all classes).
12. **Padding and length.** Restating the question, generic introductions, repeated conclusions, or going well over or under length.
13. **Presentation.** Spelling and grammar slips and inconsistent citation decide marks at the 69/70 boundary.

## 3. Building the perspective and argument

- **Thesis first.** One sentence answering the question as asked, then a short roadmap. In a problem question, start with who is advised and the headline advice.
- **A frame that does work.** An organising idea (an asymmetry between connected rules, a clash of values, a benchmark regime) that every section tests.
- **Signposting.** Topic sentences stating each section's contribution, and transitions that link back to the thesis.
- **Cases for principles.** Cite a case for what it decides. Distinguish ratio, obiter and dissent. Give facts only where they drive the point.
- **Academics in dialogue.** Name the scholar, state their argument, and then agree, qualify or reject it with reasons. Use Law Commission reports, regulator guidance and statistics as evidence.
- **The strongest counter-view,** stated fairly and answered.
- **Reasoned evaluation** against explicit criteria (coherence, certainty, fairness, policy aims). Reform proposals should be concrete and deal with their own costs.
- **A committed conclusion** that answers the question and names what remains open, with no new authority.
- **Problem-question method:** issue, rule and authority, application to the exact facts, mini-conclusion. Flag missing facts and give alternative outcomes. End with advice to each party.

## 4. Area-specific notes

**Competition law.** Frame the answer under the Competition Act 1998 s 18, with Article 102 TFEU case law persuasive under s 60A. Work through market definition, dominance, each abuse, then objective justification (necessity, proportionality, efficiencies). For tying, address separate products, coercion and foreclosure. In digital cases, use *Microsoft*, *Google Android*, *Google Shopping*, *Meta v Bundeskartellamt* (privacy and data as quality) and the effects-based approach after *Intel*. Use current CMA guidance rather than superseded guidance, and mention the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 where relevant. A common weakness: good case law with almost no academic literature, and thin advice to some of the named addressees.

**Private international law.** Post-Brexit jurisdiction runs through CPR 6.36–6.37, the PD 6B gateways, a serious issue to be tried, and *Spiliada*. *Vedanta*, *Okpabi* and *FS Cairo v Brownlie* are central for tort claims; engage with Lord Leggatt's dissent in *Brownlie* on how the gateways relate to forum conveniens. Explain why you chose your focus, and give the rationale for recognising judgments (obligation theory, comity, finality). Contrast common-law recognition (*Schibsby*, *Emanuel*, *Adams v Cape*, *Rubin*) with the Hague 2005 and 2019 Conventions, stating scope and temporal limits accurately. Give pinpoints for textbooks.

**Pensions law.** Start from the scheme's purpose and the statutory frame: Pensions Act 2004 s 255, the Investment Regulations 2005, the power of amendment, and Pensions Act 1995 s 67. On non-financial factors, use *Cowan v Scargill*, *Harries*, the Law Commission's two-part test, and the Supreme Court's local government pension scheme decision. Treat that decision critically: it was split, its comments on the test were obiter, the judgments worded the detriment limb differently, and commentators dispute it. On equalisation, be precise about mechanism (*Barber* and its temporal limit, the equal treatment rule, accrued-rights protection). Flag missing facts (registration, consent, actuarial certification, which members were surveyed) and show calculation working.

**International commercial mediation.** Benchmark the Singapore Convention against the New York Convention. Analyse Article 4 and the Article 5 refusal grounds, especially Article 5(1)(e)–(f) on mediator misconduct: the undefined seriousness standard, causation, and the clash with confidentiality. Address divergent mediator standards and evidence rules across states. Check the UK's current ratification position. A concrete reform proposal, tested against its costs, and a broad source base (UNCITRAL materials, academic and practitioner commentary) lift an answer.

**Law and medicine.** In problem questions, use the Mental Capacity Act 2005: ss 1–3 (presumption, functional test, unwise decisions), s 4 (best interests, including past wishes), and ss 24–26 (advance decisions; s 25(5)–(6) for life-sustaining treatment). Add *Re T*, *Ms B*, *Re MB* and *Bland*, Family Law Reform Act 1969 s 8 and *Gillick* for minors, and the Human Tissue Act 2004 and HTA Codes for donation. Commit on borderline points (for example whether a refusal is contemporaneous or an advance decision) and name the exact statutory condition. In essays, take a qualified stance backed by scholarship and data, and deal properly with the opposing arguments.

**AI and data protection (long-form research).** A conceptual frame is a strength, but empirical and economic claims need sources. Tie comparative material (UK GDPR and the DPA 2018 as amended, the EU AI Act) back to the thesis. Prefer fewer, deeper sections in continuous prose, and evaluate reform trade-offs cautiously.

**Applying this to other areas.** In every subject, identify the governing statute and the leading appellate authority. Find where the law is unsettled (splits, dissents, obiter, Law Commission proposals, academic criticism). Build the thesis or advice around that, and commit to a reasoned position. The 70+ answer argues rather than describes, applies the law to the exact facts, uses scholarship as part of the argument, and is precise about the law.

## 5. Grader instructions

**Step 1: Classify** the answer as essay, problem question or everyday, and use the matching table. Note any requested length.

**Step 2: Check accuracy first.** Check that each case exists, is correctly named and cited, and decides what it is cited for. Check that each statute and section is correct, in force and applies to that jurisdiction and date. Check that named academics and reports are real and fairly described. Then apply these caps before judging quality:
- **Invented authority** (case, statute, section, article): cap at **2**; cap at **1** if it supports a central conclusion.
- **Misstated core rule** that changes the outcome or advice: cap at **2**.
- **Misattributed case**, or the wrong section for a key rule: one band down per instance.
- **Outdated law** presented as current: cap at **3**, or **2** if it changes the advice.
- **Minor slips** (a wrong year, citation format): a presentation point at the 69/70 boundary only.

If an authority cannot be verified, flag it as "unverified" rather than "invented". Still, do not give a 5 if key propositions rest on unverified authority.

**Step 3: Judge quality** against the band table, the four criteria and the failure patterns in section 2.

**Step 4: Convert the result.**

| Score | Band | Estimated % | Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5** | First | 70–80 | 70–72 for a just-first with committed analysis and minor gaps; 73–76 for strong scholarship and counter-argument; 77–80 for exceptional, original work. |
| **4** | High 2:1 | 65–69 | Accurate, structured and argued, but missing one first-class element (scholarly depth, criticism of authority, or full issue coverage). |
| **3** | Low 2:1 | 60–64 | Competent but partly descriptive, generic in application, or thin on authority. |
| **2** | 2:2 | 50–59 | Largely descriptive, significant gaps or errors, or only partly answers the question. |
| **1** | Below 2:2 | <50 | Wrong law, invented authority, off the question, or unusable. |

For everyday answers, a 5 means advice a careful solicitor would be happy to give a member of the public.

**Step 5: Report** the type, the score, the estimated percentage, any accuracy flags (naming the authority), and two to four specific improvements.

## 6. Answer-writer summary (for the bot)

1. Answer the exact question in the first paragraph: a thesis, or the bottom line.
2. Give a short roadmap, and signpost how each section serves the thesis.
3. Cite only authority you are sure of, with exact sections, courts and pinpoints. Never invent a case or section.
4. Use cases for the principles they decide, and say whether a point is ratio, obiter or dissent.
5. Engage named scholars and official reports: agree, qualify or rebut, with reasons.
6. State the strongest counter-argument fairly and answer it.
7. Say where the law is unsettled, then commit to a reasoned position.
8. In problem questions, cover every issue and party, apply the law to the exact facts and figures, and flag missing facts.
9. Use the current, in-force, post-Brexit law for the right UK jurisdiction.
10. Write continuous analytical prose with a few points in depth: no bullet-point essays and no padding.
11. End with a conclusion that follows from the analysis (or with practical next steps), and keep to the requested length.
12. For everyday questions: the key rule, the main exception, any deadlines, and when to get advice, in plain English.
