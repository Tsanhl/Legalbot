# Essay answers: how first-class work is built

Target: a high 2:1 to first-class answer (70+). The patterns below describe first-class (70+) standard, contrasted
with the weaknesses typical of lower 2:1 and 2:2 essays. Area-specific corrections are in `kb/topics/<topic>/style.md`.

## 1. Before writing: decode the question
- Separate **what the question is about** (the topic) from **what it asks** (the claim you must test). Break it into
  sub-questions; if it has parts (i), (ii), answer each, labelled.
- Classify it: explanatory ("what is the law?"), normative ("what should it be?") or both. Almost every question
  needs both: explain the law, then judge it.
- Identify the **centre of gravity** and make it the centre of the essay. Typical ways to lose marks: the case law
  named in the title is not at the centre of the essay (e.g. an essay on "use" restrictions that misses *Mickelsson*
  and *Trailers* and spends words on QRs); an essay on the Court's use of citizenship that does not put the evolution
  of the Court's reasoning at its core; an essay that describes scheme types instead of analysing the statement; an
  essay that spends most of its words describing a problem instead of how it should be addressed.
- If the question is a quotation, tie every section back to the quotation.

## 2. Introduction (5–12% of words)
Must do four things in a few sentences:
1. Go straight to the issue. Do not open with background, a definition list, or secondary legislation (e.g. do not
   start with a directive when the question is about the Court's case law).
2. Define the key terms of the question ("fit for purpose" = achieves the purpose *and* is an appropriate means;
   "predictability" = ex ante anticipation of outcomes).
3. **State a qualified thesis in the first one or two sentences**, e.g. "The proposition is attractive but
   overstates the analogy ... My stance is qualified: the law should not ... but should recognise ...", or
   "[X] should not be abolished but heavily altered". A two-sided verdict is fine; a topic statement is not.
4. A roadmap of the 2–4 steps of the argument.
Bad (descriptive) opening: a list of cases and holdings with no position. Good opening: the law in one sentence
with authority, then "I shall argue that ... is challenged by A, B and C".

## 3. Body: 3–5 sections, analysis 55–85% of words
- **Argumentative headings**, not topic labels: "The Certainty Gap: Why the 'Critical Mass' is Stalling",
  "Rejecting *Reid*", "A glorified court order". Use headings to convey the argument. Keep the number of headings
  proportionate: too many in a short essay fragments it, and sections that are too brief cannot sustain meaningful
  analysis.
- Typical order: (1) short orthodox statement of the law/benchmark → (2) problems, inconsistencies, policy →
  (3) the strongest counter-argument and its rebuttal → (4) reform or reconceptualisation → conclusion.
- The stronger the essay, the less free-standing description. Exposition 0–30%; fold the law into the argument.

### Paragraph unit
**Claim → authority → analysis (why it matters / what is wrong) → counterpoint → evaluative mini-conclusion.**
End paragraphs on a judgement sentence, not a citation. Examples of the pattern:
- Commercial law: each nemo dat exception = rule + case (*Lewis v Avery*) + academic (Elliot and Battersby: the
  void/voidable line is "artificial") + counter-case (*Shogun Finance*) + "this leaves a significant gap ...".
- Medical law: "The counterargument is that ..." → next paragraph "The answer to that objection is not to
  reject all ..., but to reject an unrestricted right" → narrowed thesis grounded in the actual policy document.
- Mediation: claim → academic with page pinpoint → comparative illustration (California vs UK/Canada) →
  consequence for the thesis.
Use explicit evaluative verbs: "This is convincing because ...", "I am unpersuaded ...", "Contra X ...".

### Case law in essays
- Explain the decisions that matter properly, including the **Court's detailed reasoning** and the relevant
  paragraphs: focus on the actual contribution the Court made, with paragraph references. Vague summaries lose marks.
- Draw out **relationships between cases** (how B refines/limits A; which line prevails) and make that exploration
  central.
- Do not make points in the abstract: support each with case law (e.g. on the grey areas of *Keck*).
- Keep doctrinal steps distinct: e.g. in free movement, *justification* (Art 36 / mandatory requirements) is one
  step and *proportionality* only comes into play once a justification exists.
- Give facts only where the argument turns on them (1–2 sentences); otherwise the proposition alone.
- Address dissents, splits and obiter; be willing to criticise UKSC decisions.

### Balance
- Show both sides, then take a position: showing both sides of a complex issue is the clearest way to display
  analytical skill, especially when you then take a position. Acknowledge a disputed point and name the scholar who
  takes a different view (e.g. Colgrove).
- Take the sources further: express your own view on them.
- Reform proposals must be explained and evaluated, ideally against real examples elsewhere; explain the proposed law
  and consider existing examples around the world. Novel, specific reforms score well.
- Explain scope choices (e.g. why the focus is on tort cases).
- Comparative material (other jurisdictions, conventions) and relevant statistics score well.
- Connect sections explicitly to the central argument; do not merely assert links.

## 4. Conclusion (about 5%)
3–5 sentences: the verdict in the question's own terms, the main reason, the condition or reform. No new authority,
no new points. Return to the quotation if there was one.

## 5. Briefing papers (Biolaw format)
Concise, applied, with a worked example showing advantages and weaknesses. Still take a position, still cite the
opposing academic view, and spend words on the implications the question asks about, not on side distinctions
(e.g. a partial/complete ectogestation distinction the question does not need).

## 6. Self-check before finishing
- Does the first paragraph answer the question? Does every section advance the thesis?
- Is the case law at the heart of the title explained in detail, with relationships between cases?
- Are there named academics on both sides, and a counter-argument answered?
- Every legal proposition cited and pinpointed? Weight of authority (court, ratio/obiter, split) stated where it matters?
- Anything out of scope or peripheral cut?

## Learned from testing
- Stay within the stated word limit: budget words per section before writing, and cut secondary material (extra scholars, reform tangents, repeated reasoning) before dropping core cases or statute. Keep any required gaps list short. [2026-11-21 · ref 2026-11-21-2225/q02,q05,q08,q11,q14]
- Answer the question set even when the retrieved material lacks a leading authority or is mostly off-topic; never reframe the question to fit the sources. Where the system rules allow outside knowledge, state the established rule, tag it as they require ([background]) and analyse it; name a case or author only if you are certain it exists and decides that point. In strict or limited-material mode, analyse as far as the documents allow and say what is missing. [2026-11-21 · ref 2026-11-21-2225 (see that run's log and grades)]
- On first reliance give the full case name, with its neutral or law report citation where the documents give it or you are certain of it; otherwise the name alone. Never refer to a case by description or a party's first name. [2026-11-21 · ref 2026-11-21-2225/q02,q05,q08,q11,q14]
- In the essay body, attribute every point to its case, statute or scholar, never to 'the notes', 'the documents', 'the knowledge base' or what you have 'seen'; never quote the knowledge base's own study notes as authority; put all source limitations only in the closing 'Gaps in the knowledge base' list or the closing statement of what is missing. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- Engage with a scholar only where a retrieved passage states their argument, or where outside knowledge is allowed and you are certain of it (tagged [background]). On first mention give the author, the work's title and its year as the source shows them, and state the argument before agreeing or disagreeing. Never infer a view from a title, and list scholars you cannot support this way under Gaps. Never cite revision-guide model answers or unnamed textbooks. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- Before listing a case, statute or scholar as missing, check every retrieved passage for it and use any passage that covers it instead. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- Before relying on a judge's words, state whether they are majority ratio, obiter or dissent. In split decisions, identify the majority and the dissenters before attributing any reasoning to an individual judge. Note any later narrowing the sources show. Label non-UK cases as persuasive only and give the domestic rule alongside, or list it under Gaps. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- When the question names a statute, analyse its key provisions by section and subsection number and pair each with a case applying it where one is available, rather than resting on a single provision. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
