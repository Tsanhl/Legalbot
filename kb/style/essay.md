# Essay answers: how this student's first-class work is built

Target: a high 2:1 to first-class answer (70+). The patterns below come from marked 70–75 essays
(Law & Medicine Q3 75, PIL 75, Land Q2 74, Commercial Q2 72, Mediation 70, Pensions 71) and the model answers
(lawprof 70–74), contrasted with the 51–63 essays. Module-specific corrections are in `kb/topics/<topic>/style.md`.

## 1. Before writing: decode the question
- Separate **what the question is about** (the topic) from **what it asks** (the claim you must test)
  [Contract essay-writing lecture]. Break it into sub-questions; if it has parts (i), (ii), answer each, labelled.
- Classify it: explanatory ("what is the law?"), normative ("what should it be?") or both. Almost every question
  needs both: explain the law, then judge it.
- Identify the **centre of gravity** and make it the centre of the essay. Lost marks: "the 'use' case law was central
  to the essay title, [so] it had to be central to your essay" (EU 53, which missed *Mickelsson* and *Trailers*
  and spent words on QRs); "This was an essay about the Court's use of citizenship ... the evolution of the Court's
  reasoning ... had to be central" (EU 51); "much more of a focus on analysing the statement was required"
  (Biolaw 62, which described scheme types instead); "could engage further on the core issue of the ways the
  challenges ... should be addressed" (Biolaw 65, which spent most words describing the problem).
- If the question is a quotation, every section must be tied back to the quotation (EU 51: "how does that relate to
  the quote above?").

## 2. Introduction (5–12% of words)
Must do four things in a few sentences [Law & Med exam issues II; Contract essay lecture]:
1. Go straight to the issue. Do not open with background, a definition list, or secondary legislation
   (EU 51: "not sure it makes sense to start with the directive").
2. Define the key terms of the question ("fit for purpose" = achieves the purpose *and* is an appropriate means;
   "predictability" = ex ante anticipation of outcomes).
3. **State a qualified thesis in the first one or two sentences**, e.g. "The proposition is attractive but
   overstates the analogy ... My stance is qualified: the law should not ... but should recognise ..." (75), or
   "[X] should not be abolished but heavily altered" (model 72). A two-sided verdict is fine; a topic statement is not.
4. A roadmap of the 2–4 steps of the argument.
Bad (descriptive) opening: a list of cases and holdings with no position. Good opening: the law in one sentence
with authority, then "I shall argue that ... is challenged by A, B and C" [Law & Med exam issues II examples].

## 3. Body: 3–5 sections, analysis 55–85% of words
- **Argumentative headings**, not topic labels: "The Certainty Gap: Why the 'Critical Mass' is Stalling",
  "Rejecting *Reid*", "A glorified court order" — EU 51: "headings could have been more meaningful. Use them to convey
  your argument." Keep the number of headings proportionate: too many in a short essay fragments it (Commercial 64;
  Dissertation 68: "sections ... too brief to sustain meaningful analysis").
- Typical order: (1) short orthodox statement of the law/benchmark → (2) problems, inconsistencies, policy →
  (3) the strongest counter-argument and its rebuttal → (4) reform or reconceptualisation → conclusion.
- The higher the mark, the less free-standing description. Exposition 0–30%; fold the law into the argument.

### Paragraph unit
**Claim → authority → analysis (why it matters / what is wrong) → counterpoint → evaluative mini-conclusion.**
End paragraphs on a judgement sentence, not a citation. Examples from 70+ work:
- Commercial 72: each nemo dat exception = rule + case (*Lewis v Avery*) + academic (Elliot and Battersby: the
  void/voidable line is "artificial") + counter-case (*Shogun Finance*) + "this leaves a significant gap ...".
- Law & Medicine 75: "The counterargument is that ..." → next paragraph "The answer to that objection is not to
  reject all ..., but to reject an unrestricted right" → narrowed thesis grounded in the actual policy document.
- Mediation 70: claim → academic with page pinpoint → comparative illustration (California vs UK/Canada) →
  consequence for the thesis.
Use explicit evaluative verbs: "This is convincing because ...", "I am unpersuaded ...", "Contra X ...".

### Case law in essays
- Explain the decisions that matter properly, including the **Court's detailed reasoning** and the relevant
  paragraphs ("focus on the actual contribution that the Court made ... see especially paragraphs 55 and
  following" — EU 53). Vague summaries lose marks.
- Draw out **relationships between cases** (how B refines/limits A; which line prevails) — EU 51: "The
  exploration of the relationship between individual cases ... needs to be much more central."
- Do not make points "in the abstract": support each with case law (EU 53, on grey areas of *Keck*).
- Keep doctrinal steps distinct: e.g. in free movement, *justification* (Art 36 / mandatory requirements) is one
  step and *proportionality* only comes into play once a justification exists (EU 53).
- Give facts only where the argument turns on them (1–2 sentences); otherwise the proposition alone.
- Address dissents, splits and obiter; be willing to criticise UKSC decisions (Pensions 71; PIL 75).

### Balance
- Show both sides, then take a position: "Showing both sides of a complex issue is the clearest way to illustrate
  your analytical skills — especially when you then take a position" (Biolaw 63); "acknowledge that this is a disputed
  point — e.g. Colgrove takes a different view".
- Bring the sources "further": express your own view on them (Mediation 62).
- Reform proposals must be explained and evaluated, ideally against real examples elsewhere: "the proposed law is not
  explained nor are existing examples around the world considered" (Biolaw 65). Novel, specific reforms were praised
  (Mediation 70).
- Explain scope choices (PIL 75: "explain why the focus is on tort cases").
- Comparative material (other jurisdictions, conventions) and relevant statistics score well (Law & Med 75, Mediation 70).
- Connect sections explicitly to the central argument; do not merely assert links (Dissertation 68).

## 4. Conclusion (about 5%)
3–5 sentences: the verdict in the question's own terms, the main reason, the condition or reform. No new authority,
no new points [Contract essay lecture]. Return to the quotation if there was one.

## 5. Briefing papers (Biolaw format)
Concise, applied, with a worked example showing advantages and weaknesses (praised at 70–72). Still take a
position, still cite the opposing academic view, and spend words on the implications the question asks about,
not on side distinctions (Biolaw 63: the partial/complete ectogestation distinction was not needed).

## 6. Self-check before finishing
- Does the first paragraph answer the question? Does every section advance the thesis?
- Is the case law at the heart of the title explained in detail, with relationships between cases?
- Are there named academics on both sides, and a counter-argument answered?
- Every legal proposition cited and pinpointed? Weight of authority (court, ratio/obiter, split) stated where it matters?
- Anything off-syllabus or peripheral cut?
