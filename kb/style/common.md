# Academic writing rules (essays and problem questions)

These rules describe first-class (70+) standard in UK law assessment and follow published guidance on legal
writing and OSCOLA.

## 1. Register and language
- Formal academic English, UK spelling. **No contractions** ("isn't", "should've"): they are too informal for an
  essay.
- No colloquialisms and no flowery vocabulary. Use clear, concise sentences.
- Keep sentences short (about 2 lines) and paragraphs short (about 6 lines, 9 at most).
- No pedestrian signposting such as "This essay will now explore..." or "The next issue is..."; write like an academic
  article. A short roadmap in the introduction is fine.
- Do not rephrase or copy out the question in the text.
- Use legal terms in their legal meaning only ("intent", "reckless", "embryo", "trust"). Imprecise use loses marks:
  assault is not "threatening behaviour"; the MR for assault is not "intent to assault"; a human conceptus is no
  longer an embryo after about 8 weeks.
- Spell "judgment" for a court decision, "judgement" for an opinion.
- Write "UK", not "U.K.". No bullet points or numbered lists inside the argument of an essay: they make it read like
  a briefing note or outline. Exception: areas whose house style uses numbered headings (Pensions Law) or where the
  question asks for a briefing paper.
- Define a long term once in bold, then use the short form: **Eligible Adult Dependant** ("**EAD**"). Never
  "hereinafter".

## 2. Authority (the biggest driver of the mark)
- **Every proposition of law needs an authority** (case, statute section, regulation). Getting the law right without
  citing cases or other authority will not earn a good mark, and an answer does not improve until it applies case
  law. Give a reference for every point.
- **Never invent or mis-cite.** A non-existent case is treated as catastrophic (assume a loss of 50+ marks); an
  incorrect reference also loses heavily. If the documents do not support an authority, write
  [authority not in knowledge base].
- **State the weight of an authority**: court level, majority/dissent split, ratio vs obiter, whether overruled.
  Batting order: UKSC > CA > HC > (Pensions) Ombudsman. Even strong answers should say, for example, that the UKSC
  decision in *Palestine* was 3:2 and its remarks on the test were obiter; consider Lord Leggatt's dissent in
  *Brownlie II*; note that *Satspace* is a High Court decision and so not as definitive (or binding) as a Court of
  Appeal decision. Cite the appellate level (e.g. *Edge* in the CA, not the HC).
- **Pinpoint**: paragraph of the judgment in square brackets for E&W cases ([41]); "para 41" for CJEU cases; page
  for books/articles without paragraphs. Without pinpoints an answer is unlikely to reach a 2:1 or first, so give
  paragraph or page numbers throughout.
- **Primary law first, then academics.** Give real weight to scholarly opinion in the main body, draw on a wide range
  of academic writing, and engage with academics who take a different view. Use academics as argument partners
  (state the view, test it, accept or reject it), not as decoration: do not string citations together without
  engaging with them. Group like-minded authors; contrast opposing ones.
- Name academics without titles: "Miller argues ...", not "Professor Miller of Harvard".
- Do not refer to a case by an ambiguous short name ("*Commission*"): say *Commission v Italy* or "the *Trailers* case".
  Check that the names of judges, Advocates General and authors are right.
- Explain what a provision *says* before relying on it: "The principle of autonomy is given effect by s 4(6)" is not
  enough; say what s 4(6) requires.
- Words defined in legislation do not automatically apply to a trust deed or contract unless the document says so.
- Use the most specific provision. A common error is the right statute but the wrong or missing section
  (SGA s 18 r 2, CRA s 29, SGA s 28, Factors Act; PA 2004 s 255; Modification Regs 2006 reg 2).

## 3. OSCOLA (in chat answers)
- Case names in italics: *Braganza v BP Shipping* [2015] UKSC 17, [2015] 1 WLR 1661 [41]. Neutral citation, then
  best report (Law Reports > WLR/All ER > specialist series). Failing to italicise case names is a common error.
- Statutes: "Pensions Act 1995, s 34" (no full stop after s); regulations: "..., reg 4". EU: "Article 34 TFEU".
- Pensions Ombudsman determinations: *S Mr* [24696] Pensions Ombudsman Determination (30 December 2019);
  italicise the name only.
- Quotations in single quotes; over three lines, indent without quotes. Footnotes end with a full stop.
- In chat, cite inline in the text (plus the [S#] label). Only give footnotes/bibliography if the user asks for a
  footnoted, assessment-format answer; then the bibliography lists only sources actually cited, as Table of cases /
  Table of legislation / Other sources. For timed assessments (e.g. criminal law), in-text citation of the case name is
  enough.

## 4. Relevance and word budget
- Answer the question set, not the question you would have liked to be asked.
- Stay within the scope of the question: peripheral material is wasted words (e.g. inchoate offences or
  secondary/accessorial liability in a criminal problem that does not raise them; mental health law or
  negligence-based consent claims in a medical law question on capacity and consent).
- Spend words where the analysis is hardest, not on easy points (e.g. a simple easement issue). Dispose of dead-end
  claims in 2–3 sentences (e.g. a common intention constructive trust that clearly does not arise).
- Use the word allowance: answers hundreds of words short of the limit tend to be superficial.
- Respect any word limit given (within it, not over). Fewer points in greater depth beat many short sections.

## 5. Confidence
- Commit to conclusions. Avoid "likely/unlikely", "clearly", "obviously" as substitutes for reasoning. Do not hide
  behind the jury: make up your mind. Do not assert that something is clear or evident; show it. Where the law is
  genuinely unsettled, say so, give both readings, then pick one and say why.
- No fence-sitting on the decisive point.

## First-class standard (from the marking guide)

Aim every essay and problem answer at a 70+ mark:
- Answer the exact question in the first paragraph: a thesis, or the bottom line.
- Give a short roadmap, and signpost how each section serves the thesis.
- Cite only authority you are sure of, with exact sections, courts and pinpoints. Never invent a case or section.
- Use cases for the principles they decide, and say whether a point is ratio, obiter or dissent.
- Engage named scholars and official reports: agree, qualify or rebut, with reasons.
- State the strongest counter-argument fairly and answer it.
- Say where the law is unsettled, then commit to a reasoned position.
- In problem questions, cover every issue and party, apply the law to the exact facts and figures, and flag missing facts.
- Use the current, in-force, post-Brexit law for the right UK jurisdiction.
- Write continuous analytical prose with a few points in depth: no bullet-point essays and no padding.
- End with a conclusion that follows from the analysis (or with practical next steps), and keep to the requested length.

## Accuracy checklist (recurring errors found in testing)

Most marks lost in testing came from these errors, whatever the model. Check each before finishing:
- **Attribution.** Tie a proposition to a case, judge or paragraph only where a retrieved passage shows that case, judge or paragraph saying it. If you know the principle but not who said it, state the principle without the name or pinpoint. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Majority v dissent.** Before using a judge's words, check they were in the majority. Never present a dissent (or a losing party's argument) as the holding. A court's formation does not make a ruling more or less binding. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Section numbers.** Cite the exact subsection only where the retrieved text shows it; otherwise cite the section or Act. Never attach the consequence of one provision to another (e.g. an offence or remedy from a neighbouring section). [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Thresholds in the authority's words.** State tests at their real level: "significant influence", not "substantial"; "reasonable belief" where the statute says so; "dishonesty", not "knowledge", for accessory liability. Do not harden or soften a test, and do not overstate a holding ("readily", "always", "only"). [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Current law.** Check the provision is still in force and in its current wording. Since 1 January 2024 retained EU law is "assimilated law". Note repeals and recent amendments, and post-Brexit changes. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Special regimes first.** Before the general analysis, check for a protective or special rule that decides the point on these facts (consumer rules, pregnancy and family-leave protections, exclusive jurisdiction clauses under Hague 2005, statutory time limits). [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Facts.** Apply only the facts given. Where a condition turns on facts not stated (dates, holding periods, wording of a clause), say what must be checked instead of assuming it is met or failed. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Figures.** Work any calculation step by step from the stated figures, re-check it, and carry the same figure through to the bottom line. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Contrary material.** If a retrieved passage cuts against your conclusion, deal with it; do not ignore it. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Quotations.** Use quotation marks only for words copied exactly from a retrieved passage; otherwise paraphrase. Do not modernise quoted wording. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Citations.** Give names, years and report citations as the sources show them (party order, full names); if unsure of a citation, give the case name only. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Procedure.** Say whether a procedural step is mandatory or optional, and who brings which claim (e.g. office-holder claims, a firm's own obligations). [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
- **Consistency.** Before finishing, check that no two statements in the answer contradict each other. [2026-11-24 · ref eval/reviews/2026-11-24-manual.md]
