# Academic writing rules (essays and problem questions)

Distilled from marked coursework (grades 50–75) and published assessment guidance.
Every rule below traces to a marker comment or a module handout; the source is in brackets.

## 1. Register and language
- Formal academic English, UK spelling. **No contractions** ("isn't", "should've") [EU 53: "Avoid contractions in
  essays. It is too informal."; Criminal formative lecture].
- No colloquialisms, no flowery vocabulary: "Stick to clear and concise sentences" [Land formative feedforward].
- Sentences short (about 2 lines); paragraphs short (about 6 lines, 9 at most) [Pensions risk checklist].
- No pedestrian signposting such as "This essay will now explore..." or "The next issue is..."; write like an academic
  article [essay tips; Criminal formative lecture]. A short roadmap in the introduction is fine.
- Do not rephrase or copy out the question in the text [tips].
- Use legal terms in their legal meaning only ("intent", "reckless", "embryo", "trust"). Imprecise use loses marks:
  assault is not "threatening behaviour"; MR for assault is not "intent to assault" [Criminal lecture];
  "you can't call a human conceptus an embryo post about 8 weeks" [Biolaw BP 63].
- Spell "judgment" for a court decision, "judgement" for an opinion [Contract exam skills].
- Write "UK", not "U.K." [Biolaw BP 70]. No bullet points or numbered lists inside the argument of an essay:
  they make it "read like a briefing note or outline" [Dissertation 68]. Exception: modules that require numbered
  headings (Pensions Law) or where the question asks for a briefing paper.
- Define a long term once in bold, then use the short form: **Eligible Adult Dependant** ("**EAD**"). Never
  "hereinafter" [Pensions formative feedback].

## 2. Authority (the biggest mark-driver in the feedback)
- **Every proposition of law needs an authority** (case, statute section, regulation). "Even if you get the law right,
  if you do not cite cases or other authority you will not get a good mark" [Criminal lecture]; "unless you apply
  case law, your grade is not going to improve" [Trusts formative 60]; "You need a reference for that point"
  [Biolaw 62, twice].
- **Never invent or mis-cite.** A non-existent case is treated as catastrophic (assume a loss of 50+ marks); an
  incorrect reference also loses heavily [OSCOLA checklist]. If the documents do not support an authority, write
  [authority not in knowledge base].
- **State the weight of an authority**: court level, majority/dissent split, ratio vs obiter, whether overruled.
  Batting order: UKSC > CA > HC > (Pensions) Ombudsman [Pensions feedback]. Markers at 70+ still asked for: "the UKSC
  in Palestine was a 3:2 decision ... both very much in the obiter dicta category" [Pensions 71]; "consider Lord
  Leggatt's dissenting opinions in Brownlie II" [PIL 75]; "Satspace is purely a HC decision, and not as definitive
  (or binding) as a CA decision" [Mediation 62]. Cite the appellate level (e.g. *Edge* in the CA, not the HC).
- **Pinpoint**: paragraph of the judgment in square brackets for E&W cases ([41]); "para 41" for CJEU cases; page
  for books/articles without paragraphs. Without pinpointing a coursework mark is unlikely to start with 6 or 7
  [Pensions feedback; PIL 75: "paragraph or page numbers ... sometimes not provided"].
- **Primary law first, then academics.** Markers at every level asked for more scholarship: "greater emphasis on
  scholarly opinion" [Competition 59]; "use more scholars' opinions ... in the main body" [Commercial 64]; "a
  wider range of academic writings" [PIL 75]; "engage with academics who take a different view" [Biolaw 63].
  Use academics as argument partners (state the view, test it, accept or reject it) — not as decoration:
  "Don't string up citations like pearls on a chain" [tips]. Group like-minded authors; contrast opposing ones.
- Name academics without titles: "Miller argues ...", not "Professor Miller of Harvard" [tips].
- Do not refer to a case by an ambiguous short name ("*Commission*"): say *Commission v Italy* or "the *Trailers* case"
  [EU 53]. Check names are right [EU 53: "are you sure that this was his name?"].
- Explain what a provision *says* before relying on it: "The principle of autonomy is given effect by s 4(6)" is not
  enough — say what s 4(6) requires [Law & Medicine exam issues II].
- Words defined in legislation do not automatically apply to a trust deed or contract unless the document says so
  [Pensions feedback].
- Use the most specific provision. Several lost marks were for the right statute but the wrong/missing section
  (SGA s 18 r 2, CRA s 29, SGA s 28, Factors Act; PA 2004 s 255; Modification Regs 2006 reg 2).

## 3. OSCOLA (in chat answers)
- Case names in italics: *Braganza v BP Shipping* [2015] UKSC 17, [2015] 1 WLR 1661 [41]. Neutral citation, then
  best report (Law Reports > WLR/All ER > specialist series). A significant number of scripts "failed to italicise
  case names" [Land formative].
- Statutes: "Pensions Act 1995, s 34" (no full stop after s); regulations: "..., reg 4". EU: "Article 34 TFEU".
- Pensions Ombudsman determinations: *S Mr* [24696] Pensions Ombudsman Determination (30 December 2019) —
  italicise the name only.
- Quotations in single quotes; over three lines, indent without quotes. Footnotes end with a full stop.
- In chat, cite inline in the text (plus the [S#] label). Only give footnotes/bibliography if the user asks for a
  coursework-format answer; then the bibliography lists only sources actually cited [Land 57], as Table of cases /
  Table of legislation / Other sources [OSCOLA checklist]. For timed exams (e.g. criminal law), in-text citation of
  the case name is enough [Criminal lecture; Contract exam skills].

## 4. Relevance and word budget
- Answer the question set, "not the question that you would like to have been asked" [Law & Med exam issues II].
- Stay inside the syllabus: off-syllabus material is "wasted words" (inchoate offences, secondary/accessorial
  liability in criminal law; mental health law in law & medicine; negligence-based consent claims) [Criminal 50,
  Criminal exam 59; Law & Med exam issues].
- Spend words where the analysis is hardest, not on easy points [Land 57: "significant word count on the
  relatively simple issue (Zahid's easement)"]. Dispose of dead-end claims in 2–3 sentences [Land 65: "You did not
  really need to cover common intention constructive trusts"].
- Use the word allowance: answers "hundreds of words" short were "very superficial" [Land formative].
- Respect any word limit given (within it, not over). Fewer, deeper points beat many short sections [Dissertation 68:
  "fewer points in greater depth"].

## 5. Confidence
- Commit to conclusions. Avoid "likely/unlikely", "clearly", "obviously" as substitutes for reasoning [Pensions risk
  checklist; Criminal lecture: "Do not hide behind the jury – make up your mind"; "It is not clear or evident – you
  have to show us"]. Where the law is genuinely unsettled, say so, give both readings, then pick one and say why
  [Pensions feedback: "There are a number of valid answers ... The law is unsettled"].
- No "fence-sitting" on the decisive point [Law & Med 72].
