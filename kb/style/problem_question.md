# Problem-question answers: how this student's first-class work is built

Target 70+. Patterns from the best marked problem answers (Law & Medicine Q1 68 with most parts
"Excellent", Land Q4 70, Trusts parts a and c "very good/excellent", Commercial Q6 72) and the lawprof model answers
(70–74), contrasted with Criminal 50, Commercial Q5 60, Land formative 57, Trusts formative 60, Land Q6 65.

"Addressing a problem question is about (1) identifying the legal issues arising; (2) identifying the relevant legal
principles ...; and (3) applying those legal principles to the facts" [Land formative feedforward].

## 1. Before writing
- Read the call of the question: whom are you advising, about what? Answer that and only that. Lost marks: advising
  the wrong person ("This should have been Lila, not Ibrahim. The question is about liability that will burden the
  estate" — Trusts 68); discussing an out-of-scope party ("Nikita – not assessed"; "Who is carol?" — Criminal 50).
- **Identify the governing regime first** — it decides everything: registered vs unregistered land ("Applies
  unregistered land principles when this was a registered land scenario" = weaker answer); consumer (CRA 2015) vs
  business sale (SGA 1979) — Commercial Q5 60 missed CRA s 29 for the consumer buyer; purpose vs beneficiary trust
  (Trusts 60: "be particularly careful when distinguishing purpose and beneficiary trusts"; "This would not be a
  charitable trust but a beneficiary trust").
- List every interest/claim/offence raised by the facts, including the quiet ones. Lost marks for missing: the option
  to purchase and the consent agreement (Land formative: "Many students forgot ..."), overreaching ("a key
  omission"), transferred malice (Criminal 50), duress (Criminal exam), secret trust ("the best claim ... might very
  well be via a secret trust" — Trusts 68), subrogation and knowing receipt (Trusts 68), "trust of successive
  interests" (Trusts 60).
- List the **certain, ambiguous and missing facts** [Pensions risk checklist].

## 2. Structure
- **No essay-style introduction or conclusion.** At most one framing sentence or a numbered list of the issues
  [Criminal lecture: "Do not add introductions and conclusions ... a waste of word count. Go straight to the offence
  examination."]. No mini-intro before each offence, no "the next issue is", no "we must establish AR and MR".
- **Headings by party (or party v party), then by issue** — "follow the structure suggested in the question e.g.
  sections for each party you are advising" [Land formative]. Order: as the question lists them, or chronologically
  where priority depends on time (Land Q4: each claimant against the 2021 lender, then the 2024 lender).
- Within each claim use the logical order of the law, e.g.
  - Property: (1) what right is it? (2) validly created (formalities)? (3) does it bind the third party (registration,
    notice, overriding, overreaching)? (4) remedy/outcome.
  - Crime: offence defined (statute/case) → actus reus element by element (how courts interpret it → facts) → mens rea
    element by element, specifying direct/oblique intention or recklessness → defences → conclusion [Criminal lecture].
  - Trusts: certainties one by one → constitution → formalities → beneficiary principle/perpetuity → validity.
  - Medical law: capacity (MCA ss 1–3) → advance decision (exists s 24, valid s 25(2), applicable s 25(4)–(6), effect s 26)
    → best interests (s 4) → action [Law & Med exam issues I].
- **Mini-conclusion at the end of every party/issue** ("Conclusion: Beppe's interest does not bind BABS. It may bind
  NFB if ..."). Optionally a one-sentence-per-party final summary; never "this is for the court to decide"
  [Contract exam skills].
- Pensions Law requires lettered/numbered headings (Part I / A / 1 / 1.1 / (a)) [Pensions feedback].

## 3. The IRAC loop per issue (application 45–60% of words)
**Issue → rule with authority → application to each relevant fact → mini-conclusion.** Never a block of law followed
by a short paragraph of application [Contract exam skills]. "Remember to apply the law, not just state it" [Trusts
formative lecture]. "Repeated or rehearsed the problem question facts rather than critique and analysing them"
= weaker answer [Land formative].
- **Apply the complete test.** Partial tests are the most common error in the feedback: "The test in Woollin has a
  two part application – you have not dealt with both"; "really serious harm is also a part of the definition";
  "You should aim to address or rule out each element ... eg direct intention" (Criminal 50); "Only applied partial
  legal tests ... e.g. section 29 LRA 2002"; overlooked the exceptions in Sch 3 para 2 (Land formative); "You forgot to
  address capriciousness" (Trusts 60); missed the presumption of reliance and the effect of living rent-free (Land 65).
- **Set out the rule according to precedent and apply the case**: "You should always apply relevant case law. Like
  Boyce v Boyce"; "you need to set out the relevant rules according to precedent" (Trusts 60).
- **Reason towards the conclusion; show the reasoning.** Not "D clearly had intention" — show how the element is met
  using how the courts interpret it and the facts [Criminal lecture]. "Why would a skid cause the apprehension, why is
  this not direct intention?" (Criminal 50).
- **Level of harm / seriousness must be argued** with case law and (crime) the CPS charging standards, not asserted
  ("A broken nose constitutes really serious harm" = common mistake).
- **Draw parallels and distinctions with case facts** where the outcome is contested ("discussing the facts of
  applicable cases and drawing parallels with the problem at hand, as well as drawing in academic commentary" — Land 57).
  E.g. *XB* (specific advance decision upheld) contrasted with a vague letter; *Stribling* distinguished on the facts.
- **Causation**: address it when loss is claimed ("are the losses really caused by the breaches you identified?" —
  Trusts 68); skip it when clearly not in issue [Criminal lecture].
- Keep doctrinal categories straight: proprietary defences vs the personal knowing-receipt claim; change of position
  is a common law defence; *Roscoe v Winder* (lowest intermediate balance) only applies when the account was drawn
  down then replenished (Trusts 68). Fiduciary vs non-fiduciary duties should be distinguished (Trusts 68).
- Run the strongest rules first and say which claim is best (Pensions: power to pay → improper purpose → conflicts →
  irrelevant factors/Wednesbury, which "leads to little more than the decision being retaken").
- First-class answers also criticise doctrine inside the advice where it matters ("this line of authority is mistaken,
  but it binds, so run the constructive trust argument").

## 4. Facts, assumptions and alternatives
- **Drill into the facts**: "Are there gaps in the facts? ... What more would a court want to know?" [Land formative].
  Name each missing fact, say why it matters, then either make a stated reasonable assumption or run both branches:
  "If she has capacity ... If she lacks capacity ...", "If I am wrong and ...", "In the alternative ...".
  Praised spots: whether the scheme is a registered pension scheme (s 67 PA 1995 does not apply otherwise); whether
  members consented; that the survey covered only active members [Pensions feedback].
- Choose assumptions strategically: too easy gives fewer marks, too complicated runs out of words [Pensions feedback].
- Where registration/notice is not stated, say so and give both outcomes ("If it is unclear from the facts, make this
  clear!") [Land formative].
- Commit to the likely outcome on each branch; no fence-sitting.

## 5. Remedies and advice
- Work remedies through, not just name them: which measure (expectation vs detriment; substitutive compensation;
  falsify vs surcharge), proportionality on the facts, proprietary vs personal claims, priority on insolvency,
  defences (s 61 TA 1925, limitation, exemption clauses), apportionment between trustees.
- Give the client practical steps: record a capacity assessment; seek a declaration / Court of Protection; register a
  restriction or notice; seek directions; consult employees where required.
- Dispose of weak routes briefly and rank the claims ("Her strongest claim lies in ...").

## 6. Word allocation
Introduction 0–5%; rules about 30–35%; application 45–60%; remedies/advice 5–15%; conclusions are mini-conclusions.
Balance exposition with "analysis of the contentious elements of the question" — spend the words on the contested
facts (e.g. the nature of the tackle, levels of harm, the defences) [Criminal 50], not the easy issue [Land 57].

## 7. Self-check
- Right client, right regime, every party required by the question, nothing out of scope?
- Every element of every test addressed or expressly ruled out, with authority?
- Missing facts named, assumptions stated, alternatives run?
- A committed mini-conclusion for every issue and practical advice for the client?
