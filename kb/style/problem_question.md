# Problem-question answers: how first-class work is built

Target 70+. The patterns below describe first-class (70+) standard, contrasted with the weaknesses typical of
lower 2:1 and 2:2 answers.

Addressing a problem question means (1) identifying the legal issues arising; (2) identifying the relevant legal
principles; and (3) applying those principles to the facts.

## 1. Before writing
- Read the call of the question: whom are you advising, about what? Answer that and only that. Typical ways to lose
  marks: advising the wrong person (e.g. advising a beneficiary when the question is about liability that will burden
  the estate); discussing a party the question does not ask about.
- **Identify the governing regime first**; it decides everything: registered vs unregistered land (applying
  unregistered land principles to a registered land scenario makes a weaker answer); consumer (CRA 2015) vs
  business sale (SGA 1979), e.g. CRA s 29 for a consumer buyer; purpose vs beneficiary trust (distinguish them
  carefully: a gift for people "as T sees fit" is a beneficiary trust, not a charitable one).
- List every interest/claim/offence raised by the facts, including the quiet ones. Commonly missed: an option to
  purchase and a consent agreement; overreaching; transferred malice; duress; a secret trust (which may be the best
  claim); subrogation and knowing receipt; a trust of successive interests.
- List the **certain, ambiguous and missing facts**.

## 2. Structure
- **No essay-style introduction or conclusion.** At most one framing sentence or a numbered list of the issues.
  Introductions and conclusions waste words; go straight to the examination of the offence or claim. No mini-intro
  before each offence, no "the next issue is", no "we must establish AR and MR".
- **Headings by party (or party v party), then by issue**, following the structure the question suggests (e.g. a
  section for each party you are advising). Order: as the question lists them, or chronologically where priority
  depends on time (e.g. each claimant against the earlier lender, then the later lender).
- Within each claim use the logical order of the law, e.g.
  - Property: (1) what right is it? (2) validly created (formalities)? (3) does it bind the third party (registration,
    notice, overriding, overreaching)? (4) remedy/outcome.
  - Crime: offence defined (statute/case) → actus reus element by element (how courts interpret it → facts) → mens rea
    element by element, specifying direct/oblique intention or recklessness → defences → conclusion.
  - Trusts: certainties one by one → constitution → formalities → beneficiary principle/perpetuity → validity.
  - Medical law: capacity (MCA ss 1–3) → advance decision (exists s 24, valid s 25(2), applicable s 25(4)–(6), effect s 26)
    → best interests (s 4) → action.
- **Mini-conclusion at the end of every party/issue** ("Conclusion: B's interest does not bind the first lender. It
  may bind the second lender if ..."). Optionally a one-sentence-per-party final summary; never "this is for the
  court to decide".
- Pensions Law answers use lettered/numbered headings (Part I / A / 1 / 1.1 / (a)).

## 3. The IRAC loop per issue (application 45–60% of words)
**Issue → rule with authority → application to each relevant fact → mini-conclusion.** Never a block of law followed
by a short paragraph of application. Apply the law; do not just state it. Repeating or rehearsing the facts instead
of analysing them makes a weaker answer.
- **Apply the complete test.** Partial tests are the most common error: the *Woollin* test has two parts and both
  must be dealt with; really serious harm is part of the definition of GBH; address or rule out each element (e.g.
  direct intention); apply all of s 29 LRA 2002, including the exceptions in Sch 3 para 2; address capriciousness for
  a purpose trust; in proprietary estoppel, deal with the presumption of reliance and the effect of benefits such as
  living rent-free.
- **Set out the rule according to precedent and apply the case**: always apply the relevant case law (e.g.
  *Boyce v Boyce* on certainty of subject matter).
- **Reason towards the conclusion; show the reasoning.** Not "D clearly had intention": show how the element is met
  using how the courts interpret it and the facts. Ask, for example, why an act would cause apprehension, and why it
  is or is not direct intention.
- **Level of harm / seriousness must be argued** with case law and (crime) the CPS charging standards, not asserted
  ("A broken nose constitutes really serious harm" is a common mistake).
- **Draw parallels and distinctions with case facts** where the outcome is contested: discuss the facts of applicable
  cases, draw parallels with the problem, and bring in academic commentary. E.g. *XB* (specific advance decision
  upheld) contrasted with a vague letter; *Stribling* distinguished on the facts.
- **Causation**: address it when loss is claimed (are the losses really caused by the breaches identified?); skip it
  when clearly not in issue.
- Keep doctrinal categories straight: proprietary defences vs the personal knowing-receipt claim; change of position
  is a common law defence; *Roscoe v Winder* (lowest intermediate balance) only applies when the account was drawn
  down then replenished. Distinguish fiduciary from non-fiduciary duties.
- Run the strongest rules first and say which claim is best (Pensions: power to pay → improper purpose → conflicts →
  irrelevant factors/Wednesbury, which leads to little more than the decision being retaken).
- First-class answers also criticise doctrine inside the advice where it matters ("this line of authority is mistaken,
  but it binds, so run the constructive trust argument").

## 4. Facts, assumptions and alternatives
- **Drill into the facts**: are there gaps? What more would a court want to know? Name each missing fact, say why it
  matters, then either make a stated reasonable assumption or run both branches: "If she has capacity ... If she
  lacks capacity ...", "If I am wrong and ...", "In the alternative ...". Good examples: whether the scheme is a
  registered pension scheme (s 67 PA 1995 does not apply otherwise); whether members consented; whether a survey
  covered only active members.
- Choose assumptions strategically: too easy gives fewer marks, too complicated runs out of words.
- Where registration/notice is not stated, say so and give both outcomes.
- Commit to the likely outcome on each branch; no fence-sitting.

## 5. Remedies and advice
- Work remedies through, not just name them: which measure (expectation vs detriment; substitutive compensation;
  falsify vs surcharge), proportionality on the facts, proprietary vs personal claims, priority on insolvency,
  defences (s 61 TA 1925, limitation, exemption clauses), apportionment between trustees.
- Give the client practical steps: record a capacity assessment; seek a declaration / Court of Protection; register a
  restriction or notice; seek directions; consult employees where required.
- Dispose of weak routes briefly and rank the claims ("Her strongest claim lies in ...").

## 6. Word allocation
Introduction 0–5%; rules about 30–35%; application 45–60%; remedies/advice 5–15%; conclusions are mini-conclusions.
Balance exposition with analysis of the contentious elements of the question: spend the words on the contested
facts (e.g. the nature of a tackle, levels of harm, the defences), not the easy issue.

## 7. Self-check
- Right client, right regime, every party required by the question, nothing out of scope?
- Every element of every test addressed or expressly ruled out, with authority?
- Missing facts named, assumptions stated, alternatives run?
- A committed mini-conclusion for every issue and practical advice for the client?

## Learned from testing
- Never leave an issue unanalysed because a source is missing. Where the system rules allow outside knowledge, state the well-known rule, naming the leading case only if you are certain of it, tag it [background] and apply it. In strict or limited-material mode, analyse as far as the documents allow and say what is missing. [2026-11-21 · ref 2026-11-21-2159/q03]
- If any part of the answer comes from general knowledge or the web, make the first line the one-line notice saying so. Tag every sentence drawn from general knowledge [background], and never drop tags to look more confident. Never tag remarks about what the knowledge base contains or lacks. Put an [S#] label only on the proposition that passage actually supports, and only for passages actually retrieved; never invent labels. Keep the required 'Gaps in the knowledge base' list short and at the end. Keep every other internal artefact out of the advice: no mention of answer rules, internal notes, study-problem names or comments on the sources (e.g. 'the notes date this case 1965'). - Never misstate the standard of proof. The prosecution must prove every element so the jury is sure (beyond reasonable doubt), except where the defendant bears a legal burden (e.g. diminished responsibility, insanity: balance of probabilities). Civil claims are decided on the balance of probabilities. [2026-11-21 · ref 2026-11-21-2159; 2026-11-21-2214; 2026-11-21-2225 (see that run's log and grades)]
- State well-established law confidently, naming an authority only where the documents give it or, where outside knowledge is allowed, you are certain of it. Before concluding on an issue, use every retrieved leading or appellate authority on it for the point it governs, ahead of secondary notes. Distinguish on a named fact any authority that cuts against the client, or concede its effect; never conclude against the weight of the authorities you cite without naming the distinguishing fact. Expressly compare any authority with closely matching facts and say how the outcome transfers. Where a conclusion turns on unstated facts or procedural mechanics, say what it turns on, run the alternative and still commit to the more likely result.
