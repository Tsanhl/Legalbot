# Residence and the post-April-2025 foreign income and gains regime

## Overview
Since 6 April 2025 UK tax on overseas income and gains no longer turns on domicile. Residence decides liability, and the old remittance basis has gone. New arrivals who have been non-resident for ten consecutive tax years may claim a four-year exemption on foreign income and gains (the "FIG regime"). Inheritance tax has also moved to a residence-based test (long-term UK residence). Residence itself is still decided by the statutory residence test (SRT).

## The law

### Statutory residence test (Finance Act 2013, s 218 and Sch 45)
The SRT decides residence for income tax and CGT (and for IHT and corporation tax where those taxes ask whether an individual is resident). It is applied tax year by tax year. Work in this order:
1. **Automatic overseas tests** (if any is met, the person is non-resident):
   - resident in one or more of the previous three tax years and present in the UK for fewer than 16 days;
   - resident in none of the previous three years and present for fewer than 46 days;
   - works full time overseas (average 35 hours a week, no significant breaks) and is present in the UK for fewer than 91 days, with fewer than 31 days on which they work in the UK for more than three hours.
2. **Automatic UK tests** (if any is met, and no overseas test was met, the person is resident):
   - 183 days or more in the UK in the year;
   - a UK home for a period of at least 91 consecutive days (at least 30 of which fall in the tax year), in which the person is present on at least 30 days in the year, and during that period either no overseas home or overseas homes in each of which they spend fewer than 30 days in the year;
   - full-time work in the UK over a 365-day period with no significant break, where more than 75% of the days on which they work more than three hours are UK work days.
3. **Sufficient ties test.** If neither set applies, count days in the UK and the UK ties. Ties for everyone: family (spouse/partner or minor child resident in UK), accommodation (a place to live available for a continuous 91 days and used for at least one night in the year), work (40 days of more than three hours' UK work), and the 90-day tie (more than 90 days in either of the previous two years). "Leavers" (resident in at least one of the previous three years) also have the country tie (more midnights in the UK than in any other single country). Leavers: 16-45 days, four ties needed; 46-90 days, three; 91-120 days, two; 121-182 days, one. Arrivers (not resident in any of the previous three years): 46-90 days, all four ties; 91-120 days, three; 121-182 days, two (the country tie is not counted).
4. A day counts if the person is in the UK at midnight. Days in transit and up to 60 days for exceptional circumstances are disregarded; but a "deeming rule" counts extra days (where present at some point but not at midnight) for a person with at least three ties who was resident in at least one of the previous three tax years and has more than 30 such days.
5. **Split year treatment** (Sch 45 Pt 3, eight cases) divides a year of arrival or departure into a UK part and an overseas part for income tax and CGT. The person is still UK resident for the year as a whole; the split year only changes how income and gains of the overseas part are taxed.

### From the remittance basis to the arising basis
Until 5 April 2025, a UK-resident but non-UK-domiciled person could claim the remittance basis, paying tax on foreign income and gains only when remitted to the UK. Finance Act 2025 (s 40 and Sch 9) ended the remittance basis after 2024-25, and domicile is no longer the connecting factor for income tax and CGT. Everyone UK resident is now taxed on worldwide income and gains as they arise, subject to the regime below.

### The four-year FIG regime (FA 2025 ss 37-39)
- **Who:** an individual who becomes UK resident after at least ten consecutive tax years of non-residence.
- **What:** for the first four tax years of UK residence, foreign income and foreign gains are relieved from UK tax, whether or not they are brought to the UK. It covers foreign income (including offshore income gains), foreign chargeable gains, and distributions and benefits from non-resident trusts.
- **Claim:** made year by year in the Self Assessment return. A claim costs the person the personal allowance and the CGT annual exempt amount for that year, and the amounts claimed must be quantified. Because the claim is annual, a taxpayer can choose not to claim in years where the cost outweighs the benefit.
- **Transitional rule:** those already in their first four years of residence on 6 April 2025 (after ten years of non-residence) can use the remainder of the four years.
- **Overseas workday relief:** relief for employment income from duties performed abroad, available for the first three tax years of residence to those who qualify for the FIG regime, with no requirement to keep the earnings offshore. The relief is capped at the lower of £300,000 and 30% of the qualifying employment income.
- **Temporary Repatriation Facility (TRF, FA 2025 s 41 and Sch 10):** former remittance-basis users can designate foreign income and gains that arose before 6 April 2025 and pay a flat charge instead of the full rate. The charge is 12% for designations in 2025-26 and 2026-27, and 15% in 2027-28; the facility then closes.
- **CGT rebasing (FA 2025 s 42 and Sch 11):** individuals who claimed the remittance basis in at least one year from 2017-18 to 2024-25, and were not UK domiciled or deemed domiciled, can rebase foreign assets held on 5 April 2017 to their value on that date for disposals on or after 6 April 2025.

### IHT: long-term UK residence (FA 2025 ss 44-46 and Sch 13)
From 6 April 2025, IHT is chargeable on worldwide assets if the individual is a "long-term UK resident" (LTR): UK resident for at least 10 of the previous 20 tax years. Domicile no longer determines this. After leaving the UK an LTR stays in scope for a "tail": three years if they were UK resident for 10 to 13 of the previous 20 years, rising by one year for each additional year of residence, to a maximum of 10 years. A non-LTR is chargeable only on UK-situated assets (and certain UK-linked interests). Whether settled property is excluded property now depends on the settlor's LTR status when a charge arises, with transitional protection for some trusts made before 30 October 2024.

## Key authorities
- *R (Davies and James) v HMRC; R (Gaines-Cooper) v HMRC* [2011] UKSC 47 (Supreme Court, Lord Mance dissenting): pre-SRT residence case. HMRC's former guidance (IR20), properly read, required a distinct break in the pattern of UK life for a person to become non-resident, so the taxpayers had no legitimate expectation of being treated as non-resident on a day-count alone. Of historical interest; the SRT replaced the case-law test from 2013-14.
- The SRT is statutory; the decisions on applying the ties and day-count are tribunal decisions that turn on their facts. HMRC's guidance (RDR3, "Statutory Residence Test") is the working reference, though it is not law.

## Applying it
1. Decide residence for each tax year with the SRT, in the order above. Do not skip to ties.
2. For a newcomer: check ten years of non-residence, then the FIG claim, whether to claim (loss of allowances), and which income is "foreign".
3. For a former remittance-basis user: consider the TRF window (closes after 2027-28), rebasing and the effect on trusts.
4. Common traps: treating domicile as still relevant for income tax; forgetting the midnight rule and the deeming rule; forgetting that the FIG claim has to be made each year; ignoring IHT's LTR tail when advising a person who intends to leave.
5. Everyday question: "I'm moving abroad, am I still a UK taxpayer?" depends on days and ties; UK-source income (for example UK rental income) remains taxable even if non-resident, and UK land gains are taxable for non-residents.

## Debates and reform
- The reform was announced in March 2024 (Spring Budget) and implemented by the new government in Finance Act 2025, with changes to transitional rules (notably IHT and excluded property trusts) following consultation. Critics argue it risks an outflow of wealthy individuals; supporters point to fairness and revenue.
- The ten-year non-residence qualifier and the limited four-year period are seen as generous to genuinely new arrivals but harsh on long-stayers.
- The SRT is criticised as complex, though it gave far more certainty than the earlier case-law test.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Remittance basis abolished and FIG regime effective 6 April 2025 (Finance Act 2025); technical amendments in Finance Act 2026 Sch 3.
- TRF: 12% (2025-26, 2026-27), 15% (2027-28).
- IHT residence-based rules from 6 April 2025.
