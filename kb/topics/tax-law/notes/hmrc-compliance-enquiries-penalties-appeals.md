# HMRC compliance: enquiries, discovery assessments, time limits, penalties and appeals

## Overview
HMRC checks Self Assessment returns by opening an enquiry within a short window, or, after that window, by making a discovery assessment where tax has been lost and conditions are met. Time limits lengthen with taxpayer culpability (4, 6 or 20 years, and 12 years for some offshore matters). Penalties for inaccuracy, late filing, late payment and failure to notify are largely schedule-based. Disputes go to HMRC internal review or the First-tier Tribunal (Tax Chamber).

## The law

### Returns and enquiries (Taxes Management Act 1970, TMA)
- s 8: notice to file a Self Assessment return; filing deadline: 31 October (paper) or 31 January (online) after the tax year. Tax due on 31 January (with payments on account on 31 January and 31 July).
- s 9A: HMRC may open an enquiry by notice within 12 months of the day the return was filed (if filed on time); if late, by the quarter day after the first anniversary of the filing date. HMRC does not need to give reasons. The enquiry window closes the possibility of enquiry but not discovery.
- FA 2008 Sch 36 (which replaced the old TMA s 19A power): information notices requiring information and documents; taxpayer notices (optionally approved by the tribunal) and third-party notices (which need the taxpayer's agreement or tribunal approval); penalties for non-compliance.
- s 28A: an enquiry is closed by a closure notice stating the conclusions and amending the return; the taxpayer can apply to the tribunal to direct HMRC to close it (s 28A(4)).
- s 9ZA: taxpayers may amend a return within 12 months of the filing date. s 12B: duty to keep records: for those in business or letting property, until the fifth anniversary of the 31 January filing date; otherwise until the first anniversary (longer if an enquiry is open).

### Discovery assessments (s 29)
- Where an officer discovers that income or gains which ought to have been assessed have not been assessed, or an assessment is insufficient, or a relief is excessive, HMRC can make an assessment to recover the loss of tax.
- Where the taxpayer's return has been made and no enquiry notice (or enquiry has been closed), s 29(3) restricts HMRC: discovery assessment can be made only if (s 29(4)) the situation was brought about carelessly or deliberately by the taxpayer or a person acting on their behalf, or (s 29(5)) at the time the enquiry window closed an officer could not reasonably be expected, on the information made available, to be aware of the insufficiency.
- "Information made available" is defined in s 29(6) and (7): includes the return, accompanying documents, and information from earlier returns or provided by the taxpayer, and information that an officer could reasonably be expected to infer.
- "Discovery" has been given a broad meaning: an officer newly concluding that there is an insufficiency, including on a change of view or a new officer's fresh look, will suffice; *Tooth* (below) rejects the idea that a discovery can go stale. The s 29(5) test asks what a hypothetical officer could reasonably have been expected to be aware of from the information made available (*Langham v Veltema* [2004] EWCA Civ 193).

### Time limits
- Ordinary assessment: four years from the end of the tax year (s 34).
- Careless inaccuracy: six years (s 36(1)).
- Deliberate inaccuracy: 20 years (s 36(1A)).
- Failure to notify chargeability (s 7), and losses linked to undisclosed DOTAS schemes or promoter reference numbers: 20 years (s 36(1A)(b)-(d)), whatever the behaviour.
- Offshore matters or transfers (income tax and CGT): 12 years for any non-deliberate loss of tax, careless or not (TMA s 36A, inserted by FA 2019 s 80); deliberate cases remain at 20 years. It applies to 2013-14 onwards where the loss was careless and 2015-16 onwards otherwise, and does not apply if HMRC received overseas exchange-of-information data in time to assess within the normal limit. FA 2019 s 81 makes a similar rule for IHT.
- Claims to relief are generally time-limited to four years after the end of the tax year (TMA s 43).

### Penalties
- **Inaccuracy** (FA 2007 Sch 24): if an inaccurate document leads to understatement of tax, a penalty is chargeable by reference to the "potential lost revenue" (PLR) and the behaviour: careless (failure to take reasonable care) up to 30%; deliberate but not concealed up to 70%; deliberate and concealed up to 100%. Disclosure reduces the percentage: unprompted disclosure gives the largest reduction (to 0% for careless, 20% for deliberate, 30% for deliberate and concealed); prompted disclosure gives smaller reductions (15%, 35%, 50% minimum respectively). A "prompted" disclosure is made after the taxpayer has reason to believe HMRC has discovered or is about to discover the error. Special reduction is possible (para 11). Careless (not deliberate) penalties can be suspended for up to two years on conditions (para 14). There is no penalty if the taxpayer took reasonable care. Higher maximum percentages (up to 200%) apply to offshore matters in less co-operative territories (categories 2 and 3) and further uplifts to offshore asset moves; Budget 2026 announced a consultation on reforming the offshore penalties.
- **Failure to notify** (FA 2008 Sch 41): the same maximum percentages by behaviour (30%, 70%, 100% of PLR). For non-deliberate failures disclosure can reduce the penalty to 0% (unprompted) or 10% (prompted) if HMRC is told within 12 months of the tax first becoming unpaid, otherwise to 10% or 20%.
- **Late filing** (FA 2009 Sch 55): £100 fixed penalty one day after the deadline; daily £10 after three months for up to 90 days (maximum £900); the greater of 5% of tax due or £300 at six months; the same again at 12 months, with higher percentages (up to 100% or 70% where information is withheld). Reasonable excuse (para 23) removes the liability (not insufficiency of funds, unless caused by an event outside the taxpayer's control, nor reliance on another person unless the taxpayer took reasonable care); special reduction (para 16). Taxpayers within Making Tax Digital for Income Tax (from April 2026) instead fall under the FA 2021 points-based late submission regime.
- **Late payment** (FA 2009 Sch 56, Self Assessment balancing payments; not payments on account): 5% of the unpaid tax at 30 days after the due date; a further 5% at six months; a further 5% at 12 months; reasonable excuse and special reduction apply. For VAT (from 2023) and MTD for Income Tax users (from April 2026) the FA 2021 regime applies instead: 3% at day 15, 3% more at day 30, then 10% a year. Late payment interest runs from the due date at Bank of England base rate plus 4% (from 6 April 2025).
- **Information powers** (FA 2008 Sch 36): the initial penalty £300; daily penalties up to £60 per day; up to £3,000 for each inaccuracy in information or documents provided. Budget 2026 announced reform of Sch 36 and the computer records powers, effective from Royal Assent of Finance Bill 2026-27.
- Penalties are assessed by HMRC and appealable.

### Appeals
- TMA s 31: appeal against an assessment, closure notice, amendment or penalty within 30 days of the decision (s 31A), made to HMRC; HMRC may offer a statutory review (s 49A to 49H; the taxpayer has 30 days to accept it), or the taxpayer can notify the appeal to the First-tier Tribunal (Tax Chamber) (s 49D). The tribunal can also be asked for permission to appeal late.
- First-tier Tribunal (Tax) categories: default paper, basic, standard, complex. Costs generally not awarded except in complex cases or for unreasonable behaviour (Tribunal Procedure (First-tier Tribunal) (Tax Chamber) Rules 2009, SI 2009/273, r 10).
- Onwards: the Upper Tribunal (Tax and Chancery Chamber) on a point of law with permission, then the Court of Appeal (with permission) and Supreme Court.
- Payment before appeal: disputes about assessments of tax allow postponement of tax pending determination (s 55), but not for accelerated payment notices and follower notices.
- Late appeals: the approach is that of *Martland*: the Upper Tribunal in *HMRC v Martland* [2018] UKUT 178 (TCC) set a three-stage test: seriousness and significance of the delay, reasons for it, and an evaluation of all the circumstances.

## Key authorities
- *HMRC v Tooth* [2021] UKSC 17 (SC, joint judgment of Lord Briggs and Lord Sales, unanimous): (1) a "deliberate inaccuracy" in a document (TMA s 118(7), which triggers the 20-year limit and the s 29(4) condition) requires an intention to mislead HMRC as to the truth of the statement (or possibly recklessness as to that), not merely a deliberately made statement that turns out to be wrong; Mr Tooth had explained his position in the return's white space, so there was no deliberate inaccuracy and the late discovery assessment was out of time. (2) There is no doctrine of "staleness": a discovery does not lapse because HMRC delays acting on it; the only limits are the statutory time limits. A discovery may be made by a new officer reaching a fresh conclusion.
- *Hankinson v HMRC* [2011] EWCA Civ 1566 (CA, Lewison LJ): the officer making a discovery assessment need not consider, at the time, which of the s 29(4)/(5) conditions applies; whether a condition is met is decided by the tribunal on appeal.
- *Langham v Veltema* [2004] EWCA Civ 193 (CA): under s 29(5) the hypothetical officer must have been made aware of an actual insufficiency by the information made available; information that would merely prompt further enquiry is not enough.
- *HMRC v Martland* [2018] UKUT 178 (TCC) (UT): late appeals approach; applied the three-stage test derived from *Denton v TH White Ltd* [2014] EWCA Civ 906.
- *Perrin v HMRC* [2018] UKUT 156 (TCC): four-stage approach to reasonable excuse: establish the facts said to amount to an excuse, decide which are proved, decide objectively whether they amount to a reasonable excuse for a taxpayer in that position, and whether the failure was remedied without unreasonable delay once the excuse ended.
- *R (Davies and James) v HMRC* [2011] UKSC 47: limits on legitimate expectation arising from HMRC guidance.

## Applying it
1. Identify the return and the dates: was it filed on time, when did the enquiry window close?
2. If no enquiry was opened: can HMRC make a discovery assessment? Show the s 29(4) or (5) condition: carelessness/deliberateness, or lack of reasonable awareness from the information made available.
3. Check the time limit: 4, 6, 12 or 20 years, given the behaviour.
4. Penalties: classify behaviour (careless, deliberate, concealed), compute PLR, apply the percentage and the reduction for disclosure; check reasonable excuse.
5. Late filing/payment: apply the schedule steps.
6. Appeal: 30 days; HMRC review versus tribunal; late-appeal test; the possibility of postponement.
7. Common traps: confusing "careless" with "deliberate"; saying that HMRC must give a reason for an enquiry (it need not); forgetting the reduced limit for ordinary errors; applying the old penalty regime; treating a disagreement of legal interpretation as careless if the taxpayer adopted a reasonable position; ignoring FA 2019 for offshore matters.
8. Everyday question: "HMRC has written about a tax return from five years ago. Is it too late?" The ordinary limit is four years; six if careless; 12 or 20 in some offshore or deliberate cases.

## Debates and reform
- The relative balance of powers: HMRC's information powers and penalties versus taxpayer safeguards; the Loan Charge and discovery assessments for avoidance cases attracted criticism and a review (Morse review 2019).
- Whether the penalty regime is proportionate and the 'reasonable excuse' threshold fair; the Treasury consultations on a new penalties regime for late payment and filing (the FA 2021 points-based system for VAT and Self Assessment).
- HMRC's resources and the "Making Tax Digital" programme change how quickly enquiries are carried out.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Making Tax Digital for Income Tax: mandatory from 6 April 2026 for sole traders and landlords with qualifying income above £50,000, from April 2027 above £30,000 and from April 2028 above £20,000. Budget 2026: no late submission points for quarterly updates in 2027-28 for those joining in April 2027.
- MTD users are under the FA 2021 points-based late submission and new late payment penalties; other Self Assessment taxpayers remain under FA 2009 Schs 55 and 56.
- Budget 2026 proposals: a general duty to correct errors with Customer Correction Notices (from April 2028); more regular in-year Self Assessment payments (from April 2029).
