# Tax avoidance: from Duke of Westminster to the GAAR and DOTAS

## Overview
English law has moved from a rule that taxpayers may arrange their affairs to reduce tax so long as the legal form is respected (*Duke of Westminster*) to a purposive approach under which courts ask what the statute taxes and then look at the realistic nature of the transaction (*Ramsay* and its successors, now stated as ordinary statutory interpretation: *Barclays Mercantile v Mawson*, *UBS*, *Rossendale*). Parliament has added a general anti-abuse rule (GAAR) and disclosure regimes.

## The law

### Avoidance, evasion and mitigation
- Evasion is illegal (dishonest non-disclosure or false statements; criminal offences including cheating the public revenue and offences under the Finance Acts). Avoidance uses the law to obtain a tax advantage that Parliament did not intend. Mitigation is using reliefs as intended.
- Courts do not recognise a rule against avoidance as such; the question in each case is what the legislation, construed purposively, requires.

### Development of the judicial approach
1. **Literal formalism:** a transaction is taxed by its legal form: *IRC v Duke of Westminster* [1936] AC 1 (HL) (Lord Tomlin: a person may order affairs so that tax is less than it otherwise would be).
2. **Ramsay principle:** *WT Ramsay Ltd v IRC* [1982] AC 300 (HL). Courts may consider a pre-planned series of transactions as a whole where it contains steps inserted with no commercial purpose other than tax avoidance, when the statute is seen as taxing the real transaction. *IRC v Burmah Oil Co Ltd* [1982] STC 30 (HL) applied Ramsay to a circular scheme. 
3. **Furniss v Dawson** [1984] AC 474 (HL): a step inserted into a pre-ordained series of transactions with no commercial purpose other than avoiding tax can be ignored, and the transaction treated as a single composite one. Lord Brightman's two-part test (pre-ordained series; step inserted with no business purpose) was the high-water mark of the principle. *Craven v White* [1989] AC 398 (HL) then confined it to steps that were pre-ordained with no practical likelihood that the planned series would not take place.
4. **Retreat:** *IRC v McGuckian* [1997] 1 WLR 991 (HL) and *MacNiven v Westmoreland Investments Ltd* [2001] UKHL 6, [2003] 1 AC 311, which stressed that the question is one of construing the statute, with only certain terms having a "commercial" meaning.
5. **Purposive construction:** *Barclays Mercantile Business Finance Ltd v Mawson* [2004] UKHL 51, [2005] 1 AC 684 (HL, unanimous; single opinion of the Appellate Committee delivered by Lord Nicholls). A sale and finance-leaseback of a gas pipeline, in which the purchase price was immediately placed on deposit and in effect circulated back, still qualified for capital allowances. The House restated Ramsay as a principle of statutory construction: decide the purpose of the provision, then whether the transaction, viewed realistically, falls within it. The allowance provisions looked at what the lessor did (incur expenditure on acquiring plant for its leasing trade), and it had done so; what the seller did with the money was irrelevant. The taxpayer won. Decided the same day, *IRC v Scottish Provident Institution* [2004] UKHL 52 went the other way: a composite option scheme with no practical likelihood of its contrived risk materialising was viewed as a whole and the claimed loss failed.
6. **Tower MCashback LLP 1 v HMRC** [2011] UKSC 19, [2011] 2 AC 457 (SC, lead judgment Lord Walker): LLPs claimed first-year capital allowances on software rights bought for a price 75% of which was funded by limited-recourse loans that went round in a circle back to the lenders. Distinguishing *BMBF*, the court held that, viewed realistically, only the 25% the members actually contributed was expenditure incurred on acquiring the software rights; allowances on the loan-funded 75% were denied. HMRC won.
7. **UBS AG v HMRC and DB Group Services (UK) Ltd v HMRC** [2016] UKSC 13: the share-based bonus schemes were not within the exemption from tax for "restricted securities" on a purposive construction; the Supreme Court (Lord Reed) restated the approach: identify the statute's purpose, then ask whether the facts, viewed realistically, answer the description. The courts stated that Ramsay is not a special doctrine: it is the application of the ordinary rule of interpretation.
8. **Rossendale BC v Hurstwood Properties (A) Ltd** [2021] UKSC 16 (a business rates avoidance case): applied purposive construction in the Ramsay sense; the leases to special-purpose companies did not confer the entitlement to possession needed to make the lessee the "owner" liable for empty-property rates, so the planning failed. The court did not need a freestanding "abuse" doctrine.

### General Anti-Abuse Rule (Finance Act 2013 Part 5, ss 206 to 215A, Schs 43 to 43C)
- s 206: the rule applies to income tax, corporation tax, CGT, petroleum revenue tax, IHT, SDLT, ATED and certain other taxes; it extends to NICs through the National Insurance Contributions Act 2014 s 10. Its object is to counteract tax advantages arising from "abusive" arrangements.
- s 207: arrangements are "tax arrangements" if the main purpose, or one of the main purposes, is to obtain a tax advantage; they are "abusive" if entering into or carrying them out "cannot reasonably be regarded as a reasonable course of action in relation to the relevant tax provisions", having regard to all circumstances, including whether the result is consistent with policy objectives, whether means are contrived or abnormal, and whether arrangements exploit shortcomings. This is the "double reasonableness" test; the rule is not meant to catch ordinary tax planning (see the GAAR Guidance).
- s 209: counteraction is as "just and reasonable". Procedure: HMRC must refer the case to the GAAR Advisory Panel (Sch 43) before applying it; the tribunal must take the Panel's opinion into account (s 211).
- Penalty: FA 2013 s 212A and Sch 43C: 60% of the counteracted tax advantage.
- The GAAR does not replace SAAR (specific rules), nor Ramsay.

### Disclosure and follow-up regimes
- DOTAS (Finance Act 2004 Part 7, ss 306 to 319): promoters (and in some cases users) of notifiable arrangements must disclose them to HMRC, generally within five working days of making the scheme available or becoming aware of its implementation, based on "hallmarks" in regulations (Tax Avoidance Schemes (Prescribed Descriptions of Arrangements) Regulations 2006, SI 2006/1543). HMRC issues a scheme reference number (SRN); users must report it on returns. Penalties for non-compliance.
- Promoters of Tax Avoidance Schemes (POTAS, FA 2014 Part 5 ss 234 to 283), follower notices and accelerated payments (FA 2014 Pt 4): taxpayers in schemes defeated by a final judicial ruling may receive a follower notice and must pay the disputed tax up front.
- Penalties for enablers of defeated tax avoidance (FA 2017 s 65 and Sch 16); the serial avoiders regime (FA 2016 s 159 and Sch 18); and corporate criminal offences of failure to prevent the facilitation of tax evasion (Criminal Finances Act 2017 Part 3).
- Finance Act 2022 strengthened the promoter regime (including freezing promoters' assets and naming promoters), and Finance Act 2026 Part 6 (ss 159-222) adds further anti-promoter and information powers. Budget 2026 announced a further promoter package and a consultation on new powers against serial users.

## Key authorities
- *IRC v Duke of Westminster* [1936] AC 1 (HL): form respected; avoidance legitimate unless statute says otherwise.
- *WT Ramsay Ltd v IRC* [1982] AC 300 (HL): composite transactions; tax follows the reality (Lord Wilberforce).
- *Furniss v Dawson* [1984] AC 474 (HL): inserted steps ignored.
- *Barclays Mercantile Business Finance Ltd v Mawson* [2004] UKHL 51 (HL): principle of construction; taxpayer won.
- *Tower MCashback LLP 1 v HMRC* [2011] UKSC 19 (SC): circular loan-funded expenditure not "incurred"; HMRC won.
- *UBS AG v HMRC; DB Group Services (UK) Ltd v HMRC* [2016] UKSC 13 (SC): restated Ramsay as purposive interpretation.
- *Rossendale BC v Hurstwood Properties (A) Ltd* [2021] UKSC 16 (SC): purposive construction; no wider anti-abuse doctrine.
- *Mayes v HMRC* [2011] EWCA Civ 407 (CA): the "SHIPS 2" scheme using life insurance bonds generated a claim to "corresponding deficiency relief" against income tax. The court held that the relevant provisions (ICTA 1988 s 549) were highly prescriptive and each step had real legal effect, so on a purposive reading the self-cancelling steps could not be disregarded; the taxpayer won. Shows that Ramsay does not defeat every pre-ordained scheme.

## Applying it
1. Identify the statutory provision relied on by the taxpayer and its purpose.
2. Identify whether there is a composite scheme: pre-ordained steps, circularity, self-cancelling steps.
3. Ask whether, on a purposive construction, the realistic transaction falls within the provision: if so the relief applies even if the aim was tax saving.
4. Consider the GAAR as a back-stop: tax arrangements, abusive, double reasonableness.
5. Disclosure: has DOTAS been triggered (hallmarks), has an SRN been issued; consider follower notices and accelerated payment where the scheme has been defeated elsewhere.
6. Common traps: stating that Ramsay is a general anti-avoidance rule; assuming the GAAR catches all planning; overlooking the shift in *UBS* and *Rossendale*; forgetting that users of defeated schemes can face penalties, follower notices and accelerated payment notices.
7. Everyday question: "Is it legal to use a tax-saving scheme?" Using reliefs as intended is lawful; marketed schemes that lack commercial purpose are likely to fail and attract penalties.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the GAAR has been used much: it has been invoked sparingly, and few cases have reached tribunals, suggesting a deterrent effect.
- Rule of law concerns: certainty against the courts' flexibility.
- Whether the "purposive" approach is really different from the Ramsay doctrine, or a rebranding. Academic commentary includes the Mawson/UBS interpretive debate.
- Policy shift to target promoters and enablers, and to require up-front payment.

## Recent changes and points to check
- GAAR guidance (approved by the GAAR Advisory Panel) is updated periodically; quote the current version.
- The promoter and enabler regimes were tightened in 2022 and again in Finance Act 2026; Budget 2026 announced further measures for Finance Bill 2026-27.
