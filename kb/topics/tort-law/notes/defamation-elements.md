# Defamation - Standing, Jurisdiction and the Elements of the Claim

## Overview
Defamation (libel and slander) is the tort that protects a person's **reputation**. Its central difficulty is that every successful claim restricts speech, so the law is constantly trying to balance reputation against freedom of expression under Article 10 ECHR. The Defamation Act 2013 (DA 2013) (England and Wales; in force 1 January 2014) reshaped the tort: it added a statutory "serious harm" threshold, replaced most common law defences with statutory ones, curbed "libel tourism", moved trials away from juries and changed the publication rule for repeat publications. But the Act does not start from a blank page: the basic meaning of "defamatory", the fact/opinion distinction, reference and much of publication remain common law, so pre-2013 cases still matter. This note covers who can sue, where, and the elements a claimant must establish. Defences and remedies are in `defamation-defences.md`.

Traditionally the tort is one of strict liability. Weir's older summary (written before the 2013 Act) puts the basic common law rule this way: A is liable for saying to C something about B which would tend to make an ordinary person think worse of B, and B need not prove the statement false, that it caused harm, or that A was at fault. Falsity is presumed; truth is for the defendant to prove as a defence. The 2013 Act's serious-harm requirement has qualified the "no proof of harm" part of that statement (see below).

## The law

### A. Who can sue (standing)
1. **Living individuals** can sue. Politicians can sue in their personal capacity (e.g. *McAlpine v Bercow*). MPs, however, cannot be sued for what they say in the Commons because of absolute parliamentary privilege (see defences note).
2. **Governmental bodies cannot sue.** *Derbyshire County Council v Times Newspapers* [1993] AC 534: a democratically elected body, or any governmental body, must be open to uninhibited public criticism, and the threat of a libel action would inevitably inhibit free speech. The textbook (Steele) notes this case imported the US idea of the "chilling effect" (from *New York Times v Sullivan*) into English law, and that the council kept the option of suing in malicious falsehood, where the claimant must prove falsity, actual damage and improper motive.
3. **Political parties cannot sue**: *Goldsmith v Bhoyrul* [1998] QB 459 (Referendum Party) extended *Derbyshire* to parties.
4. **Companies can sue**, because they have legal personality: *South Hetton Coal Co v North-Eastern News Association* [1894] 1 QB 133 (claim about the poor state of houses the company let out; a company may sue over statements that make people think badly of its business).
   - But a company cannot be defamed by imputations of acts only a human can do. *ENRC v HarperCollins & Burgis* [2022] EWHC 487 (QB): the alleged meaning (involvement in murders to protect business interests) attributed acts and motives that a corporate entity simply cannot have; the test remained that of the hypothetical ordinary reasonable reader.
   - **s 1(2) DA 2013**: for a body that trades for profit, harm to reputation is not "serious harm" unless it has caused or is likely to cause the body **serious financial loss**.
   - Before the Act, *Jameel v Dow Jones & Co* [2005] EWCA Civ 75 required a "real and substantial tort": where publication in England and Wales had reached only five people, two of them truly independent of the claimant, the action was an abuse of process because it was not "worth the candle".

### B. Where can the claimant sue? (jurisdiction)
5. England and Wales was attractive to "libel tourists", partly because the defendant bears the burden of proving truth.
6. **s 9 DA 2013**: for an action against a person not domiciled in the UK, the court has no jurisdiction unless satisfied that, of all the places where the statement has been published, England and Wales is "clearly the most appropriate place" to sue. (The current version of s 9 in the official sources shows the former EU/Lugano limbs repealed; the textbook extract, written earlier, reproduces the original wording covering persons not domiciled in the UK, another Member State or a Lugano state.)
   - *Wright v Ver* [2020] EWCA Civ 672 (textbook): relevant factors include all the places of publication, the number of publications in each, the reputational damage in each, the availability of fair processes and remedies elsewhere, and barriers to access to justice. England was not clearly the most appropriate forum where publication was wider in the US and the claimant's key business relationships were there.
7. **s 10 DA 2013**: a court has no jurisdiction over a claim against someone who was not the author, editor or publisher unless it is not reasonably practicable to sue the author, editor or publisher.

### C. The elements of the claim
The claimant must show:
1. the defendant made a **defamatory statement/imputation**;
2. its publication has caused or is likely to cause **serious harm** to the claimant's reputation (s 1 DA 2013);
3. the statement **referred to the claimant**;
4. the statement was **published** (communicated to a third party).
Then consider any **defences**.

#### Element 1: a defamatory statement
- A "statement" includes words, pictures, visual images, gestures or any other way of conveying meaning (s 15 DA 2013). Common law examples: waxworks (*Monson v Tussauds* [1894] 1 QB, the spelling used in the textbook; the handout spells it "Monsoon") and a film (*Youssoupoff v MGM Pictures* (1934) 50 TLR 581).
- **Tests of defamatory meaning:**
  - *Sim v Stretch* [1936] 2 All ER 1237: the words tend to "lower the claimant in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally".
  - *Youssoupoff v MGM*: statement causing the claimant to be "shunned or avoided".
  - *Byrne v Deane* [1937] 1 KB 818: a verse on a golf club notice board implying the claimant had told the police about illegal gambling machines was not defamatory, because right-thinking people would not think less of someone for reporting a crime.
  - *Lewis v Daily Telegraph* [1964] AC 234: the reader is fair-minded, not avid for scandal, not overly suspicious and not unduly naive.
  - Ridicule can be enough: *Berkoff v Burchill* [1996] 4 All ER 1008 (calling an actor hideously ugly could lower his standing and expose him to ridicule, given that he earned his living partly from his appearance as an actor).
- **Determining meaning.** The court fixes the single "natural and ordinary meaning" the ordinary reasonable reader would take, reading the words in their whole context.
  - *Lewis v Daily Telegraph*: a report that the Fraud Squad was investigating a company did not mean the chairman was guilty of fraud; the ordinary reader would not equate investigation with guilt (an example of "popular" or "false" innuendo, meaning read between the lines, here rejected).
  - *Stocker v Stocker* [2019] UKSC 17: on Facebook (a casual medium), "tried to strangle" had to be read as an ordinary reader would read it there; the trial judge was wrong to rely on a detached dictionary definition implying an attempt to kill.
- **Innuendo:**
  - *Popular (false) innuendo*: the defamatory meaning is read between the lines by the ordinary reader (*Lewis*).
  - *Legal (true) innuendo*: the defamatory meaning arises only because readers know extra facts. *Cassidy v Daily Mirror* [1929] 2 KB 331: a photo wrongly announcing a man's engagement to another woman defamed his real wife (readers who knew her would infer she was not married to him). *Lord McAlpine v Bercow (No 2)* [2013] EWHC 1342: the tweet "Why is Lord McAlpine trending? *innocent face*", sent just after a broadcast about an unnamed senior Conservative alleged to be a child abuser, meant the claimant was that person; "innocent face" was read as insincere and ironic.
- **Fact or opinion?** This matters chiefly for the honest opinion defence, but it is decided at the meaning stage. The principles are collected in *Koutsogiannis v Random House Group* [2020] 4 WLR 25 at [16], approved in *Millett v Corbyn* [2021] EWCA Civ 567:
  1. the statement must be recognisable as comment, distinct from an imputation of fact;
  2. opinion is something which is, or can reasonably be inferred to be, a deduction, inference, conclusion, criticism, remark or observation;
  3. the ultimate question is how the words would strike the ordinary reasonable reader, and subject matter and context may be important;
  4. some statements that look like opinion are treated as fact, e.g. a "bare comment" implying the claimant did something without saying what;
  5. whether calling someone "dishonest" or "criminal" is fact or opinion depends on context, and there is no fixed rule.
  See also *Riley v Murray* [2022] EWCA Civ 1146.
- **Libel and slander.** Libel is the more permanent form (writing, but also waxworks or film); slander is transient (e.g. speech). Libel is actionable *per se*. Slander needs proof of special damage (material loss, e.g. lost business or sponsorship) except for imputations of an imprisonable crime or of unfitness for one's business. s 14 DA 2013 repealed the Slander of Women Act 1891 and provides that an imputation of a contagious or infectious disease is not actionable as slander without special damage. The distinction must now be read with the serious-harm test (*Lachaux* at [19]).

#### Element 2: serious harm (s 1 DA 2013)
- **s 1(1)**: "A statement is not defamatory unless its publication has caused or is likely to cause serious harm to the reputation of the claimant."
- **s 1(2)**: for profit-making bodies, serious harm requires actual or likely serious financial loss.
- Before the Act there was already a common law "threshold of seriousness" to exclude trivial claims, adopted in light of Article 10: *Thornton v Telegraph Media Group* [2010] EWHC 1414 (QB) (Tugendhat J); and *Jameel v Dow Jones* (above).
- The test looks both backwards and forwards: *Cooke v MGN* [2015] 1 WLR 895.
- **Leading case: *Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd* [2019] UKSC 27.** Several newspapers published articles about the claimant, a French aerospace engineer, repeating allegations by his ex-wife of domestic violence and of kidnapping their son in the UAE. The Supreme Court held:
  - s 1 created a **new threshold** of serious harm that did not exist before, higher than the old common law threshold of substantial harm;
  - whether serious harm was caused or likely is a question of fact; the court may look at the inherent tendency of the words and at evidence of their actual impact and likely future impact, so harm can be inferred from the gravity of the words, the scale of publication and the identity of the readers, and the claimant need not prove that harm has already happened;
  - s 1(1) did not revolutionise the tort: libel remains actionable without proof of material or financial loss, and the slander exceptions remain actionable per se. What s 1 adds is that the (non-financial) harm to reputation must be serious;
  - obiter: if the statement only reached people who did not in fact think less of the claimant, there is no serious harm and no liability, even if a reasonable reader would think less of them.
  - At the later trial (*Lachaux v Independent Print Ltd* [2021] EWHC 1797 (QB)) the public interest defence failed and damages were assessed at £120,000 (textbook).
- Social media: harm can be easier to show. *Riley v Murray* [2021] EWHC 3437 (QB) (textbook): 94 replies, 661 retweets and 1,764 likes within three hours supported an inference of serious harm, alongside evidence from the replies themselves.
- Causation of harm matters: *Blake & Seymour v Fox* [2024] EWHC 146. Two claimants called "paedophiles" in tweets proved serious harm; but Fox's counterclaim (being called "racist") failed because he could not show that damage to his acting career came from those tweets rather than from his own chosen public profile on racism.
- s 1(2) in practice (textbook): *Brett Wilson LLP v Persons Unknown* [2015] EWHC 2628 (QB), where serious financial loss to a law firm was inferred from a drop in enquiries turning into instructions, without exact figures; *Napag Trading Ltd v Gedi Gruppo Editoriale SpA* [2020] EWHC 3034 (QB), where serious financial loss can be inferred in the same way, but only after careful examination of the evidence.

#### Element 3: reference to the claimant
- The reasonable reader must be able to identify the claimant as the subject. Intention to refer is not required at common law.
- *Hulton v Jones* [1910] AC 20: a fictional story about "Artemus Jones", a Peckham churchwarden having an affair in Dieppe, defamed a real barrister of that name; a jury could find reasonable readers would think it referred to him.
- *Newstead v London Express Newspaper* [1940] 1 KB 377: a true report about a "Harold Newstead, 30-year-old Camberwell man" on trial for bigamy could defame another Harold Newstead of similar age living in Camberwell.
- Limits since the Human Rights Act 1998: *O'Shea v MGN* [2001] EMLR 943, where a model's look-alike in an adult advertisement could not succeed; the image was defamatory at common law, but imposing liability for an unintended look-alike would breach Article 10 (Morland J). See also *Baturina v Times* [2011] 1 WLR 1526.
- **Groups**: *Knupffer v London Express Newspaper* [1944] AC 116: an attack on the "Young Russia" party did not defame the leader of its 24-member UK branch; a class cannot be defamed as a class, nor an individual by a general reference to their class, unless the words can be shown to point to the claimant in particular.

#### Element 4: publication
- Publication means communication to at least one person other than the claimant. At common law anyone who repeats or passes on the statement is also a publisher and separately liable.
- Publication to one person was traditionally enough (e.g. the telegraph clerk in *Sim v Stretch*), but the serious-harm requirement now means very limited publication is unlikely to found a claim. *Webb v Jones* [2021] EWHC 1618: being visible on the internet does not prove substantial publication, nor does a reading so rapid and superficial that it leaves no impression or only a fleeting one ([47]-[48]). Contrast *Stocker v Stocker*, where publication to around 110 Facebook friends was accepted.
- Burden: there is no presumption that internet material has been read by many; the claimant must prove access (*Al-Amoudi v Brisard* [2006] EWHC 1062 (QB), textbook). Publication to one journalist may foreseeably lead to wide circulation (*Haji-Ioannou v Dixon* [2009] EWHC 178, textbook).
- Foreseeability: *Theaker v Richardson* [1962] 1 WLR 151, where it was foreseeable that someone other than the addressee (the claimant's husband) would open the letter, so there was publication. Contrast *Huth v Huth* [1915] 3 KB 32 (textbook): a butler who opened a letter without authority did not count. Steele treats foreseeability as effectively a remoteness rule (*Slipper v BBC* [1991] 1 All ER 165).
- Knowledge and control, especially online:
  - *Bunt v Tilley* [2006] EWHC 407 (QB): a common law publisher must have knowing involvement in publication; a purely passive, instrumental role (e.g. an internet service provider) is not enough.
  - *Tamiz v Google* [2013] EWCA Civ 68: a blogging platform host could arguably become a publisher once notified of the material and able to remove it.
  - s 5 DA 2013 now gives website operators a specific defence (see defences note).
- **Multiple publication rule and single publication rule.** At common law every fresh publication was a new cause of action: *Duke of Brunswick v Harmer* (1849) 14 QB 185 (a back copy delivered 17 years later). The Court of Appeal applied this to online archives in *Loutchansky v Times Newspapers (Nos 2-5)* [2002] QB 783, so each download was a new publication (textbook). **s 8 DA 2013** brings in a single publication rule: where a person publishes a statement to the public (including a section of it) and later publishes the same or substantially the same statement, the cause of action for the later publication is treated as accruing on the date of the first publication for the purposes of s 4A Limitation Act 1980. s 4A sets a **one-year** time limit for libel and slander. s 8 does not apply if the later publication's manner is "materially different" (relevant factors include prominence and extent: s 8(5)), and the court keeps its discretion under s 32A LA 1980 to disapply the time limit.

### D. Procedure
- **s 11 DA 2013**: defamation trials are now without a jury unless the court orders otherwise (removing libel and slander from the list of actions presumptively tried by jury in the Senior Courts Act 1981 and County Courts Act 1984). Meaning, once a classic jury question, is now usually decided by a judge, often as a preliminary issue.

## Key authorities
- *Derbyshire CC v Times Newspapers* [1993] AC 534: local authority cannot sue; chilling effect; free criticism of government.
- *Goldsmith v Bhoyrul* [1998] QB 459: political parties cannot sue.
- *South Hetton Coal Co v North-Eastern News Association* [1894] 1 QB 133: companies can sue over business reputation.
- *ENRC v HarperCollins & Burgis* [2022] EWHC 487 (QB): a company cannot be defamed by imputations of murder, which only humans can commit.
- *Jameel v Dow Jones & Co* [2005] EWCA Civ 75: "real and substantial tort"; "worth the candle"; abuse of process for minimal publication.
- *Jameel v Wall Street Journal* [2006] UKHL 44 (textbook): the majority refused to require trading companies to prove special damage; Lord Hoffmann and Baroness Hale would have required it. Lord Hoffmann said a company "has no soul" and its reputation is only a commercial asset. s 1(2) moves partly toward their view.
- *Sim v Stretch* [1936]: "right-thinking members of society" test.
- *Byrne v Deane* [1937]: reporting crime is not defamatory in right-thinking eyes.
- *Lewis v Daily Telegraph* [1964] AC 234: ordinary reader; investigation is not guilt; popular innuendo.
- *Berkoff v Burchill* [1996]: ridicule can defame.
- *Cassidy v Daily Mirror* [1929]: legal (true) innuendo from facts known to readers.
- *McAlpine v Bercow (No 2)* [2013] EWHC 1342: innuendo by tweet; "innocent face" ironic.
- *Stocker v Stocker* [2019] UKSC 17: meaning in social media context; don't use dictionaries mechanically.
- *Koutsogiannis v Random House* [2020] 4 WLR 25; *Millett v Corbyn* [2021] EWCA Civ 567: principles on meaning and fact vs opinion.
- *Lachaux v Independent Print* [2019] UKSC 27: serious harm is a new, higher threshold, proved as fact but inferable; libel still actionable per se.
- *Cooke v MGN* [2015] 1 WLR 895: serious harm looks backward and forward.
- *Blake & Seymour v Fox* [2024] EWHC 146: claimant must show the harm was caused by the statement sued on.
- *Hulton v Jones* [1910]; *Newstead v London Express* [1940]: unintentional reference.
- *O'Shea v MGN* [2001]: Article 10 limits liability for unintended reference.
- *Knupffer v London Express* [1944]: group defamation.
- *Theaker v Richardson* [1962]: foreseeable publication.
- *Bunt v Tilley* [2006]; *Tamiz v Google* [2013]: intermediaries and knowing involvement.
- *Webb v Jones* [2021]: mere online availability is not substantial publication.
- *Duke of Brunswick v Harmer* (1849): multiple publication rule (now modified by s 8).
- DA 2013 s 1 (serious harm), s 8 (single publication), s 9 (non-domiciled defendants), s 10 (non-authors), s 11 (no jury), s 14 (special damage in slander), s 15 (meaning of statement); Limitation Act 1980 s 4A (one year).

## Debate and critique
- **The balance between reputation and speech.** The handout asks whether the 2013 Act really changed the balance, whether it stops defamation from being used one-sidedly to suppress criticism, and whether codification brought clarity. Steele's view (textbook) is that in almost every respect the Act shifts the balance toward speech, and that it grew out of an unusually successful lobbying campaign. Yet reputation is now also recognised as part of Article 8. Steele asks how anyone can judge whether the "right balance" has been struck when the reform debate was almost entirely about free expression and hardly at all about reputation.
- **The value of reputation.** Mullis and Scott ('Tilting at Windmills: the Defamation Act 2013' (2014) 77 MLR 87, as quoted in Steele) argue that any libel regime must accommodate both individual rights and social interests in *both* expression and reputation, so reputation has a public value too. Lord Nicholls in *Reynolds* said the same: reputation is part of an individual's dignity and underpins many democratic choices (whom to employ, deal with or vote for), and a smeared reputation harms society as well as the individual.
- **Cane** (quoted in Steele): traditional English law prized reputation more than speech, and protected speech only through defences rather than in defining the wrong. Article 10(2), which treats reputation as a *reason to restrict* speech, seems to reverse that hierarchy.
- **The chilling effect.** The risk that the threat of litigation deters true and important speech, not just falsehoods, is mainly an argument from the functional value of free speech: the public stays uninformed. Features such as presumed falsity, strict liability and high costs have made the risk worse. s 1, s 9 and s 11 can all be read as anti-chilling measures.
- **Serious harm after *Lachaux*.** Steele notes the Supreme Court said s 1 made a significant change but not a "revolution", and she questions the reasoning that a change from the common law was "plainly intended". One worry with requiring proof of harm is cost and complexity, since it may need a preliminary trial of fact. Another is whether it undermines the idea that libel is actionable per se. *Lachaux* answered both by allowing inference. Erdos ('Serious Harm to Reputation Rights? Defamation in the Supreme Court' (2019) 78 CLJ 510) and Rudkin (2015) 26 Ent LR 13 on *Cooke v MGN* are on the reading list for this debate; the sources do not summarise their arguments.
- **Corporate claimants.** Should a profit-making company, which "has no soul" (Lord Hoffmann in *Jameel v WSJ*), sue for reputation at all? s 1(2) is a compromise. It does not require proof of special damage, only likely serious financial loss, but it singles out trading companies in a way Lord Hope (majority in *Jameel*) thought unjustified compared with charities and trade unions.
- **Strict liability for unintended reference.** *Hulton v Jones* and *Newstead* show defendants liable without fault. *O'Shea* shows Article 10 cutting this back. Is strict liability defensible for speech? Descheemaeker ('Protecting Reputation: Defamation and Negligence' (2009) 29 OJLS 603) is on the reading list on the role of fault.
- **Overlap with privacy.** Barendt (2015) 7 JML 85, Gale (2018) 26 Tort L Rev 26 and Hariharan (2021) 13 JML 186 discuss whether privacy claims can be used to win reputational damages that defamation would deny. See `privacy-misuse-of-private-information.md` (e.g. *Sicri*, and the rule in *Hayden v Dickenson* that the court looks at the "nub" of the claim).
- **Purpose of libel.** Howarth ('Libel: its Purpose and Reform' (2011) 74 MLR 845) is on the reading list on what libel is for; no summary in the sources.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Standing.** Is the claimant an individual, a company (s 1(2): serious financial loss needed, and the imputation must be one a company can bear, *ENRC*), a governmental body or political party (barred: *Derbyshire*, *Goldsmith*)? An individual politician can sue personally.
2. **Jurisdiction.** Is the defendant domiciled outside the UK? Then s 9: is England and Wales *clearly* the most appropriate place (*Wright v Ver* factors)? Is the defendant only a distributor? Then s 10.
3. **Time.** Was the claim brought within one year of first publication (s 4A LA 1980 with s 8)? Was a later republication "materially different" (s 8(4)-(5))?
4. **Statement and meaning.** Identify the words, image or post. What single natural and ordinary meaning would the ordinary reasonable reader take, in context (medium matters: *Stocker*)? Is there a popular innuendo (*Lewis*) or a legal innuendo based on facts known to some readers (*Cassidy*, *McAlpine*)? Is it fact or opinion (*Koutsogiannis* principles)? Note this now, as it decides whether truth (s 2) or honest opinion (s 3) is the relevant defence.
5. **Defamatory?** Would it lower the claimant in the eyes of right-thinking people, cause them to be shunned, or expose them to ridicule (*Sim*, *Youssoupoff*, *Berkoff*)? Watch for *Byrne v Deane*-type facts where only a section of society would disapprove.
6. **Libel or slander?** If spoken and transient, is there special damage or does an exception apply (imprisonable crime; unfitness for business)? Disease imputations need special damage (s 14).
7. **Serious harm.** Consider gravity of the imputation, extent of publication, who the readers were, and any evidence of actual or likely impact (*Lachaux*; *Riley v Murray*). Was the harm caused by *this* statement rather than the claimant's existing reputation or other publications (*Blake v Fox*)? For companies, is serious financial loss likely?
8. **Reference.** Is the claimant named, or identifiable by readers with extra knowledge? Unintentional reference (*Hulton*, *Newstead*) and look-alikes (*O'Shea*). For groups, is the group small and the words pointed enough (*Knupffer*)?
9. **Publication.** To whom, and how many? Online, was it actually read (*Webb v Jones*)? Was any third-party reading foreseeable (*Theaker*, compared with *Huth*)? Who are all the potential defendants: author, editor, publisher, platform (*Bunt*, *Tamiz*, s 5, s 10), and anyone who repeated it?
10. Then move to defences and remedies (`defamation-defences.md`).

**Typical traps:** forgetting s 1(2) for companies; treating publication to one person as automatically enough after the 2013 Act; assuming a company can sue over "human" imputations; missing that repetition creates fresh liability for the repeater (and that s 8 only protects the *same* publisher's later publications); forgetting that intention to defame or to refer is irrelevant.
