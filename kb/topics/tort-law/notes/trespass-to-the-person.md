# Trespass to the Person (Battery, Assault, False Imprisonment and Defences)

## Overview
Trespass is the oldest family of torts in English law (England & Wales). Trespass to the person protects bodily integrity and freedom of movement. It covers three torts: **battery**, **assault** and **false imprisonment**. In *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 1 WLR 1172 (at 1177) Robert Goff LJ defined them as follows:

- **Assault**: "an act which causes another person to apprehend the infliction of immediate, unlawful force on his person";
- **Battery**: "the actual infliction of unlawful force on another person";
- **False imprisonment**: "the unlawful imposition of constraint on another's freedom of movement from a particular place".

These torts matter for three reasons. First, they are actionable *per se*: the claimant does not have to prove damage. Second, they focus on whether the interference was *unlawful*, not on whether the defendant was careless. That makes them important for holding public officials such as police, prisons and the Home Office to account, and in medical consent cases. Third, they can be used in historic abuse claims, now that the limitation problems have been resolved (*A v Hoare* [2008] UKHL 6). The related intentional torts, the rule in *Wilkinson v Downton* and the statutory tort under the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, have their own note (see Links).

## The law

### A. Features common to all three torts
1. **Intention, sometimes recklessness, not negligence.** "When the injury is not inflicted intentionally but negligently ... the only cause of action is in negligence and not trespass" (Lord Denning MR, *Letang v Cooper* [1965] 1 QB 232). Iqbal v Prison Officers Association [2009] EWCA Civ 1312 accepts that recklessness can be enough.
2. **Directness.** The interference must be direct and immediate. Historically this requirement separated trespass from "case": indirect intentional harm fell under *Wilkinson v Downton*, and indirect careless harm became negligence (Hale LJ in *Wong v Parkside* [2003] 3 All ER 932, as cited by Steele).
3. **Actionable per se.** No proof of personal or financial loss is needed. Without harm, though, only nominal damages may be available (*R (Lumba) v SSHD* [2011] UKSC 12).
4. **Recovery for harm negligence will not cover.** the materials that trespass can compensate some kinds of harm that cannot be recovered in negligence.
5. **Relationship with criminal law.** These torts have close counterparts in the Offences Against the Person Act 1861. A civil claim may still go ahead when a criminal prosecution has failed (*Ashley v CC of Sussex Police* [2008] UKHL 25).

### B. Battery
A battery requires:
1. **an intentional (or reckless) application of force** to the claimant. The intention needed is to make the contact, not to cause injury or harm. In *Williams v Humphrey* (1975), a practical joke case, Talbot J held that "the intention goes to the commission of an act of force". Under **transferred intent**, a defendant who means to hit A but hits B is still liable (*Livingstone v Ministry of Defence* [1984] NILR 356);
2. that is **direct and immediate**. Courts read this requirement flexibly: a short time between the act and the contact is enough (*Scott v Shepherd* (1773) 2 Wm Bl 892; *DPP v K (A Minor)* [1990] 1 WLR 1067). Steele, citing Trindade, adds that an act which sets off an unbroken chain of consequences ending in contact counts as direct. Directness rules out claims for pure omissions and against "peripheral" parties;
3. that is **unlawful**, meaning there is no lawful justification or excuse. The contact need not be hostile. In *Collins v Wilcock* the court held that "any touching of another person, however slight, may amount to a battery". There is a general exception for "all physical contact which is generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life", such as jostling in a crowd or a friendly slap on the back. In *Wilson v Pringle* [1987] QB 237 the Court of Appeal required "hostility". Lord Goff doubted that in *Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1 (at 73), pointing out that a prank that gets out of hand, an over-friendly slap on the back, or surgery by a surgeon who wrongly thinks the patient consented can all be unlawful without being hostile.

On the facts of *Collins v Wilcock*, a police officer took hold of a woman's arm to detain her for a caution. The officer had no power to do that, so the touching went beyond generally accepted conduct and was a battery. The woman's conviction for assaulting an officer in the execution of her duty was quashed.

### C. Assault
Assault requires:
1. **Intention.** The defendant must intend, or be reckless as to whether, the claimant will fear the immediate infliction of force. Steele notes that older authority (*Read v Coker* (1853)) speaks of intent to commit a battery. *Bici v Ministry of Defence* [2004] EWHC 786 requires intent to put the claimant in fear of violence, which fits better with *R v Ireland*. In *Bici* the soldiers' intended battery could be transferred to an unintended victim, but because there was no intention to put anyone in fear, the assault claim failed.
2. **Reasonable apprehension of immediate and direct force.** The test is objective: would the reasonable person believe force was about to be used? There must be "the means of carrying that threat into effect" (*Stephens v Myers* (1830) 4 C & P 349).
   - *Stephens v Myers*: the materials reads this as an assault, because the defendant advanced on the chair with a clenched fist and was stopped only by others. Steele reports it as the opposite, with no assault because the defendant had no present means of carrying out the threat. **The two sources conflict.** Use the case for the principle, that there must be present means to carry out the threat, and be careful about stating the outcome.
   - *Read v Coker*: workmen threatened to break the plaintiff's neck if he did not leave. They had the means to do it, so this was an assault (Steele).
   - *Thomas v NUM (South Wales Area)* [1985] 2 All ER 1: striking miners shouted threats at working miners who were in buses protected by police. There was no assault, because the distance and the police presence removed immediacy and directness.
3. **Words, gestures and silence** can all amount to an assault, but context matters.
   - *R v Ireland* [1998] AC 147 (criminal case): repeated silent phone calls at night could be an assault. "A thing said is also a thing done."
   - *Tuberville v Savage* (1669) 1 Mod 3: the defendant put his hand on his sword and said "if it were not assize time I would not take such language from you". Because it was assize time, the words were a statement that he would *not* attack. No assault.
4. **No lawful justification or excuse** (see the defences below).

### D. False imprisonment
False imprisonment has two ingredients: the fact of imprisonment, and the absence of lawful authority to justify it (Lord Bridge, *R v Deputy Governor of Parkhurst Prison, ex p Hague* [1992] 1 AC 58).
1. **Intention, or recklessness, to restrict movement completely.** Bad faith is not required. In *Iqbal*, Smith LJ said the claimant must show "an intention to deprive the claimant of his liberty", not just a deliberate act. Her example was a guard who locks an office door believing everyone has gone home. That guard may be negligent but has not committed false imprisonment. The defendant need *not* know that the detention is unlawful (*R v Governor of Brockhill Prison, ex p Evans* [2001] 2 AC 19, below).
2. **Complete restriction.**
   - Even a moment can be enough. In *Walker v Commissioner of Police for the Metropolis* [2014] EWCA Civ 897, a man blocked in a doorway for a few seconds was falsely imprisoned. Steele notes the damages were £5.
   - If there is a reasonable means of escape, there is no imprisonment. In *Bird v Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742 the claimant was blocked from crossing Hammersmith Bridge but was free to go back.
   - A reasonable condition can be placed on leaving a place someone entered under a contract (*Robinson v Balmain New Ferry* [1910] AC 295, where a penny was charged to leave a wharf).
   - The essence of imprisonment is "being made to stay in a particular place by another person". The means can be barriers, guards, or threats of force or of legal process (*R (Jalloh) v SSHD* [2020] UKSC 4, Baroness Hale). In *Jalloh*, a curfew enforced by an electronic tag and criminal penalties was imprisonment, even though the claimant had sometimes broken it. Compliance was enforced, not voluntary.
3. **Acts, not omissions.** In *Iqbal*, a prisoner was kept in his cell because of an unlawful strike by prison officers. The union was not liable: there was no positive act, the confinement was not directly caused, and it was not intended. Smith LJ said there must be an intentional or reckless positive act, or an omission only in limited circumstances, that is "the direct and immediate cause of the loss of liberty". Lord Neuberger MR suggested that misfeasance in public office is the better route for deliberate inaction.
4. **Knowledge is not required.** "A person could be imprisoned without his knowing it ... while he is asleep ... Of course the damages might be diminished" (*Meering v Grahame-White Aviation* (1919) 122 LT 44). The House of Lords agreed in *Murray v Ministry of Defence* [1988] 2 All ER 521, cited by Steele. If the claimant suffered no harm, only nominal damages follow.
5. **No lawful authority.**
   - **Statutory authority**, e.g. Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 s 24A (arrest without warrant by other persons).
   - *Ex p Evans*: a governor calculated a release date consistently with the case law of the time. Later rulings showed the calculation was wrong, and the prisoner served 59 extra days. The governor was liable. The gist of the tort is unlawfulness, not bad conduct.
   - *R (Lumba) v SSHD* [2011] UKSC 12: foreign national prisoners were detained after their sentences under an unpublished blanket policy that contradicted the published policy, which favoured release. The detention was unlawful, so the tort was committed. However, the majority awarded only **nominal damages**, because the claimants would have been detained lawfully under the published policy anyway. Baroness Hale would have awarded a modest conventional sum, perhaps £500, to vindicate the constitutional right. Lords Brown and Rodger dissented on liability.
   - **Necessity and Article 5 ECHR.** *Austin v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis*: about 3,000 people were "kettled" at Oxford Circus for around seven hours during a protest that involved some violence. the materials summarises the House of Lords decision ([2009] UKHL 5) as holding the containment lawful because it was necessary to prevent an imminent breach of the peace. Steele explains the stages. The Court of Appeal ([2008] QB 660) held that this *was* imprisonment at common law but was justified by necessity. The House of Lords ([2009] 1 AC 564) and the European Court of Human Rights then held that there was no "deprivation of liberty" under Article 5 at all, because Article 5 is aimed at *arbitrary* detention.

### E. Defences (lawful justification or excuse)
The general defences of **volenti** and **illegality** apply. **Contributory negligence does not apply** to trespass to the person (*Co-operative Group (CWS) Ltd v Pritchard* [2011] EWCA Civ 329). Following *Standard Chartered Bank v Pakistan Shipping* [2003] 1 AC 959, the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 only applies to torts where contributory negligence was a defence before 1945, and it was not a defence to trespass. Earlier suggestions to the contrary (e.g. *Wasson v CC of Northern Ireland* [1987] NI 420, a riot case) will not now be followed.

1. **Consent** is the most important defence.
   - Consent is valid only if (a) the claimant agreed to being touched in the way the defendant touched her, (b) at the time, the claimant had enough maturity, intelligence and understanding to decide, and (c) nobody forced her to agree (lecture).
   - **Medical consent.** In *Chatterton v Gerson* [1981] QB 432 a nerve-removal operation caused some loss of muscle function, a risk the patient had not been told about. Her consent was still real, because she had been told "in broad terms" about the "general nature" of the procedure. A failure to warn of risks belongs in *negligence*, not battery. Consent is vitiated if information is withheld in bad faith, or if a different procedure is carried out altogether (Bristow J's example of a tonsillectomy patient who was circumcised by mistake). *Sidaway* [1985] AC 871 rejected "informed consent" as a battery doctrine. *Montgomery* [2015] UKSC 11 strengthened informed consent but in negligence only (Steele).
   - **Consent to restricted movement.** In *Herd v Weardale Steel, Coke and Coal Co* [1915] AC 67, a miner who went down the pit under his employment contract was taken to have consented to stay there until the end of his shift. Refusing to bring him up early was not false imprisonment.
   - **Invalid consent.** Consent is not valid where the claimant was misled about the *nature* of the act (*R v Williams* [1923] 1 KB 340; *AG's Reference (No 6 of 1980)* [1981] QB 715).
   - **Refusal.** A competent adult "has an absolute right to choose whether to consent to medical treatment, to refuse it ... This right of choice is not limited to decisions which others might regard as sensible" (*Re T (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* [1993] Fam 95). See also *Re MB (Caesarean Section)* [1997] 2 FLR 426. In *St George's Healthcare NHS Trust v S* [1999] Fam 26, a caesarean imposed on a competent woman was a trespass even though it was done to save the foetus (Steele). In *Re T* itself, the refusal was ineffective because of undue influence from the patient's mother.
   - **Burden of proof.** *Freeman v Home Office (No 2)* [1984] QB 524 put the burden of proving absence of consent on the claimant, and Steele criticises this. The courts have said self-defence (*Ashley*) and necessity (*Austin*, CA) are for the defendant to prove.
2. **Capacity: Mental Capacity Act 2005.**
   - s 1: capacity is presumed. A person is not to be treated as unable to decide unless all practicable steps to help them have failed, and an unwise decision does not show incapacity. Acts must be in the person's best interests, and the least restrictive option must be considered.
   - s 2: a person lacks capacity if, because of "an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain", they cannot make the decision.
   - s 3: a person is unable to decide if they cannot (a) understand the relevant information, (b) retain it, (c) use or weigh it, or (d) communicate the decision.
   - s 5(1): a person (D) who does an act in connection with the care or treatment of P is protected if D took reasonable steps to establish whether P lacked capacity, and reasonably believed both that P lacked capacity and that the act was in P's best interests. s 5(3) keeps liability for negligence.
   - In *H v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2013] EWCA Civ 69, police restrained an autistic and epileptic teenager at a swimming pool. Their conduct was unreasonable, so s 5 gave no protection, and damages were awarded for battery, false imprisonment and under the HRA (Steele).
3. **Children.**
   - Family Law Reform Act 1969 s 8: consent to medical treatment by a 16- or 17-year-old is as effective as an adult's. (the materials calls it the "Family Reform Act".) They are presumed to have capacity, subject to the MCA test.
   - Under 16s can consent if they have sufficient understanding and intelligence (*Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1986] AC 112).
   - **Refusal by a child** can be overridden by the court, or by those with parental responsibility, in the child's best interests (Children Act 1989 ss 3 and 8; *Re W* [1993] Fam 64). Section 8 of the 1969 Act lets doctors rely on a competent 16/17-year-old's consent but does not give the child a right to refuse. Even so, a refusal is a "very important consideration", and it carries more weight as the child matures.
4. **Necessity** is a "choice of evils". The classic example is slapping a choking person on the back.
   - *Re F* justified treating adults who lack capacity where the treatment is necessary and in their best interests. Lord Bridge stressed that doctors should face one standard, not two: if they act with due skill and care (*Bolam*), they should be immune in trespass as well as in negligence. This approach is now largely codified in the MCA 2005 s 5.
   - *Leigh v Gladstone*: force-feeding a hunger-striking suffragette prisoner was lawful because prison officials had a duty to preserve the lives of those in custody.
   - Steele notes that a refusal of consent overrides necessity, and that necessity alone is not enough for long-term incapacity (*Re F*).
5. **Self-defence** (*Ashley v CC of Sussex Police* [2008] UKHL 25). The defendant must have a *reasonable* belief that they are about to be attacked, and the force used must be proportionate (*Cockcroft v Smith* (1705) 11 Mod 43; *Cross v Kirkby* (2000) The Times, 5 April). The civil test is stricter than the criminal test: in crime, an honest belief is enough, but in tort the belief must be both honest and reasonable. So an officer acquitted of murder could still face a tort claim.

## Key authorities
- *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 1 WLR 1172: a police officer held a woman's arm with no power to do so. This was a battery. The case gives the standard definitions of the three torts and the "ordinary conduct of daily life" exception.
- *Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1: sterilisation of an adult who lacked capacity was lawful if in her best interests. Hostility is not required for battery, and best-interests treatment is lawful.
- *Wilson v Pringle* [1987] QB 237: horseplay case requiring "hostility". Doubted in *Re F*.
- *Letang v Cooper* [1965] 1 QB 232: a car drove over a sunbathing claimant's legs. Unintentional injury can only be claimed in negligence, with its 3-year limitation period. Trespass needs intention. Reaffirmed in *A v Hoare* [2008] UKHL 6, which departed from *Stubbings v Webb* [1993] AC 498 on limitation for deliberate assaults.
- *Fowler v Lanning* [1959] 1 QB 426: in an unintentional trespass action, the claimant must allege and prove negligence or intention.
- *Ashley v CC of Sussex Police* [2008] UKHL 25: a man was shot dead in a police raid. The battery claim could proceed even though negligence was admitted, because it served a vindicatory purpose. Self-defence in tort requires a reasonable belief.
- *Livingstone v MoD* [1984] NILR 356: transferred intent.
- *Scott v Shepherd* (1773); *DPP v K* [1990] 1 WLR 1067: flexible approach to directness.
- *Stephens v Myers* (1830): there must be present means to carry out the threat (see the conflict between sources above).
- *Thomas v NUM* [1985] 2 All ER 1: threats from a distance at police-protected buses were not an assault.
- *R v Ireland* [1998] AC 147: silent phone calls can be an assault.
- *Tuberville v Savage* (1669): words can negate a threat.
- *Bici v MoD* [2004] EWHC 786: assault requires intent to cause fear.
- *Bird v Jones* (1845): partial obstruction is not imprisonment.
- *Meering v Grahame-White Aviation* (1919); *Murray v MoD* [1988]: the claimant need not know of the imprisonment.
- *Walker v MPC* [2014] EWCA Civ 897: a few seconds of confinement in a doorway was false imprisonment.
- *R (Jalloh) v SSHD* [2020] UKSC 4: an enforced curfew is imprisonment.
- *Iqbal v POA* [2009] EWCA Civ 1312: a positive act, directness and intention to confine are needed. A strike was not false imprisonment.
- *R v Governor of Brockhill Prison, ex p Evans* [2001] 2 AC 19: strict liability for detention beyond lawful authority.
- *R (Lumba) v SSHD* [2011] UKSC 12: detention under an unlawful secret policy was false imprisonment, but damages were nominal.
- *Austin v MPC* (CA [2008] QB 660; HL [2009] 1 AC 564 / [2009] UKHL 5): kettling was justified by necessity, and there was no Article 5 deprivation of liberty.
- *Herd v Weardale Steel* [1915] AC 67: consent through the employment contract.
- *Chatterton v Gerson* [1981] QB 432: consent to the broad nature of a procedure is enough for battery.
- *R v Williams* [1923] 1 KB 340: deception as to the nature of the act vitiates consent.
- *Re T* [1993] Fam 95; *Re MB* [1997] 2 FLR 426: a competent adult's right to refuse.
- *Gillick* [1986] AC 112; *Re W* [1993] Fam 64: children's consent and refusal.
- *Leigh v Gladstone*: force-feeding a prisoner was justified by necessity.
- *Co-operative Group v Pritchard* [2011] EWCA Civ 329: no contributory negligence in trespass to the person.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 ss 1–5; Family Law Reform Act 1969 s 8; Children Act 1989 ss 3, 8; PACE 1984 s 24A; Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 ss 1, 4.

## Debate and critique
- **What is trespass for?** Steele quotes Tony Weir: tort law has more than one function, and trespass does not insist on damage because it exists to vindicate constitutional rights. Seen this way, *Lumba*'s award of only nominal damages is a "disappointment". Baroness Hale's minority approach, a modest conventional award, better reflects how fundamental the right is. Steele suggests that damages for torts actionable per se may already vindicate rights, so a separate "vindicatory damages" head may be a distraction.
- **Separate from negligence.** Since *Letang*, careless contact has belonged to negligence. Steele notes arguments that battery could be committed recklessly or carelessly, which would bring it back in line with trespass to land and goods. Courts are likely to resist this, because it would create an alternative to negligence in careless injury cases. the materials's further reading includes Devereux (2019) defending separate torts of trespass to the person, and Trindade (1982) on directness and intention in assault and battery.
- **Hostility.** *Wilson v Pringle* tried to limit battery through a "hostility" requirement. *Re F* preferred Goff's approach in *Collins*: all touching is potentially unlawful, subject to an "ordinary conduct of daily life" exception. Lord Goff called the implied-consent rationale "artificial", especially for people who cannot consent.
- **Autonomy versus best interests.** Steele explains that refusal cases treat autonomy as a "trump card" that beats both best interests and sanctity of life (*Re T*, *St George's v S*). Where the patient can neither consent nor refuse, best interests governs (*Re F*; *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] AC 789, where withdrawing treatment was treated as an omission and so not a battery). Keown argued that sanctity of life does not "give way" to best interests, but that treatment can be withheld as *futile*. Ward LJ adopted that analysis in *Re A (Conjoined Twins)* [2001] Fam 147.
- **Burden of proof on consent.** Steele argues that *Freeman*, which put the burden on the claimant, was poorly reasoned, because it confused consent with volenti.
- **Constitutional role of false imprisonment.** It is mostly used against public authorities. It runs alongside Article 5 claims under the HRA, but the two remain independent. Article 5 is broader in some respects and narrower in others (*Austin*).
- **Gender, abuse and policing.** the materials's further reading includes Godden on tort claims for rape, Conaghan on gendered harm, and Palmer and Steele on police shootings and tort (on *Ashley*). *A v Hoare* has made trespass claims for abuse more viable, with knock-on effects on vicarious liability.
- **Contributory negligence.** Steele argues that *Pritchard* is best explained by the wording of the 1945 Act, which covers torts that had the old defence, rather than by saying battery is "intentional", since harm need not be intended.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify each tort separately** for each incident: threat (assault), contact (battery), confinement (false imprisonment). Then consider *Wilkinson v Downton* or the PHA 1997 for indirect harm or a course of conduct (see the separate note).
2. **Battery.** Was the contact intended or reckless? Remember that intention to *touch* is enough, and consider transferred intent. Was it direct? Was it beyond generally acceptable everyday contact? Hostility is not required (*Re F*).
3. **Assault.** Did the claimant reasonably fear *immediate* force? Look for distance, barriers or police (*Thomas*), present means (*Stephens*), and words that negate the threat (*Tuberville*). Silence or phone calls can count (*Ireland*). Did the defendant intend to cause fear (*Bici*)?
4. **False imprisonment.** Was the restriction complete, or was there a reasonable way out (*Bird v Jones*)? How long it lasted does not matter for liability (*Walker*), only for damages. Was there a positive act (*Iqbal*)? The claimant need not know (*Meering*). Was there lawful authority (PACE s 24A, a court order, contractual conditions as in *Robinson*/*Herd*)? Was the detention later shown to be unlawful (*Evans*)? Would the claimant have been detained lawfully anyway, so that damages are only nominal (*Lumba*)?
5. **Defences, in order.**
   - **Consent.** Was it real? For medical cases, was the claimant told the broad nature of the procedure (*Chatterton*)? Was there deception as to nature? Was the claimant competent: apply MCA ss 1–3; for a 16/17-year-old, FLRA s 8; for under 16s, *Gillick*. Was there a refusal (*Re T*)? Could a child's refusal be overridden (*Re W*)?
   - **Necessity**, best interests, and MCA s 5 reasonable belief.
   - **Self-defence**: was the belief honest *and* reasonable, and was the force proportionate?
   - Do **not** argue contributory negligence (*Pritchard*).
6. **Remedies.** Damages may be nominal, compensatory, or exemplary against public officials. A declaration is also possible. Consider whether a parallel negligence claim exists, and why the claimant might still want trespass, e.g. vindication (*Ashley*).
7. **Typical traps.**
   - Treating negligent contact as battery (*Letang*).
   - Requiring hostility.
   - Assuming lack of awareness defeats false imprisonment.
   - Forgetting that partial restraint is not imprisonment.
   - Confusing the "informed consent" negligence route with battery.
   - Writing "PHA 1998". the materials heading says 1998 but the Act is the **Protection from Harassment Act 1997**.
