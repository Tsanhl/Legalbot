# Delaware Mansions Ltd v Westminster City Council [2001] UKHL 55 (case note)

> The National Archives does not hold this judgment and no other official copy could be obtained. Lord Cooke's
> words below are taken from the Court of Appeal's quotation of them in *Kirk v Brent LBC*. The facts come from
> secondary accounts. Check them against [2002] 1 AC 321 before citing.

## Overview
House of Lords, 25 October 2001. Lord Cooke of Thorndon gave the leading speech. The case is the leading authority
on recovering the cost of remedial works caused by encroaching tree roots. It holds that a continuing nuisance
gives a cause of action to the owner who has to pay for the remedy, even if damage began before that owner bought
the property, provided the defendant had notice and a chance to abate it.

## Facts
- Delaware Mansions is a block of flats in Maida Vale. A large London plane tree, owned and controlled by Westminster
  as highway authority, grew in the pavement outside. Its roots caused cracking, first reported in 1989.
- In June 1990 the freehold passed from the Church Commissioners to Flecksun Ltd. Westminster was notified of the
  damage shortly after the sale. Engineers' reports had advised removing the tree or underpinning. Westminster had
  the engineers' report before the works, cut back the roots in October 1991, and chose not to remove the tree
  (*Kirk v Brent* at [24]).
- Flecksun underpinned the building between January and July 1992, at a cost of just over £570,000, and sued.
- The trial judge (Recorder Derek Wood QC) dismissed the claim. The Court of Appeal (judgment of Pill LJ) allowed
  Flecksun's appeal, and the House of Lords affirmed.

## Held
- There was a **continuing nuisance** causing continuing damage after the change of ownership, so the new owner had
  its own cause of action (*Kirk v Brent* at [19]).
- Lord Cooke (at [33]) left open whether the cost of repairing the earlier cracking could have been recovered as soon
  as it became manifest. He said a reasonable landowner would notify the tree owner as soon as root damage was
  suspected.
- **Notice and opportunity to abate.** Relying on *Solloway v Hampshire CC* (1981) 79 LGR 449 as a warning against
  unreasonable burdens on tree owners, Lord Cooke held (at [34]) that, as a general proposition, the defendant is
  entitled to notice and a reasonable opportunity of abatement before liability for remedial expenditure arises.
  If the owner chooses to keep the tree, it may fairly be expected to bear the cost of reasonably necessary works.
  Westminster had ample notice and time before the underpinning, so it was liable.
- **Summary (at [38]):** where there is a continuing nuisance of which the defendant knew or ought to have known,
  reasonable remedial expenditure may be recovered by the owner who has had to incur it.
- In this field the label nuisance or negligence is "of no real significance" ([2002] 1 AC 321, 333, quoted in
  *Berent* at [12]).

## Later treatment
- *Kirk v Brent LBC* [2005] EWCA Civ 1701: lack of prior notice was not a knock-out point for striking out a claim.
  Constructive knowledge of the risk could still found liability, though recovering the full underpinning cost
  without notice may be difficult.
- *Berent v Family Mosaic Housing* [2012] EWCA Civ 961: the claimant's appeal failed because, before the damage, the
  defendants could not reasonably have foreseen a *real risk* of damage from their trees.
- Textbook view: *Delaware* applies foreseeability within the measured-duty type of nuisance (*Goldman v Hargrave*,
  *Leakey*). It does not convert nuisance generally into negligence.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an encroachment causing continuing damage? If so, the current owner can sue even if damage began earlier.
2. Did the defendant know, or ought it to have known, of a real risk of damage (*Berent*)?
3. Was the defendant given notice and a reasonable opportunity to abate (e.g. remove the tree) before the works?
4. Are the remedial costs reasonable? Note that removing the tree may have been the cheaper remedy.

## Common errors to avoid

- Notice under Delaware [34] need not be in writing: oral requests are effective notice and can be proved by witness evidence. Recommend a confirming letter only to ease proof; do not call oral requests legally insufficient or inherently weak. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1855/q06#1]
