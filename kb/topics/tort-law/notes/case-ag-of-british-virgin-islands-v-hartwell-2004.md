# Attorney General of the British Virgin Islands v Hartwell [2004] UKPC 12 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. The panel, date, facts and outcome below are confirmed by a law report summary
> and commentary. No paragraph pinpoints or law-report citations are given; check the report before quoting.

## Overview
Privy Council, on appeal from the Court of Appeal of the British Virgin Islands, 23 February 2004. Panel: Lord
Nicholls of Birkenhead, Lord Hope of Craighead, Lord Walker of Gestingthorpe, Baroness Hale of Richmond and Lord
Brown of Eaton-under-Heywood; Lord Nicholls delivered the judgment of the Board. The case is cited both on vicarious
liability for an employee's deliberate misuse of a weapon and on the direct negligence of an employer who entrusts a
firearm to an unsuitable officer.

## Facts
A probationary police constable abandoned his post, travelled to another island, and went into a crowded bar where
his former partner worked as a waitress. In a jealous rage he fired shots at her with his police-issue revolver. The
claimant, a British tourist in the bar, was seriously injured. He sued the Attorney General (for the government) in
negligence and on vicarious liability. He relied on two earlier incidents involving the officer as showing that he
was unfit to have access to firearms. The trial judge dismissed the claim, the BVI Court of Appeal allowed the
claimant's appeal, and the Attorney General appealed to the Privy Council.

## Held
- The Attorney General's appeal was **dismissed**; the government was liable.
- **Vicarious liability:** the government was not vicariously liable. The officer's conduct in leaving his post and
  using the gun to pursue a personal grievance was not sufficiently connected with his police duties.
- **Direct negligence:** police authorities who entrust an officer with a gun owe the public a duty to take reasonable
  care that the officer is a suitable person to have such a dangerous weapon. The duty extends to injury caused by
  misuse of the gun, whether accidental or deliberate. On the facts the senior officers' relaxed and casual response
  to the earlier incidents was a breach of that duty, so liability in negligence was established.

## Unconfirmed or omitted
- The detail of the two earlier incidents is not set out in the sources consulted.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. For vicarious liability, ask whether the deliberate wrong was closely connected with what the employee was employed
   to do. Using a work-issued item for a private grudge, after leaving duty, will tend not to be enough.
2. Consider direct liability in negligence: did the employer owe a duty in entrusting a dangerous item and breach it by
   failing to assess or supervise the employee?
3. Keep the two routes separate, since an employer may escape one and be liable under the other.
