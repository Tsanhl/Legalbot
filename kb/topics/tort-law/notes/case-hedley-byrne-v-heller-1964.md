# Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them. Decided 28 May 1963; reported in 1964.

## Overview
House of Lords (Lords Reid, Morris of Borth-y-Gest, Hodson, Devlin and Pearce). The case recognised liability in negligence for **careless statements causing pure economic loss**, based on a "special relationship" and **assumption of responsibility**, although on the facts the defendant escaped liability by disclaimer.

## Facts
Hedley Byrne, advertising agents, placed orders on credit for a client, Easipower, and would be personally liable if it defaulted. Through their bank they asked Easipower's bank, Heller & Partners, for a reference. Heller replied, free of charge, that Easipower was considered good for its ordinary business engagements, but with a statement that the reference was given without responsibility on the part of the bank. Hedley Byrne relied on it, Easipower went into liquidation, and Hedley Byrne suffered loss.

## Held
- A duty of care can arise in respect of statements (advice or information) where there is a **special relationship**: the speaker has special skill or knowledge, knows or ought to know the other will rely on the statement, and the reliance is reasonable.
- The key idea in the speeches was the voluntary **assumption of responsibility** by the speaker.
- Heller's **disclaimer** meant it had not assumed responsibility, so the claim failed.

## Why it matters
- Opened the way for compensation for pure economic loss caused by negligent misstatement, outside contract, where earlier case law had not allowed it.
- Founds the modern law on negligent misstatement; also overlaps with misrepresentation, as the statement may also induce a contract (see the contract notes).
- Disclaimers can negate assumption of responsibility, but see exclusion-clause controls.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Statement made by someone with skill or knowledge, who knew or ought to have known the claimant would rely?
2. Was the reliance reasonable, and had the speaker assumed responsibility (consider disclaimers)?
3. Breach of the standard of care, causation, and loss, then consider the alternatives in misrepresentation.md.
