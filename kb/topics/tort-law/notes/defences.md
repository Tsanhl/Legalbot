# Defences to Negligence (Contributory Negligence, Volenti, Illegality)

## Overview
Even where duty, breach and causation are proved, the defendant may escape or reduce liability by raising a defence. The burden of establishing a defence is on the defendant. The module covers three: the **partial** defence of contributory negligence (damages reduced in proportion to the claimant's share of responsibility), and the **complete** defences of voluntary assumption of risk (*volenti non fit injuria*, including waiver of liability) and illegality (*ex turpi causa non oritur actio*). The law is that of England & Wales (with some Scottish appeals, such as *Jackson v Murray*, decided in the UK Supreme Court on the same statute). In practice contributory negligence is raised constantly and has a great effect on outcomes, while volenti is now very narrow and illegality has been reshaped by *Patel v Mirza* [2016] UKSC 42.

## The law

### A. Contributory negligence (partial defence)

**Statute.** Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, s 1(1): where a person suffers damage "as the result partly of his own fault and partly of the fault of any other person", the claim is not defeated, but damages are reduced "to such extent as the court thinks just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility for the damage". Section 4 defines "fault" as negligence, breach of statutory duty or other act or omission which gives rise to liability in tort or would, apart from the Act, give rise to the defence of contributory negligence. Section 1(2) requires the court to record the total damages before reduction. Before 1945 any contributory fault was a complete bar (Lord Blackburn in *Cayzer, Irvine & Co v Carron* (1884)), softened by the "last opportunity" rule.

**Three questions (lecture):**
1. **Did the claimant fail to take reasonable care for their own safety?** (*Jones v Livox Quarries* [1952] 2 QB 608, listed in the materials as the authority for this question.) The standard is objective but adjusted for children: children can be contributorily negligent, judged by the standard of an ordinary child of that age (*Gough v Thorne*; *Young v Kent CC* [2005] EWHC 1342 (12-year-old on a skylight); *Honnor v Lewis* [2005] EWHC 747 (QB) (20% for an 11-year-old)). An intoxicated claimant is judged by their actions (*Cameron v Swan* [2021] CSIH 30: 65% reduction for a drunk pedestrian lying in the road, though the driver still owed a duty).
2. **Did that failure contribute to the claimant's damage?** It is the damage, not the accident, that must be partly caused by the claimant's fault. *Froom v Butcher* [1976] QB 286: failure to wear a seatbelt does not cause the crash but may increase the injury. Causation must be proved: *Stanton v Collinson* [2010] EWCA Civ 81 (no reduction where evidence did not show injuries would have been significantly less with a belt). Fault can include deliberate acts: *Reeves v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2000] 1 AC 360 (prisoner's suicide could be "fault"; damages reduced by 50%).
3. **By how much should damages be reduced?** The court weighs both **blameworthiness** and **causative potency** (*Davies v Swan Motor Co* [1949]; *Jackson v Murray* [2015] UKSC 5). Apportionment is rough and ready, usually in round figures.
   - *Froom v Butcher* tariff for seatbelts: 25% if wearing a belt would have prevented the injury altogether; 15% if it would have made injuries less severe.
   - *Capps v Miller* [1989] 1 WLR 839: unfastened moped helmet, 10%.
   - *Hughes v Williams* [2013] EWCA Civ 455: mother using a booster seat instead of an age-appropriate seat, 25%.
   - *Jackson v Murray* [2015] UKSC 5: 13-year-old stepped out from behind a minibus into a car driven too fast; trial judge 90%, Extra Division 70%, Supreme Court (majority) 50%. Lord Reed: the parties' "fault" is incommensurable (breach of duty versus want of regard for one's own interests), so the exercise cannot be demonstrably correct; an appellate court may interfere only where the apportionment falls outside the range of reasonable disagreement.
   - *Corr v IBC* [2008] UKHL 13: depression after an accident led to suicide; Lord Bingham would make no reduction; Lord Scott would have reduced by 20%; Lords Mance and Neuberger approached fault in terms of autonomy.

**Limits (Weir):** contributory negligence is not a defence to deceit or (per the textbook) trespass to the person; by statute it is no defence to conversion or intentional trespass to goods; the Act applies only to tort (and to contract claims only where the claim could equally be framed in tort). A driver's fault is not generally imputed to a passenger, nor a parent's fault to a child. Where there are several tortfeasors, the claimant's share is fixed under the 1945 Act and the tortfeasors' shares under the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978 (Weir's worked example).

### B. Voluntary assumption of risk (volenti non fit injuria) (complete defence)

**Principle.** Lord Herschell in *Smith v Baker* [1891]: one who has invited or assented to an act being done towards him cannot, when he suffers it, complain of it as a wrong (lecture paraphrase). The defence absolves the defendant entirely.

**Two forms (lecture, drawing on Diplock LJ in *Wooldridge v Sumner* [1963]):**
1. The claimant consents to the specific harm, or to the risk that the defendant may negligently cause harm.
2. The claimant consents (or is treated as consenting) to the defendant excluding liability for injury (waiver). Note that by Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 a business cannot exclude liability for negligently caused death or personal injury by contract term or notice (Weir).

**Requirements.** The defendant must prove the claimant (a) knew the nature and extent of the risk, and (b) voluntarily agreed to it (lecture: *Morris v Murray*; *Dann v Hamilton*). The textbook stresses three restrictions:
1. **Acceptance of the legal risk, not just the physical risk.** Lord Denning in *Nettleship v Weston* [1971]: knowledge of the risk, or willingness to take it, is not enough; the claimant must agree, expressly or impliedly, to waive any claim for negligence. The instructor in *Nettleship* had checked the learner was insured.
2. **Agreement, freely made.** *Smith v Baker & Sons* [1891] AC 325: an employee who carries on working knowing of a risk does not thereby consent ("scienti" is not "volenti", Bowen LJ in *Thomas v Quartermaine*). Scott LJ in *Bowater v Rowley Regis* [1944]: a person is not truly willing unless free to choose, with full knowledge and without constraint. Employment cases rarely satisfy this (Lord Pearce in *ICI v Shatwell*: the defence's role in master and servant cases was "inglorious" before 1891 and "insignificant" since).
3. **Consent to the negligence itself, not just to the ordinary risks of the activity.** Sporting cases: *Smoldon v Whitworth* (CA, 1996) (rugby referee failing to enforce safety rules); *Watson v British Boxing Board of Control* [2001] QB 1134; *Condon v Basi* [1985] (listed in the materials). *White Lion Hotel v James* [2021] EWCA Civ 31: intoxicated hotel guest who fell from a low window; knowledge of general risk was not acceptance of the occupier's breach; 60% contributory negligence instead (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957 s 2(5)).

**Where volenti has succeeded:**
- *ICI v Shatwell* [1965] AC 656: brothers deliberately used a forbidden and unlawful method of testing detonators; volenti barred one brother's claim against the employer based on vicarious liability for the other. Lord Reid distinguished careless collaboration (contributory negligence only) from deliberate joint disobedience (volenti). Lord Pearce inferred an implied agreement not to sue.
- *Morris v Murray* [1991] 2 QB 6: claimant drank heavily with the pilot (equivalent of 17 whiskies), drove to the airfield and helped prepare the plane; volenti succeeded. The textbook explains *Shatwell* and *Morris* as cases of deliberate collusion in creating the risk.
- Contrast *Dann v Hamilton* [1939] 1 KB 509: passenger accepting a lift from a driver who had been drinking was not volens. (Owens v Brimmell [1977] QB 859 later reduced damages for contributory negligence in such a case.)

**Statutory exclusion for passengers.** Road Traffic Act 1988 s 149: where compulsory insurance is required, any antecedent agreement purporting to negative or restrict the driver's liability to passengers is of no effect (s 149(2)), and "the fact that a person so carried has willingly accepted as his the risk of negligence on the part of the user shall not be treated as negativing any such liability" (s 149(3)). This is why *Morris v Murray* (an aircraft) could succeed while road cases cannot.

**Rescuers.** *Baker v Hopkins* (1959) (lecture): where the defendant has put a third party's person or property in danger so that the claimant is under legal or moral pressure to attempt a rescue, a rescuer who is injured is not barred by volenti.

**Suicide in custody.** *Reeves v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2000] 1 AC 360: volenti did not apply where the harm (suicide) was the very thing the defendants were under a duty to prevent (Weir; lecture lists the case under volenti).

### C. Illegality (ex turpi causa) (complete defence)

**Scope.** It denies recovery to some claimants injured while acting unlawfully. the materials identifies two situations: (1) the claimant is tortiously harmed while engaged in illegal activity; (2) the defendant's tort causes the claimant to commit an illegal act. It rests on public policy, not fairness between the parties (Lord Mansfield in *Holman v Johnson* (1775)), and the court may raise it of its own motion. "Turpitude" means criminal or quasi-criminal acts (*Les Laboratoires Servier v Apotex* [2014] UKSC 55); not every offence counts (failure to wear a seatbelt leads to contributory negligence, not illegality).

**General approach: *Patel v Mirza* [2016] UKSC 42.** The claimant paid money to the defendant to bet on shares using inside information (contrary to the Criminal Justice Act 1993 s 52); the scheme failed and he sought return of the money; illegality did not bar the claim. Lord Toulson (majority) rejected the rule-based reliance test of *Tinsley v Milligan* [1994] 1 AC 340. The rationale is that it would be contrary to the public interest to enforce a claim if doing so would harm the integrity of the legal system. To decide, the court considers the "trio" of considerations (para 101): (a) the underlying purpose of the prohibition transgressed and whether denying the claim would enhance it; (b) any other relevant public policy that denial of the claim may affect; (c) whether denial would be a proportionate response to the illegality, bearing in mind that punishment is for the criminal courts. Lord Sumption favoured a narrower rules-based "consistency" approach. *Patel* applies across civil law, including tort: *Henderson v Dorset Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust* [2020] UKSC 43; *Stoffel & Co v Grondona* [2020] UKSC 42 (mortgage fraudster could still sue negligent solicitors; illegality not central to the claim, and allowing it protected the defrauded lender).

**Tort-specific rules from *Gray v Thames Trains* [2009] UKHL 33** (confirmed as still relevant in *Henderson*):
- *Facts:* claimant suffered PTSD in the Ladbroke Grove rail crash caused by the defendants, then killed a man, pleaded guilty to manslaughter on grounds of diminished responsibility and was detained; he claimed lost earnings before and during detention, and other losses. The claim failed.
- **Narrow rule:** a claimant cannot recover for loss (e.g. detention, lost earnings during it) that flows from a sentence imposed for his own criminal act. Justified by **consistency**: the law cannot both punish someone and compensate them for the consequences (Lord Brown).
- **Wide rule:** a claimant cannot recover for loss that is a consequence of his own criminal act. Lord Hoffmann justified it by public notions of fair distribution of resources, and framed it as causation: did the claimant's crime cause the damage (*Vellino v Chief Constable* [2002]), or did it merely provide the occasion for the defendant's tort (*Revill v Newbery* [1996] QB 567)? *Henderson* restated this as a "public confidence principle" related to consistency.
- *Clunis v Camden & Islington HA* [1998] 3 All ER 180: listed in the materials alongside *Revill v Newbery* as an illegality authority (no further detail in the sources used; Weir cites it in his discussion of illicit conduct).

**Joint criminal enterprise.** *Pitts v Hunt* [1991] 1 QB 24: pillion passenger who had been drinking with the rider and encouraged reckless driving could not recover; volenti was excluded by RTA 1988 s 149(3), so the claim failed on illegality (Beldam LJ thought volenti would otherwise have succeeded). *McCracken v Smith* [2015] EWCA Civ 380: claim against the joint enterprise rider barred, but not against a negligent minibus driver, with contributory negligence reducing damages instead. *Delaney v Pickett* [2011] EWCA Civ 1532: possession of cannabis merely incidental to negligent driving; claim not barred (though compensation from the MIB failed on the agreement's crime exception, later held contrary to EU Directives).

**Proportionality and criminal claimants.** *Revill v Newbery* [1996] QB 567: burglar shot by an allotment holder sleeping in his shed; illegality rejected; even a trespasser with criminal intent is not outside the law's protection (damages were reduced for contributory negligence, which Weir criticises).

## Key authorities
- Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 ss 1(1), 4: apportionment; definition of fault.
- *Jones v Livox Quarries* [1952] 2 QB 608: claimant's failure to take reasonable care for own safety (lecture).
- *Froom v Butcher* [1976] QB 286: seatbelts; cause of damage not accident; 25%/15% tariff.
- *Jackson v Murray* [2015] UKSC 5: apportionment; blameworthiness and causative potency; 50%.
- *Reeves v Commissioner of Police* [2000] 1 AC 360: deliberate self-harm can be "fault"; 50%; volenti inapplicable.
- *Smith v Baker & Sons* [1891] AC 325: knowledge is not consent.
- *Dann v Hamilton* [1939] 1 KB 509: passenger with a drinking driver not volens.
- *ICI v Shatwell* [1965] AC 656: volenti succeeded where employees deliberately disobeyed safety rules together.
- *Morris v Murray* [1991] 2 QB 6: volenti succeeded; drunk pilot.
- *Baker v Hopkins* (1959): rescuers not volens.
- Road Traffic Act 1988 s 149(3): volenti excluded for passengers in insured vehicles.
- *Pitts v Hunt* [1991] 1 QB 24: illegality barred pillion passenger's claim.
- *Gray v Thames Trains* [2009] UKHL 33: narrow and wide rules; consistency.
- *Patel v Mirza* [2016] UKSC 42: trio of considerations; integrity of the legal system.
- *Henderson v Dorset Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust* [2020] UKSC 43; *Stoffel v Grondona* [2020] UKSC 42: Patel applies in tort; Gray survives.
- *Revill v Newbery* [1996] QB 567: illegality rejected for injured burglar.
- *Clunis v Camden & Islington HA* [1998] 3 All ER 180: illegality authority listed in the materials.

## Debate and critique
- **Who bears the reduction?** The textbook and Weir both point out that when damages are reduced in a personal injury claim the uninsured claimant bears the shortfall, while the defendant's liability insurer benefits; reductions bite hardest on the most seriously injured, whose damages mostly meet needs (care, accommodation). Social security deductions are not reduced for contributory negligence, which can leave the claimant with little (Weir).
- **Arbitrariness and unpredictability.** Lord Reed in *Jackson* admitted apportionment compares incommensurable kinds of "fault". Weir criticises the unpredictability (in *Reeves*, three Court of Appeal judges would have reduced by 0%, 100% and 50%) and the judicial tendency to use the 1945 Act to "split the difference". Goudkamp and Klar argue causative potency should play no part; Goudkamp and Nolan's empirical studies of contributory negligence (2016) 79 MLR 575 (textbook further reading).
- **Autonomy versus culpability.** *Corr v IBC* shows a split: some judges treat fault as personal choice (autonomy), others as blameworthiness. The textbook questions whether consistency with other areas requires reducing damages for a suicide caused by the defendant's negligence.
- **Is volenti obsolete?** The broad nineteenth-century version rested on fictional consent and was "appalling" in employment cases (Glanville Williams). Its modern narrowness, the rise of apportionment and RTA s 149 leave it little room; in sport it has been called "otiose". The textbook explains the surviving cases (*Shatwell*, *Morris*) as deliberate collusion in creating the risk. Weir notes the courts' unease in leaving people with the consequences of their own choices and asks whether this respects personal autonomy. Lecture reading: Jaffey [1985] CLJ 87; Lunney, "Personal Responsibility and the 'New' Volenti" (2005) 13 Tort L Rev 76.
- **Rules or discretion in illegality.** The Supreme Court divided in *Patel* between Lord Toulson's structured policy approach and Lord Sumption's rule-based consistency approach. Critics feared uncertainty; *Stoffel* insisted the evaluation is not mechanistic but also not an undisciplined discretion, and that "no profit from wrong" is no longer the true focus. *Henderson* holds that Patel is not "year zero": older authorities stand unless incompatible. The textbook notes the "wide rule" still lacks clear principle and that McLachlin J in *Hall v Hebert* would not extend the bar to ordinary personal injury compensation.
- **Law Commission.** The textbook cites the Law Commission's Consultation Paper No 189 (2009) recommending the defence be allowed only where firmly justified by its underlying policies (furthering the purpose of the rule infringed, consistency, no profit from wrong, deterrence, integrity of the legal system); the materials lists Consultation Paper No 320 *The Illegality Defence* (2010). Further reading: Davies (2010) Conv 282; Glofcheski (Legal Studies); Goudkamp, *Tort Law Defences* (2013); Green and Bogg, *Illegality After Patel v Mirza* (2018); Virgo, "The Illegality Revolution" (2018).
- **Criminal claimants and contributory negligence.** Weir argues that reducing a burglar's damages (*Revill*) rather than barring him was objectionable, against judicial dicta that apportionment gives a fairer result.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **First confirm liability** (duty, breach, causation). Then remember the defendant bears the burden of proving any defence.
2. **Contributory negligence:**
   - Did the claimant fail to take reasonable care for their own safety (seatbelt, helmet, intoxication, stepping into the road, riding with a drunk driver)? Adjust for children.
   - Did that failure contribute to the damage (not necessarily the accident)? Is there evidence (*Stanton*)?
   - Suggest a percentage: use tariffs (*Froom*: 25%/15%; *Capps*: 10%) and analogous cases (*Jackson*: 50%); weigh blameworthiness and causative potency; state a round figure.
3. **Volenti:**
   - Is it a road traffic passenger case? If so, RTA 1988 s 149(3) excludes it; move on to contributory negligence or illegality.
   - Did the claimant have full knowledge of the specific risk, and freely agree (expressly or by clear conduct) to bear the legal risk of the defendant's negligence? Mere knowledge is insufficient (*Smith v Baker*; *Nettleship*).
   - Was the claimant deliberately complicit in creating the risk (*Morris*, *Shatwell*)? Or merely going along with it (*Dann*)?
   - Is the claimant a rescuer (*Baker v Hopkins*) or an employee (pressure negates free choice)? Is the harm the very thing the defendant had a duty to prevent (*Reeves*)?
   - Any exclusion notice or term? Consider UCTA for business liability for death or personal injury.
4. **Illegality:**
   - Is the claimant's conduct criminal or quasi-criminal? Trivial or regulatory offences rarely suffice.
   - Is the loss a consequence of a sentence or of the claimant's own crime (*Gray* narrow/wide rules)? Or did the crime merely provide the occasion (*Delaney*, *Revill*)?
   - Joint criminal enterprise between claimant and defendant (*Pitts*)? Compare a third party who is not part of it (*McCracken*).
   - Apply the *Patel* trio: purpose of the prohibition; other policies; proportionality.
5. **Combine:** a complete defence ends the claim; otherwise reduce damages for contributory negligence.

**Typical traps:** arguing volenti for a car passenger; confusing knowledge of risk with consent; forgetting that the 1945 Act concerns the damage, not the accident; assuming any illegality bars the claim; omitting *Patel*. **Facts that change the outcome:** whether the vehicle needs compulsory insurance (car versus plane); whether the claimant helped create the danger; how closely the crime is linked to the injury; the claimant's age.
