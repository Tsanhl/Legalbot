# Private Nuisance

## Overview
Private nuisance is one of the land torts, along with occupiers' liability, trespass to land, public nuisance and *Rylands v Fletcher*. All of them are older than negligence. It regulates relations between neighbours by setting their mutual rights and obligations in the use of land. Winfield described it as "any unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land or some right over it"; Steele notes this is a description, not a definition. It concerns **indirect** interferences and is **actionable only on proof of damage**. It protects the right to enjoy one's property (*Corby Group Litigation Claimants v Corby BC* [2008] EWCA Civ 463, at [29]). The tort matters because it is "stricter than" negligence, focuses on the *interference* rather than the defendant's carelessness, and offers injunctions as well as damages. Recent Supreme Court decisions (*Coventry v Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13; *Fearn v Tate Gallery* [2023] UKSC 4) have reshaped it. Nuisance is only part of the control of land use: planning, pollution regulation and statutory nuisance also matter (lecture, citing *Forster v SSCLG* [2016] EWCA Civ 609).

## The law

### Three kinds of nuisance
Lord Lloyd in *Hunter v Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655 identified three kinds:
1. encroachment on a neighbour's land;
2. direct physical injury to a neighbour's land;
3. interference with a neighbour's quiet enjoyment of land, known as "amenity" nuisance.

Since *St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping* (1865) 11 HLC 642, courts have distinguished **material damage to property** from interference with use and enjoyment (**amenity harm**). The kind of harm partly decides what counts as reasonable use. Interference with easements such as rights to light is also actionable (e.g. *Colls v Home and Colonial Stores* [1904] AC 179; *Midtown v City of London Real Property* [2005] EWHC 33, a right to light acquired by prescription under s 3 Prescription Act 1832). In *Williams v Network Rail Infrastructure* [2018] EWCA Civ 1514 (Japanese knotweed), the Court of Appeal said the categories are not mutually exclusive. An encroachment can be actionable because it interferes with amenity, without physical damage. But nuisance does not protect property value as an investment, so loss of marketability alone is not enough.

the materials frames the claim as **four hurdles**:

### Hurdle 1: Standing
1. **Who can sue.** Only those with a proprietary interest in the land, such as freeholders and leaseholders. Licensees cannot sue (*Hunter v Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655; *Malone v Laskey* [1907] 2 KB 141; *McKenna v British Aluminium* [2002] Env LR 30). *Hunter* overruled *Khorasandjian v Bush* [1993] QB 727, where a daughter living in her parents' home had been allowed to sue in nuisance for harassing phone calls. The House of Lords confirmed that nuisance is a tort *against land*, not against the person.
2. **Who can be sued.** "Neighbours", though not in the *Donoghue v Stevenson* sense: the creator or adopter of the nuisance, judged by asking who has **possession and control** (*Cocking v Eacott* [2016] EWCA Civ 140).
   - **Adopting or continuing a nuisance.** In *Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880, a trespasser laid a pipe on the defendants' land with a badly placed grating. The defendants learned of it and used it to drain their fields. It blocked and flooded the neighbour, and the defendants were liable. An occupier "continues" a nuisance if, knowing or presumed to know of it, they fail to take reasonable means to end it despite ample time. They "adopt" it if they make any use of the thing that constitutes it (Viscount Maugham). Lord Atkin said negligence is not essential, but "some degree of personal responsibility" is.
   - **Natural hazards: the "measured duty of care".** In *Goldman v Hargrave* [1967] 1 AC 645 (PC), a tree was struck by lightning and the occupier let it burn out. The fire then spread. In *Leakey v National Trust* [1980] QB 485, soil and debris fell from a mound onto houses. Together these impose a positive duty to take reasonable steps against hazards on one's land, whether natural or man-made. The standard is **subjective**: it varies with the defendant's resources and abilities. *Leakey* overruled *Giles v Walker* (1890), the thistles case. In *Holbeck Hall Hotel v Scarborough BC* [2000] QB 836, a coastal erosion case, the duty arose only for known or patent defects, not latent ones.
3. **What can be claimed.** An injunction (full or partial) and/or damages for physical damage to property or for loss of amenity. Only harm to the land, including its amenity, is recoverable. **Personal injury is not compensated as such** (*Hunter*). Amenity damages are assessed as the reduction in the land's amenity value. They do not rise because more people live there, but they do reflect the property's size and value (Lord Hoffmann, *Hunter*). Older authorities on damages: *Bone v Seale* [1975] 1 WLR 797 (smell), whose analogy with personal injury awards Lord Hoffmann disapproved; *Andreae v Selfridge* [1938] Ch 1; *Halsey v Esso Petroleum* [1961] 1 WLR 683. *Raymond v Young* [2015] EWCA Civ 456 held that separate distress awards on top of amenity damages are double counting.
4. **Foreseeability.** Damages are available only for damage of a reasonably foreseeable type. Lord Goff said: "if a plaintiff is in ordinary circumstances only able to claim damages in respect of personal injuries where he can prove such foreseeability ... it is difficult to see why ... he should be in a stronger position to claim damages for interference with the enjoyment of his land" (*Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather plc* [1994] 2 AC 264, 300, following *The Wagon Mound (No 2)*). Foreseeability is not relevant to whether an injunction should be granted against a continuing nuisance.

### Hurdle 2: Emanation
1. Generally, something must pass from the defendant's land to the claimant's, such as noise, smell, smoke, dust, vibration or roots (Lord Goff, *Hunter*). The mere presence of a building is not enough. In *Hunter* itself, interference with TV reception by the Canary Wharf tower was not actionable. Rare exceptions include *Thompson-Schwab v Costaki* [1956] 1 WLR 335 (the sight of prostitutes and clients) and *Bank of New Zealand v Greenwood* [1984] (glare).
2. **Overlooking: the *Fearn* litigation.**
   - **High Court** [2018] EWHC 246 (Ch): deliberate overlooking *could* be a nuisance, reading the tort in line with Article 8. But the Tate's viewing platform served a legitimate purpose, and the claimants' floor-to-ceiling glass flats were a particularly sensitive use. Claim dismissed.
   - **Court of Appeal** [2020] EWCA Civ 104: same result, but held that overlooking is *incapable* of being a nuisance as a matter of authority and principle. The real issue was privacy, which other laws already address, and extending protection was for Parliament.
   - **Supreme Court** [2023] UKSC 4: overlooking *can* in principle be a nuisance. The majority (Lord Leggatt) held that the constant viewing and photography caused a substantial interference with the ordinary use and enjoyment of the flats ([148]). A public viewing gallery is "a very particular and exceptional use of land" that is not necessary for the ordinary use of an art museum. So the Tate could not rely on the "give and take" principle ([50], [74]). The court seemed to reject the "sensitive user" characterisation. If ordinary use caused the intrusion, the claimants would have to put up with it whatever their building design ([72]–[75]), but this use was not ordinary. Privacy interests can therefore be protected through property rights, as amenity nuisance.

### Hurdle 3: Unreasonable interference (a matrix of factors)
Not every interference is a nuisance. The question is whether there was an *unreasonable interference* with the claimant's use and enjoyment. "Reasonable user" is not the same as reasonable care: "if the user is not reasonable, the defendant will be liable, even though he may have used reasonable care and skill to avoid it" (Lord Goff, *Cambridge Water*, 299). Bramwell B's principle in *Bamford v Turnley* (1862) protects acts "necessary for the common and ordinary use and occupation of land", provided they are conveniently done. This is the rule of "give and take, live and let live".

1. **Always considered: intensity.** This covers duration, frequency and timing (*Kennaway v Thompson* [1981] QB 88; *Crown River Cruises v Kimbolton Fireworks* [1996] 2 Lloyd's Rep 533).
2. **Sometimes considered, for amenity harm only: locality.** The reasonableness of a use can depend on the character of the area (*Sturges v Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852, the materials's lead authority on locality). *St Helen's v Tipping*: locality is irrelevant where there is material injury to property (here, damage to trees). See also *Hirose Electrical v Peak Ingredients* [2011] EWCA Civ 987; *Baxter v Camden LBC (No 2)* [2001]; *Coventry v Lawrence*.
   - **Planning permission and locality.** *Gillingham BC v Medway (Chatham) Dock Co* [1993] held that planning permission had changed the character of the area. *Wheeler v JJ Saunders* [1996] Ch 19 limited that. *Coventry v Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13 involved a motor-racing stadium. Lord Neuberger held that planning permission is normally "of no assistance" to a defendant. Its terms may sometimes be a starting point as evidence. In assessing the character of the locality, the court must ignore the defendant's own activities to the extent they are a nuisance ([65]). Lord Carnwath, supported by Lord Clarke, would allow that exceptional "strategic" permissions can change the character of an area.
3. **Sometimes considered, for amenity harm: sensitivity of the claimant.** Nuisance protects only ordinary use, judged by "plain and sober and simple notions" (*Walter v Selfe* (1851)). In *Robinson v Kilvert* (1889) 41 Ch D 88, heat from a cellar damaged only special sensitive paper, and the claim failed. In *Network Rail Infrastructure v Morris* [2004] EWCA Civ 172 (railway signals interfering with electric guitars), the court used reasonableness and foreseeability, and Buxton LJ doubted the abnormal-sensitivity rule had "further life". If sensitivity only increases the *extent* of foreseeable damage, all of it is recoverable (*McKinnon Industries v Walker* [1951], the orchids case). *Fearn* [2023] UKSC 4 refocused the question on whether the defendant's use is ordinary.
4. **Sometimes considered: malice.**
   - *Christie v Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316: noise made deliberately to annoy a music-teaching neighbour was restrained, because it was not a legitimate use.
   - *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett* [1936] 2 KB 468: guns were fired to disrupt fox breeding. Damages and an injunction were granted.
   - Contrast *Bradford Corporation v Pickles* [1895] AC 587. Motive was irrelevant to diverting percolating water, because there is no right to receive that water. In Lord Macnaghten's words, it was "damage without legal injury". *Hunter* is also cited in the materials.

### Hurdle 4: Defences
The general defences of volenti and contributory negligence apply in principle, but are of limited use (Steele). The nuisance-specific defences are:
1. **Statutory authority.** There is a defence where the nuisance is the inevitable result of what the statute authorised, done without negligence (*Allen v Gulf Oil Refining* [1981] AC 1013, which also recognised *implied* authority to operate a refinery; *Manchester Corporation v Farnworth* [1930] AC 171). See also *Hatton v UK* (2003) 37 EHRR 611 and *Barr v Biffa Waste Services* [2012] EWCA Civ 312. Planning permission is *not* statutory authority.
2. **Prescription after 20 years.** The activity must have been an actionable nuisance throughout the 20 years. In *Sturges v Bridgman*, time ran only once the doctor built his consulting room.
3. **Public benefit or utility** is generally not a defence on liability, but it is relevant to remedy. As Bramwell B put it, the question is whether the activity is still worthwhile once compensation is paid (*Barr v Biffa*; *Dennis v Ministry of Defence* [2003] EWHC 793 (QB), Harrier jet noise: nuisance found, but damages awarded instead of an injunction). *Miller v Jackson* [1977] QB 966, the cricket case, is unusual in letting the public interest win.
4. **"Coming to the nuisance" is not a defence** (*Miller v Jackson*; *Sturges v Bridgman*; *Coventry v Lawrence*). There is no such defence where the claimant uses her property "for essentially the same purpose as that for which it has been used by her predecessors since before the alleged nuisance started" ([51], Lord Neuberger). But Lord Neuberger suggested ([56]) that where the *claimant* builds on or changes the use of her land, a pre-existing activity may not be a nuisance if:
   - (i) it is a nuisance only because it affects the senses of those on the claimant's land;
   - (ii) it was not a nuisance before the change;
   - (iii) it is a reasonable and otherwise lawful use;
   - (iv) it is carried out reasonably; and
   - (v) it causes no greater nuisance than before.

   These criteria were applied in *Jones v MoD* [2021] EWHC 2276 (QB), where RAF jet noise defeated a claim by would-be holiday-park developers (Steele).

### Remedies
1. **Injunction.** The usual remedy for continuing nuisances. It is discretionary and can be partial, for example limiting hours.
   - **Damages in lieu** under Lord Cairns' Act 1858 were long limited by the *Shelfer* "working rule" (*Shelfer v City of London Electric Lighting Co* [1895] 1 Ch 287): the injury must be small, capable of being estimated in money, adequately compensated by a small payment, and an injunction must be oppressive.
   - *Coventry v Lawrence* changed this. The Shelfer criteria are no longer to be applied strictly. Lord Neuberger said: "First, the application of the four tests must not be such as 'to be a fetter on the exercise of the court's discretion'. Secondly, it would, in the absence of additional relevant circumstances pointing the other way, normally be right to refuse an injunction if those four tests were satisfied. Thirdly, the fact that those tests are not all satisfied does not mean that an injunction should be granted" ([123]).
   - Public interest can always be a relevant factor ([124]), and planning permission "may provide strong support for the contention that the activity is of benefit to the public" ([125]).
   - Lord Sumption said *Shelfer* is "out of date". He suggested damages are ordinarily an adequate remedy, and that an injunction perhaps should not issue where planning permission has been granted. *Watson v Croft Promo-Sport* [2009] EWCA Civ 15 was disapproved.
2. **Damages.** For past damage and loss of amenity (*Dennis v MoD*). How to assess damages in lieu, and whether they can be gain-based (*Tamares v Fairpoint* [2007], hypothetical negotiation), remains unsettled.
3. **Abatement (self-help).** Confined to simple cases such as overhanging branches or encroaching roots, and to urgent cases (*Burton v Winters* [1993] 1 WLR 1077).

### Nuisance, Rylands and negligence overlap
Lord Walker in *Transco v Stockport MBC* [2003] UKHL 61 at [96] described nuisance as overlapping with, "indeed, is a sort of condominium with", negligence. *Sedleigh-Denfield* shows this. If the "nuisance" is the pipe, it is an ongoing state of affairs and fits private nuisance. If it is the flood, accumulated water "escaped", which points to *Rylands*. It could also be negligence, as a failure to take reasonable steps. Which action applies depends on what is characterised as the problem. In *Jalla v Shell* [2021] EWCA Civ 63, a one-off oil spill with lasting effects was not a continuing nuisance, which mattered for limitation (Steele).

## Key authorities
- *Hunter v Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655: TV interference and construction dust. Only those with proprietary interests can sue. An emanation is generally required. Nuisance is a tort to land. *Khorasandjian* overruled. Lord Cooke dissented.
- *St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping* (1865) 11 HLC 642: copper smelting damaged an estate. Locality does not excuse material damage to property.
- *Bamford v Turnley* (1862): Bramwell B's "give and take" and internalisation of costs.
- *Sturges v Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852: confectioner and doctor. Locality; prescription runs only from when the activity became a nuisance; coming to the nuisance is no defence.
- *Robinson v Kilvert* (1889); *Network Rail v Morris* [2004] EWCA Civ 172: sensitivity.
- *Christie v Davey* [1893]; *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett* [1936]; *Bradford v Pickles* [1895] AC 587: malice.
- *Kennaway v Thompson* [1981] QB 88: intensity of interference (duration, frequency, timing). Cited in *Dennis* as affirming *Shelfer*: being a public benefactor is not enough reason to refuse an injunction.
- *Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880: adopting and continuing a nuisance created by a trespasser.
- *Goldman v Hargrave* [1967] 1 AC 645; *Leakey v National Trust* [1980] QB 485; *Holbeck Hall v Scarborough* [2000] QB 836: measured duty for natural hazards.
- *Cambridge Water v ECL* [1994] 2 AC 264: foreseeability of damage type is required for damages. Reasonable user is distinct from reasonable care.
- *Coventry v Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13: motorsport noise. Planning permission is no defence and is generally irrelevant to locality. Coming to the nuisance is no defence, with qualifications. Shelfer relaxed and public interest relevant to remedy.
- *Fearn v Tate Gallery* [2023] UKSC 4: overlooking from a viewing platform was an actionable nuisance (reversing the CA [2020] EWCA Civ 104).
- *Allen v Gulf Oil* [1981] AC 1013: statutory authority, including implied authority.
- *Dennis v MoD* [2003] EWHC 793 (QB): Harrier noise was a nuisance, and damages were awarded instead of an injunction because of the public interest.
- *Miller v Jackson* [1977] QB 966: cricket. Coming to the nuisance is no defence; public interest relevant to the injunction.
- *Shelfer* [1895] 1 Ch 287: the working rule for damages in lieu, now relaxed.
- *Raymond v Young* [2015] EWCA Civ 456: amenity damages are measured by diminished use value. No separate award for distress.
- *Williams v Network Rail* [2018] EWCA Civ 1514: knotweed encroachment interfering with amenity is actionable, but loss of market value alone is not.
- *Cocking v Eacott* [2016] EWCA Civ 140: who can be sued depends on possession and control.

## Debate and critique
- **Property tort or personal tort?** *Hunter* insists that nuisance protects land. Weir finds it "odd to say that noises and smells affect the land itself, which has neither ears nor nose". He notes the restriction was a sensible response to children suing in order to get legal aid. Steele argues that Article 8 ECHR (respect for the home) supports treating the home as special, and that Lord Cooke's dissent shows the majority's position reflects policy rather than logic. Weir notes that the *Hunter* claimants later succeeded in Strasbourg on Article 8 in principle, although the interference there was justified.
- **Is nuisance strict?** Lord Goff called nuisance liability "generally ... strict". Steele prefers "stricter than negligence": careful conduct is no answer to an unreasonable interference, but foreseeability governs damages. The *Sedleigh-Denfield* and *Leakey* line uses negligence-like reasoning with a subjective standard. Gearty (1989) argued that such cases belong in negligence. Steele thinks that line is now too well established in nuisance to move. Lee, "What is Private Nuisance?" (2003), is on the reading list.
- **Planning and the public interest.** *Coventry* moves public interest from liability to remedy. Maria Lee (quoted by Lord Carnwath) said "the fact that something should go ahead in the public interest does not tell us where the costs should lie", and that nuisance survives in a regulatory state only through flexible remedies. Steele suggests Lord Neuberger may discount planning permission too much. Lord Sumption's view that damages should ordinarily suffice would greatly weaken the property right. Critics fear this lets defendants "buy" the right to commit a nuisance (compare Millett LJ in *Jaggard v Sawyer* on expropriation). Further reading includes Singh (2016) and Lee (2014) on *Coventry*.
- **Privacy through nuisance?** In *Fearn*, the CA's position (privacy is for other laws and Parliament, relying on *Wainwright*) contrasts with the UKSC majority's protection of privacy as amenity. Lees (2021) is on the reading list. The UKSC's focus on the "ordinary use" of the defendant's land revives Bramwell B's give-and-take test.
- **Environmental role.** Pontin (2013), Steele (1995) and Kumar and Frank (2018, climate-change emitters) discuss whether nuisance can address pollution. *Jalla v Shell* limits its effectiveness for long-term clean-up. *Cambridge Water* shows the courts deferring to legislation on environmental liability.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Classify the harm.** Is it physical damage, encroachment, or amenity? This affects whether locality and sensitivity are relevant (*St Helen's*). Is it continuing (injunction) or a one-off escape (consider *Rylands*)?
2. **Standing.** Does each claimant have a proprietary interest? Lodgers, children and partners without title cannot sue (*Hunter*). For a licensee, consider the PHA, negligence or privacy instead.
3. **Defendant.** Who created, adopted or continued the nuisance? For natural hazards or trespassers' acts, did the occupier know or ought to have known, and could they reasonably abate it given their resources (*Sedleigh-Denfield*, *Leakey*, *Holbeck*)?
4. **Emanation.** Did something cross the boundary? For overlooking or visual intrusion, apply *Fearn* [2023]. For blocking of TV signals or a view, *Hunter* says no.
5. **Unreasonableness.**
   - Intensity: time of day, duration, frequency.
   - Locality: ignore the defendant's own nuisance, and remember planning permission is normally irrelevant (*Coventry*).
   - Sensitivity: was the claimant's use abnormally delicate, or is the defendant's use out of the ordinary (*Fearn*)?
   - Malice (*Christie*).
   - Remember that taking care is not a defence.
6. **Defences.** Statutory authority and inevitability (*Allen*). Prescription: a full 20 years as an actionable nuisance. "Coming to the nuisance": usually no, but check Lord Neuberger's [56] criteria where the claimant changed their use. Public interest goes to remedy only.
7. **Remedies.** Injunction or damages in lieu under the flexible *Coventry* approach, weighing public interest, planning and effects on third parties. For damages: foreseeable type (*Cambridge Water*), loss of amenity value, no double counting (*Raymond*), no personal injury damages as such. Abatement only for simple or urgent cases.
8. **Traps.**
   - Treating planning permission as a defence.
   - Allowing non-owners to sue.
   - Applying the locality test to physical damage.
   - Forgetting that foreseeability matters for damages but not for an injunction.
   - Ignoring that *Fearn* reversed the CA.
