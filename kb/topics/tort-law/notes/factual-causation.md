# Factual Causation

## Overview
Negligence is actionable only on proof of damage, so the claimant must show that the defendant's *breach of duty* (not just the defendant's conduct) caused the harm. The starting point is the "but for" test, applied on the balance of probabilities. Most of the difficult law, and most of the essay material, concerns the cases where that test fails or cannot be answered: cumulative causes (*Bonnington*), increase in risk (*McGhee*, *Fairchild* and the mesothelioma line), successive causes (*Baker v Willoughby*, *Jobling*), and claims framed as the loss of a chance (*Hotson*, *Gregg v Scott*). Jurisdiction: England and Wales (with some Scottish and Privy Council authority treated as persuasive or applied in English courts).

Causation is the link that explains why *this* defendant must pay *this* claimant for *this* loss. The module splits it into two questions:
1. Factual causation: would the claimant have suffered the loss but for the breach?
2. Legal causation: is the breach the kind of "but for" cause that justifies liability (remoteness and intervening acts)? See `legal-causation-and-remoteness.md`.

## The law

### 1. The basic rule: "but for" on the balance of probabilities
1. Ask: but for the defendant's breach, would the damage still have occurred? If yes, the breach is eliminated as a cause; if no, it is at least one contributing cause (*Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428).
2. The claimant must prove this on the balance of probabilities (more likely than not). Certainty is not required (Weir).
3. The question is about the *breach*: compare what actually happened with what would have happened had the defendant acted carefully, not with what would have happened had the defendant not acted at all (Steele).
4. Where several possible agents may have caused the harm and the claimant cannot show the breach was probably the cause, the claim fails (*Wilsher v Essex Area Health Authority* [1988] 1 All ER 871 / [1988] AC 1074).

### 2. Hypothetical conduct in the counterfactual
1. Hypothetical acts of the claimant: if the claimant would not have used a safety precaution even if provided, the breach did not cause the harm (*McWilliams v Sir William Arrol & Co Ltd* [1962] 1 WLR 295, HL (Sc): steel erector would not have worn a safety belt).
2. Hypothetical acts of the defendant: the defendant cannot rely on the argument that it would have acted negligently in any event; under *Bolitho* the hypothetical conduct is tested against the *Bolam* standard (Steele).
3. Hypothetical acts of third parties: the question is simply one of probability, and *Bolam* does not apply (*Gouldsmith v Mid-Staffordshire General Hospitals Trust* [2007] EWCA Civ 397). On hypothetical negligent treatment by a third party, see *Wright v Cambridge Medical Group Ltd* [2011] EWCA Civ 669 (Lord Neuberger's analysis, not adopted by the other judges).

### 3. Material contribution to damage (cumulative causes)
1. Where the harm is produced by the accumulation of a tortious and a non-tortious source, and the but-for test cannot separate them, it is enough that the tortious source made a material (more than negligible) contribution to the damage (*Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw* [1956] AC 613: pneumoconiosis from "guilty" and "innocent" silica dust).
2. In *Bonnington* the defendant was liable for the whole of the (indivisible) disability. Later cases have restricted this for divisible or cumulative conditions, limiting the defendant to its share (*Holtby v Brigham & Cowan (Hull) Ltd* [2000] 3 All ER 423, asbestosis), and in *Rahman v Arearose* [2001] QB 351 a psychiatric condition was treated as divisible (criticised by Weir).
3. Lord Phillips in *Sienkiewicz* stated that where two agents operate cumulatively and simultaneously, *Bonnington* applies: for an indivisible disease the contributing tortfeasor is liable in full; for a divisible disease, only for its share.
4. Medical cases: *Bailey v Ministry of Defence* [2008] EWCA Civ 883 (negligent post-operative care and non-tortious pancreatitis both contributed to the weakness that led to aspiration and brain damage; liability on a material contribution basis). Waller LJ's summary: if, on the evidence, the injury would have happened anyway from the non-tortious cause, the claim fails; if "but for" is proved, it succeeds; if medical science cannot establish "but for" but the negligent contribution was more than negligible, the "but for" test is modified and the claimant succeeds.
5. *Williams v Bermuda Hospitals Board* [2016] UKPC 4: negligent delay of at least 140 minutes in treating appendicitis materially contributed to a single continuing septic process that injured heart and lungs; the hospital was liable. The Privy Council said *Bailey* did not depart from "but for" at all (the pancreatitis was simply the claimant's pre-existing vulnerability).

### 4. Material increase in risk: McGhee and the Fairchild exception
1. *McGhee v National Coal Board* [1973] 1 WLR 1: failure to provide washing facilities after work in brick kilns; the evidence could not show that washing would probably have prevented dermatitis. The House of Lords held the defender liable, treating a material increase in risk as equivalent to a material contribution to the injury.
2. *Wilsher* later said *McGhee* "added nothing to the law"; *Fairchild* reinstated it.
3. *Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services Ltd* [2002] UKHL 22; [2003] 1 AC 32: claimants exposed to asbestos negligently by several employers contracted mesothelioma; science could not say which exposure caused it. The House of Lords held each employer liable (jointly and severally in Fairchild itself) on proof that each materially increased the risk. It is an admitted exception to normal causation principles.
4. Conditions: Lord Bingham's six conditions (successive employers, each under a duty to protect against the known risk of mesothelioma, each in breach, claimant contracts mesothelioma, other causes discounted, but science cannot show which exposure caused it). Lord Hoffmann's five features (duty specifically aimed at the risk of the disease; duty intended to give a right to compensation; greater exposure means greater risk; science cannot prove whose asbestos caused it; claimant contracted the very disease). the materials highlights Lord Rodger's factors at [170]: (1) evidential gap; (2) material risk; (3) capable of causing injury; (4) eventuation of the kind of risk created by the wrongdoing; (5) same agency, or operating in substantially the same way.
5. Limits stressed in the materials: the "single agent" requirement and the scientific impossibility of proof; arguably confined to mesothelioma (but see *Sienkiewicz*, *IEG v Zurich*, *Heneghan*). Steele: *Fairchild* does not apply to a *Wilsher*-type case of different risks; you cannot sue just for being put at risk (*Rothwell* line; Weir cites *Rothwell v Chemical and Insulating Co* [2006] EWCA Civ 27).
6. *Barker v Corus plc* [2006] UKHL 20; [2006] 2 AC 572:
   - Extension: *Fairchild* applies even where one possible source of exposure was non-tortious (here a period of self-employment). Self-exposure that was itself negligent is dealt with by contributory negligence.
   - Restriction: liability under the exception is not joint and several but proportionate (several), according to each defendant's share of the exposure (duration, intensity). Lord Hoffmann and Lord Scott reasoned that the damage is the creation of the risk, which is divisible; Baroness Hale relied on fairness; Lord Rodger dissented.
7. Compensation Act 2006, s 3 reverses *Barker*'s apportionment rule for mesothelioma only: a "responsible person" who negligently or in breach of statutory duty exposed the victim to asbestos, where the victim contracted mesothelioma and science cannot determine which exposure caused it, is liable for the whole damage, jointly and severally (s 3(2)). Contribution claims and contributory negligence are preserved (s 3(3)); contributions are by default by relative length of exposure (s 3(4)); s 3(7) regulations allow recovery of unrecoverable contributions via the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (Steele).
8. *Sienkiewicz v Greif (UK) Ltd* [2011] UKSC 10; [2011] 2 AC 229 (with *Willmore v Knowsley MBC*): one negligent employer plus background environmental asbestos. Section 3 does not change *how* causation is proved (it only makes liability joint and several), but *Fairchild* applies to a single tortious exposure; the test is whether the defendant materially increased the risk, satisfied here with a contribution to risk estimated at 18 per cent. The "doubling of risk" test was rejected for *Fairchild* cases; statistical estimates of contribution to risk do not remove the "rock of uncertainty".
9. *Durham v BAI (Run Off) Ltd* (the "Trigger" litigation) [2012]: for insurance purposes, the employer's liability is triggered from first negligent exposure, though the actionable injury is the mesothelioma.
10. *International Energy Group Ltd v Zurich Insurance plc* [2015] UKSC 33: Barker remains good law at common law outside s 3 (Lord Mance: Trigger accepted causation in a "weak or broad" sense but did not undermine proportionate liability). The insurer on risk for part of the period must indemnify in full, then seek contribution from other insurers (and the employer as "self-insurer" for uninsured periods).
11. *Heneghan v Manchester Dry Docks Ltd* [2016] EWCA Civ 86: the exception extended to asbestos-induced lung cancer; since s 3 does not apply outside mesothelioma, damages were proportionate to contribution to risk.

### 5. Indeterminate causes
the materials lists *Summers v Tice* (1948) and *Fitzgerald v Lane* [1987] under "indeterminate causes" without further detail.

### 6. Multiple sufficient (successive) causes
1. *Baker v Willoughby* [1970] AC 467: leg injured by the defendant's negligent driving; later the claimant was shot in the same leg by robbers and it was amputated. The first defendant remained liable for the continuing effects of the original injury (lost amenity and earnings) despite the later tort. Lord Pearson: the original accident caused a "devaluation" of the claimant, for which the original tortfeasor remains responsible.
2. *Jobling v Associated Dairies* [1982] AC 794: back injury at work, followed before trial by an unrelated disease that would have caused total incapacity anyway. The defendant was liable only until the disease supervened, because damages take account of the "vicissitudes of life". The House doubted the reasoning in *Baker*.
3. The distinction (later tort vs natural illness) is hard to justify (Steele: "there really is no good answer"; Weir: odd to hold that being the victim of a tort is not a vicissitude of life). *Gray v Thames Trains* [2009] UKHL 33 considered the conflict.
4. Performance Cars v Abraham [1962] 1 QB 33 is listed on the reading list under successive causes (no further detail in the materials).

### 7. Loss of chance
1. Personal injury: the claimant must prove on the balance of probabilities that the breach caused the injury; a lost chance of a better outcome is not itself recoverable damage.
2. *Hotson v East Berkshire AHA* [1987] AC 750: misdiagnosed hip injury; 75 per cent likely that avascular necrosis would have developed anyway. Trial judge awarded 25 per cent of damages; House of Lords reversed. The fall was, on the balance of probabilities, the sole cause.
3. *Gregg v Scott* [2005] UKHL 2; [2005] 2 AC 176: negligent failure to refer a lump that was cancerous delayed treatment by about nine months; chance of "cure" (10-year survival) fell from 42 per cent to 25 per cent. By 3:2 the House rejected the claim: the claimant could not show that the negligence probably caused the adverse outcome, and loss of chance could not ground a medical negligence claim.
4. Contrast economic loss: loss of a chance is recoverable where the outcome depended on the hypothetical acts of a third party (*Chaplin v Hicks* [1911] 2 KB 786 (contract); *Kitchen v Royal Air Force Association* [1958] 1 WLR 563; *Allied Maples v Simmons & Simmons* [1995] 4 All ER 907, affirmed in *Perry v Raleys Solicitors* [2019] UKSC 5). What the claimant himself would have done must be proved on the balance of probabilities.

## Key authorities
- *Barnett v Chelsea and Kensington HMC* [1969] 1 QB 428: casualty doctor refused to examine men who had drunk arsenic-laced tea; negligent, but the deceased would have died even with proper treatment. Classic "but for" failure.
- *McWilliams v Sir William Arrol* [1962] 1 WLR 295: no belt provided, but the deceased would not have worn one; no causation.
- *Wilsher v Essex AHA* [1988] AC 1074: excess oxygen was one of several possible causes of a premature baby's blindness; claimant failed to prove causation; *McGhee* confined.
- *Bonnington Castings v Wardlaw* [1956] AC 613: material contribution to cumulative dust disease suffices; liable in full.
- *McGhee v NCB* [1973] 1 WLR 1: material increase in risk treated as material contribution.
- *Fairchild v Glenhaven* [2002] UKHL 22: mesothelioma; liability for materially increasing risk where science cannot identify the cause.
- *Barker v Corus* [2006] UKHL 20: exception applies despite non-tortious exposure; liability proportionate (reversed for mesothelioma by statute).
- Compensation Act 2006, s 3: mesothelioma defendants liable jointly and severally for the whole damage.
- *Sienkiewicz v Greif* [2011] UKSC 10: single tortious exposure plus environmental exposure; material increase in risk (18 per cent) enough; doubling-of-risk test rejected in this context.
- *Durham v BAI* (Trigger) [2012]: employer's responsibility begins at first negligent exposure (insurance context).
- *IEG v Zurich* [2015] UKSC 33: *Barker* still the common law outside s 3.
- *Heneghan v Manchester Dry Docks* [2016] EWCA Civ 86: *Fairchild* extended to lung cancer; proportionate damages.
- *Bailey v MoD* [2008] EWCA Civ 883; *Williams v Bermuda Hospitals Board* [2016] UKPC 4: material contribution in clinical negligence.
- *Baker v Willoughby* [1970] AC 467 and *Jobling v Associated Dairies* [1982] AC 794: successive causes; tort vs natural supervening event.
- *Hotson v East Berkshire AHA* [1987] AC 750 and *Gregg v Scott* [2005] UKHL 2: no recovery for lost chance in personal injury.
- *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 41 (discussed by Weir): failure to warn of a small (under 2 per cent) risk of spinal surgery; claimant would have delayed and had the operation later; liability imposed, the majority treating this as a departure from conventional causation justified by the duty to warn. The reading list includes Robson and Swift, "Risk disclosure, causation and the role of Chester" (2019) 35(4) PN 207.

## Debate and critique
- What counts as "causation"? Stapleton ("Cause-in-fact and the scope of liability for consequences" (2003) 119 LQR 388) argues for two steps only: factual historical involvement, then a frankly normative question about the scope of liability for consequences. Hart and Honoré instead treat ordinary-language "causal" ideas (normal vs abnormal conditions) as part of legal causation. Stapleton calls their three-step approach "inconvenient and obfuscatory".
- Limits of "but for": Stapleton ("Unnecessary Causes" (2013) 129 LQR 39) argues "but for" fails for cumulative, non-binary cases (two fires each sufficient to burn a building) and that contribution to the causal process should itself count as a factual cause. The question whether the breach "made a difference" is then moved to damages (the "no better off" principle), where the law must choose a benchmark (absent the individual defendant's contribution, or absent all tortious contributions). Stapleton and Steel ("Causes and Contributions" (2016) 132 LQR 363) say *Williams* can be reached only with a causal concept broader than "but for", contrary to the Privy Council's own explanation.
- Is *Fairchild* principled? Lord Bingham justified it by weighing the injustice to a duty-breaking employer against the far greater injustice of denying a remedy where science, not the claimant, is at fault; otherwise employers could escape by hiring workers already exposed elsewhere. Lord Hoffmann: a strict rule would "empty the duty of content". Critics (Court of Appeal in *Fairchild*; Lord Brown in *Sienkiewicz*: "the law tampers with the 'but for' test of causation at its peril") warn of incoherence and speculative claims; Lord Phillips in *Gregg* warned against piecemeal special tests destroying the coherence of the common law. Further reading: Morgan, "Lost causes in the House of Lords: Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services" (2003) MLR; Goldberg (ed), *Perspectives on Causation* (2011).
- Apportionment and insolvency: Lord Rodger (dissenting in *Barker*) argued the push for proportionate liability was driven by insolvent defendants and insurers, and that the courts were shifting the risk of insolvency onto innocent claimants. Parliament agreed for mesothelioma (s 3), leaving the common law (proportionate) and statute (joint and several) out of line, a split confirmed in *IEG v Zurich*. Weir criticises the courts' readiness (*Holtby*, *Rahman*) to treat truly indivisible harms as divisible, and argues the risk of untraceable co-tortfeasors should fall on tortfeasors, who are insured and can seek contribution.
- Scope of the exception: is it confined to mesothelioma and "single agents"? *Heneghan* extended it to lung cancer, which has many causes; Lord Neuberger and Lord Reed in *IEG* said it applies to any disease with the unusual features of mesothelioma. Lord Phillips suggested the single-agent criterion is now justified by the cumulative nature of fibre exposure.
- Loss of chance: Lord Nicholls (dissenting in *Gregg*) called it "irrational and indefensible" that a 45 per cent prospect gives nothing while 55 per cent gives full damages, and that the doctor's duty would be hollow. Lord Hoffmann relied on a deterministic view of the world (everything has a cause; lack of knowledge is handled by the burden of proof); Baroness Hale feared "heads you lose everything, tails I win something" and the destabilisation of ordinary personal injury claims; Lord Phillips stressed this was a diminished rather than lost chance (the claimant was still alive). Helen Reece ("Losses of Chances in the Law" (1996) 59 MLR 188) distinguishes deterministic cases (like *Hotson*, where the answer was knowable at the time) from quasi-indeterministic ones, where loss-of-chance reasoning is appropriate; Steele says Lord Hoffmann did not represent her argument accurately. Weir defends *Hotson*: losing a chance of keeping a leg is not the same kind of thing as losing a chance of financial gain.
- Policy of *Baker* vs *Jobling*: no convincing reconciliation; academic and judicial doubts on *Baker*.
- The "hook" argument (Stapleton 2003): use an actionable injury (e.g. spread of a tumour) as a hook to hang a lost-chance claim on; rejected by Lord Hoffmann in *Gregg* as resembling the "control mechanisms" that disfigure psychiatric harm.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the breach precisely (what should the defendant have done?). Causation is tested against that careful alternative.
2. Apply "but for" on the balance of probabilities. Ask what the claimant, the defendant and third parties would have done in the hypothetical (*McWilliams*, *Bolitho*, *Gouldsmith*).
3. If more than one possible cause:
   - Are the causes cumulative, operating together (same process, dust, weakness, sepsis)? Use material contribution to damage (*Bonnington*, *Bailey*, *Williams*). Then: divisible harm means liability for the share (*Holtby*); indivisible harm means liability in full.
   - Are the causes distinct, alternative agents (as in *Wilsher*)? The claimant must prove "but for"; the *Fairchild* exception is unavailable.
   - Is it a single-agent, scientifically unprovable case (mesothelioma, or possibly analogous diseases)? Apply *Fairchild*/*Barker*/*Sienkiewicz*: did each defendant materially increase the risk? Non-tortious exposures do not bar the claim (*Barker*, *Sienkiewicz*).
4. Damages in a *Fairchild* case: mesothelioma means s 3 Compensation Act 2006 (whole damage, joint and several, contribution between defendants, possible contributory negligence for negligent self-exposure). Other diseases mean proportionate liability (*Barker*, *IEG*, *Heneghan*).
5. Successive events: was the later event a tort (*Baker*: original tortfeasor stays liable for continuing effects) or a natural illness or vicissitude (*Jobling*: liability ends when it supervenes)?
6. Medical misdiagnosis or delay: is the claim framed as loss of a less-than-50 per cent chance? It fails (*Hotson*, *Gregg*). Look for an alternative: did the delay itself cause provable extra injury, pain or treatment (Lord Hope in *Gregg*), or did it materially contribute to a single process (*Williams*)?
7. Economic loss turning on a third party's hypothetical actions: loss-of-chance damages may be available (*Allied Maples*).
8. Traps: confusing "D's conduct" with "D's breach"; applying *Fairchild* to multi-agent facts; forgetting that s 3 is mesothelioma-only; treating a statistic of under 50 per cent as automatically fatal where material contribution is the right test; forgetting to move on to legal causation and remoteness.
