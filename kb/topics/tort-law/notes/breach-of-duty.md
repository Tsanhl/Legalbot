# Breach of Duty (Standard of Care)

## Overview
Once a duty of care is established, the claimant must show the defendant breached it, i.e. fell below the standard of care the law requires. Negligence is often summarised as duty + breach + causation, minus defences; if breach is not shown, the claim fails. Breach involves two questions: (1) what standard did the law require in the circumstances (a question of law), and (2) did the defendant's conduct fall below it (a question of fact). In England & Wales the benchmark is the objective "reasonable person", adjusted in recognised ways (children, professionals, sport, emergencies), and the court weighs factors such as probability of harm, seriousness, cost of precautions and social value of the activity. Breach is where most negligence litigation is actually fought (Weir), and it raises deep questions about what "fault" means in tort.

## The law

### 1. The two-stage inquiry
1. **Setting the standard (law):** how ought a reasonable person in the defendant's position to have behaved? Determined by common law and statute.
2. **Applying the standard (fact):** did what the defendant actually did fall short? Weir stresses that decisions on breach are really findings of fact, not precedents: they illustrate the rule that the care required is that called for in all the circumstances. Appellate courts are reluctant to interfere with trial findings (*Barber v Somerset CC* [2004] UKHL 13, as discussed by Weir).

### 2. The reasonable person and the objective standard
- The reasonable person has been described as "the man on the Clapham omnibus" (attributed to $1), "a traveller on the London underground" (Lord Steyn, *McFarlane v Tayside Health Board* [1999]), and "the anthropomorphic conception of justice" (Lord Radcliffe, *Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC* [1956]).
- Lord Macmillan in *Glasgow Corporation v Muir* [1943] AC 448: the test is impersonal; it "eliminates the personal equation and is independent of the idiosyncrasies of the particular person" whose conduct is in question; the reasonable person is free from both over-apprehension and over-confidence.
- Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks (1856) (Baron Alderson, cited by Weir): negligence is omitting to do what a reasonable person would do, or doing what a prudent and reasonable person would not do.
- The standard is independent of the defendant's own capacities:
  - *Nettleship v Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691 (CA): a learner driver owes the same standard as a skilled, experienced and careful driver; "her incompetent best is not good enough" (Lord Denning, paraphrased). Lord Denning's reasons: consistency with the criminal law; practicality (one standard for all passengers and pedestrians); and compulsory insurance (the risk should fall on the insured). Salmon LJ dissented on the standard owed to the instructor. Damages were reduced for the instructor's contributory negligence. Compare the Australian *Imbree v McNeilly* [2008] HCA 40, which abandoned a variable standard for supervising passengers; Kirby J saw compulsory insurance as decisive.
  - *Roberts v Ramsbottom* [1980] 1 All ER 7: a driver who had a stroke but continued to drive erratically was liable.
  - *Mansfield v Weetabix Ltd* [1998] 1 WLR 1263: a driver who suffered an unforeseen medical condition that he did not know of was not liable (the "blackout" type case).
  - *Dunnage v Randall* [2015] EWCA Civ 673: a visitor suffering from schizophrenia who set fire to himself and injured the claimant was negligent; the objective standard is not adjusted for physical or mental impairment; only if the condition entirely eliminates fault, so that the defendant did nothing himself (as with *Mansfield*), is there no breach. The only recognised adjustment is for children. (J Goudkamp and M Ihouma, 'A Tour of the Tort of Negligence' (2016) 32 PN 137, cited in the textbook, argue *Mansfield* actually varied the standard.)
  - The standard does not require perfection: *Birch v Paulson* [2012].
- It is the defendant's conduct in the particular incident that counts, not their general record (Weir).

### 3. Modifications of the objective test
**Children.** The standard is that of an ordinary child of the same age. Salmon LJ in *Gough v Thorne* [1966]: "any ordinary child of 13½... not a paragon of prudence; nor... a scatter-brained child". *Mullin v Richards* [1998] 1 WLR 1304 (CA): two 15-year-olds fencing with plastic rulers; the question was what an ordinarily prudent and reasonable 15-year-old schoolgirl would have foreseen; no liability.

**Ordinary parents and domestic settings** (textbook): *Perry v Harris* [2008] EWCA Civ 907 (bouncy castle; standard of the reasonable parent, not knowledge of detailed official guidance); *Ellis v Kelly* [2018] EWHC 2031 (QB) (mother not partly responsible for letting an 8-year-old go to the park with older cousins).

**Special skill and the Bolam test.** A person who exercises or professes a special skill is judged by the standard of the ordinary skilled member of that profession or calling (Winfield and Jolowicz). *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582 (McNair J): the test is "the standard of the ordinary skilled man exercising and professing to have that special skill"; a professional is not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of professional opinion, even if others disagree. This applies to all professionals exercising a special skill.

Modifications of Bolam:
1. **Rank/post, not experience:** *Wilsher v Essex Area Health Authority* [1987] QB 730 (CA): the standard is set by the post held in the team (e.g. a specialist neonatal unit), not by the individual's inexperience. *FB v Rana* [2017] EWCA Civ 334: some tasks (history taking) must be done equally competently whatever the rank. *Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50: an A&E receptionist must take reasonable care not to give misleading information about waiting times; standard of an averagely competent and well-informed receptionist in an emergency department.
2. **Professional practice must be logical and reasonable:** *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232 (HL): the court is not bound to accept expert evidence that a practice is proper; the body of opinion must be "responsible, reasonable and respectable" and capable of withstanding logical analysis, having weighed risks and benefits. But Lord Browne-Wilkinson said it would very seldom be right to reject a competent expert's genuine view. Earlier examples: *Hucks v Cole* (1968, [1993] 4 Med LR 393); *Edward Wong Finance v Johnson Stokes & Master* [1984] AC 296 (universal Hong Kong conveyancing practice still negligent).
3. **Information and consent:** *Sidaway v Bethlem Royal Hospital* [1985] AC 871 had applied Bolam to risk disclosure. *Chester v Afshar* [2004] UKHL 1 emphasised patient autonomy. *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11: Bolam no longer governs disclosure. A doctor must take reasonable care to ensure the patient is aware of any **material risks** of recommended treatment and of reasonable alternatives. A risk is material if a reasonable person in the patient's position would be likely to attach significance to it, or the doctor knows or should know that this particular patient would. Exceptions: therapeutic exception (disclosure seriously detrimental to health), which must not be abused, and necessity. Materiality cannot be reduced to percentages; the duty involves dialogue, not bombarding the patient with information.

**Sport and play.** *Wooldridge v Sumner* [1963] 2 QB 43: a participant concentrating on winning who makes an error of judgment in the agony of the moment is not negligent towards a spectator unless showing reckless disregard for safety. *Condon v Basi* [1985] 1 WLR: footballer liable for a reckless foul tackle; standard is reasonableness in the special circumstances. *Blake v Galloway* [2004] EWCA Civ 814: in consensual horseplay, breach requires recklessness or a very high degree of carelessness.

**Emergencies.** Conduct in an emergency not of the defendant's making is judged in that light (Weir; *Ng Chun Pui v Lee Chuen Tat* [1988] RTR 298, coach driver reacting to a car cutting in).

### 4. Factors in setting the standard (the "balancing" exercise)
1. **Probability of harm.** *Bolton v Stone* [1951] AC 850: a cricket ball hit out of the ground about six times in 28 years struck the claimant; the risk was so small a reasonable person could disregard it; no breach. *Roe v Minister of Health* [1954] 2 QB 66: contamination through invisible cracks in ampoules was not foreseeable given knowledge at the time, so no negligence (but it would be negligent once the risk became known).
2. **Seriousness of the potential injury.** *Paris v Stepney Borough Council* [1951] AC 367: employer should have given goggles to a one-eyed worker because the consequences of an eye injury to him were so grave.
3. **Cost and practicability of precautions.** *Latimer v AEC Ltd* [1953] AC 643: flooded factory floor; closing the factory was out of proportion to the risk; no breach.
4. **Social value/utility of the activity.** *Watt v Hertfordshire County Council* [1954] 1 WLR 835 (social value); *Scout Association v Barnes* [2010] and *Marshall v Osmond* [1983]. Asquith LJ in *Daborn v Bath Tramways* [1946] (quoted in *Humphrey v Aegis Defence Services* [2016] EWCA Civ 11): a sufficiently important purpose justifies assuming abnormal risk. *Tomlinson v Congleton BC* [2003] UKHL 47 (Weir): the social cost of preventing many people from harmless activities to protect a few who ignore prohibitions.
5. **Combining the factors.** *The Wagon Mound (No 2)* [1967] 1 AC 617 (PC): even a small risk must be guarded against unless there is a valid reason for ignoring it; Lord Reid explained *Bolton v Stone* as permitting neglect of a small risk only where eliminating it would involve considerable difficulty or expense and the activity was lawful and worthwhile. Discharging oil into Sydney Harbour had no justification, so it was negligent even though fire was unlikely. The textbook compares the "Learned Hand" formula (B < PL, *US v Carroll Towing* (1947)) but says English courts treat breach as evaluative, not mathematical.
6. **Conduct of others.** Weir: a reasonable person anticipates common forms of folly (Lord Uthwatt), and occupiers must expect children to be less careful (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957). *Orchard v Lee* [2009] EWCA Civ 295 (no further detail in the sources used).

### 5. Statutory interventions
- **Compensation Act 2006 s 1:** in a claim in negligence or breach of statutory duty, when deciding whether the defendant should have taken particular steps, the court "may" consider whether requiring them might prevent a desirable activity being undertaken (at all, to an extent or in a way) or discourage people from undertaking functions connected with it. The Explanatory Notes say it does not alter the standard of care; commentary agrees it reflects the existing law. *Sutton v Syston RFC* [2011] EWCA Civ 1182: parties agreed it did not change the common law.
- **Social Action, Responsibility and Heroism Act 2015 (SARAH):** applies when the court is determining the steps a person was required to take to meet a standard of care (s 1). The court "must have regard to" whether the person was acting for the benefit of society (s 2), whether they demonstrated a predominantly responsible approach towards others' safety (s 3), and whether they were acting heroically by intervening in an emergency (s 4). Commentary says it makes no change to substantive law but makes courts address these issues systematically.

### 6. Proof of breach and res ipsa loquitur
- The burden is on the claimant on the balance of probabilities.
- **Res ipsa loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself"): *Scott v London and St Katherine Docks* (1865) (bags of sugar falling from a crane). Where the thing was under the management of the defendant and the accident is one that would not ordinarily happen if proper care were used, that is reasonable evidence of negligence in the absence of explanation. the conditions: (i) an accident of a kind that does not normally happen without negligence; (ii) the cause was under the defendant's control; (iii) no explanation of the actual cause (one text repeats the control condition; the textbook frames the third condition as absence of explanation).
- It does not reverse the burden of proof: *Ng Chun Pui v Lee Chuen Tat* [1988] RTR 298 (PC): it is a description of evidence from which negligence may be inferred; if the defendant gives a plausible explanation consistent with due care, the inference may fall away; the burden stays on the claimant throughout.

## Debate and critique
- **Is negligence really "fault"?** The textbook notes that because the standard is objective and not adjusted to the defendant's abilities, negligence contains an element of strict liability: a learner or a mentally ill defendant may be "negligent" without being morally blameworthy. Lord Denning in *Nettleship* openly said the law was moving from "no liability without fault" to "on whom should the risk fall?". John Gardner ("The Negligence Standard: Political Not Metaphysical" (2017) 80 MLR 1) argues the choice between negligence-based and strict responsibility is political (about fair and efficient allocation of responsibility), not a reflection of basic moral truths.
- **Insurance.** Kirby J in *Imbree v McNeilly* saw tort as a "hybrid" shaped by compulsory insurance. In *Dunnage*, the textbook suggests the presence of an insurer influenced the court's insistence on the objective standard; without insurance the loss might have fallen on the defendant's widow.
- **Deference to professions (Bolam).** J L Montrose ((1958) 21 MLR 259) warned that Bolam risks equating due care with what is usually done: "experts may blind themselves by expertise". Brazier and Miola ("Bye-Bye Bolam: A Medical Litigation Revolution?" (2000) 8 Med L Rev 85) argued medical cases were treated more deferentially than other professions and that ethical issues (consent, incapacity, mature minors) had been inappropriately "Bolamised". Sheldon (in Sheldon and Thomson (eds), *Feminist Perspectives on Health Care Law* (1998)) rethinks the "responsible body of medical men". Weir notes courts reassure public bodies that Bolam limits liability, but doubts such predictions.
- **Montgomery.** Farrell and Brazier ((2016) 42 J Med Ethics 85) welcomed a patient-centred shift but said it would change practice little and does not mean unfettered autonomy. J and E Montgomery ((2016) 42 J Med Ethics 89) argued the decision is inconsistent with professional guidance, undermines shared decision-making by separating advice from treatment, overlooks caesarean risks and may infantilise patients. The textbook suggests Montgomery recasts patients as rights-bearing citizens and may open the whole Bolam era to reconsideration.
- **Degrees of negligence.** Weir argues English law should recognise gross negligence, since courts already grade fault when apportioning under the 1945 and 1978 Acts; this would be useful in claims against public bodies and in sport.
- **Compensation culture legislation.** The 2006 Act s 1 was intended to "send a message" without changing the law; SARAH 2015 has been criticised as unnecessary and possibly harmful. R Mulheron, "Legislating Dangerously: Bad Samaritans, Good Society, and the Heroism Act 2015" (2017) 80 MLR 88. The textbook worries that s 3 may invite defendants to lead evidence of general responsibility, increasing uncertainty.
- **Economic analysis.** The Learned Hand formula is sometimes read as an economic theory of negligence; the textbook argues English courts are evaluative and openly assess the worth of defendants' activities rather than calculating.
- **Further reading:** Weir, *An Introduction to Tort Law* ch 3; Cane, *Atiyah's Accidents, Compensation and the Law* ch 2.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the precise act or omission alleged to be careless.** Weir: choose the aspect of conduct that actually caused the harm (speeding versus driving without a licence).
2. **Set the standard.** Who is the defendant and what were they doing?
   - Ordinary activity: reasonable person.
   - Learner or inexperienced: no allowance (*Nettleship*; *Wilsher* for post-holders).
   - Medical condition: liable unless the condition removed all control and was unknown (*Mansfield*, *Dunnage*, *Roberts*).
   - Child: ordinary child of that age (*Mullin*).
   - Professional: Bolam, subject to Bolitho; for risk disclosure, Montgomery.
   - Sport/play or emergency: adjusted for the circumstances; recklessness threshold in sport and horseplay.
3. **Weigh the factors:** likelihood (*Bolton*), knowledge at the time (*Roe*), seriousness including the claimant's known vulnerability (*Paris*), cost and practicality (*Latimer*), social value (*Watt*; Compensation Act s 1; SARAH ss 2-4), and whether elimination was easy and costless (*Wagon Mound (No 2)*).
4. **Statutes:** mention s 1 of the 2006 Act where a desirable activity is involved; SARAH where the defendant was volunteering, acting responsibly, or intervening heroically. Note they probably do not change the result.
5. **Apply to the facts:** did the defendant fall short? Use the facts given (warnings, frequency of previous incidents, signage, guidance followed).
6. **Proof:** if the claimant cannot explain how the accident happened, consider res ipsa loquitur; check control and whether the defendant has a plausible explanation.
7. **Move on to causation:** breach that made no difference fails at the causation stage (Weir's 20 mph example).

**Typical traps:** treating Bolam as decisive without considering Bolitho; applying Bolam to consent after Montgomery; lowering the standard for a learner or a nervous novice; forgetting that the objective standard is assessed with the knowledge available at the time (*Roe*); saying res ipsa reverses the burden of proof. **Facts that change the outcome:** history of previous similar incidents; the claimant's known vulnerability; availability of cheap precautions such as warnings; whether the defendant's role involved claiming a skill; emergency or sporting context.
