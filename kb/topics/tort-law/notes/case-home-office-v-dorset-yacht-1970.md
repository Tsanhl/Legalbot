# Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co Ltd [1970] AC 1004 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. Also cited as [1970] UKHL 2 and [1970] 2 All ER 294. Check pinpoints against the
> report.

## Overview
House of Lords, 6 May 1970 (one source gives 7 May). Lord Reid, Lord Morris, Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Pearson and Lord
Diplock sat; Viscount Dilhorne dissented (4-1 against the Home Office). The decision was on a preliminary question of
whether a duty of care could arise, so no final findings on breach were made at this stage.

## Facts
On 21 September 1962 a group of borstal trainees was working on Brownsea Island in Poole Harbour under three Home
Office officers. Seven escaped at night when the officers had gone to bed, boarded a yacht and in doing so collided with
and damaged the claimants' yacht. The claimants sued the Home Office in negligence. The Home Office admitted that it was
liable vicariously if the officers were. Earlier courts held that a duty of care could exist, and the Home Office
appealed.

## Held
- The appeal was **dismissed**: the claim could proceed, because the borstal officers could owe the claimants a duty.
- Officers have a duty to take reasonable care to prevent those under their control from causing damage where there is
  a manifest risk of that damage if they are careless. Public policy did not require immunity for them.
- The trainees' own deliberate acts did not prevent liability, because their escape and damage were a foreseeable
  result of the alleged negligence of the officers.
- Lord Reid's speech treated Lord Atkin's neighbour principle in *Donoghue v Stevenson* as a statement of principle
  which should be applied unless there is a good reason to exclude it.

## Unconfirmed or omitted
- The precise wording of the speeches of Lord Diplock, Lord Morris and Lord Pearson is not given because it was not
  confirmed by two sources.
- Whether the officers were in fact negligent was left for trial.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify a defendant with control over a third party who then injures or damages the claimant's property (for
   example a custodian or public authority).
2. Establish the special relationship of control and a manifest, foreseeable risk to the claimant, who must be a
   person within the area of risk, not the public generally.
3. Treat the third party's deliberate act as foreseeable, not as a bar to causation.
4. Consider policy arguments (public authority) and conclude whether a duty arises, leaving breach to be dealt with
   separately.
