# Privacy - Misuse of Private Information

## Overview
English law (England and Wales) has **no general tort of invasion of privacy** (*Wainwright v Home Office* [2004] 2 AC 406). It does have a tort of **misuse of private information** (MPI), developed after the Human Rights Act 1998 out of the equitable action for breach of confidence and shaped by Articles 8 (private life) and 10 (expression) ECHR. The claimant must show (1) a **reasonable expectation of privacy** in the information, and if so (2) that, on balance, their Article 8 right is not outweighed by the defendant's Article 10 right (*ZXC v Bloomberg LP* [2022] UKSC 5 at [47]). The tort matters because it now protects celebrities, children and suspects under police investigation against press disclosure. Unlike defamation, where under *Bonnard v Perryman* [1891] 2 Ch 269 an interim injunction is generally refused if the defendant intends to plead truth, MPI claims can obtain **interim injunctions** before publication, often the only remedy of real value. They are not readily granted, however. Under s 12(3) HRA the applicant must generally show it is more likely than not to succeed at trial (*Cream Holdings Ltd v Banerjee* [2004] UKHL 44), a higher threshold than *American Cyanamid*.

## The law

### A. Background: why a new tort was needed
1. The older torts gave only patchy protection. Battery needs "hostile" contact. Private nuisance depends on an interest in land, so it is not personal. Harassment needs an "oppressive and unacceptable" course of conduct. Steele (textbook) cites *Kaye v Robertson* [1991] FSR 62 as the classic gap: an actor photographed in hospital while in a coma could only get a remedy through a creative use of malicious falsehood, when the real wrong was the intrusion and publication.
2. **Breach of confidence** (equity, not tort) was what remained. Its elements (*Coco v A N Clark (Engineers) Ltd* [1969] RPC 41):
   - the information has the necessary quality of confidence;
   - it was imparted in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence;
   - there was unauthorised use of it (to the claimant's detriment).
   Early examples:
   - *Prince Albert v Strange* (1849) 1 Mac & G 25: private etchings sent to a printer were published.
   - *Pollard v Photographic Co* (1889) 40 Ch D 345: a photographer used the claimant's portrait for Christmas cards, breaching an implied term that the photos were for her own use only.
   - *Argyll v Argyll* [1967] Ch 302: the Duke was restrained from giving the press confidences from the marriage.
   Its limit was that it depended on a confidential relationship, not on how personal the information was.
3. **Human Rights Act 1998.** Courts are public authorities (s 6) and must act compatibly with Convention rights. Article 8 protects respect for private and family life, home and correspondence, subject to necessary and proportionate interferences (Art 8(2)). Formally, the HRA creates no new cause of action between private parties. Baroness Hale in *Campbell* said that where a cause of action exists, the court must act compatibly with both parties' rights ([132]). Lord Hoffmann accepted that the Convention is aimed at the state, but saw "no logical ground" for giving less protection against private persons ([49]-[50]). They differed on the mechanism of horizontal effect, not on whether Article 8 values govern disputes between private parties (textbook). In practice, Convention values were channelled into the existing action ("horizontal effect"). Steele explains that s 12 HRA, which requires regard to Article 10 in any case where relief might affect expression, was also used in *Douglas v Hello!* [2001] QB 967 to give horizontal effect to the rights that qualify Article 10, including Article 8.
4. **"Shoehorning" and separation.** Privacy was first squeezed into breach of confidence:
   - *Campbell v MGN* [2004] 2 AC 457;
   - *HRH Prince of Wales v Associated Newspapers* [2006] EWCA Civ 1776 (the Prince had a reasonable expectation of privacy in his diaries);
   - *Douglas v Hello! (No 3)* [2006] QB 125.
   Then:
   - ***Vidal-Hall v Google Inc* [2015] EWCA Civ 311**: the claimants complained about Google's use of their browsing data (cookies). Google was domiciled in the US, so the claim could be served out of the jurisdiction only if it was a tort. The Court of Appeal held that confidence and privacy protect different interests and are now "two separate and distinct causes of action", so MPI is a tort ([21]).
   - ***PJS v News Group Newspapers* [2016] UKSC 26**: MPI offers "greater protection" than breach of confidence ([25]). Privacy can survive partial disclosure, whereas confidentiality tends to be lost once information is published (textbook).

### B. Stage 1: reasonable expectation of privacy
Commentary breaks this into three questions.

**(i) Is the information private?** These categories have been recognised (the list is not closed):
- **Family and children.**
  - *Murray v Express Newspapers (Big Pictures)* [2008] EWCA Civ 446: a claim for a toddler photographed in the street with famous parents was not struck out.
  - *Weller v Associated Newspapers* [2015] EWCA Civ 1176: Weller's children photographed shopping and in a café won damages and an injunction. Lord Dyson MR said the family element distinguished it from Naomi Campbell popping out for milk or Sir Elton John in a baseball cap outside his gate ([61]).
  - *PJS*: the interests of the children of the person in a kiss-and-tell story count (Baroness Hale, textbook).
  - But the *bare fact* of a family relationship is not normally private: *Hutcheson v News Group Newspapers* [2011] EWCA Civ 808 (information about a chef's father-in-law and business associate was publishable where family and business matters were intertwined).
- **Home and personal relationships.**
  - *McKennitt v Ash* [2006] EWCA Civ 1714: a friend's book about touring with a folk singer disclosed personal information shared in friendship (including health); there was a reasonable expectation of privacy.
  - *Richard v BBC* [2018] EWHC 1837 (Ch): filmed and broadcast coverage of a police search of Sir Cliff Richard's home.
- **Health**: *Campbell* (Narcotics Anonymous treatment); *McKennitt*.
- **Sex life**: *Mosley v News Group Newspapers* [2008] EWHC 1777 (QB) (a sado-masochistic party); confirmed in *PJS v NGN* [2016] AC 1081.
- **Finances**: *Immerman v Tchenguiz* [2010] EWCA Civ 908.
- **Personal correspondence**: *HRH Duchess of Sussex v Associated Newspapers* [2021] EWCA Civ 1810 (a private letter to her father).
- **Being under police investigation, up to the point of charge**:
  - *Richard v BBC*.
  - Confirmed by ***Bloomberg LP v ZXC* [2022] UKSC 5**, which stressed the harm caused by publicising that someone is under criminal investigation.
  - Steele notes *Khuja v Times Newspapers* [2017] UKSC, where an injunction was refused to stop someone being named in reports of a trial of others; the majority treated it as a narrow decision about reporting court proceedings, and *Richard* distinguished it.
  - *Sicri v Associated Newspapers* [2020] EWHC 3541 (QB): a man arrested after the Manchester Arena bombing and not charged succeeded.
- **Privacy in public places.** Under the old confidence approach there could be no privacy in public. That is no longer the law. In *Von Hannover v Germany* [2004] EMLR 21 (ECtHR) a princess was photographed in public, including dining with her fiancé. The court said protection of private life extends beyond the family circle to a social dimension, and even well-known people have a "legitimate expectation" of protection ([69]). See also *Campbell*, where she was photographed in the street.

**(ii) Do the facts give rise to a reasonable expectation of privacy?** Apply the **"Murray factors"**: *Murray* [2008] EWCA Civ 446 at [36]. This is a broad question taking account of all the circumstances, including:
- the attributes of the claimant;
- the nature of the activity;
- the place;
- the nature and purpose of the intrusion;
- absence of consent, and whether it was known or could be inferred;
- the effect on the claimant;
- the circumstances in which and purposes for which the information reached the publisher.

Points on applying the factors:
- **Consent** can be decisive. In *Duchess of Sussex*, sharing some material with the authors of a forthcoming book was not consent to publication in a national newspaper.
- **Photographs** are treated as more intrusive than words. Lord Hoffmann in *Campbell* at [72] said a picture may be more vivid than words. *Campbell* identified five pieces of information:
  1. she was a drug addict;
  2. she was receiving treatment;
  3. the treatment was at Narcotics Anonymous;
  4. details of the treatment;
  5. a photo of her leaving a meeting.
  Items 3-5 were treated as medical-type information carrying a particularly high expectation of privacy. Items 1-2 were publishable because she had publicly denied taking drugs (see "hypocrisy" below).
- In *Douglas v Hello! (No 3)*, a couple who had sold exclusive wedding photographs to OK! still got damages for distress caused by a rival magazine's unauthorised photos. Lord Hoffmann in the HL ([2007] UKHL 21, textbook) analysed OK!'s own claim as one in confidence over commercially valuable information.
- **Partial disclosure** does not end privacy: *PJS*, where the story had appeared abroad and online, but the injunction was maintained. See also *Sicri* (knowledge of a few neighbours).
- **Objective or subjective?** The test is objective: what would a person of ordinary sensibilities feel if placed in the claimant's position and faced with the same publicity (Lord Hope, *Campbell* at [99]). But it has a subjective element. Whether to disclose was "plainly something which an individual was entitled to decide for herself" (Lord Hoffmann at [53]), and the tort protects "informational autonomy" (Baroness Hale at [134]). An expectation that no reasonable person would have (oversensitivity) is not protected.

**(iii) Has the defendant misused (or threatened to misuse) the information?**
- "Misuse" is wider than publication. The focus is on the right to control the dissemination of information about one's private life (Sedley LJ, *Douglas v Hello!* [2001] QB 967, 1001).
- In *Gulati v MGN* [2015] EWHC 1482 (Ch) (phone hacking), a claimant recovered although none of his information was ever disclosed to anyone else.
- But misuse requires **positive conduct**, not a negligent omission. *Smith v TalkTalk* [2022] EWHC 1311 (QB): hackers exploited a weakness in the defendant's systems to access customer data, and the defendant's negligent storage was not misuse.

### C. Stage 2: balancing Article 8 against Article 10
1. Article 10 protects freedom to hold opinions and to receive and impart information. Under Art 10(2) it may be restricted where necessary to protect, among other things, the reputation or rights of others or information received in confidence.
2. **Neither right has automatic priority.** The method comes from Lord Steyn in *Re S (A Child)* [2004] UKHL 47 at [17]:
   1. neither article as such takes precedence;
   2. where they conflict, focus intensely on the comparative importance of the specific rights claimed in the individual case;
   3. take account of the justifications for interfering with or restricting each right;
   4. apply the proportionality test to each.
   *PJS* reaffirmed this, holding the Court of Appeal wrong to think s 12 HRA *enhances* the weight given to Article 10, even at the interim stage (Lord Mance, textbook).
3. **s 12(4) HRA**: the court must have particular regard to the importance of freedom of expression. Where the material is journalistic, literary or artistic, it must have regard to (a) the extent to which the material has become, or is about to become, available to the public and (b) whether publication is or would be in the public interest. Any relevant privacy code also counts (s 12(4)(b)).
4. **From "role models" to real public interest.**
   - Early cases leaned toward the press. *A v B plc* [2002] EWCA Civ 337: footballers are role models and the public is inevitably interested (Lord Woolf at [43]). *Ferdinand v MGN* [2011] EWHC 2454 (QB): as England captain, the claimant's role carried an expectation of high standards ([89]), which supported publishing his affair.
   - Later, courts demanded a *real* public interest. Baroness Hale in *Jameel v WSJ* [2006] UKHL 44 at [147] said the most vapid tittle-tattle about footballers' wives interests many people, but no one could claim a real public interest in it. *PJS* at [22] held that criticism of supposed infidelity cannot be a cover for kiss-and-tell stories with no public interest in the legal sense.
   - Where there is a genuine public interest, courts protect it. *AAA v Associated Newspapers* [2013] EWCA Civ 554: photos of a baby alongside claims that her father was a prominent politician. The claim failed because the mother had been inconsistent about how private the information was, and it raised a matter of general public interest.
5. **The *Axel Springer* criteria** (*Axel Springer AG v Germany* [2012] ECtHR, stressing the press's essential democratic role) structure the balance:
   1. contribution to a debate of public interest;
   2. how well known the person is;
   3. their prior conduct;
   4. how the information was obtained and its accuracy;
   5. the content, form and consequences of publication;
   6. the severity of the sanction.
   They were applied in *Richard v BBC*, *Duchess of Sussex* and *ZXC*.
6. **Specific public interest arguments:**
   - **Iniquity / crime**: "there is no confidence in iniquity". There is usually a public interest in exposing crime, but not absolutely; it depends on seriousness (*Brake v Guy* [2021] EWHC 670 (Ch), [2021] 4 WLR 71).
   - **Hypocrisy / correcting a false image**: where a public figure presents a false image and makes untrue public statements, the press may normally put the record straight (Lord Nicholls, *Campbell* at [24]). That is why the facts of Campbell's addiction and treatment were publishable, though the details and photos were not.
7. **Richard v BBC on the balance** (textbook): the balance came down strongly for privacy. A journalist obtained advance information about the raid and pressured the police into giving access. Adding dramatic pictures to the story materially increased the intrusion. Mann J rejected the argument that the claim threatened constitutional press freedom and needed Parliament's authority: the HRA itself supplies the authority, and any change in the press's position comes from the Act, not from judge-made principle. As between the BBC and the police (who admitted liability), the BBC bore the greater share of responsibility.

### D. Remedies
1. **Interim injunctions** are the main remedy, because once information is out the harm cannot be undone. *A v B plc* at [11] described a pre-publication injunction as the only remedy of any value.
2. **s 12 HRA** applies to any relief that might affect freedom of expression:
   - s 12(2): if the respondent is neither present nor represented, no relief unless the applicant has taken all practicable steps to notify them, **or** there are compelling reasons not to notify;
   - s 12(3): no restraint before trial unless the applicant is **likely to establish** that publication should not be allowed;
   - s 12(4): particular regard to freedom of expression (above).
3. **Comparison with defamation.** Defamation applies a stricter test. *Hayden v Dickenson* [2020] EWHC 3291 (QB) at [52], applying *Bonnard v Perryman* [1891] 2 Ch 269: no interim injunction where the defendant will plead truth, honest opinion or public interest. A claimant cannot avoid this by pleading privacy if the "nub" of the claim is reputation.
4. **Damages** can be awarded for distress and loss of control over information, and in lieu of an injunction in suitable cases. Both remedies can be combined: in *McKennitt v Ash* there was an injunction plus £5,000.
   - After *Mosley*, Steele notes, there were calls for exemplary damages because awards had been too low to deter.
   - ***Gulati v MGN* [2015] EWCA Civ 1291** (textbook): phone-hacking awards of up to £80,000 were upheld. Damages for MPI are a matter of English domestic law, not s 8 HRA or Strasbourg scales, and the awards were not to be scaled back. Arden LJ condemned MGN's "disgraceful" conduct.
   - Steele also notes that disgorgement (gain-based) damages are often seen as appropriate.
5. **Reputation in privacy claims.** In *Sicri*, Warby J suggested reputational damage should not be claimed in a privacy action, because the two torts differ in structure and procedure (textbook).

### E. Intrusion without disclosure
Steele notes that not every invasion of privacy involves publication. Physical intrusion is dealt with, if at all, by trespass, nuisance, *Wilkinson v Downton* and harassment. *Wainwright v Home Office* [2004] 2 AC 406 (a humiliating strip search of prison visitors in breach of the Prison Rules) refused to create a general intrusion tort. Lord Hoffmann distinguished privacy as an *underlying value* from privacy as a legal *principle*. He said Strasbourg does not require a high-level privacy principle (*Earl Spencer v UK*; *Peck v UK*), that detailed areas such as CCTV call for legislation (*Malone v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1979] Ch 344), and that ss 6-7 HRA already fill gaps against public authorities.

## Debate and critique
- **A tort by judicial development.** Is it legitimate for courts to build a privacy tort through the HRA when *Wainwright* says there is no general privacy tort and the HRA creates no new causes of action? Steele shows the formal position (s 6 does not authorise new torts) and the reality: through s 12 and the "shoehorning" into confidence, the courts did create a new action. Phillipson ('Transforming Breach of Confidence? Towards a Common Law Right of Privacy under the Human Rights Act' (2003) 66 MLR 726) and Morgan ('Privacy, Confidence, and Direct Effect: "Hello" Trouble' (2003) 62 CLJ 44). Mann J's reply in *Richard* is that the HRA supplies the democratic authority.
- **What is privacy?** Privacy is a "fuzzy" concept. Warren and Brandeis ('The Right to Privacy' (1890) 4 Harv L Rev 193) argued that a right "to be let alone" was implicit in the common law, by analogy with protection of person and property, and was needed because of the press and new technology. Steele asks whether privacy is one right or (following Prosser) several distinct protected interests, and notes Wacks's view ('The Poverty of Privacy' (1980) 96 LQR 73) that privacy has become confused with other issues. English law protects one specific interest, control over private *information*, not intrusion as such. Baroness Hale's "informational autonomy" and Lord Hoffmann's "entitled to decide for herself" place the tort's rationale in autonomy and dignity.
- **Equal weight and the press.** Moving from the "role model" cases (*A v B*, *Ferdinand*) to *PJS* shows the courts insisting on a real public interest. Steele places this against gross press abuses (the phone hacking in *Gulati*) and the problem of controlling online material. But the UK Government's 2021 Consultation on the Human Rights Act suggested that expression and journalism were the rights under threat. Any move to give Article 10 priority would change the tort fundamentally.
- **Privacy and defamation compared.** Defamation strikes its balance through a fixed pattern of elements, defences and remedies (truth is always a defence; prior restraint is almost unavailable). Privacy balances directly case by case, and interim injunctions are readily available (textbook). Yet both protect Article 8 interests, since reputation is now within Article 8. Can the difference be justified? Barendt ('An Overlap of Defamation Law and Privacy?' (2015) 7 JML 85), Gale ('Defamation and the Misuse of Private Information: A Comparative Analysis' (2018) 26 Tort L Rev 26) and Hariharan ('Damages for reputational harm: can privacy actions tread on defamation's turf?' (2021) 13 JML 186) address this. The sources list them but do not summarise them. The tension is clearest in police investigation cases: publicising a suspect's arrest damages reputation, yet the claim is in privacy (*ZXC*, *Sicri*). Warby J in *Sicri* warned against recovering reputational loss through privacy, and *Hayden v Dickenson* stops claimants evading *Bonnard v Perryman* by relabelling.
- **Police investigations.** Moreham ('Privacy, Reputation and Alleged Wrongdoing: Why Police Investigations Should Not Be Regarded as Private' (2019) 11 JML 142; 'Privacy and Police Investigations: ZXC v Bloomberg' (2021) 80 CLJ 5) questions treating investigations as private, as her title indicates. Against her, *ZXC* and *Richard* stress the serious and lasting harm of publicity before charge, and Steele notes the public may not reliably separate suspicion from guilt.
- **Scope of "misuse".** *Gulati* (misuse without disclosure) and *TalkTalk* (no liability for negligent security) show the boundaries are still being drawn. Data breaches are left to data protection law and negligence rather than this tort.
- **Damages and deterrence.** Should awards be compensatory, vindicatory, exemplary or gain-based? *Mosley*, *Gulati* and the arguments for disgorgement damages (Witzleb (2009) 29 OJLS 1,) are relevant here.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the cause of action.** Is this MPI (private information disclosed or used) or breach of confidence (a confidential relationship or commercially confidential information, as in *Douglas (No 3)*'s OK! claim)? Is it really a reputation claim, in which case consider defamation and the *Hayden* "nub" point? Is it physical intrusion without information (trespass, harassment, *Wainwright* problem)?
2. **Stage 1(i): is the information private?** Match it to the recognised categories: health, sex life, family and children, home, finances, correspondence, police investigation before charge. Is it merely a bare fact (*Hutcheson*)? Was the claimant in public (*Von Hannover*, *Campbell*, *Weller*)?
3. **Stage 1(ii): reasonable expectation?** Go through the Murray factors in turn: who the claimant is (a child? a public figure?), the activity, the place, how and why the information was obtained (long lens, hacking, insider leak), consent and its limits (*Duchess of Sussex*), the effect on the claimant, and how the publisher came by it. Are there photographs (*Campbell*)? Has the information already been partly published (*PJS*)? Apply the objective "ordinary sensibilities" test, but give weight to the claimant's own choices about disclosure.
4. **Stage 1(iii): misuse?** Has there been or will there be publication, or other positive use such as accessing voicemail (*Gulati*)? Is it only a negligent failure to protect data (*TalkTalk*)?
5. **Stage 2: balance.** Neither right has priority (*Re S*, *PJS*). Go through the *Axel Springer* criteria: does it contribute to a debate of general interest or is it mere gossip (*Jameel* [147]; *PJS* [22])? Is there a political or public role (*AAA*)? Prior conduct, especially a false public image (*Campbell* [24])? Is there iniquity, and how serious (*Brake v Guy*)? How was it obtained? What is the form (photos, intimate details, children's interests)? How severe would the sanction be? Distinguish publishing the *core fact* (perhaps justified) from publishing *details and photos* (often not), as *Campbell* did.
6. **Remedy.** Interim injunction: s 12(3) (likely to establish that publication should not be allowed); s 12(2) notice rules for applications without notice; s 12(4) public interest and availability (*PJS*: online availability elsewhere does not necessarily defeat it). Damages for distress and loss of control (*Gulati*; *McKennitt*), possibly in lieu of an injunction. Who is liable and in what share (*Richard*: BBC and police)?

**Traps:** saying English law has a general privacy tort (it does not: *Wainwright*); treating a public place as automatically non-private; giving Article 10 priority because of s 12 (wrong: *PJS*); treating "the public is interested" as "in the public interest"; forgetting children's separate interests; trying to use privacy to get an injunction against a damaging *false* allegation (defamation rules apply to the nub); using MPI for negligent data loss (*TalkTalk*).

- Gulati v MGN Ltd [2015] EWCA Civ 1291 (Arden LJ) upheld damages for loss of control/autonomy, treating them as domestic tort damages, not s 8 HRA/Art 41 awards. [2015] EWHC 1482 (Ch) is Mann J at first instance; cite the right court, no invented pinpoints. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1855/q05#0]
- Don't reverse chronology: the 'reasonable person of ordinary sensibilities placed in the claimant's position' test comes from Lord Hope in Campbell v MGN [2004] UKHL 22 at [99]; later cases such as ZXC v Bloomberg apply it, and Campbell cannot 'approve' them. - Don't say Lord Hoffmann held Art 8 binds only public authorities: in Campbell [49]-[50] he accepted the Convention targets the state but saw 'no logical ground' for less protection against private persons.
