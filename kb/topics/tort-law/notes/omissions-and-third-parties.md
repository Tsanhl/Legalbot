# Duty of Care – Omissions and Liability for Third Parties

## Overview
The common law of England and Wales generally makes people liable for *causing* harm, not for *failing to prevent* it. There is no general duty to rescue, to warn or to protect others from dangers created by nature or by third parties. Duties to act do arise in recognised exceptions, most of which rest on a special relationship (control, assumption of responsibility, status) or on the defendant having created or adopted the danger. Since *Michael* (2015), *Robinson* (2018) and *Poole* (2019), the Supreme Court has made this distinction, recast as "making things worse" versus "failing to make things better", central to the whole duty of care inquiry.

## The law

### A. Pure omissions: the general rule
1. **No general duty to act for the benefit of others.** The rule is that you must not harm your neighbour, not that you must save them from harm. So the common law does not impose liability for **pure omissions** (Lord Goff in *Smith v Littlewoods* [1987] AC 241; Lord Hope in *Mitchell v Glasgow CC* [2009] UKHL 11).
2. **The reasons** (*Stovin v Wise* [1996], per Lord Hoffmann):
   - requiring someone to consider others' safety when *acting* is less of an invasion of liberty than imposing a duty to rescue or protect;
   - the "why pick on me?" problem;
   - the law cannot require someone who is doing nothing to spend money for another's benefit.
3. **Foreseeability is not enough.** Lord Keith's example in *Yuen Kun Yeu v A-G of Hong Kong* [1988] AC 175, 192 (adopted in *Mitchell*): if foreseeability sufficed, someone who saw another about to walk over a cliff and did not shout a warning would be liable.
4. **The modern framing.** Lord Reed in *Robinson* and *Poole*: negligence generally imposes duties not to cause harm, not duties to confer benefits (including protection from harm by others). Duties to provide benefits are normally undertaken voluntarily, through contract, promises or trusts. In *Poole* he preferred "causing harm (making things worse)" against "failing to confer a benefit (not making things better)", partly because the act/omission line is hard to apply. He described the distinction as "inherent in the nature of the tort of negligence" in *Robinson*.
5. **Acts versus omissions in the course of acting.** Failing to brake while driving is simply bad driving, treated as an act. The classic pure omission is failing to warn a stranger. Sandy Steel suggests the test is whether D's conduct makes the victim worse off than they would have been "independently of the defendant's body or resources" (Steele ch 4). Lord Hoffmann in *Gorringe* described a pure omission as "doing nothing at all".

### B. Pure omissions vs omissions in pre-existing relationships
The rule targets *pure* omissions, not omissions inside a pre-existing relationship that already carries positive duties:
- **parent/carer–child, school–child**: *Barnes v Hampshire CC* [1969]; *Carmarthenshire CC v Lewis* [1955];
- **employer–employee**: *Wilsons & Clyde Coal Co Ltd v English* [1938]. An employer must ensure a safe system of work, which includes proper materials, competent fellow workers and proper supervision;
- Weir adds occupier–visitor, bailee–bailor and doctors. In each of these, one party must take active steps for the other.

### C. Exceptions: when a duty to act arises (following Horsey & Rackley 2023)
The exceptions increase proximity between the parties. They overlap, and writers group them differently.
1. **Control.** D has a high degree of control over C, or express responsibility for C. *Reeves v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2000]: police were liable for negligently failing to prevent the suicide of a *sane* prisoner in their cells. The duty arose because he was in their custody and they had reason to know he was a suicide risk.
2. **Assumption of responsibility** (by contract, employment, or conduct). *Costello v Chief Constable of Northumbria Police* [1999]: one police constable owes another a duty to go to her assistance if she is attacked. Failing to give physical help exposed the claimant to unnecessary risk of injury.
3. **Creating or adopting a dangerous situation.** *Capital & Counties plc v Hampshire CC* [1997] (the Hampshire action): a fire officer shut down the premises' sprinkler system, the fire spread rapidly and destroyed the building, and the fire authority was liable. This was making things worse, not merely failing to help.
4. **Status.** Lord Reed in *Robinson* lists the exceptions as:
   - a voluntary assumption of responsibility ("close or akin to contract");
   - having assumed a status carrying responsibility to prevent harm (parent, *in loco parentis*);
   - an omission occurring where D has acted so as to create or increase a risk of harm.
5. **Occupiers and dangers on their land.** Responsibilities of an occupier for dangers arising on his land that threaten neighbours (*Goldman v Hargrave* [1967] 1 AC 645) can be explained by the occupier's exclusive right of possession (*Robinson*). Weir: an occupier must take reasonable steps to stop a danger on his land, whether from nature or third parties, from harming his neighbour's property.

### D. Liability for the acts of third parties
**General rule**: no one is under a duty to prevent third parties (T) from causing damage to C (*Smith v Littlewoods* at 270, per Lord Goff; *Michael* at [97]). It is "one thing" to require someone who acts to take care, and "another matter" to make them pay for failing to prevent harm caused by someone else (Lord Toulson, *Michael*).

**Exceptions**:
1. **Special relationship between D and C**: a "pre-tort" relationship creating sufficient proximity:
   - *contract*: *Stansbie v Troman* (commentary gives the year as [1984]; check the citation before relying on it);
   - *assumption of responsibility*: *Al-Najar v Cumberland Hotel* [2019];
   - *express or implied undertaking*: *Costello*. This must be a direct, specific undertaking (*Palmer v Tees Health Authority* [1999]; *Mitchell v Glasgow CC* [2009]).
2. **Special relationship between D and T**: D exercises control over T, or has assumed responsibility for T's actions (*Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* [1970] AC 1004).
3. **D creates a source of danger** that is "sparked off" by third parties (*Topp v London Country Bus* [1993]; Lord Goff in *Smith v Littlewoods*).
4. **Failure to deal with a known danger**: D knows, or ought to know, that a third party has created a danger on D's property that D should do something about (*Smith v Littlewoods* [1987]; *Clark Fixing v Dudley MBC* [2001]).

**Lord Brown's list in *Mitchell*** was treated in *Robinson* and *Poole* as an accurate statement of the bases of liability (Steele). A may be liable for C's crime against B where:
- A is **vicariously liable** for C (for example C is A's employee);
- A is under an **obligation to supervise** C and fails to (*Dorset Yacht*);
- A **specifically creates a risk**, for example by arming C (*A-G of the British Virgin Islands v Hartwell* [2004]);
- A **assumes specific responsibility** for B's safety (*Costello*).

Landlords are not responsible for their tenants' antisocial behaviour: *Smith v Scott* [1973]; *Hussain v Lancaster CC* [2000]; *Mowan v Wandsworth LBC* [2001].

**Lord Toulson in *Michael*** described two well-recognised exceptions:
1. **control** by D over T, with foreseeability of T harming someone in close proximity. This needs two special relationships, D–T and D–C (Tipping J, *Couch v A-G* [2008] NZ);
2. **assumption of responsibility** under *Hedley Byrne*/*Spring v Guardian Assurance*. This covers contract, fiduciary relationships, employer–employee, school–pupil and health professional–patient, and the list is not exhaustive. It "should not be expanded artificially", because courts sometimes use the phrase where responsibility is really imposed rather than assumed.

### E. Link to causation
Horsey & Rackley note an overlap between third-party duty problems and causation, especially *novus actus interveniens*. Commentary parks this until causation is covered. Steele also notes that omissions can raise special issues of breach, causation and remoteness as well as duty.

## Key authorities
- ***Smith v Littlewoods Organisation Ltd* [1987] AC 241 (HL, Scottish appeal)**:
  - *Facts*: trespassers broke into the defendants' empty cinema and started a fire that spread to and damaged neighbouring property, including a church (Weir).
  - *Held*: no liability.
  - *Lord Goff*: the common law does not impose liability for pure omissions, and there is no general duty to prevent third parties causing damage. He identified exceptions, including where D negligently causes or permits a source of danger to be created that third parties foreseeably "spark off", and where D knows or has the means of knowing that a third party has created a fire risk on his premises and fails to take reasonable steps (Lord Goff at 274, quoted in *Alame v Shell* [2025] EWHC 1539 (KB)).
  - What is reasonable depends on the facts: keeping intruders out, telling the police, warning neighbours, or for a large company employing watchmen.
  - Weir says the decision is best read, as by Lord Griffiths, as finding no *breach* rather than no *duty*, given the occupiers' special relationship.
- ***Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* [1970] AC 1004 (HL)**:
  - *Facts*: Borstal boys escaped while their officers were asleep and damaged a yacht nearby.
  - *Held*: a duty was owed. There was control over the boys, and the yacht owner was in the "immediate vicinity" (proximity).
  - Lord Diplock: the Good Samaritan parable shows omissions which English law does not make actionable. The question "Am I my brother's keeper?" receives "a restricted reply", but not a wholly negative one.
  - Lord Reid: "mere accidental propinquity" does not oblige anyone to help another in distress. There may be a moral duty, but a legal one is impracticable.
  - Weir notes the case is now read as turning on the officers having *brought the danger* to the island.
- ***Stovin v Wise* [1996] AC 923 / *Gorringe v Calderdale* [2004] UKHL 15**: the existence of a statutory power or general public law duty does not of itself create a common law duty to act. See public-authorities-duty-of-care.md. Lord Hoffmann in *Stovin* (at 949): "the default position" is that a person who suffers loss is not prima facie entitled to compensation.
- ***Mitchell v Glasgow City Council* [2009] UKHL 11**:
  - *Facts*: M and his neighbour Drummond were council tenants. Drummond had repeatedly threatened to kill M. The council called Drummond to a meeting and threatened him with eviction, without warning M. Drummond went home and killed M.
  - *Held*: no duty to warn. There was no control akin to *Dorset Yacht* and no assumption of responsibility. Lord Rodger at [63]: there was no undertaking showing the council "had made themselves responsible for protecting Mr Mitchell".
  - Policy concerns were present: not hampering social-landlord functions, and the onerousness of such a duty.
- ***Reeves v Commissioner of Police for the Metropolis* [2000] 1 AC 360**: police liable for failing to prevent the suicide of a sane detainee known to be a suicide risk. This is the control exception.
- ***Costello v Chief Constable of Northumbria Police* [1999] ICR 752**: an officer's duty to assist a colleague under attack. Assumption of responsibility.
- ***Capital & Counties plc v Hampshire CC* [1997] QB 1004**: liability for turning off the sprinklers, which made things worse. No liability for simply failing to put out a fire.
- ***A-G of the British Virgin Islands v Hartwell* [2004] UKPC 12**: an officer unfit to have firearms was given access to a police revolver and shot a tourist in a bar. The police authority owed the public a duty to take reasonable care that he was suitable to be entrusted with such a weapon, and breached it. This is the creation-of-risk exception (described in *Begum v Maran*).
- ***Begum v Maran (UK) Ltd* [2021] EWCA Civ 326**:
  - *Facts*: a shipbroker sold a vessel to be broken up at a notoriously unsafe Chattogram (Bangladesh) yard, where a worker fell to his death.
  - *Held*: the claim that the broker owed a duty under the **"creation of danger" exception** (traced to Lord Goff in *Smith v Littlewoods*) was not struck out. It was arguable, though an "unusual extension" of an existing category.
  - Insisting that third-party cases be "corralled into a pen marked 'pure omissions'" can be distracting and artificial. Lord Reed's "making things worse / not making things better" is clearer ([59]–[60]).
  - Only in a relatively extreme case will the creation-of-danger exception apply ([63]).
  - This area is "at the forefront" of negligence's development, so strike-out was inappropriate ([71]).
  - The issues were decided on assumed facts at an interim stage. The case is England and Wales law, applied to a foreign incident, and the claim also faced a Rome II limitation issue.
- ***Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police* [2015] UKSC 2**: the general rule and the two exceptions (above). Facts are in public-authorities-duty-of-care.md.
- ***Robinson* [2018] UKSC 4** and ***Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25**: the harm/benefit distinction is fundamental. In *Poole*, Lord Reed said a public body, like an individual, may owe a duty to protect where it created the danger or assumed responsibility.
- **Listed with only the summary above**: *Stansbie v Troman*; *Al-Najar v Cumberland Hotel* [2019]; *Palmer v Tees HA* [1999]; *Topp v London Country Bus* [1993]; *Clark Fixing v Dudley MBC* [2001]; *Barnes v Hampshire CC* [1969]; *Carmarthenshire CC v Lewis* [1955]; *Wilsons & Clyde Coal v English* [1938]; *Hudson v Ridge Manufacturing Co* [1957] 2 QB 348 (listed in the case list only).

## Debate and critique
- **Is the act/omission distinction defensible?**
  - Lord Reed treats it as inherent in negligence and not a substitute for policy.
  - Morgan, 'Nonfeasance and the End of Policy?' (2019) 35 PN 32, says it "needs to be explained and justified, not just postulated as an axiom". Other systems recognise duties to rescue, and public authorities have positive public-law and Article 2 duties.
  - Further reading on the theme: Morgan, 'Maintaining the elegant façade of the acts-omissions distinction' (2022) 81 CLJ 245; Linden, 'Toward tort liability for bad Samaritans' (2016) 53 Alberta LR 837.
  - Sandy Steel, 'Rationalising Omissions Liability in Negligence' (2019) 135 LQR 484, supports recasting the line as making the victim worse off.
- **Bad Samaritans and morality.** Weir accepts that English law, unlike many systems, imposes no duty to rescue a stranger even at no risk. He argues legal standards should not track "delicate morality" or there would be no room for generosity. Lord Reid in *Dorset Yacht* draws the same moral/legal line.
- **Feminist and ethics-of-care critiques.** the reading includes Steele, 'Duty of Care and Ethics of Care: Irreconcilable Difference?' (2012). It questions whether a law built on non-interference between strangers can recognise duties that come from relationships of care and dependency.
- **"Assumption of responsibility" is elastic.**
  - Lord Toulson warns against expanding it artificially.
  - Morgan calls it potentially "vacuous" or even "mendacious" and another channel for policy.
  - Steele notes its content is shifting. In *Poole* the Supreme Court accepted it need not be voluntary or specific: it can be "inherent in the role" a public body performs (for example under a statutory scheme), or arise from specific conduct.
  - By contrast, *X & Y v Hounslow LBC* [2009] EWCA Civ 286 required a specific assumption of responsibility by words or deeds. *Rowley v Secretary of State for Work and Pensions* [2007] EWCA Civ 598 held that exercising a statutory function is not itself an assumption.
- **Weir on innocent creation of danger.** One who innocently causes a danger may come under a duty to try to prevent damage from it. Examples: a motorist who without fault knocks down a pedestrian cannot leave him lying in the road; an occupier who knows a trespasser lies injured on his land must surely help. Weir says *Dorset Yacht* is now read this way.
- **Duty or breach?** Weir's reading of *Smith v Littlewoods* (no breach, per Lord Griffiths, rather than no duty, per Lord Goff) shows the same policy can be expressed at different stages of the analysis.
- **Third parties and scope of duty.** In *Alame v Shell* [2025] EWHC 1539 (KB), the court referred to the Supreme Court in *Meadows* recommending a "scope of duty" question in third-party and omission cases: what risks of harm did D owe a duty to protect C against? *Meadows* is mentioned only in that context in the sources.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Characterise the conduct.**
   - Did D make things worse (a positive act, or an omission in the course of an activity, like failing to brake)? Then ordinary principles apply (*Robinson*).
   - Or did D merely fail to confer a benefit, or fail to stop harm from nature or a third party? Then the starting point is **no duty**.
   - Avoid artificial labelling (*Begum v Maran*).
2. **Look for a pre-existing relationship carrying positive duties**: employer, school/parent, occupier, bailee, doctor, contract.
3. **Run through the exceptions**:
   - Did D **control** C (*Reeves*) or T (*Dorset Yacht*: was C in the "immediate vicinity"/proximate)?
   - Did D **assume responsibility** to C, by specific words or conduct, undertakings or reliance (*Costello*; contrast *Mitchell*, *Michael*)? Was the undertaking specific (*Palmer*)?
   - Did D **create or increase** the danger (*Capital & Counties* sprinklers; *Hartwell* firearm; *Topp*; *Begum*)?
   - Did D **know, or ought D to have known, of a danger** created by a third party on D's land, and fail to take reasonable steps (*Smith v Littlewoods*, *Goldman v Hargrave*)?
   - Does D hold a **status** such as parent or *in loco parentis*?
4. **Identify the likely harm.** Third-party crime against a large class (all women, all householders) points against proximity (see *Hill*). An identified victim helps, but is not enough without an exception (*Mitchell*, *Michael*).
5. **If an exception applies, define the scope of the duty.** Ask what risks D had to guard against, then move to breach. What is reasonable may be limited by D's means (*Goldman v Hargrave*, as described by Lord Goff).
6. **Cross-check causation.** Third-party conduct may also be a *novus actus* (see the causation notes).
7. **Traps**:
   - treating foreseeability of harm as enough;
   - forgetting that landlords are not liable for tenants' crimes;
   - assuming a public body's statutory powers create a duty to act (*Stovin*/*Gorringe*);
   - treating any phone call or contact as an assumption of responsibility (*Michael*: passing on a call and asking the caller to keep the line free was not enough).
