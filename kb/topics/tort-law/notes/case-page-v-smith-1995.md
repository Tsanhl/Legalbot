# Page v Smith [1996] AC 155 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. Also reported as [1995] 2 WLR 644 and [1995] UKHL 7. No paragraph pinpoints are
> given; check the report before quoting.

## Overview
House of Lords, 11 May 1995. Panel: Lord Keith of Kinkel, Lord Ackner, Lord Browne-Wilkinson, Lord Jauncey of
Tullichettle and Lord Lloyd of Berwick. The appeal was allowed **3 to 2**: Lord Lloyd gave the leading speech for the
majority, with Lord Ackner and Lord Browne-Wilkinson; **Lord Keith and Lord Jauncey dissented**. The case sets the rule
for **primary victims** of psychiatric injury.

## Facts
Page was driving when Smith's negligent driving caused a collision of moderate severity. Page suffered no physical
injury. He had suffered for many years from myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME, also called chronic fatigue syndrome), which
was in remission, and the accident caused a recurrence. Smith accepted that he was negligent but
argued that psychiatric injury was not reasonably foreseeable. The trial judge found for Page, and the Court of Appeal
reversed that decision on foreseeability.

## Held
- The House of Lords **allowed Page's appeal** by a majority and remitted the outstanding issue of causation to the
  Court of Appeal (the follow-on decision is reported as *Page v Smith (No 2)*).
- Page was a primary victim, since he was directly involved in the accident and was within the range of foreseeable
  physical injury.
- For a primary victim, the question is whether the defendant could reasonably foresee that his conduct would expose
  the claimant to the risk of personal injury, whether physical or psychiatric. It is not necessary to show that
  psychiatric injury of the particular kind was foreseeable.
- The "person of ordinary fortitude" requirement from the secondary victim cases does not apply, and the defendant
  takes the claimant as he finds him (the eggshell skull rule).
- The minority (Lords Keith and Jauncey) would have required that psychiatric injury itself was foreseeable in a
  person of ordinary fortitude, which they thought was not shown.

## Later treatment
- In *Rothwell v Chemical & Insulating Co Ltd* [2007] UKHL 39 the House declined to extend *Page v Smith* to a
  psychiatric illness that developed from anxiety about a risk of future disease, rather than as an immediate result
  of the incident.
- The majority reasoning has been widely criticised by commentators.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Decide whether the claimant is a primary victim (in the zone of physical danger, or reasonably believing himself to
   be) or a secondary one. If secondary, use *Alcock*.
2. For a primary victim, ask whether some personal injury, physical or psychiatric, was reasonably foreseeable from the
   defendant's conduct.
3. If so, a duty is owed and it does not matter that the injury actually suffered was of a different kind or more severe
   because of an existing vulnerability.
4. Say whether a recognised psychiatric illness exists and deal with causation.
