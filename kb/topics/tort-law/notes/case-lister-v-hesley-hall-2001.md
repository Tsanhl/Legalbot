# Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd [2001] UKHL 22 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. Reported at [2002] 1 AC 215 (also [2001] 2 All ER 769). No paragraph
> pinpoints are given; check the report before quoting.

## Overview
House of Lords, judgment 3 May 2001. Panel: Lord Steyn, Lord Clyde, Lord Hutton, Lord Hobhouse of Woodborough and Lord
Millett, each of whom gave a speech; Lord Steyn's is usually treated as the leading one. The House replaced the older
"unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act" approach with a close connection test for an employer's vicarious
liability for an employee's intentional wrong.

## Facts
Hesley Hall Ltd ran a school (Wilsic Hall, Doncaster) and a boarding annex, Axeholme House, as a commercial enterprise.
Boys aged about 12 to 15 boarded there between 1979 and 1982. The company employed a warden to look after them outside
school hours, and he sexually abused several boys. The company did not know of it. The abuse came to light years later,
and the warden was imprisoned. The former pupils sued the company on the basis of vicarious liability.

## Held
- The appeal was **allowed**, reversing the Court of Appeal. The company was vicariously liable for the warden's abuse.
- The Court of Appeal's earlier decision in *T v North Yorkshire CC* (also known as *Trotman*), which had held a
  senior teacher's sexual abuse of a pupil on a school trip to be outside his employment, was overruled.
- The Salmond approach, which asked whether the wrongful act was an unauthorised mode of doing something authorised,
  was regarded as unsuitable for deliberate wrongs of this kind.
- The test is whether the wrongdoing was so closely connected with the employment that it would be fair and just to hold
  the employer vicariously liable. The warden was entrusted with the care of the boys, and the abuse was bound up with
  that task.
- The fact that employment merely gave the employee the opportunity to commit the tort is not enough on its own.

## Later treatment
- The close connection test has been applied in later cases, for example to an employee's fraud (*Dubai Aluminium Co
  Ltd v Salaam* [2002] UKHL 48) and to a nightclub doorman's stabbing (*Mattis v Pollock* [2003] 1 WLR 2158), and has
  been restated by the Supreme Court; check the current formulation before relying on *Lister* alone.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Check that the wrongdoer is an employee (or in a relationship akin to employment) and that a tort was committed.
2. Ask what the employee was employed to do. Was the wrongful act closely connected with that role, for example caring
   for or supervising the claimant?
3. Apply the "fair and just" question to the connection, treating the scope of the job broadly but not treating mere
   opportunity as enough.
4. Compare facts that merely provide the setting (time and place) with facts showing a real link to the duties.
5. Conclude whether the employer is vicariously liable.
