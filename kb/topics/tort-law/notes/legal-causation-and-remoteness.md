# Legal Causation, Remoteness and Intervening Acts

## Overview
Once the breach is shown to be a "but for" cause, the law still asks whether it is the kind of cause that justifies liability for the particular loss. the materials splits this into (1) remoteness, where losses too far removed from the breach are not recoverable, tested by reasonable foreseeability of the *kind* of damage (*The Wagon Mound (No 1)*), qualified by the egg-shell skull rule; and (2) *novus actus interveniens*, where an intervening act or event breaks the chain of causation. Recent Supreme Court authority adds a separate "scope of duty" question (*SAAMCO*, *Khan v Meadows*). This is England and Wales law; *The Wagon Mound* is a Privy Council decision (from Australia) accepted as representing English law.

## The law

### 1. Structure: how many questions?
1. Traditional two stages: cause in fact, then cause in law or remoteness (Steele).
2. *Khan v Meadows* [2021] UKSC 21 (Lords Hodge and Sales) proposed six questions in sequence: (1) actionability; (2) scope of duty (what risks is the duty meant to guard against?); (3) breach; (4) factual causation; (5) duty nexus (is the particular head of loss connected to the subject matter of the duty?); (6) legal responsibility (is the loss too remote, is there a different effective cause including novus actus, or a failure to mitigate?).
3. Lord Burrows in the same case preferred a "relatively conventional" seven questions: duty; breach; factual causation; remoteness; legal causation (intervening acts); scope of duty (SAAMCO); defences. He noted that questions 4 to 6 overlap and may reach the same result.

### 2. Remoteness: reasonable foreseeability of the kind of damage
1. Old rule: a negligent defendant was liable for all *direct* consequences, foreseeable or not (*Re Polemis and Furness Withy & Co* [1921] 3 KB 560: plank dropped into a hold caused an unforeseeable spark and fire that destroyed the ship).
2. Current rule: the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind which a reasonable person would have foreseen at the time of the breach (*Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock & Engineering Co Ltd (The Wagon Mound (No 1))* [1961] AC 388). Re Polemis "should no longer be regarded as good law". Judged by foresight, not hindsight.
3. What must be foreseen is the *type* or *kind* of harm, not the precise manner in which it occurs or its extent:
   - *Hughes v Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837: burns were foreseeable; that they came about through an unforeseeable explosion was irrelevant. Lord Reid: the defender escapes only if the damage differs "in kind" from what was foreseeable; here "a known source of danger, the lamp, ... behaved in an unpredictable way".
   - *Jolley v Sutton LBC* [2000] 3 All ER 409 / [2000] 1 WLR 1082: Wagon Mound and Hughes "in no way suggest[ed] that the precise manner in which the injury occurred nor its extent had to be foreseeable" (Lord Steyn). Lord Hoffmann: the foreseeable risk was broadly that children would "meddle with the boat at the risk of some physical injury"; children's ingenuity should never be underestimated.
   - Contrast *Doughty v Turner Manufacturing Co Ltd* [1964] 1 QB 518: a splash of hot liquid was foreseeable, but burns from an unforeseeable explosion were held too remote; and *Tremain v Pike* [1969] 1 WLR 1556 (rat's urine disease vs rat bite; remarks obiter). Steele calls the lower court case law inconsistent.
4. The Wagon Mound (No 2) [1966] 2 All ER 709 (cited by Weir; relied on by Lord Hoffmann in Jolley): a risk that is real, though small, is not one that can be "brushed aside as far-fetched".

### 3. The egg-shell skull rule (exception)
1. If some physical injury of the foreseeable kind occurs, the defendant is liable for its full extent even if, because of the claimant's pre-existing susceptibility, the harm is far worse than could be foreseen: the tortfeasor takes the victim as he finds him (*Smith v Leech Brain & Co Ltd* [1962] 2 QB 405: a burn on the lip from molten metal triggered cancer and death; employer liable for the death).
2. Lord Parker CJ reconciled it with Wagon Mound: the test is whether the *type* of injury (the burn) was foreseeable; its extent depends on the victim's constitution.
3. Extended to psychiatric vulnerability: *Malcolm v Broadhurst* [1970] 3 All ER 508 ("no difference in principle between an egg-shell skull and an egg-shell personality"); *Page v Smith* [1995]; *Robinson v Post Office* [1974] 1 WLR 1176 (allergic reaction to an anti-tetanus injection).
4. Impecuniosity: *The Liesbosch Dredger* [1933] AC 449 (no recovery for losses due to the claimant's lack of funds) was departed from in *Lagden v O'Connor* [2004] 1 AC 1067, so financial vulnerability is now treated similarly.

### 4. Novus actus interveniens: intervening acts and events
General: there is no recovery for harm caused by an intervening act or event that breaks the chain of causation. Steele: foreseeability alone does not answer these cases; the courts look at normality, the voluntariness and character of the intervention, and the purpose and scope of the duty.

**(a) Acts of the claimant.** Unreasonableness is the key criterion.
1. *McKew v Holland & Hannen & Cubitts (Scotland) Ltd* [1969] 3 All ER 1621: pursuer with a leg weakened by the employer's negligence descended steep stairs with no banister; when his leg gave way he jumped to the landing and broke his other leg. His own unreasonable conduct broke the chain.
2. *Spencer v Wincanton Holdings* [2009] EWCA Civ 1404: amputee fell while refuelling without his prosthesis; not so unreasonable as to break the chain, so contributory negligence applied instead. Sedley LJ: foreseeability has to be explained in terms of remoteness, not the reverse; the guiding principle is fairness. Similar: *Dalling v Heale* [2011] EWCA Civ 365.
3. Suicide: *Reeves v Commissioner of Police for the Metropolis* [2000] 1 AC 360: police owed a duty to prevent a known suicide risk in custody. The suicide, the "very act which ought to have been prevented", could not break the chain (Lord Hoffmann). Damages were reduced for contributory negligence.
4. *Corr v IBC Vehicles Ltd* [2008] UKHL 13; [2008] AC 884: workplace accident caused depression leading to suicide. Not a novus actus. Lord Bingham: it was not a voluntary, informed decision by an adult of sound mind, because his capacity was impaired by depression caused by the tort. Lord Scott reasoned via the egg-shell skull rule and *Page v Smith*.
5. Contrast *Gray v Thames Trains* [2009] UKHL 33: a criminal act by the claimant himself (committed after psychiatric injury caused by the defendant) broke the chain or was treated as a supervening cause (Steele: decisive difference is criminality and responsibility).

**(b) Acts of third parties.** Relevant where the third party exploits an opportunity created by the defendant, responds to danger (e.g. as a rescuer), or deliberately does wrong.
1. *Knightley v Johns* [1982] 1 WLR 349: the first defendant negligently crashed in a tunnel; a police inspector, contrary to standing orders, sent the claimant officer on a motorcycle back against the traffic, and he was hit. The inspector's positive, negligent act was a "new cause disturbing the sequence of events", making the injury too remote from the first defendant's wrong. Steele: the case shows that ordinary negligence by a rescuer is not enough, but a very unusual sequence of events can be; this is a matter of opinion.
2. See also *Wright v Lodge* [1993] 4 All ER 299 (reckless lorry driver: original negligent driver liable to her passenger but not to victims on the other carriageway) and *Rouse v Squires* [1973] 2 All ER 903.
3. Deliberate wrongdoing: *Weld-Blundell v Stephens* [1920] AC 956 (Lord Sumner): where a third party deliberately chooses to do wrong, the tendency is to find the chain broken.
4. But if the very purpose of the duty was to guard against the third party's act, the act cannot break the chain: *Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* [1970] AC 1004 (escaping Borstal trainees damaged a yacht; the damage was "the very kind of thing" the officers ought to have seen as likely; Lord Reid required the intervention to be "very likely", not merely foreseeable); *Stansbie v Troman* [1948] 2 KB 48 (decorator left a door unlocked; burglary was the very thing to be guarded against).
5. Where there is no special duty, deliberate third-party wrongdoing is usually outside the defendant's responsibility: *Lamb v Camden LBC* [1981] QB 625 (squatters in a house vacated after the council's burst water main; Lord Denning analysed it as policy: whose job was it to keep squatters out?); *Perl (Exporters) Ltd v Camden LBC* [1984] QB 342 (no duty to stop thieves entering via one's own premises to reach a neighbour's).

**(c) Intervening medical treatment.**
1. *Webb v Barclays Bank* [2001] EWCA Civ 1141: negligent medical treatment made necessary by the defendant's tort will typically not break the chain, since although unreasonable it is not freely chosen.
2. *Robinson v Post Office* [1974] 1 WLR 1176: the doctor's negligence did not break the chain (it was not even a "but for" cause). the materials states that negligent treatment breaks the chain only if extreme or "gross". Weir: if the doctor "cuts off the wrong leg" the original tortfeasor's liability ends.

### 5. Scope of duty (purpose of the rule)
1. *Gorris v Scott* (1874) LR 9 Exch 125: sheep washed overboard; the statutory duty to pen them aimed at preventing contagion, not drowning, so no liability (Weir).
2. *SAAMCO v York Montague* [1997] AC 191 (Lord Hoffmann): a negligent valuer is liable only for the consequences attributable to what made the act wrongful (the overvaluation), not the whole loss from a market fall. Mountaineer example: a doctor who negligently certifies a knee as fit is not liable for an avalanche injury.
3. *Khan v Meadows* [2021] UKSC 21: the GP was consulted specifically about the risk of the child having haemophilia. The claimant recovered the costs associated with haemophilia but not those from the child's unrelated autism, which was outside the risk the duty guarded against. Scope of duty is a general principle, not confined to financial advice (see also *Manchester Building Society v Grant Thornton* [2021], *Hughes-Holland v BPE* [2017] UKSC 21).

## Key authorities
- *Re Polemis* [1921] 3 KB 560: direct consequences test (overruled in effect by Wagon Mound).
- *The Wagon Mound (No 1)* [1961] AC 388 (PC): oil spill in Sydney Harbour; fouling damage foreseeable, fire not (thin furnace oil on water hard to ignite); no liability for fire. Foreseeability of kind of damage is the test.
- *Hughes v Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837: unforeseeable explosion, foreseeable burns; liable.
- *Jolley v Sutton LBC* [2000]: abandoned boat; boy injured jacking it up; broad description of the foreseeable risk; liable (25 per cent contributory negligence).
- *Doughty v Turner* [1964] 1 QB 518: explosion (as opposed to a splash) held unforeseeable; no liability.
- *Smith v Leech Brain* [1962] 2 QB 405: egg-shell skull survives Wagon Mound.
- *McKew v Holland* [1969] 3 All ER 1621: claimant's unreasonable conduct breaks the chain.
- *Corr v IBC* [2008] UKHL 13: suicide due to tort-induced depression is not a novus actus.
- *Reeves v MPC* [2000] 1 AC 360: suicide which the duty was meant to prevent is not a novus actus.
- *Knightley v Johns* [1982]: rescuer's grossly negligent positive act breaks the chain.
- *Weld-Blundell v Stephens* [1920]: deliberate third-party wrongdoing tends to break the chain.
- *Dorset Yacht* [1970] AC 1004; *Stansbie v Troman* [1948]: "very thing" the duty aimed at.
- *Webb v Barclays Bank* [2001]; *Robinson v Post Office* [1974]: medical negligence rarely breaks the chain unless gross.
- *SAAMCO* [1997]; *Khan v Meadows* [2021] UKSC 21: scope of duty.

## Debate and critique
- Wagon Mound's rationale: Viscount Simonds thought it unjust that a slight act of negligence causing trivial foreseeable damage should lead to liability for grave unforeseeable damage; he wanted to escape the "never-ending and insoluble problems of causation". Glanville Williams ("The Risk Principle" (1961) 77 LQR 179) welcomed this as collapsing remoteness into one question of negligence relative to a particular risk. Steele: the ambition failed. "Type of harm" is so generously construed (*Hughes*, *Jolley*, *Smith v Leech Brain*) that unforeseeable harm is often recovered, which sits awkwardly with the fairness rhetoric; and causal language (novus actus) survives.
- Weir's critique: foreseeability is not even the test of negligence, only a factor; once negligence is shown, consequences must be truly exceptional ("Who would have thought it?") to escape. He suggests "normality" as a more objective idea; he also notes the German distinction between initial harm and ulterior harm.
- Egg-shell skull: Hart and Honoré call the reconciliation with Wagon Mound "mere window dressing". Williams defended the rule as a justified exception because "human bodies are too fragile". Weir thinks it dubious policy for the accident-prone and neurotic (*Page v Smith*).
- Jolley and breach: Lord Hoffmann's argument (no extra precaution was needed to avoid the wider risk) would, without the "kind of damage" limit, abolish a separate remoteness inquiry and even reverse Wagon Mound itself (Steele).
- Intervening acts: foreseeability does not explain why deliberate acts insulate the defendant more than negligent ones, or positive acts more than omissions. Weir suggests asking whether "the buck has passed" to someone in control and free to act. Steele favours analysis based on the purpose and scope of the duty (Dorset Yacht, Stansbie, Reeves, Perl), or the "normal/abnormal" approach of Hart and Honoré. Williams conceded that here "we reach the limits of predictability". Many commentators think *McKew* should have been a contributory negligence case (Weir defends it).
- Where does remoteness fit now? With scope of duty and intervening cause carved out as separate questions (*Khan v Meadows*), Steele argues remoteness is left a "thin and unsatisfactory" foreseeability test. Stapleton (2003) prefers one open "scope of liability for consequences" stage. Lord Burrows accepted remoteness might be wide enough to embrace SAAMCO.
- Further reading: Stapleton, "Cause-in-fact and the scope of liability for consequences" (2003) 119 LQR 389; Stapleton, "Choosing what we mean by 'causation' in the law" (2008) 73 Missouri LR 43; Goldberg (ed), *Perspectives on Causation* (2011).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm factual causation first (see `factual-causation.md`).
2. Scope of duty: what risk was the duty aimed at? Does the head of loss claimed fall within it (*SAAMCO*, *Khan v Meadows*, *Gorris v Scott*)?
3. Remoteness: describe the kind of harm that was foreseeable at the time of breach (personal injury? property damage by fouling? fire?). Did the harm that occurred fall within that description? Remember that manner and extent need not be foreseen (*Hughes*, *Jolley*). Note any *Doughty*-type argument that the mechanism produced a different kind of harm.
4. Unusual severity: is there a pre-existing physical, psychiatric or financial vulnerability? Apply the egg-shell skull rule (*Smith v Leech Brain*, *Page v Smith*, *Lagden v O'Connor*), provided some foreseeable harm of that type occurred.
5. Intervening act? Classify it:
   - Claimant's own act: was it unreasonable (*McKew*: break), or merely careless (*Spencer*: contributory negligence)? A suicide caused by tort-induced illness does not break the chain (*Corr*), nor does a suicide the duty was meant to prevent (*Reeves*).
   - Third party: innocent or negligent reaction in an emergency or rescue usually does not break the chain; a reckless or grossly negligent positive act may (*Knightley*, *Wright v Lodge*); deliberate wrongdoing usually does (*Weld-Blundell*), unless it was the very thing the defendant had to guard against (*Dorset Yacht*, *Stansbie*).
   - Medical treatment: ordinary negligence does not break the chain (*Webb*); gross negligence might.
   - Natural event: see *Jobling* (supervening illness) in `factual-causation.md`.
6. If the chain is not broken but the claimant was at fault, consider contributory negligence under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 (see `defences.md`).
7. Traps: requiring foresight of the precise accident; forgetting the egg-shell skull rule; treating any later negligence as a novus actus; ignoring the purpose of the duty when the intervening act is exactly what the duty targeted.
