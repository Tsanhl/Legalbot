# Defamation - Defences and Remedies

## Overview
Because a claimant only has to prove a defamatory, seriously harmful, published statement referring to them, and does not have to prove falsity or fault, most of the real contest in a defamation case is over **defences**. Here the law of England and Wales does most of its balancing between reputation and freedom of expression (Article 10 ECHR). The Defamation Act 2013 (DA 2013) abolished the common law defences of justification, fair comment and *Reynolds* privilege, replacing them with statutory **truth (s 2)**, **honest opinion (s 3)** and **publication on a matter of public interest (s 4)**. It also added a defence for **website operators (s 5)** and a **peer-review privilege (s 6)**. Absolute and qualified privilege remain mostly common law. Innocent dissemination and offer of amends remain under the Defamation Act 1996 (DA 1996). The handout lists six defamation-specific defences: truth, honest opinion, privilege (absolute and qualified), public interest, offer of amends and innocent dissemination. This note also covers remedies (damages, the new court orders under ss 12-13, and injunctions).

Common law cases decided before the Act remain relevant: the Explanatory Notes say s 2 is meant broadly to reflect the existing law, and earlier case law is a helpful but non-binding guide (textbook). Section 16 also keeps the old law for causes of action that arose before 1 January 2014.

## The law

### 1. Truth (s 2 DA 2013), replacing justification
1. It is a defence for the defendant to show that the **imputation** conveyed by the statement is **substantially true** (s 2(1)). The burden is on the defendant, because falsity is presumed.
2. Motive is irrelevant. A defendant who did not know or care whether the statement was true, or who acted from spite, can still rely on truth. The one exception (textbook) is s 8 Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974: for statements about a *spent* conviction, truth is defeated by malice, meaning a dominant motive to injure. In *Greenstein v Campaign Against Antisemitism* [2021] EWCA Civ 1006 there was not enough evidence of malice.
3. **Substantial truth**: the defendant need not prove every detail, only the "sting": *Chase v News Group Newspapers* [2003] EMLR 11. At common law the justification had to be "as wide as the charge".
   - *Depp v News Group Newspapers* [2020] EWHC 2911 (QB): "wife beater" was substantially true where 12 of the 14 alleged incidents of violence were proved.
   - *Vardy v Rooney* [2022] EWHC 2017 (QB) ("Wagatha Christie"): truth succeeded.
4. **Multiple imputations** (s 2(2)-(3)): where a statement conveys two or more distinct imputations and some are not proved, the defence does not fail if, given the ones proved true, the unproved ones do not seriously harm the claimant's reputation.
   - Contrast the common law outcome in *Grobbelaar v News Group Newspapers* [2002] UKHL 40: the Sun alleged a goalkeeper took bribes to fix matches. Taking bribes was proved but match fixing was not. The sting was match fixing, so the Sun was liable, though only £1 damages was awarded.
5. Meaning sets the target. The claimant pleads meanings, and the defendant must prove the truth of a meaning that removes the sting. In *Riley v Murray* [2021] EWHC 3437 (QB) (textbook), truth failed where the defendant's quick tweet gave the worst reading of a deeply ambiguous tweet as though it were its only meaning.
6. s 2(4) abolishes common law justification and repeals s 5 Defamation Act 1952.

### 2. Honest opinion (s 3 DA 2013), replacing fair comment
The defendant must show three conditions. The claimant can then defeat the defence (fourth stage).
1. **Statement of opinion** (s 3(2)). This is decided at the meaning stage using the *Koutsogiannis* principles (see `defamation-elements.md`).
   - *British Chiropractic Association v Singh* [2010] EWCA Civ 350: saying the BCA "happily promotes bogus treatments" with "not a jot of evidence" was a value judgment about the evidence, i.e. opinion, not a factual claim that the BCA knew the treatments were bogus.
   - *Butt v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2019] EWCA Civ 933: calling someone an extremist (holding views contrary to British values) was opinion on the facts (the claim was later settled in the claimant's favour after it emerged his inclusion was a mistake).
   - See also *Millett v Corbyn* [2021] EWCA Civ 567.
2. **The basis of the opinion is indicated, in general or specific terms** (s 3(3)). This reflects *Joseph v Spiller* [2010] UKSC 53 (a dispute between a band and a booking agency). Calling conduct "dishonourable" is comment coupled with an allegation of unspecified conduct. The defendant must identify, at least in general terms, what led to the comment, but need not set out enough evidence for readers to judge the opinion for themselves. Steele notes Parliament adopted this conclusion in drafting s 3(3).
3. **An honest person could have held the opinion** on the basis of (a) any fact that existed when the statement was published, or (b) anything asserted as fact in a "privileged statement" published before it (s 3(4)). Under s 3(7), privileged statements are those protected by s 4, s 6, or ss 14-15 DA 1996. This test is objective. The defendant need not prove every supporting fact, just a sufficient true factual basis.
4. **Defeat** (s 3(5)-(6)): the defence is defeated if the *claimant* shows the defendant did not actually hold the opinion. Where the defendant publishes someone else's (the author's) opinion, it is defeated if the defendant knew or ought to have known the author did not hold it.
5. s 3(8) abolishes fair comment. Unlike the old defence, s 3 has no "public interest" requirement and does not use the concept of "malice". Steele traces the move from malice to honesty to Lord Nicholls in *Cheng v Tse Wai Chun* [2000] 4 HKC 1, who doubted that intent to injure should defeat fair comment, since many commentators want to influence opinion against their target.

### 3. Privilege
Some occasions are so important that speakers must be able to talk freely. On these occasions freedom of expression takes priority over reputation, even for a defamatory statement.

**(a) Absolute privilege.** This is complete protection, regardless of motive or falsity. Examples from the handout:
- parliamentary privilege under the Bill of Rights 1688 (MPs cannot be sued for what they say in the Commons);
- reports, papers, votes and proceedings ordered to be published by either House;
- contemporaneous reports of judicial proceedings (s 14 DA 1996, which s 7(1) DA 2013 extends to any UK court, any court abroad, and international courts and tribunals);
- statements in court proceedings, domestic and international;
- communications between certain officers of state.

**(b) Qualified privilege (mainly common law).** The categories are not closed.
1. The defence applies where the maker has an interest or a duty (legal, social or moral) to communicate, and the recipient has a corresponding interest or duty to receive: *Adam v Ward* [1917] AC 309. Reciprocity is essential.
2. It is defeated by **malice**. *Horrocks v Lowe* [1975] AC 135: a councillor accused the claimant at a council meeting of misleading the council. The occasion was privileged. If the defendant honestly believed the statement was true and was not reckless, dislike of the claimant, or wanting to damage them, is not malice unless that was the *dominant* motive. Lord Diplock (textbook): malice means either knowing falsehood (or recklessness as to truth) or a dominant improper motive. Proof that the defendant did not believe the statement is generally conclusive.
3. **Statutory qualified privilege**: s 15 and Sch 1 DA 1996 cover fair and accurate reports of, for example, public meetings, press conferences and notices from legislatures and governments. s 7 DA 2013 widened these to "anywhere in the world" and changed "public concern" to "public interest".
4. **Peer-reviewed statements (s 6 DA 2013)**: a new category. A statement in a scientific or academic journal is privileged if it relates to a scientific or academic matter and was independently reviewed for merit, before publication, by the editor and one or more experts. Reviewers' assessments published in the same journal and fair and accurate extracts or summaries are also covered. It is defeated by malice (s 6(6)).

### 4. Publication on a matter of public interest (s 4 DA 2013), replacing Reynolds
**Background: *Reynolds v Times Newspapers* [2001] 2 AC 127.** A former Taoiseach sued over an article saying he had deliberately and dishonestly misled the Irish parliament and cabinet. The House of Lords created a form of qualified privilege for responsible journalism on matters of public concern. But the defence failed on the facts: given how serious the allegation was, the omission of the claimant's side of the story was fatal. Lord Nicholls listed ten non-exhaustive factors:
1. seriousness of the allegation;
2. nature of the information (and how far it is a matter of public concern);
3. the source;
4. steps taken to verify;
5. status of the information;
6. urgency;
7. whether comment was sought from the claimant;
8. whether the article included the claimant's side;
9. tone;
10. circumstances and timing of publication.
- *Jameel v Wall Street Journal* [2006] UKHL 44 (textbook): the majority stressed the liberalising spirit of *Reynolds*. Lord Hoffmann warned that, in the hands of a judge hostile to that spirit, the ten factors could become ten hurdles.
- *Flood v Times Newspapers* [2012] UKSC 11: the defence protected the original publication (allegations of police corruption) but not its continued online publication after the claimant was cleared (the handout says "acquitted"). Steele notes that *Flood*'s remarks on editorial judgment are now reflected in s 4(4).
- **Reportage**: where the public interest lies in the *fact* that an allegation was made, not its truth, the duty to verify does not apply (*Roberts v Gable* [2008] QB 502; *Al-Fagih v HH Saudi Research* [2001] EWCA Civ 1634; *Charman v Orion* [2007] EWCA Civ 972). This does not cover reports that adopt or embellish the allegation (*Galloway v Telegraph Group* [2006] EWCA Civ 17). This is now in s 4(3).

**The statutory defence (s 4).**
- s 4(1): the defendant must show that (a) the statement was, or formed part of, a statement on a matter of public interest, and (b) the defendant reasonably believed that publishing it was in the public interest.
- s 4(2): the court must have regard to all the circumstances.
- s 4(3): for an accurate and impartial account of a dispute to which the claimant was a party, the court must disregard a failure to verify the imputation (reportage).
- s 4(4): the court must allow for editorial judgment as it considers appropriate.
- s 4(5): the defence covers both fact and opinion.
- s 4(6): the *Reynolds* defence is abolished.
- ***Serafin v Malkiewicz* [2020] UKSC 23** (Lord Wilson at [74]) sets out three elements: (i) the statement was on a matter of public interest; (ii) the defendant believed publishing it was in the public interest; (iii) that belief was reasonable. Parliament removed the list of *Reynolds*-based factors from the Bill and substituted "all the circumstances", so the *Reynolds* factors are **not a checklist**, though some may be relevant to whether the belief was reasonable ([69]). The Court approved *Economou*'s view that the rationale of s 4 is the same as *Reynolds*.
- ***Economou v de Freitas* [2018] EWCA Civ 2591**: the father of a woman prosecuted for perverting the course of justice (after she accused the claimant of rape) contributed to media coverage criticising the claimant and the CPS. The defence applied. *Reynolds* factors can be considered but are not exhaustive, and the reasonableness of belief must take account of the fact that the defendant was not a journalist. Steele notes the defence is not limited to the media.
- See also *Banks v Cadwalladr* [2022] EWHC 1417 (QB) and [2023] EWCA Civ 219 (the handout names the case without detail). *Lachaux* (2021 trial) is an example of the s 4 defence failing (textbook).

### 5. Secondary publishers and websites
**(a) Innocent dissemination, s 1 DA 1996.** This puts on a statutory basis, without abolishing, the common law protection for distributors such as libraries and newsagents. The defendant must show that they:
1. were not the author, editor or (commercial) publisher;
2. took reasonable care in relation to publication; and
3. did not know, and had no reason to believe, that what they did caused or contributed to publishing a defamatory statement.
s 1(3) excludes from author, editor or publisher anyone only involved in printing, distributing or selling, processing electronic media, broadcasting live without effective control over the speaker, or operating a communications system without effective control over the user. Protection ends once the defendant is put on notice. *Godfrey v Demon Internet* [2001] QB 201 (textbook): an ISP that failed to remove a posting after notice had published it and could not rely on s 1. In *Bunt v Tilley* the ISPs were not publishers at all, and were also protected by reg 13 of the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002.

**(b) s 10 DA 2013.** The court has no jurisdiction against a non-author, editor or publisher unless it is not reasonably practicable to sue the author, editor or publisher. Steele says this aims to remove the temptation to target "soft" distributors, which itself causes a chilling effect.

**(c) Website operators, s 5 DA 2013.**
- It is a defence for the operator to show it did not post the statement (s 5(2)).
- The defence is defeated if the claimant shows (a) it could not identify the poster (meaning it lacked enough information to sue them), (b) it gave the operator a notice of complaint, and (c) the operator failed to respond in line with the regulations (s 5(3)-(4)).
- It is also defeated by malice in relation to the posting (s 5(11)). Moderating posts does not by itself defeat it (s 5(12)).
- The Defamation (Operators of Websites) Regulations 2013 (SI 2013/3028) govern the notice procedure. As the handout summarises: if the poster cannot be contacted, the operator must take the material down on request; otherwise the poster decides whether to keep it up but must give contact details.

### 6. Offer of amends (ss 2-4 DA 1996, unchanged by the 2013 Act)
1. The offer must be in writing and must offer (a) a suitable correction and sufficient apology, (b) to publish them in a reasonable and practicable manner, and (c) to pay agreed or court-determined compensation and costs. The handout adds that the defendant must admit being wrong.
2. If accepted, the claim ends. If damages are not agreed, the court assesses them with a discount for the offer (textbook). Lord Judge LCJ in *KC v MGN Ltd (Costs)* [2013] EWCA Civ 3 said the aim is "vindication without litigation".
3. **Unaccepted offer as a defence (s 4)**: the fact that the offer was made is a defence. But there is no defence if the offeror knew or had reason to believe the statement referred to the claimant (or was likely to be understood that way) and was both false and defamatory. It is presumed, unless shown otherwise, that the offeror did not know. A defendant who relies on the offer cannot rely on any other defence.
   - The handout puts it as: available only for unintentional defamation, i.e. where the maker knew the statement referred to the claimant but honestly and reasonably believed it true, or in cases of mistaken identity.
   - *Milne v Express Newspapers* [2005] 1 WLR 772 (textbook): "reason to believe" in s 4(3) is not a negligence test. It requires a state of mind like *Horrocks* malice (knowledge of falsity or recklessness as to it), which is quite different from the due-care test in s 1.

### 7. Remedies
1. **Damages** have purposes beyond ordinary tort compensation: to vindicate and repair reputation, and (per the handout) to punish and deter. Damages are "at large", and exemplary damages are available. Historically juries set them, often at very high levels. The Court of Appeal stepped in (textbook):
   - *Sutcliffe v Pressdram* [1991] 1 QB 153: £600,000 to a convicted murderer's wife showed the jury did not grasp the value of money, so juries needed more guidance.
   - *Rantzen v MGN* [1994] QB 670: the court used its power under s 8 Courts and Legal Services Act 1990 to substitute awards, reading "excessive" in light of Article 10.
   - *John v MGN* [1997] QB 586: juries may be told about personal injury award levels, and counsel and judge may suggest a bracket. Bingham MR said juries had been like "sheep loosed on an unfenced common, with no shepherd".
   Juries are now rare (s 11 DA 2013).
2. **s 12 DA 2013**: when the claimant wins, the court may order the defendant to publish a **summary of the judgment**. The parties agree the wording, time and place, and failing agreement the court settles them. (The handout describes this as publishing "a copy of the decision". The statute refers to a summary.)
3. **s 13 DA 2013**: the court may order a website operator to remove the statement, or a non-author, editor or publisher to stop distributing, selling or exhibiting material containing it.
4. **Injunctions**: a final injunction can prevent repetition after the claimant wins at trial. **Interim** injunctions before trial are hard to get in defamation. Under *Bonnard v Perryman* [1891] 2 Ch 269 (as explained in *Hayden v Dickenson* [2020] EWHC 3291 (QB) at [52]), the court will not restrain publication if the defendant says it will defend the statement under ss 2-4 DA 2013 (truth, honest opinion, public interest). This is a higher threshold than the "likely to establish" test in s 12(3) HRA 1998, which governs privacy. A claimant cannot get round this by relabelling the claim: the court looks at whether the "nub" of the claim is protecting reputation.

## Key authorities
- DA 2013 s 2 (truth), s 3 (honest opinion), s 4 (public interest), s 5 (website operators), s 6 (peer-reviewed journals), s 7 (extends report privileges), s 10 (secondary publishers), s 11 (no jury), s 12 (summary of judgment), s 13 (removal orders).
- DA 1996 s 1 (innocent dissemination), ss 2-4 (offer of amends), ss 14-15 and Sch 1 (report privileges).
- *Chase v NGN* [2003]: substantial truth of the sting.
- *Grobbelaar v NGN* [2002] UKHL 40: common law approach to multiple stings; nominal damages.
- *Depp v NGN* [2020]; *Vardy v Rooney* [2022]: truth succeeded.
- *BCA v Singh* [2010] EWCA Civ 350: value judgment about scientific evidence is opinion.
- *Joseph v Spiller* [2010] UKSC 53: basis of the opinion need only be indicated in general terms; now s 3(3).
- *Butt v SSHD* [2019] EWCA Civ 933: "extremist" as opinion.
- *Adam v Ward* [1917] AC 309: reciprocal duty and interest.
- *Horrocks v Lowe* [1975] AC 135: malice means a dominant improper motive or knowing or reckless falsity.
- *Reynolds v Times Newspapers* [2001] 2 AC 127: responsible journalism; ten factors (abolished by s 4(6) but still relevant guidance).
- *Jameel v WSJ* [2006] UKHL 44: liberal application of *Reynolds*.
- *Flood v Times* [2012] UKSC 11: defence for original but not continued online publication; editorial judgment.
- *Serafin v Malkiewicz* [2020] UKSC 23: three elements of s 4; *Reynolds* factors not a checklist.
- *Economou v de Freitas* [2018] EWCA Civ 2591: s 4 available to non-journalists; reasonableness judged accordingly.
- *Roberts v Gable* [2008]; *Galloway v Telegraph* [2006]: reportage; now s 4(3).
- *Godfrey v Demon Internet* [2001]; *Bunt v Tilley* [2006]: ISPs and notice.
- *Milne v Express Newspapers* [2005]: s 4(3) DA 1996 state of mind.
- *John v MGN* [1997]: control of jury awards.
- *Bonnard v Perryman* [1891]; *Hayden v Dickenson* [2020]: rule against prior restraint in defamation.

## Debate and critique
- **Did the 2013 Act get the balance right?** The handout asks whether the Act changed the balance between reputation and speech, made defamation less of a one-sided tool for suppressing criticism, codified the common law clearly, and altered the tort's basic nature. Steele argues the statute mostly restates and builds on judicial developments (e.g. *Joseph v Spiller* in s 3(3), *Flood* in s 4(4), reportage in s 4(3)), with Parliament working "in partnership" with the courts and leaving key discretion, especially over "public interest", to judges. On this view the Act is evolutionary, not revolutionary. Mullis and Scott's title, "Tilting at Windmills" ((2014) 77 MLR 87), signals scepticism about the Act's effect. Steele uses their work to argue that reputation has social value too and that the reform debate neglected it.
- **Truth and Article 8.** Steele asks: if reputation falls under Article 8, why should true but trivial and damaging disclosures always be protected? Privacy law balances each case directly, but defamation's structure (truth is a complete defence whatever the motive) leaves no room for that question. This is a key contrast with misuse of private information.
- **Burden of proof on the defendant.** Presumed falsity is said to drive libel tourism and the chilling effect. The 2013 Act did not reverse the burden but tackled the effects in other ways (s 1, s 9, s 4).
- **From *Reynolds* to s 4.** Under *Reynolds*, judges made many decisions about proper journalistic conduct after the event, and the ten factors risked becoming "ten hurdles" (Lord Hoffmann in *Jameel*), giving editors little confidence before publishing. s 4 puts the focus on the defendant's reasonable belief and editorial judgment. But *Serafin* also said the underlying principles are the same as *Reynolds*, so how much has really changed? *Economou* extends the defence to citizen-speakers, which matters in the social media age.
- **Malice versus honesty.** Following *Cheng v Tse Wai Chun*, s 3 drops "malice" in favour of a subjective honesty test, while qualified privilege keeps *Horrocks* malice. Steele explains the reason: wanting to injure is not inconsistent with commentary, since persuading people is the point of comment.
- **Online intermediaries.** ss 5 and 10 with the 2013 Regulations aim to stop claimants targeting platforms and distributors instead of authors. But anonymous posters remain a problem, and the s 5 notice system relies on operators handling complaints properly.
- **Damages.** Libel awards have looked generous next to personal injury awards for intangible harm. The judicial reforms in *Rantzen* and *John* were controversial (Steele argues *Rantzen* misread Lord Goff's remarks in *AG v Guardian (No 2)*). Lord Hoffmann's later comments (Privy Council, *The Gleaner*) stress that reputation needs protecting, a point Steele says is "too readily forgotten" in the push for freer speech.
- **Remedies compared with privacy.** Defamation almost never allows interim injunctions (*Bonnard v Perryman*), whereas privacy often does (s 12(3) HRA). Steele (textbook ch 15) calls the different structures hard to justify, given that both protect Article 8 interests. *Hayden v Dickenson* polices the boundary by looking at the "nub" of the claim.

## Applying it to a problem question
Once you have established the elements (see `defamation-elements.md`), work through the defences in this order:
1. **Fact or opinion?** Decide this first, because it decides whether s 2 or s 3 is relevant. s 4 covers both.
2. **Truth (s 2).** What is the sting? Can the defendant prove it substantially true (*Chase*)? If there are several imputations, do the unproved ones cause serious harm once the proved ones are taken into account (s 2(3); contrast *Grobbelaar*)? Is it a spent conviction, so that malice matters?
3. **Honest opinion (s 3).** Is it recognisable as opinion (*Singh*, *Butt*)? Does it indicate its basis, even generally (*Joseph v Spiller*)? Could an honest person hold it on facts existing at publication or in a prior privileged statement? Can the claimant show the defendant did not hold it, or for a republished opinion, that the defendant knew or ought to have known the author did not?
4. **Privilege.** Is the occasion absolutely privileged (Parliament, court proceedings, contemporaneous court reports)? If not, is there a reciprocal duty and interest (*Adam v Ward*), such as an employer's reference or a report to police or regulators? If so, is there malice in the *Horrocks* sense? Is it a fair and accurate report under Sch 1 DA 1996, or a peer-reviewed article (s 6)?
5. **Public interest (s 4).** Is the subject of public interest? Did the defendant actually believe publication was in the public interest, and was that belief reasonable in all the circumstances? Use *Reynolds* factors as guidance only (*Serafin*): verification, seeking comment, tone, urgency, source. Is it neutral reportage of a dispute (s 4(3))? Allow for editorial judgment (s 4(4)). Is the defendant a non-journalist (*Economou*)? For online archives, has the position changed since first publication (*Flood*)?
6. **Secondary defendants.** Is the defendant a platform or distributor? Consider s 10 (can the author be sued?), s 5 (website operators: was the poster identifiable, was a notice given, was there a response?), and s 1 DA 1996 (reasonable care, no reason to believe, notice).
7. **Offer of amends.** Did the defendant make a qualifying written offer? If refused, can the defendant rely on it, remembering that it then cannot use any other defence, and the *Milne* test on knowledge or reason to believe?
8. **Remedies.** Damages (vindication; aggravating factors; possible exemplary damages), an order to publish a summary of the judgment (s 12), removal or cessation orders (s 13), and a final injunction. For an *interim* injunction, remember *Bonnard v Perryman*: if the defendant intends to plead truth, honest opinion or public interest, an injunction is very unlikely, and relabelling the claim as privacy will not help if its nub is reputation (*Hayden*).

**Traps:** running "truth" when the statement is opinion (or the reverse); forgetting the claimant bears the burden of defeating honest opinion under s 3(5); treating *Reynolds* factors as a checklist; missing that an offer of amends defence excludes all others; assuming a platform is a publisher before notice (*Bunt*) or protected after notice (*Godfrey*, now s 5); forgetting malice defeats qualified privilege but is irrelevant to truth (except for spent convictions).
