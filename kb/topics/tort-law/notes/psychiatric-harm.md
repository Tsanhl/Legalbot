# Psychiatric Harm (Duty of Care)

## Overview
Psychiatric harm is a form of personal injury: a mental illness or disturbance brought about by the defendant's careless act or omission. English law (England & Wales) is willing to compensate it, but it treats "pure" psychiatric harm (harm not accompanied by physical injury) far more cautiously than physical injury. A set of "control mechanisms" decides who is owed a duty: first, the distinction between ordinary grief and a recognised psychiatric illness; second, the distinction between primary and secondary victims; and, for secondary victims, the restrictive Alcock criteria. This topic matters because it is one of the clearest examples of the courts openly drawing policy lines (some of which the House of Lords itself has called arbitrary), and so it is fertile ground for both problem questions and critical essays.

the materials places psychiatric harm within the wider duty of care framework: when judges decide whether a duty is owed they look at (i) how the harm was caused (positive act, omission, third party), (ii) the type of defendant (e.g. a public body), and (iii) the type of harm (physical or psychiatric). This note deals with the third question.

## The law

### 1. Threshold: a recognised psychiatric illness, not mere distress
- The claimant must prove a **positive, recognised psychiatric illness**. Normal emotions such as grief, distress, anxiety or sorrow, however intense, are not actionable. Lord Bridge in *McLoughlin v O'Brian* [1983] 1 AC 410 said that the first hurdle is to show "not merely grief, distress or any other normal emotion, but a positive psychiatric illness" (paraphrased; see the textbook extract). He also noted that anxiety neurosis or reactive depression may qualify as recognisable illnesses.
- So the materials's first distinction is: mere emotional suffering (no liability) versus recognised psychiatric injury or illness (liability possible).

### 2. Why the law restricts recovery: the policy reasons
Lord Steyn in *White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1999] 2 AC 455 summarised the arguments for restricting recovery (as set out in the materials):
1. It is hard to draw a line between acute grief and psychiatric illness, and psychiatric diagnosis is more uncertain (fear of sham claims, doubts about causation).
2. Wider availability of compensation might discourage rehabilitation.
3. The class of possible claimants would grow sharply (the "floodgates" argument).
4. It may be unfair to defendants (and to insurers and ultimately all policy holders) to impose liability out of proportion to the negligent conduct.

### 3. Primary victims
A primary victim is someone directly involved in the incident as a participant, rather than as a passive witness of harm to others (Lord Oliver's distinction in *Alcock*).

**(a) Physically injured claimants.** Those who suffer physical injury routinely recover for associated mental effects, including psychiatric illness (textbook).

**(b) Claimants in the zone of physical danger.**
- *Dulieu v White* [1901] 2 KB 669: a claimant may recover for illness caused by shock arising from a reasonable fear of immediate personal injury to oneself. Kennedy J limited this to fear for one's own safety.
- *Page v Smith* [1996] AC 155 (HL): where it is reasonably foreseeable that the defendant's negligence might cause the claimant physical injury, the claimant can recover for psychiatric injury even if psychiatric injury itself was not foreseeable and even though no physical injury in fact occurred. Physical and psychiatric injury are treated as one kind of damage ("personal injury"). Foreseeability is judged prospectively, at the time of the negligence (not with hindsight).
- In *Page v Smith*, Lord Lloyd said the test in every case is whether the defendant can reasonably foresee that his conduct will expose the claimant to a risk of personal injury, and referred to the Limitation Act 1980 s 38(1) definition of personal injuries, which includes "any impairment of a person's physical or mental condition".

**(c) The eggshell (thin) skull rule.** A defendant is liable for the full extent of the harm caused even if the claimant suffers more than expected because of a pre-existing weakness (*Brice v Brown* [1984] 1 All ER 997, per the materials). For primary victims, this means an unusually vulnerable claimant can still recover (as in *Page v Smith*, where the claimant's pre-existing ME was exacerbated by a moderate collision).

**(d) Primary victims not physically endangered.** The textbook stresses there is a wide range of cases in which a duty in respect of psychiatric harm is recognised without any physical danger:
- *Stress at work*: *Walker v Northumberland County Council* [1995] 1 All ER 737 (employer liable for second breakdown, once it was foreseeable); *Hatton v Sutherland* [2002] EWCA Civ 76 guidelines (approved in *Barber v Somerset County Council* [2004] UKHL 13): no special control mechanisms; the threshold question is whether this kind of harm to this particular employee was reasonably foreseeable; an employer can usually assume the employee can withstand normal job pressures unless it knows of a particular vulnerability. *BAE Systems v Konczak* [2017] EWCA Civ 1188 on apportioning divisible harm.
- *Assumption of responsibility and contractual relationships*: *Leach v Chief Constable of Gloucestershire* [1999] 1 WLR 1421 (an "appropriate adult" in interviews of a serial killer; possible duty regarding counselling and false assurances); *McLoughlin v Jones* [2002] QB 1312 (solicitor and client); *Swinney v Chief Constable of Northumbria* [1997] QB 464 (police informer).
- *Birth cases*: *YAH v Medway NHS Foundation Trust* [2018] EWHC 2964 (QB): a mother whose child was injured during birth was a primary victim, because mother and child are treated as one until birth, so she was endangered; *Page v Smith* was taken to show that "shock" is not essential for a primary victim. The textbook also notes *A (A Child) v Calderdale and Huddersfield NHS Foundation Trust* [2017] EWHC 824 (QB) and *Zeromska-Smith v United Lincolnshire Hospitals NHS Trust* [2019] EWHC 980 (QB) (stillbirth). the materials lists YAH as a "compare" case against the eggshell skull/zone-of-danger approach.

**(e) Limits: fear of future harm.** *Rothwell v Chemical & Insulating Co Ltd* [2007] UKHL 39: employees with pleural plaques after asbestos exposure could not recover; one claimant who developed an anxiety neurosis about future disease was owed no duty. *Page v Smith* was distinguished as concerning an event (a collision) that had actually happened, not apprehension that a feared event might happen in the future. Lord Hoffmann applied a presumption of ordinary fortitude (borrowed from the stress-at-work cases), which suggests at least that control device can apply to primary victims outside *Page v Smith*.

### 4. Secondary victims
A secondary victim suffers psychiatric injury as a result of witnessing (or perceiving) someone else being injured, killed or endangered; they are not directly involved in the accident.

**Step 1: foreseeability of psychiatric injury in a person of ordinary fortitude.** It must be foreseeable that a person of "ordinary fortitude" (or "customary phlegm") would suffer a recognised psychiatric injury in the circumstances (*Bourhill v Young* [1943] AC 92 (HL)). The textbook highlights three departures from normal foreseeability:
1. It is an exception to the eggshell skull rule: an unusually susceptible secondary victim may not be owed a duty.
2. It is psychiatric injury itself that must be foreseeable (not just personal injury).
3. Foreseeability is judged with hindsight, on the events as they actually happened ("specific foreseeability"), as Lord Wright suggested in *Bourhill* and Lord Lloyd explained in *Page v Smith*.

**Step 2: the Alcock control mechanisms** (*Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1992] 1 AC 310 (HL), Hillsborough relatives). Building on Lord Wilberforce in *McLoughlin v O'Brian*:
1. **Close tie of love and affection with the immediate victim.** Presumed (rebuttably) for parent/child and spouses; others, even siblings, must prove it (one of the Alcock claimants was a fiancée). Lord Ackner accepted that some relatives or friends may have ties comparable to those of a parent or spouse. In *Alcock*, a man who lost two brothers and another who lost a brother-in-law failed because no special closeness was proved.
2. **Proximity in time and space** to the accident or its **immediate aftermath**. Mrs McLoughlin, who reached the hospital within about an hour or two and saw her family before they were cleaned up, was within the margin; identifying a relative in the mortuary about eight hours later in *Alcock* was not.
3. **Direct perception** of the event or its immediate aftermath with one's own unaided senses. Being told by a third party is not enough. Live television broadcasts in *Alcock* did not count because broadcasting guidelines prevented showing identifiable individuals suffering (a breach might even be a novus actus); the House left open that some live broadcasts might suffice (the balloon example).
4. **Sudden shock.** The illness must be caused by a sudden, shocking event, i.e. a "sudden appreciation" of an objectively horrifying event, rather than a gradual process.

**Later applications of the shock and proximity requirements** (textbook):
- *Liverpool Women's Hospital NHS Foundation Trust v Ronayne* [2015] EWCA Civ 588: a series of clinical events leading to a relative's serious illness did not qualify; the requirement was described as "arbitrary and pragmatic".
- *Young v MacVean* [2015] CSIH 70 (Scotland): a gradual dawning realisation at the aftermath was insufficient.
- *King v Royal United Hospitals Bath NHS Foundation Trust* [2021] EWHC 1576 (QB): events recognised as horrifying were still not an objectively shocking event in the Alcock sense.
- *Taylor v A Novo* [2013] EWCA Civ 194: claimant witnessed her mother's collapse and death some weeks after a workplace accident; no recovery, because the claimant was not proximate to the original accident.
- *Paul v Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust* [2022] EWCA Civ 12: the Court of Appeal felt bound by *Taylor v Novo* to reject clinical negligence secondary victim claims where the death occurred later, separate from the negligence, but said that on a clean sheet it would have seen force in allowing them, and that the issue was for the Supreme Court. (The sources available here do not record any later Supreme Court decision; check the current position.)
- Bystanders: Lord Ackner in *Alcock* did not rule out a claim by a bystander to an exceptionally horrific event (petrol tanker crashing into a school), but in *McFarlane v EE Caledonia* [1994] 2 All ER 1 the Court of Appeal refused to undermine the control devices in this way.

### 5. Rescuers, employees and involuntary participants
- **Rescuers.** *White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1999] 2 AC 455: police officers who helped at Hillsborough were not primary victims merely because they were rescuers or employees. A rescuer who was not exposed to (and did not reasonably believe they were exposed to) risk of physical injury is a secondary victim and must satisfy Alcock. *Chadwick v British Railways Board* [1967] 1 WLR 912 was reinterpreted as a case where the rescuer was himself within the range of foreseeable physical injury. The employment relationship did not change this. Lord Goff dissented, saying this introduced a new and artificial control mechanism.
- *Monk v Harrington* [2008] EWHC 1879 (QB) (cited in the textbook as [2008] EWHC Civ 1879 (QB); [2009] PIQR P3): a site foreman who crawled under a fallen platform to help was a rescuer but not physically endangered or reasonably fearful, so not a primary victim; his belief in his own responsibility was unreasonable.
- **Involuntary participants.** Lord Oliver in *Alcock* treated as primary victims those whom the defendant's negligence puts in the position of being, or believing they are about to be or have been, the involuntary cause of another's death or injury: *Dooley v Cammell Laird & Co Ltd* [1951] 1 Lloyd's Rep 271 (crane operator whose load fell towards fellow workers because of a fault). Lord Hoffmann in *White* left this category open.

## Key authorities
- *Dulieu v White* [1901] 2 KB 669: pregnant barmaid shocked when a van crashed into the pub; recovery for illness caused by reasonable fear for one's own safety; early recognition of nervous shock claims.
- *Bourhill v Young* [1943] AC 92 (HL): source of the "ordinary fortitude/customary phlegm" requirement for those not physically endangered.
- *McLoughlin v O'Brian* [1983] 1 AC 410 (HL): mother arrived at hospital about two hours after the crash and saw her injured family; recovery under the "immediate aftermath" extension; Lord Wilberforce's three elements (class of persons, proximity, means of shock); Lord Bridge preferred a foreseeability-based, case-by-case approach (later abandoned).
- *Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1992] 1 AC 310 (HL): Hillsborough relatives; all claims failed; leading authority for the secondary victim control mechanisms.
- *Page v Smith* [1996] AC 155 (HL): minor collision, no physical injury, exacerbation of ME; primary victim within the zone of physical danger recovers for psychiatric harm without proof that psychiatric harm was foreseeable.
- *White v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police* [1999] 2 AC 455 (HL): police officers at Hillsborough; rescuers and employees not physically endangered are secondary victims; Lord Steyn's policy list and "thus far and no further"; Lord Hoffmann on distributive justice.
- *Rothwell v Chemical & Insulating Co Ltd* [2007] UKHL 39: anxiety and psychiatric illness from fear of future asbestos disease not recoverable; *Page v Smith* confined.
- *Liverpool Women's Hospital NHS Foundation Trust v Ronayne* [2015] EWCA Civ 588: no "sudden shock" from a sequence of hospital events; requirement is arbitrary and pragmatic.
- *YAH v Medway NHS Trust* [2018] EWHC 2964 (QB): mother of a child injured at birth treated as a primary victim.
- *Brice v Brown* [1984] 1 All ER 997: eggshell skull/personality principle (lecture).
- *Dooley v Cammell Laird* [1951] 1 Lloyd's Rep 271: involuntary participant recovered.
- *Hatton v Sutherland* [2002] EWCA Civ 76 / *Barber v Somerset CC* [2004] UKHL 13: guidelines for occupational stress claims.
- Limitation Act 1980 s 38(1): "personal injuries" includes impairment of mental condition (relied on by Lord Lloyd in *Page v Smith*).

## Debate and critique
- **Arbitrariness openly admitted.** The House of Lords in *White* accepted that the control devices are arbitrary and cannot be fully justified by principle. Lord Steyn said the only sensible strategy was "thus far and no further"; Lord Hoffmann said the search for principle had been "called off" in *Alcock* and that the task was a practical attempt to keep the law looking fair between citizens. The textbook reads this as an abandonment of incremental common law development (and of the Caparo approach) for this category.
- **Distributive justice (Lord Hoffmann in *White*).** It would offend ordinary people's sense of fairness for police officers to recover when bereaved relatives in *Alcock* were sent away with nothing. Lord Goff's dissent replied that tort compensates only where responsibility is established, so it is misleading to talk of one class being "better off"; each claim should be judged by the same principles.
- **The close-ties requirement.** Jane Stapleton ("In Restraint of Tort", in Birks (ed), *The Frontiers of Liability* (1994)) argued that turning brothers of Hillsborough victims away for having "only" brotherly love is disreputable, and that it is grotesque for relatives to have to prove their love while defendants attack it. The textbook adds that citing "Cain and Abel" against a presumption of fraternal affection added insult to injury. S Hedley ("Morbid Musings of the Reasonable Chief Constable" [1992] CLJ 16) pointed out that a defendant could never actually foresee the relationships of those watching, so "foreseeability" here is really a control device.
- **Weir's view.** Tony Weir (Introduction to Tort Law) argues the restrictions reflect both a technical need to limit claimants and a value judgment that mental harm is treated as less serious than physical harm. He calls *Page v Smith* a "very dubious decision", criticises the courts' attempts to widen liability (close loving relationship; "immediate aftermath") as making things worse, and argues that clear, even artificial, rules are better here because uncertainty prolongs claimants' distress. He is sceptical of the Law Commission's reform proposals.
- **Page v Smith under pressure.** Bailey and Nolan, "The Page v Smith Saga: A Tale of Inauspicious Origins and Unintended Consequences" (2010) 69 CLJ 495 (lecture reading). The textbook notes Lord Goff in *White* criticised *Page v Smith*'s generous foreseeability rule; others criticise its apparently narrow definition of primary victim (physical endangerment), which *White* adopted although Lord Lloyd may not have meant it to be exclusive. After *Rothwell*, the textbook suggests *Alcock* (all control devices) and *Page v Smith* (none) may become isolated special cases, with "specific foreseeability" as a general control device elsewhere.
- **Physical v psychiatric distinction.** Lord Wilberforce in *McLoughlin* and Kennedy J in *Dulieu* recognised that mind and body cannot be cleanly separated. The textbook argues that the real problem is often not the type of harm but the absence of a physical mechanism of causation; the broad range of primary victim cases (stress, assumption of responsibility) suggests nothing is intrinsically problematic about psychiatric harm as such.
- **Ordinary fortitude and bias.** The textbook queries whether the "ordinary fortitude" standard hides male bias (what fortitude is expected of a pregnant woman?) and what reaction to colleagues' violent deaths is "ordinary" (*McFarlane*).
- **Rescuers and helpers.** *Monk v Harrington* forces claimants to argue they feared for their own safety rather than that they were appalled by what they saw at close quarters, which the textbook questions.
- **Clinical negligence.** *Ronayne*, *King*, *Taylor v Novo* and *Paul* show the shock and proximity rules sit uneasily with deaths following medical negligence; in *Paul* the Court of Appeal admitted there was "no logical reason" for the rules it felt bound to apply.
- **Further reading listed in the materials:** Conaghan & Mansell, *The Wrongs of Tort* (1999) 34-44; Giliker (2000) 20 LS 19 on damages for mental distress; Mullany and Handford, *Tort Liability for Psychiatric Damage* (2nd edn 2006); Teff, *Causing Psychiatric and Emotional Harm* (2009); Nolan on *Alcock* in *Landmark Cases in the Law of Tort* (2010); Case (2010) Tort L Rev 33 on *White*; Mulheron (2008) 19 KLJ 81 on the "patchwork quilt" of primary victims. The textbook also refers to Law Com No 249, *Liability for Psychiatric Illness* (1998).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there a recognised psychiatric illness?** Look for a diagnosis (PTSD, depression, anxiety neurosis). Grief, upset or fear alone: no claim.
2. **Was the claimant physically injured or within the zone of physical danger?** If physical injury was reasonably foreseeable, treat as a primary victim under *Page v Smith*: no need to show psychiatric harm was foreseeable, no ordinary-fortitude requirement, eggshell skull applies (*Brice v Brown*). Check whether the fear was reasonable (*Dulieu*).
3. **Is it a fear-of-the-future case?** If the feared harm has not happened (exposure to risk only), *Rothwell* bars the claim.
4. **Is there another primary-victim route?** Employer and stress (*Hatton* guidelines: foreseeability for this employee, signs of vulnerability); assumption of responsibility or contract (*Leach*, *McLoughlin v Jones*); mother in birth cases (*YAH*); involuntary participant who believes they caused the death (*Dooley*).
5. **If secondary victim, apply each step:**
   - Ordinary fortitude: would a person of normal resilience foreseeably suffer psychiatric illness, judged with hindsight? An unusually vulnerable claimant fails at this point.
   - Close tie: spouse, parent, child presumed (rebuttable, e.g. estrangement); sibling, friend, colleague must prove it.
   - Proximity: present at the scene or the immediate aftermath? Measure time (one to two hours in *McLoughlin* versus eight hours in *Alcock*) and whether the victim was still in the state caused by the accident.
   - Own unaided perception: told by phone or third party fails; TV coverage usually fails unless it shows something as impactful as direct sight.
   - Sudden shock: a single horrifying event versus gradual realisation or a sequence of hospital events (*Ronayne*); a later collapse separate from the original negligence (*Taylor v Novo*, *Paul*).
6. **Rescuer?** Was the rescuer actually in danger or reasonably believed so? If not, apply Alcock (*White*; *Monk*).
7. **Then check breach, causation and remoteness** as usual; in stress claims consider apportionment of divisible harm (*Konczak*).

**Typical traps:** treating a rescuer as automatically a primary victim; forgetting that the ordinary fortitude rule does not apply to primary victims; assuming spouses always win (presumption is rebuttable); counting the hospital visit days later as "immediate aftermath"; ignoring that the claimant must have a recognised illness. **Facts that change the outcome:** timing of arrival; how the claimant learned of the event; whether the claimant was themselves at risk; evidence of the relationship; whether the event was a single sudden incident.
