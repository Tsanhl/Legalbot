# Duty of Care – Public Bodies (Fire, Ambulance, Police)

## Overview
Public bodies are state-funded organisations such as local councils, education authorities and emergency services. Commentary focuses on the fire service, the ambulance service and the police. In England and Wales they are generally subject to the same negligence principles as private persons. They are liable when their positive acts directly cause personal injury or property damage, or make a situation worse. They are generally not liable for failing to confer a benefit, for example failing to prevent harm caused by fires, illness or criminals, unless an exception such as assumption of responsibility applies. The police cases (*Hill*, *Smith*, *Michael*, *Robinson*) are the most contested, and the HRA 1998 now provides a parallel, sometimes wider, route to redress.

## The law

### 1. General principles
1. **Positive acts.** A duty generally arises where a public body, exercising its powers, negligently causes personal injury or property damage by direct action, or makes the situation worse (*East Suffolk Rivers Catchment Board v Kent* [1941]).
2. **No duty to confer benefits.** Outside that, there is a general rule against a duty of care. Commentary this is the one area where the defendant's status as a public body shapes whether a duty exists. Lord Reed in *Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25 put it differently. Like private persons, public bodies do not generally owe a duty to confer benefits on individuals, for example by protecting them from harm (*Sheppard v Glossop Corpn* [1921]; *East Suffolk*). A duty to protect may still arise where the body created the source of danger or assumed responsibility to protect the claimant (*Dorset Yacht*, as explained in *Gorringe* at [39]).
3. **Same principles as private persons.** Public authorities are generally subject to the same principles of negligence as private individuals, "except to the extent that legislation requires a departure" (*Poole*, summarising *Robinson* and *Michael*). Lord Hoffmann in *Stovin v Wise*: for positive acts, public authority liability is "in principle the same as that of a private person but may be restricted by its statutory powers and duties".
4. **Statutory powers do not create duties to act.**
   - *Stovin v Wise* [1996] AC 923 and *Gorringe v Calderdale* [2004] UKHL 15: the mere existence of a statutory power, or of a broad, unenforceable public-law duty, cannot ground a common law duty to take positive action. Some independent common law reason is needed.
   - *Stovin*: a highway authority knew of a dangerous junction where a bank obscured visibility, had agreed it should be removed, but did nothing further. It owed no duty to remove the bank. Lord Nicholls dissented.
   - *Gorringe*: no duty to repaint "SLOW" on the road before the brow of a hill.
   - Lord Hoffmann in *Gorringe* at [38]: this does not apply where the authority has actually done acts, entered relationships or undertaken responsibilities. For example, a hospital's duty rests on accepting a professional relationship with the patient.
   - Lord Steyn in *Gorringe* separated two questions. For breach of statutory duty: does the statute show an intention to create a private remedy? For negligence against a statutory background: does the statute *exclude* a private law remedy?
   - The policy/operational distinction used under *Anns* was rejected in *Stovin* (Lord Reed in *Poole*).

### 2. Fire service
- **No duty to respond** to an emergency call, nor to arrive and try to fight a fire.
- **Once it attends, it must not positively make things worse.** There is no duty where the claimant is worse off merely because the fire service failed to do something. This position is shaped by wider policy concerns. Compare:
  - *Capital & Counties plc v Hampshire CC* [1997]: the Hampshire action, where liability followed from the fire officer turning off the sprinkler system so the fire spread and destroyed the building;
  - *John Munroe (Acrylics) v London Fire and Civil Defence Authority* [1997];
  - *Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints v West Yorkshire Fire and Civil Defence Authority* [1997].
- Steele: in *Capital & Counties* the Court of Appeal held that a fire service attending a fire was not in sufficient proximity with the owner to owe a duty. Its duties were owed to the public at large. This was supported by *Alexandrou v Oxford* [1993] 4 All ER 328 (no duty from police attending a burglar alarm). Liability followed only where the brigade turned off the sprinklers, the owners' own defence, so the reasoning followed *East Suffolk*.
- Lord Hoffmann in *Gorringe* approved *Capital & Counties*. The general public-law duty to provide efficient fire-fighting services under s 1 Fire Services Act 1947 did not create a common law duty.

### 3. Ambulance service
- ***Kent v Griffiths* [2001] QB 36 (CA)**: the ambulance service is part of the NHS, which owes a recognised duty of care to patients as individuals. It is therefore distinguishable from the fire service. The duty arises once the service **accepts the call** by the individual. It includes a duty to respond and to respond without carelessness.
- *Facts* (Steele, Weir): an ambulance failed to arrive promptly, without convincing explanation. The patient suffered a severe attack that timely arrival would probably have prevented. The trial judge found the crew had even tried to falsify the records.
- In *Michael*, Lord Toulson distinguished *Kent v Griffiths* because there "the call handler gave misleading assurances that an ambulance would be arriving shortly" (reliance and assumption of responsibility).

### 4. Police
1. **Positive acts causing physical injury: duty owed.** *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4. Police owe a duty when, by direct and positive action, they cause personal injury or property damage. This is an established category of liability for positive negligent conduct that foreseeably and directly causes physical injury. Lord Reed: there is "no general rule that the police are not under any duty of care when discharging their function of preventing and investigating crime". They owe a duty where ordinary principles impose one, unless statute or the common law provides otherwise.
2. **Failing to protect from third parties: generally no duty.** The police are not normally under a duty to protect individuals from a danger they did not themselves create, including injury by third parties, absent special circumstances such as an assumption of responsibility (*Robinson*). They may owe a duty to protect someone from a danger they *created*, including one from human agency (*Dorset Yacht*; *Hartwell*).
3. **Operational negligence vs policy.** Liability may attach to operational negligence, meaning the way a job is done. There is no liability for policy matters, such as how police prioritise investigations and allocate resources.
4. **Investigation and suppression of crime.**
   - *Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [1989]: police owe a general duty to society to investigate and suppress crime, but this is not a private duty to particular individuals.
   - The courts' stance against a duty has been strong even where the victim was identifiable, almost a blanket protection for how police conduct investigations: *Smith v Chief Constable of Sussex* [2008]; *Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales* [2015].
   - The dissents of Lord Kerr and Lady Hale in *Michael* leave the door open for future claimants, especially in domestic violence cases.
5. **Duties beyond positive acts (assumption of responsibility).** *Swinney v Chief Constable of Northumbria* [1997] (police and informants, an example of assumption of responsibility in Steele); *Reeves v Commissioner of Police* [2000] (custody/suicide); *Costello v Chief Constable of Northumbria* [1999] (duty to assist a colleague).

### 5. Human Rights Act 1998 dimension
- Under the HRA, public authorities (including the police) may be directly liable for breaching Convention rights (ss 6–8).
- *Positive operational duty under Article 2* (from *Osman v UK*): there is a breach where the authorities knew or ought to have known of "a real and immediate risk to the life of an identified individual" from a third party's criminal acts and failed to take reasonable measures within their powers (Lord Dyson in *Rabone v Pennine Care NHS Trust* [2012] UKSC 2, quoting *Osman*).
- *Article 3 investigative duty*: in *D v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2018] UKSC 11, the Worboys victims recovered HRA compensation because serious *operational* failures in investigating their assaults breached the Article 3 investigative duty, even though no negligence duty would lie.
- HRA claims are not tort claims. They have a one-year limit (extendable) and discretionary damages following Strasbourg principles. Courts have refused to "gold plate" Convention rights by building matching negligence duties (Lord Toulson, *Michael*). Lord Brown in *Van Colle/Smith* said developing the common law was "simply unnecessary" given the HRA remedy.

## Key authorities
- ***East Suffolk Rivers Catchment Board v Kent* [1941] AC 74**: a public body is liable for making things worse, not for failing to improve. Its principle was rejected in *Anns* and is now revived (*Robinson*, *Poole*).
- ***Stovin v Wise* [1996] AC 923** and ***Gorringe v Calderdale* [2004] UKHL 15**: a statutory power or broad public duty does not itself found a duty to act. Lord Reed in *Poole* gave these cases wide significance. Steele argues they decide only that narrower point.
- ***Capital & Counties plc v Hampshire CC* [1997] QB 1004 (CA)**: the fire service owes no duty to fight a fire effectively, but is liable for positively making it worse (turning off the sprinklers).
- ***John Munroe v London Fire and Civil Defence Authority* [1997]** and ***Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints v West Yorkshire Fire and Civil Defence Authority* [1997]**: listed for comparison with *Capital & Counties*. Weir explains that of several fire claims, the only one to succeed was where the brigade made things worse.
- ***Kent v Griffiths* [2001] QB 36 (CA)**: the ambulance service owes a duty once it accepts a call.
- ***Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [1989] 1 AC 53 (HL)**:
  - *Facts*: the claimant's daughter was the last victim of serial killer Peter Sutcliffe. The claim alleged a negligent police investigation.
  - *Held*: struck out. Lord Keith gave two independent reasons. First, **no proximity**: the killer was unknown, and the victim was not at special risk merely by being young and female, since "all females" are potential victims of a habitual rapist. Second, **public policy**:
    - liability would not improve police standards;
    - it might lead to defensive policing;
    - it would draw courts into policy and discretionary decisions about lines of inquiry and resources;
    - it would divert police manpower to defending claims;
    - closed investigations would have to be reopened.
  - Lord Keith likened this to the barristers' immunity in *Rondel v Worsley* (later abolished by *Arthur JS Hall v Simons* [2002]).
- ***Osman v Ferguson* [1993] 4 All ER 344 (CA)** / ***Osman v UK* [1999] 1 FLR 193**:
  - *Facts*: a teacher became obsessed with a pupil, harassed the family, and eventually shot the boy and killed his father. The police knew of his conduct.
  - *Court of Appeal*: arguable proximity, but the claim was struck out by applying *Hill*'s policy.
  - *Strasbourg*: applying *Hill* as a "watertight" blanket immunity was a disproportionate restriction on access to court, breaching Article 6(1).
- ***Z v UK* [2001] 2 FLR 612**: the Strasbourg court accepted it had misunderstood the role of policy in negligence in *Osman*. No-duty findings are not "immunities" and do not breach Article 6.
- ***Brooks v Commissioner of Police for the Metropolis* [2005] UKHL 24**:
  - *Facts*: Duwayne Brooks witnessed the racist murder of Stephen Lawrence and was attacked himself. He claimed the police owed him duties as a victim and key witness.
  - *Held*: no duty. Lord Steyn said *Hill* should be reformulated as an **absence of duty** rather than a blanket immunity, and that not every remark in *Hill* survives ("a more sceptical approach" to public functions is now needed). But *Hill*'s "core principle" stands: general duties to victims and witnesses would lead to an unduly defensive approach.
  - Counsel conceded that *Hedley Byrne*-type assumptions of responsibility fall outside *Hill*.
  - *Robinson* later recast *Brooks* as a positive-act case denied for other reasons.
- ***Van Colle v Chief Constable of Hertfordshire Police; Smith v Chief Constable of Sussex Police* [2008] UKHL 50**:
  - *Van Colle*: an HRA-only claim by a prosecution witness who was murdered. It failed because there was no real and immediate risk to life on the *Osman* test.
  - *Smith*: a common law claim only. The claimant told the police his former partner had made explicit threats, and was then seriously injured by him. The majority held **no duty**: Lord Hope said the *Hill*/*Brooks* principle is a matter of public policy applied generally, and Lord Brown said class-based exclusion is better.
  - **Lord Bingham dissented**, proposing a "liability principle". If a member of the public gives an officer apparently credible evidence that a third party, whose identity and whereabouts are known, presents a specific and imminent threat to his life or physical safety, the officer owes a duty to take reasonable steps to assess the threat and, if appropriate, prevent it.
- ***Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police* [2015] UKSC 2**:
  - *Facts*: Ms Michael rang 999 saying her former partner had found her with another man and would return to hit her. She also said he threatened to kill her, but the call handler did not log this. The call was passed between forces and downgraded from a 5-minute to a 60-minute response. Fifteen minutes later she called again, screaming, and was found stabbed to death.
  - *Majority (Lord Toulson)*: **no duty in negligence**. This was an omissions case, governed by the general principle that D is not liable for harm caused by T absent control or assumption of responsibility. It was not special treatment for police. The same applies to regulators, building control and highway authorities (*Yuen Kun Yeu*, *Davis v Radcliffe*, *Murphy*, *Stovin*, *Gorringe*).
  - No assumption of responsibility arose: the handler promised only to pass the call on and asked her to keep her phone free.
  - The court refused to develop the common law to match Articles 2 and 3, or to "gold plate" Convention rights.
  - The HRA claim was allowed to go to trial.
  - **Lord Kerr dissented**, on the basis of proximity and finding the *Hill*/*Brooks* policy reasons not compelling (a *Caparo*-based approach). Baroness Hale also dissented, relying on the *D v East Berkshire* analogy (Steele).
- ***Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4**: positive acts causing injury in the course of an arrest give rise to a duty under ordinary principles. *Hill* and *Smith* were reinterpreted as omissions cases. *Calveley*, *Elguzouli-Daf* and *Brooks* were positive-act cases where a duty was denied for other reasons. Facts are in duty-of-care-principles.md.
- ***Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25**: a public body has no duty to protect a family from neighbours' harassment absent an assumption of responsibility. An assumption may arise from conduct under a statutory scheme if it meets the *Hedley Byrne*/*Spring* criteria, or from specific facts.
- ***Swinney v Chief Constable of Northumbria* [1997] QB 464**: police failure to safeguard informants' interests, used by Steele as an example of assumption of responsibility. In *Smith*, Lord Hope quoted Ward LJ's remark in *Swinney* that "the greater public good outweighs any individual hardship".
- ***D v East Berkshire Community Health NHS Trust* [2005] UKHL 23**: listed for comparison. The Court of Appeal ([2004] QB 558) held that the HRA undermined the policy grounds in *X v Bedfordshire*. The Supreme Court in *Poole* accepted that outcome was correct in principle (Steele ch 1). Lord Rodger's dictum is in duty-of-care-principles.md.
- ***James-Bowen v Commissioner of Police* [2018] UKSC 40**: no duty from the Commissioner to officers when settling claims involving allegations against them. The case was decided on policy, applying the "fair, just and reasonable" ingredient to a novel situation, shortly after *Robinson* (Steele).
- ***Calveley v Chief Constable of Merseyside* [1989] AC**: no duty to officers subject to unduly prolonged disciplinary investigation. Anxiety and reputation damage are not recoverable, it was not foreseeable that investigation would cause illness, the claim would meet economic-loss obstacles, and it was contrary to public policy (as described in *James-Bowen*).
- ***D v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2018] UKSC 11** and ***Rabone v Pennine Care* [2012] UKSC 2**: HRA operational duties (above).

## Debate and critique
- **Policy or principle?**
  - Commentary presents the police cases as policy-driven, operating almost as an immunity.
  - Lord Reed in *Robinson*/*Poole* says the outcomes follow from the ordinary principle that there is no duty to protect against third parties, and that policy reasoning was a symptom of *Anns*.
  - Steele finds this a "somewhat curious" reinterpretation. Decisions reasoned on policy are said to be right on principles their judges never used. And in *D v Commissioner* [2018], the same court explained tort's position in policy terms, with Lord Neuberger calling the no-duty view "entirely defensible" but so too the opposite view.
  - Morgan (2019) 35 PN 32 argues the nonfeasance principle must be justified, and that "assumption of responsibility" will become a conduit for policy.
- **Fire versus ambulance.** Weir and Steele find the *Capital & Counties*/*Kent* distinction unconvincing. Weir suggests the real differences are:
  - reliance (told an ambulance is not coming, one can make other arrangements);
  - personal injury versus property;
  - that fire-damaged premises are almost always insured, so the fire claimants were "probably insurance companies in disguise".
  Steele suggests *Kent* may reflect the impact of adverse trial findings (falsified records).
- ***Stovin* and *Gorringe*: narrow or broad?** Steele argues these cases decide only that a statutory power or broad duty cannot alone found a duty to act. They should not apply where positive steps are taken, as in *East Suffolk*, or to welfare decisions such as *X v Bedfordshire*. So *Poole*'s wider reading is "novel". Craig (*Administrative Law*, quoted by Lord Steyn in *Gorringe*) argues the mere presence of discretion does not make a matter non-justiciable: "there can be discretion even in the hammering of a nail". Justiciable discretion belongs at the breach stage.
- **Human rights and immunity.** Lord Hoffmann, writing extra-judicially ('Human Rights and the House of Lords' (1999) 62 MLR 159), attacked *Osman v UK* for intruding on what he saw as social welfare and budgetary questions. *Z v UK* later retreated from *Osman*. The Government's 2021 HRA consultation criticised the *Osman* "threat to life" duties as diverting police resources (Steele ch 1).
- **Two regimes.** The HRA now provides remedies where tort will not (*Michael* HRA claim; *DSD*), to "victims" in a wider sense. McBride, 'Michael and the Future of Tort Law' (2016) PN 14, sets out "ambitious", "personalist" and "contractual" views of positive rights against the state. Whether tort is "backward" depends on which view is right. Lord Neuberger in *DSD*: tort should not be widened to match human rights law, and human rights law should not be narrowed to match tort.
- **Feminist critique of police non-liability.** Reading includes Conaghan, 'Challenging and redressing police failures in the context of rape investigations: The civil liability route' (2015) 5(1) feminist@law. It also includes 'Trust in the police? Police negligence, invisible immunity and disadvantaged claimants' in Richardson and Rackley (eds), *Feminist Perspectives on Tort Law* (2012). They highlight how the no-duty rule bears on victims of domestic and sexual violence, which links to the *Michael* dissents.
- **Lord Bingham's liability principle** in *Smith* is a ready-made reform proposal. It is narrowly framed (credible evidence, identified third party, specific and imminent threat) to meet floodgates concerns. It was rejected by the majority, but dissenting voices (Bingham, Kerr, Hale) keep it alive.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the defendant**: fire service, ambulance/NHS, police or another authority. Apply ordinary principles first (*Poole*).
2. **Did the body make things worse by a positive act?**
   - Police injuring a bystander during an arrest: a duty is owed (*Robinson*).
   - Firefighters turning off sprinklers: duty and liability (*Capital & Counties*).
   - Then move to breach. Note that the emergency context may affect the standard of care (breach note).
3. **Or did it fail to confer a benefit?** Late arrival, failure to fight a fire, failure to arrest a dangerous person, failure to investigate competently. The starting point is **no duty**. Then check the exceptions:
   - **Ambulance**: has the call been accepted? Then a duty to respond with reasonable care (*Kent v Griffiths*). Were assurances given and relied on?
   - **Fire**: no duty to attend or fight effectively. Was the situation made worse?
   - **Police**: was there a specific assumption of responsibility through express assurances or reliance (*Swinney*, *Costello*)? Contrast *Michael*, where the handler only passed the call on. Was the victim in police custody or control (*Reeves*)? Did the police create the danger (*Hartwell*, *Dorset Yacht*)?
   - **Statutory power alone?** It is insufficient (*Stovin*, *Gorringe*).
4. **Investigation and suppression of crime.** An identified victim and credible threats still do not create a duty in negligence (*Smith*, *Michael*). Mention Lord Bingham's liability principle and the *Michael* dissents for critique marks.
5. **Policy vs operational**: complaints about resource allocation or priorities are not actionable. The manner of carrying out a task may be.
6. **Consider an HRA claim in parallel.** Article 2 (real and immediate risk to an identified person's life, known or ought to be known: *Osman*) or Article 3 (seriously flawed investigation: *DSD*). Note the one-year limitation and different damages.
7. **Traps**:
   - describing police protection as an "immunity" (it is now framed as no duty: *Brooks*);
   - assuming *Hill* bars every claim against the police (*Robinson*);
   - treating the ambulance and fire positions as the same;
   - overlooking causation (would a prompt response have prevented the harm?).
