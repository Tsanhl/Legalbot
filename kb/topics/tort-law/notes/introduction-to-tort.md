# Introduction to Tort Law and the Tort of Negligence

## Overview
Tort is the law of civil (non-criminal) wrongs for which the law gives a remedy, usually compensation payable by the wrongdoer (or someone answerable for them) to the person wronged. It is not one rule but a collection of separate wrongs (negligence, trespass, nuisance, defamation, misuse of private information and so on), each protecting particular interests against particular kinds of interference. Negligence dominates the module and most tort litigation, so this note also sets out the basic structure of a negligence claim and the significance of *Donoghue v Stevenson*. The law described is that of England and Wales (with *Donoghue* itself a Scottish appeal, Scots and English law being treated as the same on the point).

## The law

### 1. What a tort is
1. A tort is a civil wrong for which the law provides a remedy, enforceable by one party against the other. Other civil wrongs exist that are not torts, notably breach of contract and breach of equitable obligations (lecture one; Steele ch 1).
2. There is no generally accepted definition that cleanly separates torts from all other civil wrongs. Steele describes torts as the most diverse group of civil wrongs in English law. the materials calls tort the "Swiss Army knife of the common law".
3. Tort sits inside the law of obligations alongside contract, restitution (unjust enrichment) and trusts. Peter Cane (*The Anatomy of Tort Law*, 1997, extracted in Steele) says that unlike contract, tort is purely "protective": it protects interests created elsewhere (for example property rights) rather than creating them. A central feature of private law is "correlativity": one party's obligation matches the other's right, and every cause of action in tort has one set of elements about the victim's position and another about the injurer's conduct.
4. The list of torts is not closed. Bernard Rudden once counted 70 identifiable torts. Torts can be revived (misfeasance in public office) or newly recognised (misuse of private information, shaped by the Human Rights Act 1998). Courts have also rejected proposed new torts, such as "unlawful exile" (*Chagos Islanders v Attorney General* [2003] EWHC 2222) and unlawful interference with body parts (*In re Organ Retention Litigation* [2005] QB 506) (Steele ch 1).

### 2. Terminology
- **Claimant**: the party who sues. This term has been used in England and Wales since 1999 ("plaintiff" before then, and still in other common law systems). In Scotland the parties are the pursuer and the defender, and the subject is called "delict".
- **Tortfeasor**: the party who committed the tort. This is not always the **defendant**, for example where an employer is sued under vicarious liability or an insurer is sued under the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Acts 1930/2010. In most claims the defence is in practice run and paid for by a liability insurer (Steele ch 1).
- The House of Lords was the highest court until 2009. The UK Supreme Court took over from October 2009, and both are of the highest authority.

### 3. Which interests tort protects
- Some torts protect specific interests and others protect several. the materials maps interests to torts as follows:
  - **Personal integrity (physical and mental), including self-determination**: negligence, occupiers' liability, employers' liability, product liability, trespass to the person (assault, battery, false imprisonment), the rule in *Wilkinson v Downton*, the Protection from Harassment Act 1997, public nuisance.
  - **Property**: negligence, occupiers', product and employers' liability, trespass to land, public and private nuisance, *Rylands v Fletcher*.
  - **Financial loss (pure and consequential)**: negligence, product liability, defamation, public and private nuisance.
  - **Possession, use and enjoyment of land**: trespass to land, nuisance, *Rylands v Fletcher*, negligence, harassment claims.
  - **Reputation**: defamation (libel and slander), breach of confidence.
  - **Privacy**: negligence, trespass to land, nuisance, breach of confidence, the "tort" of invasion of privacy, harassment claims.
- Not every interference with an interest is actionable. the materials's example is *McFarlane v Tayside Health Board* [2000]. There is no general principle that says when conduct will give rise to tort liability, and public policy is a common feature of the decisions (lecture one).
- Steele adds that broad labels such as "property interests" can mislead. Negligence protects against *damage* to property. Nuisance protects *use and enjoyment* of land. Trespass to land protects *possession* and needs no loss in value. Conversion protects *title* to goods against dealings inconsistent with the owner's rights.
- Some torts need actual damage. Others are "actionable per se", although without damage only nominal damages are likely (*Lumba v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2011] UKSC 12, cited in Steele).

### 4. Fault, intention and strict liability
- Torts can be grouped by the conduct they require: intention (for example deceit, trespass to the person), carelessness (negligence) or no fault (strict liability, for example conversion and *Rylands*). Steele notes there are many strict and intentional torts but only one general tort based on negligence.
- Liability does not always mean the defendant was blameworthy (Steele ch 1). Three examples:
  - A learner driver is held to the objective standard of care even though she personally could not have driven better.
  - A careful accumulator of a dangerous thing that escapes may be liable because the risk of the activity is placed on them.
  - An honest person who takes someone else's umbrella home must compensate the owner, to protect the owner's property right.

### 5. The aims of tort law (lecture one)
Tort law looks both backward and forward:
- **Backward**: it addresses the wrong already done and the harm caused.
- **Forward**: it regulates behaviour and responds to risks of future harm.

It protects interests prospectively (preventing or deterring harm) and retrospectively (compensating and distributing losses). the materials lists the usual functions as:
1. (corrective) justice;
2. compensation;
3. deterrence;
4. inquiry and/or publicity.

### 6. Human Rights Act 1998
- The HRA lets individuals hold the state liable. It also shapes claims between private individuals, because courts must keep their decisions on the existence and scope of tort liability in line with the Act (lecture one).
- Section 6 makes it unlawful for a public authority to act incompatibly with a Convention right, and s 6(3) includes courts and tribunals within "public authority". Section 7 creates a free-standing right of action against public authorities, with a one-year limitation period that can be extended. Section 8 governs remedies: damages are discretionary and must reflect Strasbourg principles. An HRA action is **not** a tort action (Steele ch 1).
- Section 12 requires particular regard to freedom of expression when relief might affect it, and restricts injunctions that would restrain publication before trial.

### 7. The tort of negligence: its elements
To succeed in negligence the claimant must establish all of the following (lecture one):
1. the defendant (D) owed the claimant (C) a **duty of care**;
2. D **breached** that duty;
3. the breach **caused** C's loss (including that the loss is not too remote / is within the scope of the duty);
4. there is **no applicable defence**.

The claim fails if any element is missing. the materials's "negligence equation" is: duty + breach + causation − defences = negligence.

## Key authorities
- ***Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 (HL)**. A friend bought the claimant a bottle of ginger beer in a café in Paisley. The bottle was opaque. After she had drunk some, the remainder was poured out and a decomposing snail came out. She claimed shock and gastro-enteritis and sued the manufacturer, with whom she had no contract. The case was decided on assumed facts as a preliminary point of law. By a majority, the House of Lords held that a manufacturer owes the ultimate consumer a duty of care. Its significance (lecture one):
  - it is the starting point of the modern single tort of negligence;
  - it establishes that a manufacturer owes a duty to the ultimate consumer;
  - it rejects the "privity fallacy" (the idea that no one outside the contract could sue);
  - Lord Atkin attempted a general test for duty: the **neighbour principle**.
  - Lord Atkin's dictum (at 580): take "reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour". Your neighbours are those "so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation".
  - Lord Atkin also said that "there must be, and is, some general conception of relations giving rise to a duty of care", of which the decided cases are only instances. He used "proximity" to mean not physical closeness but "close and direct relations".
  - Lord Macmillan said the fact that the consumer was not party to the contract of sale becomes irrelevant once the case is treated as one of delict, and that "the categories of negligence are never closed" (quoted in Steele).
  - Lord Buckmaster dissented. He relied on *Winterbottom v Wright* and Alderson B's warning that if the law goes "one step beyond" the contracting parties "there is no reason why we should not go fifty". He also relied on *Mullen v Barr & Co* (a mouse in a bottle) and the fear of unmanageable liability for widely distributed goods (Steele).
  - Steele notes that to this day it is not known whether there really was a snail in the bottle.
- **Post-*Donoghue* expansion**: the majority view only gradually won acceptance. By the 1970s negligence came to be seen as "an ocean of liability for carelessly causing foreseeable harm, dotted with islands of non-liability, rather than as a crowded archipelago of individual duty situations" (Ibbetson, *A Historical Introduction to the Law of Obligations* (1999) 192–3, quoted in the materials one). In other words, the courts appeared to work from a very broad principle of near-default liability for careless harm. The retreat from that position is covered in duty-of-care-principles.md.
- ***McFarlane v Tayside Health Board* [2000]**: cited in the materials one as an example that not every interference with a person's interests is recognised by tort. the materials gives no further detail.
- ***Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964]**: Weir identifies *Donoghue* (1932) and *Hedley Byrne* (1963) as the two major judicial steps that widened tort liability. *Hedley Byrne* extended negligence to conduct that damages only the claimant's pocket.
- **Human Rights Act 1998 ss 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 12**: see section 6 of The law above.

## Debate and critique
- **Defining tort and its unity.** Cane argues tort is best understood as a system of ethical principles of personal responsibility, unpacked into three parts: protected interests, sanctioned conduct and remedies. He is sceptical of rigid categories, holding that different causes of action with different criteria should not be available on the same facts. Steele hesitates. If each tort describes a different way an interaction can be wrong, it is not obvious that one approach to the facts is the right one. This sort of thinking has contributed to the near demise of strict liability under *Rylands*, and to classifying trespass to the person as an "intentional" tort. *Ashley v Chief Constable of Sussex Police* shows trespass, misfeasance and negligence can all arise on the same facts with different implications.
- **The dominance of negligence.** Steele observes that tort courses are dominated by negligence, which makes them unrepresentative of the whole subject. Negligence has become a very general principle while other wrongs have stayed confined. "Fault-based" thinking has also seeped into other torts. The generalisation of negligence also shows the dangers of a general approach: much difficult case law exists only to set limits, mainly through the duty of care.
- **Functions versus wrongs.** Steele notes that "functional" accounts of tort (as a loss-distribution and compensation system) are less fashionable than they were, but the evidence is "overwhelming" that tort is in practice used to distribute losses. Cane (*Atiyah's Accidents, Compensation and the Law*, 8th edn 2013) points to measures that support the fault-based system:
  - compulsory third-party motor insurance;
  - compulsory employers' liability insurance;
  - the Motor Insurers' Bureau.
  Steele cites Baker and Siegelman's suggestion that "little or nothing makes sense in Tort law except in the light of liability insurance", while noting that the effect of insurance varies from context to context. Further reading flagged in the materials one includes Morgan, 'Tort, Insurance and Incoherence' (2004) 67 MLR 384, and Goldberg, 'Twentieth-Century Tort Theory' (2003) 91 Georgetown LJ 513.
- **Moral duty versus legal duty.** Weir stresses that legal standards should not be pushed up to those of "delicate morality", or there would be no room for generosity. In English law (unlike many systems) there is no duty to rescue a stranger: "you may ignore an infant drowning in a pond unless it is your infant or your pond or you are the lifeguard". Lord Atkin made the same point: the moral rule to love your neighbour becomes, in law, the narrower rule that you must not injure your neighbour. This sets up omissions-and-third-parties.md.
- **Fault and accident compensation.** Weir notes that Britain is unusual in giving traffic accident victims no damages unless someone was at fault. France and Germany have stricter regimes for road accidents. Industrial accident victims, by contrast, can often recover without proving the employer was to blame.
- **Historical trend.** Weir describes about a century of development almost uniformly in claimants' favour, mostly by statute. Examples: tortfeasor death no longer bars claims (1934), contributory negligence no longer a complete bar (1945), Crown proceedings (1947), UCTA 1977, harassment (1997), HRA 1998. Rare contractions include the reversal in 1991 of the 1978 local authority building-inspection liability.
- **"Compensation culture".** There is political rhetoric that people over-claim, driving up costs and discouraging healthy risk-taking. Empirical reviews suggest overall claiming has not been rising (Steele). Lewis and Morris, 'Tort Law Culture in the United Kingdom' (2012) 3 JETL 230, find increased claiming only in road traffic claims, which they explain by the institutionalised claims environment (conditional fee agreements, referral fees), and say this is exceptional. Legislative responses include:
  - an overhaul of civil litigation costs;
  - the Defamation Act 2013;
  - s 69 Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, which reversed employers' strict liability for breach of statutory duty;
  - the Social Action, Responsibility and Heroism Act 2015, which requires courts to take account of the merits of deserving defendants' actions.
  Steele concludes that policy-makers are "defendant-centred", and that courts also show a renewed culture of restraint. Lecture reading: Lord Dyson, 'Compensation Culture: Fact or Fantasy?' (2013); Williams, 'State of Fear: Britain's "Compensation Culture" Reviewed' (2005) 25 LS 499.
- **Human rights and tort.**
  - On horizontal effect, Wade argued for direct horizontal effect ('Horizons of Horizontality' (2000) 116 LQR 217), supported by Morgan (2002) 22 LS 259. Others accepted only indirect effect, in a weak form (courts influenced by the rights) or a strong form (a duty to develop the common law compatibly).
  - In practice, *Campbell v MGN* [2004] 2 AC 457 denied that new causes of action arise from the HRA but endorsed changing breach of confidence to protect privacy. Steele says this has not been repeated elsewhere, so a "strong" horizontality has not been generally accepted.
  - Courts have sometimes refused to develop negligence because HRA ss 7–8 offer a more suitable remedy (*Watkins v Home Office* [2006] UKHL 17; *Van Colle/Smith* [2008] UKHL 50; *Michael* [2015] UKSC 2).
  - The 2021 Government consultation on HRA reform criticised a "rights culture", echoing the "compensation culture" critique.
  - See also Steele, 'Damages in Tort and under the Human Rights Act' (2008) 67 CLJ 606 (lecture reading).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the interest harmed** (body, mind, property, pure economic interests, land, reputation, privacy) and list every tort that protects it. Several may run on the same facts, as in *Ashley*.
2. **Identify the parties.** Who is the claimant? Who is the tortfeasor, and is the defendant someone else (an employer vicariously liable, an insurer)?
3. **Check whether damage is needed.** Negligence requires actual damage. Trespass torts are actionable per se.
4. **For negligence, work through the four elements in order**: duty → breach → causation/remoteness → defences. Say clearly that failure at any stage defeats the claim.
5. **Duty first.** Is this an established category, like *Donoghue* (manufacturer–consumer), road users or doctor–patient? If not, go to the novel-case approach (duty-of-care-principles.md). Watch for special-duty problems: omissions/third parties, public authorities, psychiatric harm, pure economic loss.
6. **Public authority defendant?** Consider whether an HRA claim (ss 6–8) runs in parallel, noting its one-year limit and discretionary damages.
7. **Common traps**:
   - assuming every careless harm is actionable (see *D v East Berkshire*, Lord Rodger, in duty-of-care-principles.md);
   - assuming a contract between other parties blocks a tort claim (the "privity fallacy" rejected in *Donoghue*);
   - assuming moral fault equals legal liability (the no duty to rescue rule).
