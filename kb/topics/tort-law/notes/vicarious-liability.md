# Vicarious Liability and Non-Delegable Duties

## Overview
Vicarious liability is a form of secondary, strict liability: a defendant who is personally blameless is made liable for a tort committed by another, usually an employee. The tortfeasor remains liable too; the point is to give the claimant a defendant able to pay. It can also arise in partnership and agency (*Dubai Aluminium v Salaam* [2003]), but the module concentrates on employment and relationships "akin to employment". The law has expanded sharply since *Lister v Hesley Hall* (2001) and *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* (2012), largely through institutional child abuse litigation. In 2020 the Supreme Court drew boundaries in *Barclays Bank* and *WM Morrison*. Non-delegable duties are a different, *primary* form of liability for another's fault (*Woodland v Swimming Teachers Association* [2013]). England and Wales law, with strong Canadian influence (*Bazley v Curry*).

## The law

### Three hurdles (lecture structure)
1. There is a relevant relationship between defendant and tortfeasor: employment, or a relationship akin to employment.
2. The tortfeasor committed a tort.
3. There is a sufficient connection between that relationship and the tort: traditionally "course of employment", now the "close connection" test.

Steele calls hurdles 1 and 3 the "two limbs" and stresses (following Lord Phillips in *Various Claimants*) that they involve a synthesis: the second stage looks at the connection between the *relationship* and the tort, not just between the tortfeasor and the tort.

### Hurdle one: the relationship
1. Employees, yes; independent contractors, never (a "foundational principle"). The customer is not liable for the contractor (Weir: the bus passengers are not liable for the driver).
2. Identifying a contract of service:
   - *Ready Mixed Concrete v Minister of Pensions and National Insurance* [1968] 2 QB 497: A agrees, for remuneration, to provide his own work and skill in performing a service for B, under B's control to a sufficient degree, on terms consistent with a contract of service. Look at the substance of the relationship; the parties' own label is not decisive. Relevant: who owns the tools and who bears the chance of profit or risk of loss (MacKenna J).
   - *Market Investigations Ltd v Minister of Social Security* [1969]: no exhaustive list of considerations, and no fixed rules on their weight (Cooke J). This is the "economic reality" approach, combining factors.
   - Control: *Stevenson Jordan & Harrison Ltd v Macdonald & Evans* [1952] (Denning LJ: control is not universally correct; the "integration" idea, i.e. whether the work is an integral part of the business or merely accessory to it).
   - Integration: *JGE v Trustees of the Portsmouth Roman Catholic Diocesan Trust* [2012] (also known as *E v English Province of Our Lady of Charity* [2012] EWCA Civ 938): a priest's relationship with the diocese was akin to employment; "function triumphed over form".
3. Relationships akin to employment: *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56 (Christian Brothers). Lord Phillips' five policy factors (or "incidents") making vicarious liability fair, just and reasonable:
   (i) the employer is more likely to have the means to compensate and can be expected to have insured;
   (ii) the tort results from activity undertaken by the employee on the employer's behalf;
   (iii) the activity is likely to be part of the employer's business activity;
   (iv) by employing the employee to carry on the activity, the employer created the risk of the tort;
   (v) the employee was, to a greater or lesser degree, under the employer's control.
   The brothers, bound by vows rather than contract and paying their earnings to the institute, were in a relationship closer than employment; the institute was jointly vicariously liable with the diocesan bodies.
4. *Cox v Ministry of Justice* [2016] UKSC 10: prisoner working in a prison kitchen negligently injured the catering manager; the MoJ was vicariously liable. Lord Reed at [20]: a non-employment relationship can give rise to vicarious liability where the individual carries on activities as an integral part of the defendant's business, for its benefit, and the wrongdoing is a risk created by the defendant assigning those activities to the individual. Factor (i) is unlikely to be independently significant ("employers insure themselves because they are liable: they are not liable because they have insured themselves"); control (v) is less significant than once thought. A profit motive is not required.
5. *Armes v Nottinghamshire CC* [2017] UKSC 60: the local authority was vicariously liable for abuse by foster parents. They were integrated into the council's child-care organisation, the placement created a risk, the council exercised significant control (approval, inspection, removal), and foster parents rarely have means or insurance. (No non-delegable duty was found: see below.)
6. *Barclays Bank v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 13 (Lady Hale): a doctor who examined job applicants for the bank and sexually assaulted them was "in business on his own account" (portfolio of clients, paid per report, free to refuse work, own insurance). The relationship was not akin to employment. The question remains, "as it has always been", whether the tortfeasor is carrying on business on his own account or is in a relationship akin to employment. The five incidents help only in doubtful cases; the focus is on "the details of the relationship, and its closeness to employment". Applied in *JXJ v The Province of Great Britain of the Institute of Brothers of the Christian Schools* [2020] EWHC 1914.
7. Borrowed employees: *Mersey Docks and Harbour Board v Coggins & Griffith (Liverpool) Ltd* [1947] AC 1. The general employer (the Board, which let out a crane with its driver) remained liable; there is a strong presumption against transfer, and the key question is who is entitled to control the way the work is done (Lord Porter). *Hawley v Luminar Leisure Ltd* [2006] EWCA Civ 18: a doorman supplied by an agency but controlled, uniformed and embedded in the nightclub's business made the club his deemed employer.
8. Dual vicarious liability: *Viasystems (Tyneside) Ltd v Thermal Transfer (Northern) Ltd* [2005] EWCA Civ 1151 (reported [2006] 2 WLR 428): a fitter's mate negligently crawled through ducting; both the subcontractor and the labour supplier were entitled to control him and both were vicariously liable (contribution 50/50). May LJ focused on control; Rix LJ on whether the worker was so much part of both businesses that it is just for both to answer.

### Hurdle two: a tort by the tortfeasor
The employee must have committed a tort. Vicarious liability is not limited to negligence: it covers harassment under statute (*Majrowski v Guy's and St Thomas's NHS Trust* [2006] UKHL 34), battery, sexual assault, theft, and misuse of private information. Steele: the employee commits the tort, and the employer is liable. Lord Sumption in *Woodland*: the defendant "commits no tort himself" but is liable as a matter of public policy. Which tort matters (e.g. for limitation: *A v Hoare*, where the right action on *Lister*-type facts is trespass for the abuse itself).

### Hurdle three: connection with the employment
1. Traditional Salmond test: an act is in the course of employment if it is (a) authorised by the employer, or (b) a "wrongful and unauthorised mode of doing some act authorised by the master". This fitted deliberate wrongdoing badly; sexual abuse of children could not be described as a mode of caring for them (*Mohamud* at [39]). *Trotman v North Yorkshire CC* [1999] LGR 584 applied Salmond to deny liability for a deputy head's abuse; it was overruled in *Lister*.
2. Close connection test: *Lister v Hesley Hall Ltd* [2001] UKHL 22; [2002] 1 AC 215. A boarding-house warden abused boys in his care. The employer was liable because the torts were "so closely connected with his employment that it would be fair and just to hold the employers vicariously liable" (Lord Steyn); the abuse was "inextricably interwoven" with his duties. Lord Millett relied on risks reasonably incidental to the business; Lord Hobhouse analysed it as the school's own duty entrusted to the warden (closer to a non-delegable duty).
3. *Dubai Aluminium Co Ltd v Salaam* [2003] 2 AC 366 (Lord Nicholls): the wrongful conduct must be so closely connected with acts the employee was authorised to do that, for the purposes of liability to third parties, it may fairly and properly be regarded as done in the ordinary course of employment. This is the authoritative general statement (*WM Morrison*, 2020).
4. Various Claimants (2012): where the defendant used the abuser to further its own interests in a way that created or significantly enhanced the risk of abuse, there is a close connection ("strong causative link"). The boys' triple vulnerability and the brothers' placement in a residential school "greatly enhanced the risk".
5. *Mohamud v Wm Morrison Supermarkets plc* [2016] UKSC 11: a kiosk attendant racially abused a customer and then followed him to his car and assaulted him. Liability followed. Lord Toulson's two questions: (1) what functions or "field of activities" had been entrusted to the employee; (2) was there sufficient connection between that position and the wrongdoing to make it right for the employer to be liable "under the principle of social justice which goes back to Holt CJ"? The attack was an "unbroken sequence of events", a "seamless episode", and he was ordering the customer off his employer's premises.
6. *WM Morrison Supermarkets plc v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 12: an employee with a grudge, given payroll data to pass to auditors, published it online to harm the employer. No vicarious liability. Lord Reed: *Mohamud* did not change the law; "unbroken sequence" referred to the capacity in which the employee acted, not to timing or causation; motive is highly material; the employee was on a "frolic of his own", pursuing a personal vendetta, not furthering the employer's business. "Fairly and properly" is not an invitation to apply a personal sense of justice. Child sexual abuse cases have applied a "tailored" approach focused on factors such as conferral of authority over victims.
7. Traditional sub-rules (listed in the materials):
   - Incompetents and misguided enthusiasts: *Century Insurance v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board* [1942] AC 509.
   - Frolics and detours: *Smith v Stages* [1989] 1 All ER 833; *Whatman v Pearson* (1868) LR 3 CP 422; *Storey v Ashton* (1869) LR 4 QB 476; *Williams v Hemphill* 1966 SC (HL) 31 (driver diverted at the boys' request was still liable; Weir would explain it by the carrier's own duty to the passengers).
   - Express prohibitions: *Twine v Bean's Express Ltd* (1946) 175 LT 131; *Rose v Plenty* [1975] ICR 4 (milkman forbidden to use child helpers, yet in the course of employment when he injured the boy, since the prohibited act was done in furthering the employer's business).
   - Criminal acts: *Lister*; *Mattis v Pollock* [2003] EWCA Civ 887 (bouncer went home, fetched a knife and stabbed a patron; club liable); *N v Chief Constable of Merseyside Police* [2006]; *Maga v Birmingham Roman Catholic Archdiocese Trustees* [2010] EWCA Civ 256; *Mohamud*.
   - Weir: Lloyd v Grace, Smith & Co [1912] AC 716 (via Lord Steyn in *Lister*) shows liability is not defeated because the employee acted for his own benefit.

### Consequences
- The employer has a right of indemnity against the employee (*Lister v Romford Ice and Cold Storage Co* [1957], Weir), though it is rarely used.
- Contribution between dual or joint vicariously liable parties arises under the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978 (*Viasystems*).

### Non-delegable duties (primary liability)
1. A non-delegable duty is the defendant's own duty to see that care is taken; performance may be delegated but responsibility may not. Liability is primary, even if the defendant was not at fault and the wrongdoer was an independent contractor.
2. Established categories: the employer's duty to employees to provide a safe system of work, competent staff and safe equipment (*Wilsons & Clyde Coal Co Ltd v English* [1938] AC 57; *McDermid v Nash Dredging* [1987] AC 906). Extra-hazardous activities (*Honeywill & Stein v Larkin* [1934] 1 KB 191) were confined to their facts in *Biffa Waste* [2008] EWCA Civ 1238 as "anomalous".
3. *Woodland v Swimming Teachers Association* [2013] UKSC 66: a child injured in a school swimming lesson run by independent contractors. The education authority owed a non-delegable duty. Lord Sumption's defining features: (1) the claimant is a patient or child or otherwise especially vulnerable or dependent; (2) there is an antecedent relationship placing the claimant in the defendant's custody, charge or care, from which a positive duty to protect can be imputed (an element of control); (3) the claimant has no control over how the defendant performs its obligations; (4) the defendant has delegated an integral part of the positive duty, with its custody and control, to a third party; (5) the third party was negligent in performing the very function delegated, not in some collateral respect. The duty arises only so far as fair, just and reasonable.
4. *Armes v Nottinghamshire CC* [2017] UKSC 60: no non-delegable duty for children in foster care. Under the statutory scheme the authority's duty was to arrange and monitor the placement, not to perform daily care itself, and a duty to ensure the children's safety was "too demanding". But such a duty can be breached by a deliberate wrong (departing from the Australian *NSW v Lepore*).
5. Other primary duties: care in selecting and supervising contractors (Occupiers' Liability Act 1957, s 2(4)(b); *Gwilliam v West Herts Hospital NHS Trust* [2003] QB 443; *Bottomley v Todmorden Cricket Club* [2003] EWCA Civ 1575) and in supervising employees (e.g. *Hudson v Ridge Manufacturing* [1957] 2 QB 348: an employer was liable for a known prankster's horseplay).

## Key authorities
- *Lister v Hesley Hall* [2001] UKHL 22: warden's abuse; close connection test replaces Salmond.
- *Dubai Aluminium v Salaam* [2003] 2 AC 366: authoritative statement of close connection; partnership.
- *Various Claimants v Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56: relationship akin to employment; five factors; enhanced risk.
- *Cox v MoJ* [2016] UKSC 10: prisoner worker; integral part of business, risk created by assigning activity.
- *Mohamud v WM Morrison* [2016] UKSC 11: petrol kiosk assault; field of activities, seamless episode.
- *Armes v Nottinghamshire CC* [2017] UKSC 60: foster parents; vicarious liability yes, non-delegable duty no.
- *Barclays Bank v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 13: independent business; no vicarious liability.
- *WM Morrison v Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 12: data leak by vendetta; no close connection.
- *Woodland v Swimming Teachers Association* [2013] UKSC 66: non-delegable duty of schools.
- *Viasystems* [2005] and *Hawley v Luminar* [2006]: dual or transferred employment.
- *Mersey Docks v Coggins* [1947] AC 1: borrowed employees; who controls the method of work.
- *Ready Mixed Concrete* [1968]; *Market Investigations* [1969]: tests of employment.
- *Rose v Plenty*; *Twine v Bean's Express*: prohibitions; *Century Insurance*: listed under incompetents and misguided enthusiasts; *Mattis v Pollock*: violent bouncer.

## Debate and critique
- Justifications (Steele): (a) justice or enterprise risk: whoever takes the benefit of an activity should bear its inherent risks (Lord Toulson in *Mohamud*: "The risk of an employee misusing his position is one of life's unavoidable facts"); (b) incentive or deterrence: liability pushes employers to improve selection and supervision beyond the reasonable person standard (McLachlin J in *Bazley v Curry* [1999] 2 SCR 534); (c) loss spreading or deep pockets. Atiyah (*Vicarious Liability in the Law of Tort*, 1967) argued that no one reason suffices but their combined effect may be overwhelming, and that loss spreading is broadly sound as the most convenient and efficient way to compensate. Weir thinks debating rationales is pointless because the scope of the rule is not determined by the rationale; he suggests the principle may be that you answer for what you get people to do for you.
- Policy creep: Lord Reed in *Cox* downplayed the "deep pockets" factor, but revived it in *Armes*. The five factors were criticised as indeterminate; *Barclays* restricted them to doubtful cases. Giliker, "Can the Supreme Court Halt the Ongoing Expansion of Vicarious Liability: Barclays and Morrison in the UK Supreme Court" (2021) 37(2) PN 55, discusses whether the 2020 cases really halted the expansion.
- Independent contractor rule: Sir Richard Buxton ((2020) 79 CLJ 217) argues *Barclays* clings to a 19th-century rule (*Quarman v Burnett* (1840)) unsuited to modern outsourcing, where contractors are integrated into the business and highly controlled; if employers take the benefits of outsourcing they should bear its burdens. McKendrick ((1990) 53 MLR 770) on casual and shifting workforces. Steele: there is no longer a binary divide; falling outside "akin to employment" is not the same as being an independent contractor.
- Close connection: vague and evaluative. *Morrison* insists on orthodox reasoning from precedent, not "social justice", and makes motive material. But its "individual agenda" reasoning is hard to square with the abuse cases and *Mohamud* itself, and the suggestion of a "tailored" approach for child sexual abuse raises questions (why only sexual abuse, why only children?) (Steele). Applied in *Trustees of the Barry Congregation of Jehovah's Witnesses v BXB* [2021] EWCA Civ 356. See also Lord Hope, "Tailoring the Law on Vicarious Liability" (2013) 129 LQR 514; Giliker, "Lister Revisited: Vicarious Liability, Distributive Justice and the Course of Employment" (2010) 126 LQR 521; Bell, "The Basis of Vicarious Liability" (2013) 72 CLJ 17; Williams, "Vicarious Liability of the Roman Catholic Church for Sexual Abuse by a Priest" (2010) 26 PN 113.
- Vicarious vs primary: Weir argues *Lister* confused vicarious liability with the employer's own (delegated) duty to the boys; Lord Hobhouse's speech shows the employer's relationship with the victim was what really mattered (it explains why a gardener's abuse would not make the school liable). Glanville Williams ("Liability for Independent Contractors" [1956] CLJ 180) called the non-delegability dictum in *Dalton v Angus* "one of the leading sophistries in the law of tort". Lord Sumption in *Woodland* warned of the exception "eating up the rule", since non-delegable duties are inconsistent with fault-based negligence. Tofaris, "Vicarious Liability and Non-delegable Duty for Child Abuse in Foster Care: A Step Too Far?" (2016) 79(5) MLR 884, on *Armes*.
- Insurance: vicarious liability (not attribution) matters because insurers generally will not cover liability for the assured's own intentional wrongs (Steele).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the tortfeasor and confirm they committed a tort against the claimant (any tort: negligence, battery, harassment, misuse of private information).
2. Relationship:
   - Employee? Apply control, integration and economic reality (*Ready Mixed Concrete*, *Market Investigations*). Labels do not decide it.
   - Not an employee? Ask first whether they are carrying on an independent business of their own (*Barclays*). If clearly so, stop: no vicarious liability. If doubtful, use the five incidents (*Various Claimants*, *Cox*, *Armes*).
   - Borrowed or agency worker? Who controlled the method of the work (*Mersey Docks*, *Hawley*)? Could both be liable (*Viasystems*)?
3. Connection:
   - Was the act within the "field of activities" entrusted (*Mohamud*)? Was it so closely connected with authorised acts that it can fairly and properly be regarded as done in the ordinary course of employment (*Dubai*, *Morrison*)?
   - Look at motive (furthering the employer's business, however misguidedly, vs a personal frolic or vendetta: *Morrison*), timing and place (*Mohamud*, *Mattis*), conferral of authority or power over vulnerable victims (*Lister*, *Various Claimants*), and whether the employment created or enhanced the risk.
   - Express prohibition? It does not prevent liability if the prohibited act was done in the course of the job (*Rose v Plenty* vs *Twine*). Careless or over-zealous performance remains within the course of employment (*Century Insurance*). Travel and detours: *Smith v Stages*.
4. If vicarious liability fails, consider primary liability: a non-delegable duty (*Woodland* features: vulnerable claimant in the defendant's care, delegated integral function), the employer's own duties to employees (*Wilsons & Clyde*), negligent selection or supervision (*Hudson v Ridge*; OLA 1957 s 2(4)(b)).
5. Remember the tortfeasor remains personally liable; mention contribution and indemnity where relevant.
6. Traps: treating the five factors as a checklist in every case (post-*Barclays*); reading *Mohamud* as a pure "unbroken sequence" or causal test (rejected in *Morrison*); forgetting that vicarious liability requires a tort by someone; confusing non-delegable duty (primary) with vicarious liability (secondary).
