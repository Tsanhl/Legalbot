# Campbell v MGN Ltd [2004] UKHL 22 (case note)

> The National Archives does not hold this judgment, so no official copy is in the KB. Facts and paragraph
> references below were checked against a full copy of the House of Lords opinions. Check them against the
> report ([2004] 2 AC 457) before citing.

## Overview
House of Lords, 6 May 2004: Lord Nicholls of Birkenhead, Lord Hoffmann, Lord Hope of Craighead, Baroness Hale of
Richmond, Lord Carswell. By 3-2 (Lord Hope, Baroness Hale, Lord Carswell; Lords Nicholls and Hoffmann dissenting)
the House allowed Naomi Campbell's appeal and restored the trial judge's order. It is the foundation of the modern
action for **misuse of private information**. The House was unanimous on the principles. It divided only on how
they applied to the facts (Lord Hoffmann at [36]).

## Facts
- On 1 February 2001 the *Daily Mirror* ran a front-page story, "Naomi: I am a drug addict". It said she was
  attending Narcotics Anonymous (NA), gave details of her attendance, and printed covertly taken photographs of her
  in the street outside an NA meeting (Lord Nicholls at [2]-[6]).
- She had publicly denied taking drugs. Her counsel therefore conceded that the paper could publish (1) the fact of
  her addiction and (2) the fact that she was having treatment. She challenged only (3) the fact that the treatment
  was at NA, (4) the details of it, and (5) the photographs ([23]-[24]).
- Morland J ([2002] EWHC 499 (QB)) awarded £2,500 plus £1,000 aggravated damages for breach of confidence and
  under the Data Protection Act 1998. The Court of Appeal ([2002] EWCA Civ 1373, [2003] QB 633) allowed MGN's
  appeal ([10]).

## Held
- **Nature of the action.** Breach of confidence no longer needs an initial confidential relationship. Lord
  Nicholls at [14]: "The essence of the tort is better encapsulated now as misuse of private information."
- **Stage 1, is the information private?** The touchstone is whether the claimant had a *reasonable expectation of
  privacy* in the disclosed facts (Lord Nicholls at [21]). He warned at [22] that the "highly offensive" formulation
  (*ABC v Lenah Game Meats*) is a stricter test and mixes in proportionality issues. Lord Hope at [94] treated it as
  useful only where there is room for doubt. The test is objective: what a reasonable person of ordinary
  sensibilities would feel in the claimant's position, not the reader's view (Lord Hope at [99]).
- **Correcting a false image.** Where a public figure presents a false image, the press may normally put the record
  straight (Lord Nicholls at [24]).
- **Stage 2, balancing.** Neither Article 8 nor Article 10 has precedence (Lord Nicholls at [12]). The court weighs
  them in the circumstances.
- **Majority.** Health information is private and confidential (Baroness Hale at [145]). The photographs added to the
  information, showed the place, and risked harm to her recovery, and there was no need to publish them (at
  [155]-[157]). Lord Hope ([123]-[125]) and Lord Carswell ([169]-[171]) held that categories (3)-(5), together,
  were an unjustified intrusion.
- **Dissent.** Lord Hoffmann held that the extra detail and photographs fell within the margin of editorial
  judgment ([59], [77]). Lord Nicholls also dissented.

## Later treatment
- *Vidal-Hall v Google* [2015] EWCA Civ 311, [2016] QB 1003 held that misuse of private information is a tort.
- *Murray v Express Newspapers* [2008] EWCA Civ 446 summarised Lord Nicholls's principles and set out the
  "Murray factors" for stage 1.
- *Bloomberg LP v ZXC* [2022] UKSC 5 calls *Campbell* "seminal" (para 45) and applies its two-stage test, with
  *In re S (A Child)* [2004] UKHL 47 on the balancing exercise.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify each item of information separately, as the House did with the five categories.
2. Stage 1: reasonable expectation of privacy (objective, from the claimant's position; Murray factors).
3. Note any public "false image" that the press may correct. Ask whether the publication went further than that
   correction needed.
4. Stage 2: balance Article 8 against Article 10. Treat photographs and health information as weighty.
