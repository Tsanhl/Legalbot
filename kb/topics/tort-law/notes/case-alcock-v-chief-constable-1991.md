# Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police [1992] 1 AC 310 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. Also cited as [1991] UKHL 5. The BAILII record gives the date as 28 November
> 1991 (one secondary source misprints it as 28 December). No paragraph pinpoints are given.

## Overview
House of Lords, 28 November 1991. Panel: Lord Keith of Kinkel, Lord Ackner, Lord Oliver of Aylmerton, Lord Jauncey of
Tullichettle and Lord Lowry. The leading authority on when a **secondary victim** can recover damages for psychiatric
injury caused by negligence. The claimants lost, but the House set out the conditions that later claimants must meet.

## Facts
The case came from the Hillsborough stadium disaster in 1989, when a crush occurred at an FA Cup semi-final after
police ordered a gate to be opened. Many spectators were killed and many were injured. (Sources give differing figures
for the dead and injured.) The Chief Constable admitted negligence towards those who were killed or injured. Relatives
and others who were not themselves in danger claimed damages for psychiatric illness; ten claimants' cases reached the
House. Some had been in the stadium, some watched on live television, and some learned later of the deaths, including
by identifying bodies.

## Held
- The claims **failed**: the House of Lords dismissed the claimants' appeals.
- A secondary victim (someone made ill by what happens to another) must show psychiatric harm was reasonably
  foreseeable in a person of ordinary fortitude, and satisfy three requirements of proximity: a close tie of love and
  affection with the primary victim, closeness in time and space to the accident or its immediate aftermath, and
  perception of the event or its aftermath through their own unaided senses.
- The shock must be sudden, not the product of a gradual process.
- Watching the live television coverage did not meet the perception requirement. Identifying a body at the mortuary
  some hours later fell outside the immediate aftermath.
- A close tie of love and affection is presumed between parents and children and between spouses (and, per one
  source, fiancés); other relationships, such as siblings, must be proved.

## Unconfirmed or omitted
- The result for each claimant is not set out.
- Later developments (for example on secondary victims in medical negligence cases) are not covered here.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the claimant as primary or secondary victim. If secondary, apply the *Alcock* control mechanisms.
2. Check each: relationship (presumed or proved); proximity in time and space, including the immediate aftermath;
   direct perception without being told by others or via broadcast; and a sudden shock.
3. Ask whether psychiatric injury (not just grief) was reasonably foreseeable in a person of ordinary fortitude and
   whether the claimant has a recognised psychiatric illness.
4. If all are met, a duty may be owed; address breach and causation separately.
