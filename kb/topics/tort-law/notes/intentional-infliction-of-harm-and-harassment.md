# Intentional Infliction of Harm (Wilkinson v Downton) and Statutory Harassment

## Overview
Trespass covers only *direct* interferences. When someone deliberately harms another *indirectly*, for example by saying something shocking that causes a breakdown, the common law answer is the **rule in *Wilkinson v Downton*** [1897] 2 QB 57. The Supreme Court in *Rhodes v OPO* [2015] UKSC 32 renamed it the tort of "wilful infringement of the claimant's right to personal safety" and restated its elements. The common law tort does not compensate mere distress. That gap is largely filled by the **Protection from Harassment Act 1997**, which creates a statutory tort covering a course of conduct that causes alarm or distress. Both topics were taught at the end of the trespass to the person lectures (England & Wales).

## The law

### A. The rule in Wilkinson v Downton
**Origin.** In *Wilkinson v Downton*, as a practical joke, the defendant falsely told the claimant that her husband had been badly injured in an accident. She suffered a violent nervous shock with serious physical consequences. Wright J held that the defendant had "wilfully done an act calculated to cause physical harm to the [claimant] – that is to say, to infringe her legal right to personal safety, and has in fact thereby caused physical harm to her". With no justification alleged, that stated a good cause of action. Wright J *imputed* the intention: the statement was so plainly likely to produce grave effects that an intention to produce them had to be imputed.

**The current test** (*Rhodes v OPO* [2015] UKSC 32, Baroness Hale and Lord Toulson) has three elements:
1. **Conduct element.** The defendant must use "words or conduct directed towards the claimant for which there is no justification or reasonable excuse". The burden of proof is on the claimant.
   - The conduct may be directed at a group: shouting "fire" in a cinema is directed at the audience.
   - When the publication is to a wide audience, justification is judged against that audience, not the claimant alone.
   - "The right to report the truth is justification in itself." It is hard to imagine liability for speech that is not deceptive, threatening or possibly abusive. Whether abusive but truthful words could be actionable was left open.
2. **Mental element.** The defendant must *intend* to cause physical harm or severe mental or emotional distress.
   - **Imputed intention is abolished**. It is a "vestige of a previous age", and the court contrasted it with *inferring* intention as a matter of fact.
   - **Recklessness is not enough**. This departs from the suggestion in *Wainwright* that recklessness might do.
   - It is enough to intend *severe distress* that in fact results in a recognised illness. The court drew a loose analogy with the eggshell skull rule.
   - Lord Neuberger added that some statements have consequences so obvious that the maker "cannot realistically say that those consequences are unintended". That approach was applied in *C v WH* [2015] EWHC 2687 (QB), a case of a school staff member grooming a pupil (Steele).
3. **Consequence element.** The claimant must suffer physical harm or a **recognised psychiatric illness**. Emotional distress alone is not enough, and the tort is **not actionable per se**.

The court said, obiter, that these three elements together contain the tort sufficiently. A separate requirement of "extreme, flagrant or outrageous" conduct, as in Canada, is therefore unnecessary.

### B. Protection from Harassment Act 1997
1. **s 1(1)** says a person must not pursue a course of conduct (a) "which amounts to harassment of another", and (b) "which he knows or ought to know amounts to harassment of the other".
2. **s 1(2)** sets the knowledge test: a person ought to know if "a reasonable person in possession of the same information would think the course of conduct amounted to harassment".
3. **s 1(3)** provides defences where the person shows the conduct was (a) pursued to prevent or detect crime, (b) pursued under any enactment or rule of law, or (c) reasonable in the particular circumstances.
4. **s 3** makes an actual or apprehended breach of s 1(1) actionable in civil proceedings by the victim. Damages may cover "any anxiety caused by the harassment and any financial loss". The primary remedy is an **injunction**.
5. **s 7** deals with interpretation:
   - harassing includes "alarming the person or causing the person distress" (s 7(2));
   - a "course of conduct" in relation to a single person needs conduct on **at least two occasions** (s 7(3));
   - "conduct" includes speech;
   - the victim must be an individual.
6. **Seriousness threshold.** Mere irritation or annoyance is not enough. The conduct must be "oppressive and unacceptable". In *Majrowski v Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Trust* [2006] UKHL 34, Lord Nicholls said that to cross from regrettable to unacceptable, the misconduct must be grave enough to sustain criminal liability under s 2.
7. **Course of conduct is a question of fact.** In *James v DPP* [2009] EWHC 2925 (Admin) the incidents had to be similar in type and context, and fewer incidents spread further apart are less likely to qualify. The test was read broadly, to cover cases where the victim had a duty to contact the defendant.
8. **Who can sue.** In *Levi v Bates* [2015] EWCA Civ 206, the defendant's campaign targeted the claimant's husband and included publishing a shared address and phone number. The wife could sue. Claims extend beyond the targeted individual to those "foreseeably, and directly, harmed by the course of targeted conduct ... to the extent that they could properly be described as victims of it" (Briggs LJ). Sympathetic distress alone is not enough.
9. **Damage and foreseeability.** In *Jones v Ruth* [2011] EWCA Civ 804, personal injury caused by harassment during neighbouring building works was recoverable without proof that personal injury was foreseeable. Once harassment is proved, the defendant is liable for the injury and loss that flow from it (Patten LJ).
10. **Crime-prevention defence.** In *Hayes v Willoughby* [2013] UKSC 17, the s 1(3)(a) defence was held to carry an implied requirement of *rationality*, including good faith, a logical connection with the evidence, and no arbitrariness. Rationality is a minimum objective standard that is less demanding than reasonableness. Lord Reed dissented.
11. **Vicarious liability and corporate defendants.**
    - An employer may be vicariously liable under the Act (*Majrowski*). There is no vicarious liability for unconnected individual acts by different employees with no common purpose (*Daniels v MPC* [2006] EWHC 1622).
    - A company can itself satisfy the "ought to know" test. *Ferguson v British Gas Trading* [2009] EWCA Civ 46 involved computer-generated threatening bills. *Roberts v Bank of Scotland* [2013] EWCA Civ 882 involved 547 automated calls.

## Key authorities
- *Wilkinson v Downton* [1897] 2 QB 57: a false report of a husband's injury caused nervous shock and was actionable. It distinguished *Victorian Railways Commissioners v Coultas* (1888) because the act was wilful. A small sum for the train fare was also recovered in deceit.
- *Janvier v Sweeney* [1919] 2 KB 316: mentioned in *Rhodes* and *Wainwright* as following *Wilkinson*.
- *Wainwright v Home Office* [2003] UKHL 53: a mother and son were strip-searched in breach of prison rules on a visit and were distressed. The claim failed because there was no sufficient intention and no recognised psychiatric harm. Lord Hoffmann left open recovery for intentionally caused distress but said "imputed intention will not do". The case also held there is no general common law tort of invasion of privacy.
- *Rhodes v OPO* [2015] UKSC 32: a pianist's autobiography described the childhood sexual abuse he suffered, and an injunction was sought on behalf of his vulnerable son. It failed on all three elements: the book was justified (freedom of expression), there was no intention, and the consequence element was not made out. The Court of Appeal's partial injunction, which allowed only a "bowdlerised" version, was set aside. This is the leading restatement.
- *Wong v Parkside Health NHS Trust* [2003] 3 All ER 932: workplace bullying. There was no common law harassment tort before the 1997 Act, and *Wilkinson* had not been extended to distress.
- *C v WH* [2015] EWHC 2687 (QB): an obvious consequence was treated as intended.
- *Majrowski* [2006] UKHL 34: vicarious liability under the PHA, and the "oppressive and unacceptable" threshold.
- *Levi v Bates* [2015] EWCA Civ 206: indirect victims who are foreseeably and directly harmed can sue.
- *James v DPP* [2009] EWHC 2925 (Admin): what counts as a course of conduct.
- *Jones v Ruth* [2011] EWCA Civ 804: no extra foreseeability requirement for damage.
- *Hayes v Willoughby* [2013] UKSC 17: rationality limit on the crime-prevention defence.
- *Ferguson v British Gas* [2009] EWCA Civ 46; *Roberts v Bank of Scotland* [2013] EWCA Civ 882: harassment by corporate systems.
- *Raymond v Young* [2015] EWCA Civ 456: separate damages for distress under the PHA alongside nuisance amenity damages were double counting (see the private-nuisance note).
- Protection from Harassment Act 1997 ss 1, 3, 7.

## Debate and critique
- **Intention.** Lord Hoffmann in *Wainwright* said Wright J "devised a concept of imputed intention which sailed as close to negligence as he felt he could go", because *Coultas* stood in the way of liability for careless shock. *Rhodes* removed imputation and recklessness. Steele notes the door may remain open through Lord Neuberger's "obvious consequences" approach. That approach resembles the US Restatement's "substantial certainty" form of intention.
- **Distress as damage.** Academics have supported extending *Wilkinson* to distress, and in the US the equivalent tort covers "severe emotional distress" from outrageous acts. English law declined to do this (*Wong*, *Wainwright*, *Rhodes*). Steele observes that the intention required (to cause severe distress) and the damage required (illness) do not precisely match.
- **Free speech.** *Rhodes* makes justification central and treats truthful speech as almost always justified. Dickinson's case comment ("A Ruritanian ordeal", (2015) 131 LQR 542). further reading also includes Lunney (2002) on the practical-joke context of *Wilkinson*, Bridgeman and Jones (1994) on harassing and outrageous acts, and Conaghan (1996) on gendered harm and remedying sexual harassment.
- **Statute versus common law.** The PHA goes further than the common law in two ways: distress is recoverable and the knowledge test is objective. Its limits are the course-of-conduct requirement (a single serious incident will not do) and the seriousness threshold borrowed from the criminal offence. *Khorasandjian v Bush* [1993] QB 727 had tried to protect a harassed non-owner through nuisance. That route was rejected in *Hunter v Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655, partly because the 1997 Act was coming (see the private-nuisance note).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was the harm **direct**? If so, consider battery or assault first. If indirect, for example words or a hoax, go to *Wilkinson*.
2. **Conduct.** Were words or conduct *directed at* the claimant, or at a group that includes them? Was there any justification or excuse? Truthful publications to the public are usually justified (*Rhodes*).
3. **Mental element.** Is there evidence of actual intention to cause physical harm or *severe* distress? Can it be inferred as a matter of fact from obvious consequences (Lord Neuberger; *C v WH*)? Recklessness or a joke "gone wrong" with no such intention is not enough.
4. **Consequence.** Is there physical injury or a *recognised psychiatric illness*? If there is only distress, the claim fails at common law, so turn to the PHA.
5. **PHA 1997.**
   - Are there at least two incidents of a similar type and context (s 7(3); *James*)?
   - Is the conduct "oppressive and unacceptable" (*Majrowski*)?
   - Would a reasonable person with the same information think it was harassment (s 1(2))?
   - Is the claimant a direct victim (*Levi v Bates*)?
   - Do any s 1(3) defences apply? For crime prevention, check the rationality limit (*Hayes*).
   - Remedies: an injunction, plus damages for anxiety, financial loss and injury (*Jones v Ruth*).
   - Is there an employer to hold vicariously liable (*Majrowski*)?
6. **Traps.**
   - Citing *Wainwright*'s suggestion that recklessness suffices. *Rhodes* has overtaken it.
   - Treating a single incident as a course of conduct.
   - Double recovery of distress damages alongside nuisance (*Raymond v Young*).
