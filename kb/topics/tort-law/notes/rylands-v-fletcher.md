# The Rule in Rylands v Fletcher

## Overview
The rule in *Rylands v Fletcher* imposes liability, without proof of negligence, on an occupier who brings something dangerous onto their land in the course of a non-natural use, when it escapes and causes foreseeable damage to a neighbour's land. Private nuisance deals with *ongoing* interference. *Rylands* protects against an **isolated escape** from a neighbour's land. In England & Wales it is now a distinct branch of private nuisance (*Cambridge Water*; *Transco*; *Northumbrian Water v McAlpine* [2014] EWCA Civ 685). It does not apply in Scotland. In Australia it has been absorbed into negligence (*Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* (1994) 179 CLR 520). The rule is a staple essay topic because it is a rare example of strict liability at common law that the courts have hemmed in so heavily that Lord Hoffmann said the intellectual effort spent on it "has brought forth a mouse" (*Transco*).

## The law

### The original rule
*Rylands v Fletcher* (Exchequer Chamber (1866) LR 1 Ex 265; House of Lords (1868) LR 3 HL 330). The defendant mill owners had independent contractors build a reservoir. The contractors found old mine shafts but did not seal them properly. When the reservoir was filled, water flooded through the shafts into the claimant's mine. The defendants themselves were not negligent.

Blackburn J held: "the person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape." He suggested defences of the claimant's default, vis major and act of God.

In the House of Lords, Lord Cairns added the requirement of **non-natural use**, and Lord Cranworth confirmed that no negligence was needed.

### The elements today
commentary treats the claim as three hurdles: standing; an escape of a dangerous thing in the course of a non-natural use; and defences. The central hurdle breaks into four requirements.

**Standing.** Since *Rylands* is a branch of nuisance, *Hunter v Canary Wharf* applies. The claimant needs an interest in, or (on some authority) occupation of, the affected land. **Personal injury is not recoverable** (*Transco*, Lords Bingham and Hobhouse; Steele). *Hale v Jennings* [1938], the Chair-o-Plane case, is older authority allowing personal injury, which Steele treats as overtaken. Weir puts it this way: it is now clear "beyond even undergraduate fantasies" that the victim of personal injury cannot rely on it. Pure economic loss is also excluded (*D Pride & Partners v Institute for Animal Health* [2009] EWHC 655 (QB)).

1. **D brings onto their land, for their own purposes, something likely to do mischief if it escapes** (*Transco v Stockport MBC* [2003] UKHL 61; *Stannard v Gore* [2012] EWCA Civ 1248).
   - The thing must be *accumulated*. Natural accumulations, as in *Smith v Kenrick* (1849) or *Giles v Walker* (1890), fall outside the rule. They are now dealt with by the "measured duty" in nuisance (*Leakey*).
   - Lord Bingham in *Transco* set a **high threshold**. D must have done something which he recognised, or ought reasonably to have recognised, "as giving rise to an exceptionally high risk of danger or mischief if there should be an escape, however unlikely an escape may have been thought to be".
   - The accumulation must be for D's *own* purposes. In *Carstairs v Taylor* (1871), rainwater in guttering was collected as much for the claimant's benefit.
2. **It escapes** (*Read v J Lyons & Co* [1947] AC 156). Escape means escape "from a place where the defendant has occupation of or control over land to a place which is outside his occupation or control" (Viscount Simon). An explosion inside a munitions factory that injured an inspector on site was not an escape.
   - **Fire.** In *Stannard v Gore* the court held that the *thing brought onto the land* must escape, not a fire it starts or worsens. Escape of fire falls within the rule only where the fire itself was brought on or deliberately or negligently started. Starting a fire may well be an ordinary use. *Musgrove v Pandelis* [1919] 2 KB 43 was "relegated to a footnote" (Ward LJ). See also the Fires Prevention (Metropolis) Act 1774 s 86 (no action for a fire that "accidentally" begins).
3. **The use is non-natural** (*Rickards v Lothian* [1913] AC 263; *Cambridge Water*; *Stannard v Gore*; *Transco*).
   - Lord Moulton in *Rickards* said it must be "some special use bringing with it increased danger to others, and must not merely be the ordinary use of the land or such a use as is proper for the general benefit of the community". On the facts, a domestic water supply that overflowed when a basin was maliciously blocked was an ordinary use.
   - In *Cambridge Water*, the storage of chemicals in substantial quantities at a tannery was "cannot fall within the exception" of natural use. Lord Goff said that local employment or general benefit to the community does not make a use natural.
   - In *Transco*, Lord Bingham said "ordinary user" is a better test than "natural user". The rule applies only where the use is "extraordinary and unusual" at the relevant time and place. That is not the same as "unreasonable": a use can be out of the ordinary yet reasonable. He found little help in asking about the general benefit to the community.
   - Lord Hoffmann in *Transco* treated insurability as a guide. The question is whether the damage was something against which the occupier, or the claimant, could reasonably be expected to have insured. A water pipe serving a block of flats, larger than a domestic pipe but smaller than a main, was "a perfectly normal item of plumbing". It was not non-natural, and nothing dangerous had been accumulated.
4. **It causes foreseeable damage of the relevant type** (*Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather* [1994] 2 AC 264).
   - Lord Goff held that foreseeability of the *type* of damage is a prerequisite. He reasoned from the "likely to do mischief" wording in Blackburn J's statement and from the historical link with nuisance (Newark; *The Wagon Mound (No 2)*).
   - On the facts, a solvent (PCE) spilled in small quantities over years seeped into an aquifer and contaminated a borehole 1.3 miles away. Nobody could reasonably have foreseen that damage, so the claim failed.
   - the view, and the common reading, is that the *escape* need not be foreseeable. Only the *damage*, assuming an escape, must be. *Transco* confirms this: the criterion is met "however unforeseeable the escape" (Lord Bingham, [10]), and D is liable "even if he could not reasonably have foreseen that there would be an escape" (Lord Hoffmann, [33]).
   - Wilkinson's terms are "semi-foreseeability" (the escape is presumed) and "full foreseeability" (the escape itself must be foreseeable). Steele argues semi-foreseeability fits the strict nature of the rule better.

### Defences
The general defences of **statutory authority**, **contributory negligence** and **consent** apply. The special *Rylands* defences are:
1. **Fault of the claimant, or express or implied consent** (*Ponting v Noakes* [1894] 2 QB 281). In *Colour Quest v Total Downstream* [2009] EWHC 540 (Comm), where a carelessly overfilled fuel tank exploded, consent was accepted as a defence but was vitiated by negligence.
2. **Unforeseeable act of a stranger** (*Perry v Kendricks Transport* [1956] 1 WLR 85; *Rickards v Lothian*, where a third party maliciously blocked the basin). A *foreseeable* intervention may not count (*Shiffman v Order of St John* [1936]; *Hale v Jennings*).
3. **Act of God** (*Nichols v Marsland* (1876) 2 Ex D 1, extraordinary rainfall; *Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway* [1917] AC 556).
4. **Statutory authority.** Lord Hoffmann in *Transco* said the rule does not apply to works authorised by statute in the absence of negligence (relying on *Geddis v Bann Reservoir* (1878); see also *Dunne v NW Gas Board* [1964]). In Lord Hoffmann's analysis, statutory regimes such as Water Industry Act 1991 s 209, Environmental Protection Act 1990 s 73(6) and Nuclear Installations Act 1965 s 7 may displace the rule altogether.

### Where the rule stands (Lord Hoffmann's summary in *Transco*)
1. It is a remedy only for damage to land or interests in land, so disputes are usually between property insurers and liability insurers.
2. It does not apply to works authorised by statute, which are often the riskiest.
3. It is not very strict, since it excludes the most common causes of escapes: vandalism and unusual natural events.
4. Where none of those causes applies, negligence can usually be inferred anyway.
5. There is a broad, ill-defined "natural use" exception.

Counsel could find no reported case since the Second World War in which a *Rylands* claim had succeeded. Even so, the House refused to abolish the rule: abolition would be "too radical a step" after nearly 150 years. Lord Bingham added that abolition might upset the assumptions on which statutory strict liability schemes were enacted. In *Cambridge Water*, Lord Goff said it would "lead to a more coherent body of common law principles if the rule were to be regarded essentially as an extension of the law of nuisance to cases of isolated escapes from land" (306).

### Overlap with nuisance and negligence
Lord Walker in *Transco* [96] described nuisance (and so *Rylands*) as a "condominium" with negligence. *Sedleigh-Denfield v O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880 can be analysed three ways:
- the blocked pipe as an ongoing state of affairs, which is private nuisance;
- the flood as an "escape" of accumulated water, which is *Rylands*;
- a failure to take reasonable steps, which is negligence.

Lord Sumption in *Woodland v Swimming Teachers Association* [2013] UKSC 66 described *Rylands* as an example of a **non-delegable duty** arising from the antecedent relationship between neighbouring occupiers. Steele notes he did not address the fact that *Rylands* requires no negligence, not even the contractor's.

## Key authorities
- *Rylands v Fletcher* (1866) LR 1 Ex 265; (1868) LR 3 HL 330: a reservoir flooded a neighbouring mine. Strict liability for the escape of a thing likely to do mischief; non-natural use added by Lord Cairns.
- *Read v J Lyons* [1947] AC 156: an explosion inside a factory was not an escape, and personal injury was doubted.
- *Rickards v Lothian* [1913] AC 263: the "ordinary use" test, and the act of a stranger.
- *Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather* [1994] 2 AC 264: foreseeability of damage type required. Chemical storage is non-natural. *Rylands* is part of nuisance. Lord Goff left strict liability for high-risk activities to Parliament, citing Law Commission Report No 32 (1970).
- *Transco v Stockport MBC* [2003] UKHL 61: a burst water pipe to a block of flats was ordinary use. The rule is retained but confined, with an exceptionally high risk threshold (Lord Bingham), an insurability guide (Lord Hoffmann), and no personal injury.
- *Stannard v Gore* [2012] EWCA Civ 1248: fire spreading to neighbouring property. Fire cases rarely fall within the rule; *Musgrove* sidelined.
- *Nichols v Marsland* (1876); *Greenock Corporation v Caledonian Railway* [1917]: act of God.
- *Perry v Kendricks Transport* [1956]: unforeseeable act of a stranger.
- *Ponting v Noakes* [1894]: the claimant's own fault.
- *Burnie Port Authority v General Jones* (1994) 179 CLR 520 (Aus HC): *Rylands* absorbed into negligence through a non-delegable duty.
- *Northumbrian Water v Sir Robert McAlpine* [2014] EWCA Civ 685: an isolated escape is actionable only within *Rylands*, a distinct branch of nuisance.
- Fires Prevention (Metropolis) Act 1774 s 86.

## Debate and critique
- **Origins and rationale.** Lord Hoffmann, drawing on Simpson's history, linked the decision to public anxiety after the Bradfield reservoir burst in 1864, which killed about 250 people. He read *Rylands* as "an isolated victory for the internalisers", that is, for making enterprises bear the risks they create, against a 19th-century trend towards no liability without fault. Simpson describes Blackburn J's radical view that strict liability was the norm and fault the exception. Lord Hobhouse defended the rule as soundly based, arguing that the confusion comes from later additions. Steele replies that this does not explain arbitrary limits such as the escape requirement.
- **Nuisance or a general principle?** Lord Goff rejected developing *Rylands* into strict liability for "ultra-hazardous" activities, as in the US (Restatement (Second) §519). He gave three reasons: *Read v Lyons* blocks it; the Law Commission rejected such a test in 1970; and environmental liability is better left to statute. Steele thinks the third reason is the weakest. Fleming said *Read v Lyons* "prematurely stunted the development of a general theory of strict liability". Nolan, "The Distinctiveness of Rylands v Fletcher" (2005), and Murphy, "The Merits of Rylands v Fletcher" (2004).
- **Non-natural use.** Newark showed that Lord Cairns used "natural" in three different senses. Since *Rickards* it has come to mean "ordinary". Steele notes this resembles Bramwell B's "give and take" in *Bamford v Turnley*. Lord Hoffmann's insurability test gives the rule a policy basis but shrinks it further. Steele asks who *ought* to bear the cost of insurance, and Steele and Merkin (2013) discuss insurance between neighbours after *Stannard*.
- **How strict is it really?** Broad defences (act of God, stranger, statutory authority) and the foreseeability requirement weaken it. Thayer (1916) argued that negligence, which demands high care for dangerous activities, would reach the same results in most cases. That reasoning underlies *Burnie*. Weir sees *Read v Lyons* as the corollary of *Donoghue v Stevenson*: if negligence is normally sufficient for liability, it is also normally necessary.
- **Modern uses.** further reading includes Costello (2014) on reviving *Rylands* for fracking damage and Meeran (2021) on litigation against multinationals.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is this an isolated escape?** If the interference is ongoing, use private nuisance. If the problem is carelessness, consider negligence as well. Characterise "the nuisance" carefully (*Sedleigh-Denfield*).
2. **Claimant.** Do they have an interest in the land affected? Reject personal injury and pure economic loss heads. Property damage and land damage are recoverable.
3. **Defendant.** Are they the occupier or controller of the land from which the thing escaped? Was the thing accumulated for their own purposes, not naturally present?
4. **Dangerous thing.** Did D recognise, or ought D to have recognised, an *exceptionally high risk* if it escaped (*Transco*)? Water in ordinary pipes is not enough. Large chemical stores, explosives, or a substantial reservoir may be.
5. **Escape.** Did it cross from land D controls to land outside D's control (*Read*)? For fire, did the thing itself escape, or was fire brought on or started deliberately or negligently (*Stannard*)?
6. **Non-natural use.** Was the use extraordinary and unusual for that place and time (*Transco*; *Rickards*)? Public or community benefit does not help (*Cambridge Water*). Was the risk one people commonly insure against (Lord Hoffmann)?
7. **Foreseeability.** Assuming an escape, was damage of this *type* foreseeable (*Cambridge Water*)? The escape itself need not be foreseeable (*Transco*).
8. **Defences.** Act of God (truly extraordinary nature), unforeseeable act of a stranger, the claimant's fault or consent, statutory authority (no liability without negligence), contributory negligence. Check whether a statutory scheme displaces the rule.
9. **Conclude realistically.** Successful claims are rare. Run negligence and nuisance in the alternative.
10. **Traps.**
    - Claiming personal injury.
    - Treating an explosion within the premises as an escape.
    - Requiring the escape to be foreseeable.
    - Forgetting that *Transco* confirmed the rule survives in England despite *Burnie*.
