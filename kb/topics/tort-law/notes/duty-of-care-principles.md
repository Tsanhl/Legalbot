# Duty of Care – Basic Principles

## Overview
Duty of care is the first hurdle in negligence. If the defendant owed the claimant no duty in respect of the kind of loss suffered, there is no liability, however careless the defendant was and however much harm resulted. The law is England and Wales. It has moved through several stages:
1. Lord Atkin's general "neighbour principle" (*Donoghue*, 1932);
2. Lord Wilberforce's universal two-stage test (*Anns*, 1978);
3. *Caparo* (1990), with its three "ingredients" and its call for incremental development;
4. the Supreme Court's reinterpretation in *Michael* (2015), *Robinson* (2018) and *Poole BC v GN* (2019).

On the current approach there is no single "Caparo test". Established categories are simply applied, and only genuinely novel cases call for incremental development by analogy.

## The law

### 1. Not all harm is actionable
The starting point is that the world is full of harm for which the law gives no remedy. Lord Rodger in *D v East Berkshire Community Health NHS Trust* [2005] UKHL 23 at [100] gave the examples of a fiancé whose fiancée leaves him for his best friend and who becomes clinically depressed. However badly he was treated, no duty of care was owed and no damages are recoverable. Such intimate matters are left to the individuals (lecture two). So whether a duty is owed is crucial, and the courts have struggled to find one test that answers it.

### 2. *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562: the neighbour principle
- You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour. Your "neighbours" are persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.
- Proximity in Lord Atkin's sense is not mere physical closeness but "close and direct relations". It was intended as an *additional* limit on foreseeability. In *Donoghue* it was linked to the absence of a reasonable opportunity for intermediate inspection of the goods (Steele ch 4).

### 3. *Anns v Merton LBC* [1978] AC 728: the two-stage test (now overruled)
- *Facts* (Steele): lessees of flats sued the local authority after cracks appeared. The foundations were 2ft 6in deep, not the 3ft or more shown in the approved plans. The claim was put on the alternative bases of a negligent failure to inspect or a negligent inspection.
- *Lord Wilberforce's test* (at 751–2). Building on *Donoghue*, *Hedley Byrne* and *Dorset Yacht*, it is no longer necessary to fit the facts into an earlier duty situation. Instead:
  1. Is there sufficient proximity or neighbourhood such that carelessness may be likely to cause damage? If so, a **prima facie duty** arises.
  2. Are there any considerations which ought to **negative, reduce or limit** the scope of the duty, the class of persons owed, or the damages?
- In the materials's words: a duty arises wherever harm is reasonably foreseeable unless policy reasons deny or restrict it.
- *Anns* was overruled by the House of Lords in *Murphy v Brentwood DC* [1991] 1 AC 398. Courts had already departed from its approach in *Caparo* (Steele).

### 4. *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605: the three ingredients and incrementalism
- *Facts*: auditors prepared F plc's annual accounts under ss 236–237 Companies Act 1985. The claimants bought shares, eventually taking the company over, relying on the published accounts, which were inaccurate. The Court of Appeal found a duty to them as existing shareholders. The House of Lords held **no duty** was owed either to them as investors or as shareholders. This was pure economic loss caused by a statement, so a *Hedley Byrne* "special relationship" was needed and foreseeability was not enough.
- *Lord Bridge* (617–18): in addition to foreseeability of damage, the necessary ingredients are a relationship of **"proximity" or "neighbourhood"** and that the situation is one where the court considers it **"fair, just and reasonable"** to impose a duty of a given scope. These concepts are not precise enough to be practical tests. They are "little more than convenient labels". The law has moved towards "the more traditional categorisation of distinct and recognisable situations".
- He endorsed Brennan J in *Sutherland Shire Council v Heyman* (1985) 60 ALR 1, 43–44: the law should develop novel categories of negligence "incrementally and by analogy with established categories". It should not develop by a "massive extension of a prima facie duty of care" restrained only by indefinable negativing considerations.
- He also stressed that the law treats kinds of damage differently: injury to person or property is one thing, pure economic loss is quite another.
- *Lord Oliver* (632–5):
  - Proximity is "no more than a label", describing circumstances from which, pragmatically, courts conclude a duty exists.
  - The three requirements are often just "facets of the same thing".
  - Searching for a single formula is "to pursue a will-o'-the wisp".
  - Cases can be broadly grouped, for example failure to prevent harm by a third party (*Dorset Yacht*, *Smith v Littlewoods*), negligent performance of a statutory duty, and negligent statements.
- *How it was read*: *Caparo* was widely treated as a "three-stage test" (lecture two). The three questions were:
  1. Was it reasonably foreseeable that a failure to take care could damage the claimant (see, eg, *Palsgraf v Long Island Railroad Co* (1928) 162 NE 99)?
  2. Was there proximity between C and D?
  3. Is it fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty?
- Steele stresses that the three factors were not meant to operate as stages. No factor takes priority, and policy became part of the positive case for a duty rather than a mere limiting factor. *Caparo* also reversed any "presumption" of duty from *Anns*: there must be positive reasons to impose a duty in a novel case.

### 5. *Robinson*, *Darnley* and *Poole*: the current approach
1. **There is no general "Caparo test".** Lord Reed in *Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2018] UKSC 4 at [21] adopted Lord Toulson in *Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police* [2015] AC 1732 at [106]. The view that one *Caparo* test applies to every claim mistakes "the whole point" of *Caparo*. *Caparo* repudiated the idea of a single test in favour of precedent and incremental development by analogy.
2. **Established categories: just apply them.** Many duty situations are settled: motorists to other road users, manufacturers to consumers, employers to employees, doctors to patients. Where a duty is established, justice and reasonableness have already been built into the principle. It is "unnecessary and inappropriate" to reconsider whether the duty is fair, just and reasonable (*Robinson* at [26]–[27]; *Darnley* at [15]). That is subject only to the Supreme Court departing from a line of authority. Nor can "justice and reasonableness" be used to throw out established principles. Lord Reed cited Hobhouse LJ in *Perrett v Collins*: a defendant within an established category cannot escape by appealing to "some vaguer concept of justice or fairness".
3. **Novel cases: incremental development by analogy.** Only where established principles give no answer do courts go further. They then identify the legally significant features of the closest analogous authorities and weigh the reasons for and against liability to decide whether a duty would be "just and reasonable" (*Robinson* at [27], [29]). Lord Mance at [83] accepted that the *Caparo* considerations are material here.
4. **Specific tests for specific problems.** Binding authority answers the duty question where it exists. More precise tests govern particular areas. For psychiatric harm, for example, courts look to *Alcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire* [1992] and *Page v Smith* [1996] rather than *Caparo* (lecture two). In most cases duty is not in doubt.
5. **Organising principles emerging after *Robinson*/*Poole*** (Steele):
   - the distinction between causing harm and failing to confer a benefit (acts and omissions);
   - the distinction between physical injury and pure economic loss;
   - "assumption of responsibility" as an exception to the no-duty-to-benefit rule.
   Public authorities are generally subject to the same principles as private persons unless legislation requires otherwise (*Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25, summarising what *Robinson* clarified).

## Key authorities
- ***Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562**: manufacturer–consumer duty, the neighbour principle, rejection of the privity fallacy. See introduction-to-tort.md.
- ***Anns v Merton LBC* [1978] AC 728**: universal two-stage test (foreseeability/proximity giving a prima facie duty, then policy negativing it). Overruled by *Murphy v Brentwood* [1991]. Now "part of legal history" (Steele).
- ***Ross v Caunters* [1980] Ch 297**: an example of *Anns* thinking. Megarry V-C found a prima facie duty from solicitors to an intended beneficiary because it was "obvious" carelessness would harm her, treating proximity as a matter of foreseeability. Its simple approach was said in *White v Jones* [1995] 2 AC 207 not to survive *Caparo* (Steele).
- ***Home Office v Dorset Yacht Co* [1970] AC 1004**: one of Lord Wilberforce's "trilogy" supporting a general approach. It is now relied on as a control/third-party case. See omissions-and-third-parties.md.
- ***Caparo Industries plc v Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605**: no duty from auditors to investors or shareholders buying shares. It set out the three ingredients and incrementalism. It is now read as standing chiefly for incremental development.
- ***Marc Rich & Co AG v Bishop Rock Marine Co Ltd (The Nicholas H)* [1996] AC 211**: listed in the materials two's case list. No further detail is given in the sources read.
- ***Customs and Excise Commissioners v Barclays Bank plc* [2007] 1 AC 181**: listed in the materials two. No further detail is given in the sources read.
- ***Bourhill v Young* [1942] 2 All ER 396**: listed in the materials two (foreseeability of the claimant). No further detail is given in the sources read.
- ***Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police* [2018] UKSC 4**:
  - *Facts*: two officers arresting a suspected drug dealer on a busy Huddersfield shopping street fell, with the suspect, onto a frail 76-year-old woman, injuring her.
  - *Courts below*: the trial judge found negligence but no duty, because of *Hill*. The Court of Appeal applied *Caparo*, found no duty and added that there was no breach.
  - *Supreme Court*: reversed. This was not a novel case. It was the application of established principles about **positive acts causing physical injury** ([30]). Because there was a reasonably foreseeable risk of injury to pedestrians close to the suspect, especially vulnerable ones, if the arrest was attempted, the officers owed them a duty. Reasonable foreseeability of injury was sufficient ([74]).
  - *Significance*: it ended the "Caparo test" reading and placed *Hill* and *Michael* in the different category of omissions.
- ***Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50**:
  - *Facts*: the claimant had a head injury from an assault. An A&E receptionist told him he would wait 4–5 hours to be seen. He left after 19 minutes, collapsed later and had an extradural haematoma, leaving permanent brain damage and hemiplegia.
  - *Held*: not a novel situation. It fell squarely within the established duty of those who run casualty departments to take reasonable care not to cause patients physical injury (*Barnett*, 435–436). The scope of that duty extends to not giving misleading information that may foreseeably cause physical injury ([16]).
  - Lord Lloyd-Jones at [15]: absent a "general principle" or "universal touchstone", the common law uses established categories.
- ***Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales Police* [2015] UKSC 2**: Lord Toulson's [106] statement that *Caparo* repudiated a single test. Facts and holding are in public-authorities-duty-of-care.md.
- ***Poole BC v GN* [2019] UKSC 25**:
  - *Facts*: a family, including a child "in need" under s 17 Children Act 1989, was housed by the council next to a neighbouring family with a known history of anti-social behaviour and suffered harassment and abuse.
  - *Held*: no arguable duty.
  - *Lord Reed's three points clarified by Robinson*: (1) *Caparo* recommends incrementalism, not a tripartite test; (2) harming the claimant is distinct from failing to protect the claimant; (3) public authorities are subject to the same principles as private persons unless statute requires otherwise.
  - Lord Reed also said *Caparo* had been "widely misunderstood" as an elaboration of *Anns*, which led to policy evaluations courts were "not well equipped" to make.
- ***Steel v NRAM* [2018] UKSC 13**: the Supreme Court again said *Caparo* did not set out a three-part test. This was in a pure economic loss case, where the emphasis was on assumption of responsibility (Steele ch 4).
- ***Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [1989] 1 AC 53**, ***Smith v Chief Constable of Sussex Police* [2008] UKHL 50**, ***D v East Berkshire* [2005] UKHL 23**: listed in the materials two. See public-authorities-duty-of-care.md.

## Debate and critique
- **Kidner on proximity under *Anns*.** In *Anns*, Lord Wilberforce made proximity a function of foreseeability. In *Donoghue* it had been an extra, independent limit. Kidner, 'Resiling from the Anns Principle' (1987) 7 LS 319, argued this hid the historical and policy reasoning that pre-*Anns* cases had done under the "coded" heading of proximity (Steele).
- **The prima facie duty problem.** Under *Anns*:
  - negligence threatened to spread into unpredictable areas (Ibbetson's "ocean of liability");
  - established restrictions were reopened;
  - "no duty" outcomes came to need exceptional justification, sometimes framed as "immunities" (*Hill*), which later caused trouble under Article 6 ECHR.
- **Principle and policy.** Steele uses Dworkin's distinction: principles describe rights, and are generalisable and individuated; policies describe collective goals. Foreseeability fits "principle". Post-*Caparo* proximity and "fair, just and reasonable" are much more policy-laden. Lord Nicholls in *Fairchild* [2003] said the law "must be principled" and that the basis of distinctions must be transparent. Millner (*Negligence in Modern Law*, 1967) argued the duty concept works at two levels: a fact-based level (foreseeability, largely dispensable) and a notional, policy-based level that defines the tort's scope and responds to social change. Nolan, 'Deconstructing the Duty of Care' (2013) 129 LQR 559, similarly separates factual and notional duty, but argues "duty" has become an obstacle to understanding the law.
- **Witting**: Steele cites Witting's description of the "irresistible force" against generalisation shown in *Caparo*. Lecture reading: Witting, 'Duty of Care: An Analytical Approach' (2005) 25 OJLS 33.
- **Is *Robinson* principled or just categorical?** Steele argues Lord Reed's "principle" is not content-based in Dworkin's sense. It means settled doctrine applied without wide-ranging reasoning about consequences, while the reasons why categories became settled stay unexamined.
- **Lord Mance's concurrence in *Robinson***:
  - Courts recognising duties do make policy choices. He quoted Lord Reid: "we do not believe in fairy tales any more".
  - He questioned how the *boundaries* of an "established category" are fixed. Why is "positive act causing physical injury" the category, rather than "injury caused in the investigation and suppression of crime"?
  - He pointed to *Smith v Ministry of Defence* [2014] AC 52 (combat immunity) as a case treated as novel even though it was about positive acts.
- **Lord Hughes in *Robinson*** saw policy as the ultimate reason for no police duty to victims. Lord Reed replied that the absence of such a duty rests on the long-established principle of no liability for harm caused by third parties without a recognised exception. He added that courts are "not policy-making bodies" but must judge consequences in novel cases.
- **Morgan, 'Nonfeasance and the End of Policy?' (2019) 35 PN 32**:
  - the acts/omissions principle "needs to be explained and justified, not just postulated as an axiom";
  - other legal systems take different views on rescue;
  - public authorities have extensive positive duties in public law and under Article 2 ECHR;
  - "assumption of responsibility" is vacuous and will be "another conduit for policy reasoning".
  He urges courts to analyse openly all the arguments for and against liability.
- **The rest of the problem.** *Robinson* and *Poole* do not say how to decide when to take an incremental step. Steele predicts that "pragmatism will continue to be on the menu". Indeed, months after *Robinson*, *James-Bowen v Commissioner of Police* [2018] UKSC 40 treated policy considerations as decisive in a novel case (Steele ch 5).
- **Further reading (lecture two)**: McBride, 'Duties of Care—Do They Really Exist?' (2004) 24 OJLS 417; Morgan, *Great Debates in Tort Law* (2022) chs 6–7; Steele, 'Duty of Care and Ethics of Care: Irreconcilable Difference?' in Richardson and Rackley (eds), *Feminist Perspectives on Tort Law* (2012). Steele also lists Goudkamp, 'A Revolution in Duty of Care?' (2015) 131 LQR 519, and Beurmann, 'The New Adventures of the Three Stage Test' (2019) 35 PN 247.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **What kind of harm?** Physical injury or property damage from a positive act is usually an established category. Psychiatric harm, pure economic loss, omissions/third-party harm and public authority defendants are "special duty problems" with their own rules.
2. **Is there binding authority or an established category?** Driver–road user, manufacturer–consumer, employer–employee, doctor/hospital–patient (*Darnley*), police officers whose positive acts injure bystanders (*Robinson*). If so, state the duty and move on. Do **not** run *Caparo* (*Robinson* [26]–[27]; *Darnley* [15]).
3. **Check scope.** Does the established duty extend to this kind of harm or conduct? For example, *Darnley* extended the A&E duty to misleading information about waiting times.
4. **If the situation is novel:**
   - Find the closest analogies and identify their legally significant features.
   - Ask whether extending the duty is an incremental step.
   - Weigh the reasons for and against, including reasonable foreseeability, proximity and whether a duty would be fair, just and reasonable (*Caparo*, as reinterpreted by *Robinson* [29]).
   - Mention policy arguments (floodgates, defensive practice, conflict with statutory functions, alternative remedies) as part of that judgment.
5. **Act or omission?** If D failed to protect C from harm caused by a third party or by nature, go to omissions-and-third-parties.md. The default is no duty unless an exception applies.
6. **Public body?** It is subject to ordinary principles (*Poole*). See public-authorities-duty-of-care.md.
7. **Traps**:
   - calling *Caparo* a "three-stage test" without qualification;
   - applying *Caparo* to a straightforward road-accident or medical case;
   - citing *Anns* as good law;
   - forgetting that foreseeability alone does not create a duty in omissions or economic-loss cases.
