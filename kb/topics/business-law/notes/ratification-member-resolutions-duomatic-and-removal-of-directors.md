# Ratification and member decision-making

## Overview
Members take decisions by ordinary resolution (more than 50% of votes cast), special resolution (at least 75%) or, in private companies, by written resolution. Informal unanimous assent can bind a company under the *Duomatic* principle. Ratification of a director's breach is possible only by member resolution under s 239, with the director and connected members' votes excluded. A director can be removed by ordinary resolution at a meeting whatever the articles or service contract say (s 168), though *Bushell v Faith* shows that weighted voting rights can make removal impossible.

## The law
**Types of resolution (Part 13).**
- s 281(1): a private company's members decide by written resolution (Chapter 2) or at a meeting (Chapter 3); a public company's members must meet (s 281(2)). Where the Act requires a resolution but does not say which kind, an ordinary resolution suffices unless the articles require more (s 281(3)). s 281(4) preserves the rules about things done informally and about estoppel from alleging a resolution was not duly passed; this keeps the *Duomatic* principle alive.
- s 282: an ordinary resolution is passed by a simple majority (on a show of hands, a majority of members present; on a poll, a majority of votes). s 283: a special resolution requires a majority of not less than 75%. s 284: on a show of hands each member has one vote (proxies as the Act provides); on a poll, one vote per share (or, for a company without share capital, per member) unless the articles provide otherwise. The articles may confer enhanced voting rights on particular shares or on particular resolutions; this is the foundation of *Bushell v Faith*.
- **Written resolutions (private companies only)**: ss 288-300. A resolution may be proposed by the directors (s 291) or by members holding at least 5% of the total voting rights, or any lower percentage set by the articles (s 292). A written resolution cannot be used to remove a director under s 168 or an auditor under s 510 (s 288(2)). A member signifies agreement by sending to the company an authenticated document (s 296); it may not be revoked (s 296(3)); the resolution is passed when the required majority of eligible members agree (s 296(4)); the resolution lapses after the period in the articles or, if none, 28 days from circulation (s 297). An ordinary resolution needs more than 50% of eligible votes, a special resolution not less than 75%, calculated by reference to total eligible votes, not votes cast (s 282, s 283).
- Meetings: directors may call (s 302); members holding at least 5% of paid-up voting capital can require the directors to call a general meeting (s 303); the directors must call it within 21 days of the request and hold it within 28 days after the notice (s 304); a general meeting requires at least 14 days' notice, except a public company AGM, which requires 21 days (s 307(1)-(2)); shorter notice can be agreed by a majority in number of the members entitled to attend and vote who together hold at least 90% of the voting shares in a private company (or a higher figure up to 95% set by the articles) and 95% in a public company, but not for a public company AGM, which needs all members' agreement (ss 307(4)-(6), 337(2)).

**Ratification (s 239).** Where a director has acted in negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust, the company may ratify the conduct by member resolution (s 239(2)). The votes of the director (if a member) and of any member connected with him are disregarded, both for written resolutions (those members are not "eligible members") and at meetings, though they may attend and speak and count towards the quorum (s 239(3)-(4)). The section applies to former directors and shadow directors, and, for this purpose, the exclusion in s 252(3) of persons who are themselves directors does not apply (s 239(5)). A director who is a member and a co-director who is a connected person are both caught. s 239(6) preserves the validity of a decision taken by unanimous consent of the members (the statutory hook for the *Duomatic* principle) and the directors' power to agree not to sue or to settle claims; s 239(7) preserves other enactments and common law rules imposing additional requirements for valid ratification, and rules about acts incapable of ratification. The common law limits still apply: a majority cannot ratify a fraud on the minority, or an act that would be an illegality or that would infringe the company's creditors' rights where the company is insolvent (*West Mercia*, see creditor duty note).

**Authorisation in advance.** s 180(4)(a) (see directors' duties note) preserves the rules by which the company may authorise what would otherwise be a breach. Prior authorisation, if informed, is a defence rather than ratification.

**The *Duomatic* principle.** Where all the members who have the right to attend and vote at a general meeting assent to a matter within the company's powers, that agreement can bind the company even though no meeting was held and no resolution formally passed. The assent must be informed (members must know the substance of what they agree to), unanimous (all eligible members, though they need not agree at the same time), and the matter must be one the members could lawfully have decided by resolution; it cannot override a statutory requirement that cannot be dispensed with (for example a procedure designed to protect creditors or others). It applies in private companies and, with care, to public ones. Assent may be inferred from conduct, but it must be unqualified and objectively established; members who did not know the relevant facts have not assented (*EIC Services Ltd v Phipps* [2003] EWHC 1507 (Ch)). Where shares are held by a nominee, the assent of the beneficial owner who in practice makes the decisions suffices (*Ciban*). The principle has been applied to approve director remuneration, to waive notice formalities, and to authorise acts outside the directors' authority.

**Removal of directors (s 168).** A company may by ordinary resolution at a meeting remove a director before the end of his term, notwithstanding anything in any agreement between the company and him (s 168(1)). Special notice (28 days) is needed for the resolution (s 168(2); s 312) and the director has the right to make representations (s 169). The removal does not affect compensation or damages for termination of the appointment or any related appointment (s 168(5)(a)); a service contract is breached by removal and the director can claim contractual damages, subject to the rules on mitigation and the notice clause. It cannot be done by written resolution (s 288(2)(a)).

**Weighted voting.** The articles may provide that a share carries weighted votes on a resolution to remove a director. In *Bushell v Faith* [1970] AC 1099 (HL) the articles provided that a share held by a director carried three votes on a resolution to remove the holder as director; the House of Lords (Lord Morris dissenting) upheld it, so that the director holding one-third of the shares could not be removed. The section then in force (Companies Act 1948 s 184) applied "notwithstanding anything in its articles", but the majority held that Parliament had only required an ordinary resolution and had not restricted the company's freedom to attach different voting rights to shares. The decision is generally accepted as still good law under s 168, which requires an ordinary resolution "notwithstanding anything in any agreement between [the company] and [the director]". Such clauses are unsuitable for listed companies.

## Key authorities
- *Re Duomatic Ltd* [1969] 2 Ch 365 (Buckley J): payments of directors' remuneration drawn without a general meeting resolution were validated because the holders of all the voting shares had assented informally; "where it can be shown that all shareholders who have a right to attend and vote at a general meeting of the company assent to some matter which a general meeting of the company could carry into effect, that assent is as binding as a resolution in general meeting would be". A compensation payment for loss of office was not validated, because the statute required disclosure to all members, including non-voting preference shareholders, and that had not been made.
- *Ciban Management Corp v Citco (BVI) Ltd* [2020] UKPC 21, [2021] AC 122 (Lord Burrows for the Board): *Duomatic* applies to the assent of the ultimate beneficial owner who makes the decisions in place of the registered shareholder, and can confer ostensible as well as actual authority; a beneficial owner who kept himself in the background and let another act for the company could not deny assent even to a document he did not know about.
- *Bushell v Faith* [1970] AC 1099 (HL): weighted voting right on removal resolution is valid; a shareholder's protection in a quasi-partnership.
- *Russell v Northern Bank Development Corp Ltd* [1992] 1 WLR 588 (HL): an agreement among shareholders not to vote for an increase in capital without all parties' consent was valid and enforceable between them as a personal contract; the company's own undertaking in the same agreement was unenforceable as a fetter on its statutory powers.
- *Pender v Lushington* (1877) 6 Ch D 70: a member's right to vote is a personal right and the chairman cannot disregard votes.
- *Smith v Croft (No 2)* [1988] Ch 114 (Knox J): a minority shareholder could not pursue a derivative action where the majority of the independent shareholders (those not implicated in the wrong) did not want it to proceed; the forerunner of s 263(4).

## Applying it
1. Identify the decision and the required majority: ordinary, special or other (the articles may require more); check whether the company is private (written resolution possible) and whether the Act prescribes a procedure.
2. For ratification of a breach, ask: (a) is the breach of a kind the members can ratify (not illegal or ultra vires or a fraud on the minority); (b) were the votes of the director and connected members excluded (s 239(3)-(4), with "connected" per ss 252-254); (c) was there full disclosure to the members. If the majority consists only of the wrongdoer and connected persons, the ratification fails.
3. If no formal resolution was passed, ask whether *Duomatic* applies: were all voting members aware of and in agreement with the substance? If one member did not know or dissented, it does not apply.
4. For director removal: check notice and procedure under s 168, whether a weighted-vote clause in the articles exists, any shareholders' agreement, and the director's contract damages; for a quasi-partnership consider an unfair prejudice petition (see members' remedies).
5. Common traps: written resolution for removal (not allowed); counting a connected person's vote; ignoring that a *Duomatic* assent has to be informed; forgetting that a single member company can act by sole-member decisions recorded (s 357).
Everyday version: the owners of a small company can usually approve things by simply all signing a written resolution, but they cannot get rid of a director that way, and they cannot validate their own wrongdoing by outvoting a minority.

## Debates and reform
- Whether *Duomatic* should be confined to formalities, or can validate substantive decisions; whether it conflicts with creditor protection requirements.
- Whether s 239 (disregarding the director's votes) is adequate, since a director's friends who are not "connected" can still vote; and the relationship with derivative claim permission (s 263(2)(c)(ii): ratified conduct bars permission).
- Whether *Bushell v Faith* is consistent with s 168 and the policy against fettering the right of removal.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No substantive change to the Part 13 resolution and meeting rules since they came into force (2007-2009); traded companies have additional rules (for example s 307A on notice).
- Short notice agreements: see s 307(4)-(6) above.
