# Partnerships, limited liability partnerships and choosing a business vehicle

## Overview
A general partnership is not a separate legal person in England and Wales: it is a relationship between the partners, each of whom is personally liable for the firm's obligations. A limited liability partnership (LLP) is a corporate body with its own legal personality (LLPA 2000 s 1(2)) which gives members limited liability but retains partnership-style internal flexibility and tax transparency. Choosing between a sole trader, general partnership, LLP and company is mainly about liability, tax, control and disclosure.

## The law
**General partnership (Partnership Act 1890).**
- s 1(1): partnership is "the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit". Business includes every trade, occupation or profession (s 45). A partnership must involve a business, which is carried on in common (jointly), with a view to profit (intention to make profit is enough, actual profit is not).
- s 2 sets rules for determining existence. Joint ownership of property, sharing of gross returns, does not by itself create a partnership (s 2(1)-(2)). Receipt of a share of profits is prima facie evidence of partnership but does not of itself make the recipient a partner, and in particular not where it is received in repayment of a debt by instalments, as remuneration of an employee or agent, as an annuity to a deceased partner's widow or child, as interest at a profit-related rate under a written loan contract, or for the sale of goodwill (s 2(3)). The court looks at the whole relationship: intention, joint control, sharing of profit and loss, holding out, and the way the parties describe themselves.
- s 5: every partner is an agent of the firm and of his other partners for the purposes of the partnership business, and the acts of a partner done in carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm bind the firm, unless the partner has in fact no authority and the third party knows that, or does not know or believe him to be a partner.
- s 9: every partner is liable jointly with the other partners for all debts and obligations of the firm incurred while a partner. s 10: where a partner's wrongful act or omission in the ordinary course of the firm's business, or with the authority of co-partners, causes loss or injury to a non-partner, the firm is liable to the same extent as the partner. s 11 (misapplication of money received) and s 12 (liability for wrongs: joint and several) apply. Practically, the partners' personal assets are exposed to the firm's liabilities, and a partner may be sued for the whole amount of a firm's liability and seek contribution.
- s 14 (holding out): a person who by words or conduct represents himself, or knowingly permits himself to be represented, as a partner is liable as a partner to anyone who gives credit on the faith of that representation. s 17: a new partner is not liable for pre-admission debts, and a retiring partner remains liable for debts incurred before retirement unless released by agreement (novation). Under s 36 a retiring partner is also liable for later debts to persons who dealt with the firm before the change and had no notice of it, and to others unless the retirement was advertised in the Gazette.
- s 24: default rules unless otherwise agreed: (1) all partners share equally in capital and profits and contribute equally to losses; (2) the firm indemnifies partners for payments made in the ordinary and proper conduct of business; (3) a partner advancing money beyond agreed capital is entitled to interest at 5% per annum; (4) no interest on capital before profits; (5) every partner may take part in management; (6) no partner is entitled to remuneration for acting in the business; (7) no new partner without the consent of all existing partners; (8) differences about ordinary matters are decided by a majority, but no change in the nature of the business without unanimity; (9) books to be kept at the place of business and available to all partners.
- Duties: good faith between partners (ss 28-30): a duty to render true accounts and information; to account for secret profits from the partnership business or property (s 29); not to carry on a competing business without consent (s 30), each flows from the fiduciary character of partnership.
- Dissolution: s 32 (fixed term, single venture, notice), s 33 (bankruptcy or death of a partner, unless otherwise agreed), s 35 (court may dissolve on grounds including incapacity, misconduct, persistent breach, business can only be carried on at a loss, or just and equitable), s 39 (right of partners to apply partnership property to pay debts) and s 44 (distribution of assets: first losses, then debts to non-partners, then partners' advances, then capital, then surplus in profit shares).
- Insolvency: partnerships cannot be wound up as such; an insolvent partnership may be put into a partnership voluntary arrangement or wound up as an unregistered company under the Insolvent Partnerships Order 1994, and the partners individually may be bankrupt.

**Limited partnerships (LPA 1907)** have at least one general partner with unlimited liability and limited partners who contribute capital and who must not take part in management. The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 contains limited partnership reforms (a registered office at an appropriate address, a registered officer for corporate general partners, identity verification, annual confirmation statements). They had not been brought into force by November 2026 and await commencement regulations, after which a six-month transition will apply.

**Limited liability partnerships (LLPA 2000).**
- s 1(2)-(3): an LLP is a body corporate with legal personality separate from that of its members, formed by incorporation under the Act, with unlimited capacity. s 1(5): the law relating to partnerships does not apply to an LLP except as otherwise provided.
- s 2: incorporation requires two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view to profit to subscribe an incorporation document delivered to the registrar, with a statement of compliance. The incorporation document must state an appropriate registered office and registered email address, give details of members and designated members, state that each individual member's identity is verified and that no member is disqualified, and include a statement of initial significant control (s 2(2), as amended by the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023).
- s 4: members are the subscribers and any person who joins by agreement; membership ends by agreement or reasonable notice. A member is not regarded as employed by the LLP unless, if he and the other members were partners, he would be regarded as employed by a partnership (s 4(4)). s 4A (in force since 1 October 2009): if an LLP carries on business with fewer than two members for more than six months, a member who knows it is liable jointly and severally with the LLP for debts contracted after that time.
- s 5: members' mutual rights and duties are governed by agreement between members or between the LLP and its members; in the absence of agreement, by default provisions in regulations (the Limited Liability Partnerships Regulations 2001, SI 2001/1090, which supply defaults broadly similar to PA 1890 s 24 (equal sharing, no remuneration), but there is no implied right to expel a member).
- s 6: every member is an agent of the LLP; the LLP is not bound by something a member does if the member has no authority and the third party knows that, or does not know or believe him to be a member (s 6(2)); ex-members are treated as members unless the third party has notice or a notice has been delivered to the registrar (s 6(3)); the LLP is liable for a member's wrongful act or omission in the course of its business or with its authority (s 6(4)). A member is personally liable for his own torts, but for negligent advice only if he personally assumed responsibility to the client (applying *Williams v Natural Life Health Foods Ltd* [1998] 1 WLR 830); normally the claim is against the LLP alone.
- s 8: designated members are responsible for filing and compliance duties; if there are none, or fewer than two, every member is designated.
- Members' liability: the LLP is liable for its own debts; a member has no personal liability for the LLP's debts, except under a personal guarantee, under s 4A, for his own tort, and, in insolvency, under IA 1986 s 214A, applied by regulation (clawback of withdrawals in the two years before winding up where the member knew or had reasonable grounds to believe the LLP could not pay its debts).
- Tax: LLPs are treated as tax-transparent for income tax and capital gains tax (s 10 LLPA): members are taxed as partners on their share of profits; the "salaried member" rules (Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005 ss 863A-863G, from 6 April 2014) treat some members as employees for tax. Corporation tax does not apply to LLPs carrying on business with a view to profit.
- Workers' rights: members may qualify as "workers" despite being members (*Bates van Winkelhof v Clyde & Co LLP* [2014] UKSC 32: a member of an LLP could be a "worker" for the whistleblowing legislation on the basis of the true relationship, and did not need to be an employee).

**Choosing a vehicle (summary).**
- *Sole trader*: simplest; unlimited liability; taxed as an individual; no public filing; no separate personality.
- *General partnership*: easy to form (even informally); unlimited, joint liability for firm debts; flexible profit-sharing; tax-transparent; the partnership agreement is the key document.
- *LLP*: limited liability; flexible internal governance; tax-transparent; must file accounts and a confirmation statement; public disclosure of members; typical for professional firms and investment structures.
- *Company limited by shares*: separate personality and limited liability; board and shareholder structure; the company pays corporation tax (small profits rate 19% for profits up to £50,000, main rate 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief between; unchanged since 1 April 2023, and the government's Corporate Tax Roadmap commits to keeping the main rate at 25% for this Parliament), directors' duties, public register of members and persons with significant control, and accounts filing; stricter on extracting profits (dividends only from distributable profits).
- Other: community interest company, company limited by guarantee for non-profits, charitable incorporated organisation. Lenders often require personal guarantees whichever limited-liability vehicle is chosen.

## Key authorities
- *Cox v Hickman* (1860) 8 HLC 268: sharing of profits by creditors of a business does not itself make them partners; the real question is whether the relationship of agency and mutual trust exists (a source of s 2).
- *Mann v D'Arcy* [1968] 1 WLR 893: the parties' labels and a share of profits are indicators; the question is whether business is carried on in common.
- *Khan v Miah* [2000] 1 WLR 2123 (HL): a partnership exists once the parties have agreed to carry on a business in common and have begun to take steps towards doing so (such as acquiring premises), even before trading begins; the partnership is "carrying on" business.
- *Bates van Winkelhof v Clyde & Co LLP* [2014] UKSC 32, [2014] 1 WLR 2047: LLP member as a "worker".
- *Pooley v Driver* (1876) 5 Ch D 458 (Jessel MR): whether a profit-sharing "lender" is a partner depends on the effect of the whole agreement, not the labels used; the contributors were held to be partners.

## Applying it
1. Is there a partnership? Apply s 1 and s 2: business, in common, view to profit; consider profit-sharing, control, and the label used. A group of friends running a side business together without incorporating is probably a partnership by default.
2. Who is liable? Partners are jointly liable for debts (s 9) and for wrongs (ss 10-12). Apply s 5 for authority: was the act in the usual course of business? Did the third party know the partner lacked authority? Consider holding out (s 14) and incoming/outgoing partner rules (s 17).
3. Internal disputes: look at the partnership agreement; if silent, the s 24 defaults (equal shares; majority for ordinary matters; unanimity for admission and changing the business; no salary). Consider fiduciary duties and dissolution (s 35).
4. LLP: check the LLP agreement; the LLP is the contracting party; apply s 6 for authority; individual members' liability for their own negligence; s 4A single-member rule; and the clawback rule on insolvency.
5. Vehicle choice: compare liability, tax (income tax and NIC for individuals versus corporation tax and dividend/salary mix), regulation (professional rules that restrict structures), financing (lenders and personal guarantees), disclosure and administration.
Everyday version: two friends who start trading together are probably partners with unlimited personal liability for each other's business debts; incorporating or forming an LLP limits that exposure but brings filing duties and (for companies) restrictions on taking money out.

## Debates and reform
- The Law Commissions' joint work on partnership law reform (final report in 2003) proposed giving partnerships legal personality; the proposal was not enacted.
- LLP members' status as "partners" or "employees" for employment rights and the extent of the salaried-member tax rules.
- Whether limited liability for small-scale traders protects them in practice, given personal guarantees; and whether the transparency changes introduced for LLPs and limited partnerships by recent economic crime legislation are proportionate.

## Recent changes and points to check
- LLPs: the registered email address and appropriate registered office rules applied from 4 March 2024; identity verification of members applied from 18 November 2025 (existing members verify during a 12-month transition). Section 4A (single-member liability) is older, dating from 2009.
- Limited partnerships: the ECCTA 2023 reforms had not commenced by November 2026.
