# Capital maintenance, unlawful distributions and financial assistance

## Overview
English company law protects creditors by requiring a company to keep its share capital intact and to pay dividends only out of profits. The core rules are: a company may not return capital to shareholders except by an authorised route (reduction under Part 17, buy-back under Part 18); distributions must be made out of "profits available for the purpose" (Part 23); and a public company and its UK subsidiaries (public or private) may not give financial assistance for the acquisition of the public company's shares, nor may a public company give assistance for the acquisition of shares in its private holding company (Part 18 Chapter 2). The general prohibition on financial assistance by private companies for the acquisition of their own or a private parent's shares was abolished from 1 October 2008. Disguised distributions, such as sales at an undervalue to a shareholder, can still be unlawful.

## The law
**Maintenance of capital.** *Trevor v Whitworth* (1887) 12 App Cas 409 (HL): a company cannot buy its own shares from members; paid-up capital is the creditors' fund and cannot be returned other than through the statutory processes. The rule is now in s 658 (a limited company has no power to acquire its own shares except in accordance with the Act) and s 641 (reduction of share capital). A private company may reduce capital by special resolution supported by a solvency statement (s 641(1)(a)) or by special resolution confirmed by the court (s 641(1)(b)). Redemption or purchase of own shares out of distributable profits or the proceeds of a fresh issue is permitted under Part 18 Chapters 3-4; a private company may also use capital (Chapter 5, s 709 onwards), subject to a directors' statement of solvency with an auditor's report, a special resolution, publicity and a creditor objection period.

**Distributions (Part 23).**
- s 829: a "distribution" is every description of distribution of a company's assets to its members, in cash or otherwise, except: an issue of bonus shares; a reduction of capital by extinguishing or reducing liability on partly paid shares or repaying paid-up capital; redemption or purchase of own shares out of capital (including the proceeds of a fresh issue) or out of unrealised profits under Part 18 (a buy-back out of distributable profits is therefore a distribution); and distributions of assets to members on winding up.
- s 830(1)-(2): a company may only make a distribution out of profits available for the purpose, being its accumulated, realised profits so far as not previously utilised by distribution or capitalisation, less its accumulated realised losses so far as not previously written off in a reduction or reorganisation of capital. This is a test applied to all companies; profits and losses are cumulative, so a current-year profit cannot be paid out if there are accumulated realised losses.
- s 831 (public companies only): additionally a public company may distribute only if its net assets are not less than the aggregate of its called-up share capital and undistributable reserves, and only to the extent that the distribution does not reduce them below that aggregate.
- ss 836-839: the amount available is determined by reference to "relevant accounts" (last annual accounts, or interim or initial accounts), which must have been properly prepared and, for a public company, filed. A distribution by a company to its members must not be made on the basis of accounts that are not properly drawn up.
- s 845: where a company that has profits available for distribution transfers a non-cash asset to a member, the amount of the distribution is the excess (if any) of the asset's book value over the consideration received, so a transfer at not less than book value is a zero distribution; s 846 treats any unrealised profit included in the asset's book value as realised for this purpose.
- s 847: where a distribution is made in contravention of Part 23, a member who knows or has reasonable grounds for believing that it is so made is liable to repay it, or its value if not in cash (s 847(2)); this is without prejudice to any other obligation (s 847(3)). The section does not apply to financial assistance in breach of ss 678-679 or to a payment for redemption or purchase of own shares (s 847(4)).
- Directors who authorise an unlawful distribution are liable to the company for breach of duty (as well as for the amount in equity), which may be relieved under s 1157 and which can be pursued by a liquidator.

**Disguised distributions.** A transaction at an undervalue with a shareholder can be treated as an unlawful distribution if, in substance, it returns value to a member in his capacity as such.
- *Aveling Barford Ltd v Perion Ltd* [1989] BCLC 626 (Hoffmann J): a company with no distributable profits sold property, at a deliberate undervalue, to a company controlled by its controlling shareholder; the sale was an unlawful return of capital that could not be validated by shareholder approval, and the buyer, knowing the facts, was liable as constructive trustee of the proceeds of resale.
- *Progress Property Co Ltd v Moorgarth Group Ltd* [2010] UKSC 55, [2011] 1 WLR 1: a company sold its shares in a subsidiary to a company in the same group as part of a wider deal, and its new owners later alleged a substantial undervalue. The Supreme Court (Lord Walker giving the leading judgment) held that the sale was not an unlawful distribution in disguise: whether a sale at an undervalue is a disguised distribution depends on its substance, and a genuine commercial sale, entered into in good faith by directors who believed the price was fair, is not a distribution even if the price proves too low. The court looks at the true nature and purpose of the transaction, not simply at the gap between price and value. *Aveling Barford* was explained as a case where the transaction was in truth a return of capital to the sole shareholder.
- *Ridge Securities Ltd v IRC* [1964] 1 WLR 479 and *Re Halt Garage (1964) Ltd* [1982] 3 All ER 1016: payments to shareholders or directors that are not genuinely remuneration or consideration (for example, "remuneration" for a director who did no work, in *Halt Garage*) can be disguised gifts out of capital, and the excess is recoverable by the company or its liquidator.

**Financial assistance (Part 18 Chapter 2, ss 677-683).**
- s 677(1) defines "financial assistance" widely: a gift; a guarantee, security or indemnity (other than an indemnity for the indemnifier's own neglect or default), release or waiver; a loan or any other agreement under which the giver's obligations are to be fulfilled when the other party's obligations remain unfulfilled, and novations or assignments of such rights; and any other financial assistance where the net assets are reduced to a material extent or the company has no net assets.
- s 678(1): where a person is acquiring or proposing to acquire shares in a public company, it is not lawful for that company, or a subsidiary of it, to give financial assistance directly or indirectly for the purpose of the acquisition, before or at the same time as the acquisition. s 678(3): the same for assistance after the acquisition to reduce or discharge a liability incurred for the purpose of the acquisition where the company is a public company. s 678(2) and (4): exceptions where the principal purpose is not to give the assistance for the acquisition, or the assistance is only an incidental part of some larger purpose of the company, and given in good faith in the interests of the company.
- s 679: it is unlawful for a **public** company that is a subsidiary of a **private** company to give financial assistance for the acquisition of shares in the private holding company (a similar structure applies to post-acquisition debt discharge).
- ss 681-682: unconditional exceptions (for example, distributions, allotment of bonus shares, a payment under a court order) and conditional exceptions for certain assistance by a company (for example, lending in the ordinary course of business by lenders, employee share schemes).
- A **private company** can now give financial assistance for the purchase of its own shares, or its private holding company's shares, without the whitewash procedure that formerly applied. The prohibition remains where the shares acquired are in a public company (any UK subsidiary, public or private, is then caught by s 678) or where the assisting company is a public subsidiary of a private holding company (s 679). In leveraged buy-outs, check the status of the target and of every assisting subsidiary.
- Consequences of breach: criminal liability of the company and officers (s 680); the transaction may be unenforceable against the company if the lender knew of the circumstances; directors' potential liability for breach of duty; constructive trust consequences for recipients.
- *Brady v Brady* [1989] AC 755 (HL): the "larger purpose" exception requires a genuine larger corporate purpose, not merely that the acquisition is the means to a corporate end; the exception is construed narrowly. *Chaston v SWP Group plc* [2002] EWCA Civ 1999: financial assistance includes assistance given for due diligence costs where those costs are for the purpose of the acquisition, and what matters is the commercial reality, not just the form.

## Key authorities
- *Trevor v Whitworth* (1887) 12 App Cas 409 (HL): a company cannot buy its own shares; the paid-up capital fund must be protected.
- *Re Exchange Banking Co (Flitcroft's Case)* (1882) 21 Ch D 519 (CA): directors who paid dividends out of capital had to repay; a dividend cannot be paid out of capital.
- *Aveling Barford Ltd v Perion Ltd* [1989] BCLC 626: undervalue sale to a shareholder's company as an unlawful return of capital.
- *Progress Property Co Ltd v Moorgarth Group Ltd* [2010] UKSC 55: substance is decisive; a genuine commercial sale at a low price is not a distribution; limits the reach of *Aveling Barford*.
- *Brady v Brady* [1989] AC 755 (HL): narrow construction of the larger purpose exception.
- *Chaston v SWP Group plc* [2002] EWCA Civ 1999: wide approach to financial assistance in a commercial context.
- *Bairstow v Queens Moat Houses plc* [2001] EWCA Civ 712, [2001] 2 BCLC 531: directors who knowingly caused the company to pay dividends that were unlawful (no distributable profits on properly prepared accounts) were liable to repay the whole amount, as trustees of the company's property; it was no answer that lawful dividends could have been paid had different accounts been prepared.

## Applying it
1. Identify the payment: is it a "distribution" under s 829 (cash, in kind, or in substance)? If it is a dividend, check s 830 profits using the **last relevant accounts** (accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses); public company: also s 831.
2. If it is not on its face a dividend (e.g., a sale of an asset to a shareholder at below market value, excessive payments to a director-shareholder, or a loan waiver), ask whether, in substance and intention, it was a genuine commercial transaction (*Progress Property*), or a return of value to a member (*Aveling Barford*).
3. Liability: the member who received (knows or has reasonable grounds to believe, s 847); the directors who approved (breach of duty, s 172-174, the creditor duty if insolvent, *Sequana*); relief under s 1157 if they acted honestly and reasonably.
4. Financial assistance: are the shares being acquired in a public company (then the company and any UK subsidiary are prohibited from assisting, s 678), or is the assisting company a public subsidiary of a private target (s 679)? If the giver and the target are both private, there is no statutory prohibition (but directors' duties, the distribution rules and the creditor duty still apply).
5. Check a possible reduction of capital or a buy-back out of capital as the lawful alternative; check solvency statements.
6. Common traps: relying on accounts that are not properly prepared; forgetting that realised losses carried forward bar a dividend; assuming that financial assistance prohibitions apply to private companies; treating undervalue as automatically a distribution.
Everyday version: a company can pay a dividend only if it has made enough accumulated realised profit; if the directors pay out more, the directors and shareholders who knew can be made to put it back.

## Debates and reform
- Whether capital maintenance is still an effective protection for creditors given that it is a rule about legal capital and accounts, not a solvency test; the solvency statement route for private companies suggests a shift toward solvency-based regimes. There is no current government or Law Commission proposal to replace the regime.
- The uncertainty about "realised profits" for accounting purposes after changes in accounting standards; the ICAEW/ICAS guidance (TECH 02/17BL) is relied upon in practice.
- The scope of the *Progress Property* approach: whether intention rather than effect is the right test for disguised distributions.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Creditor duty: legality under Part 23 does not guarantee that directors have complied with their duties; an otherwise lawful dividend can breach the creditor duty once it is engaged (*Sequana*).
- The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 accounts-filing reforms (software-only filing, removal of abridged accounts) were moved to April 2028; in October 2026 the government paused the requirement for small companies and micro-entities to file profit and loss accounts. None of this changes the Part 23 rules on "relevant accounts".
