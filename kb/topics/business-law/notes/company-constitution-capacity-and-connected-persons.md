# The company constitution: articles as a contract, alteration, limits on authority, connected persons

## Overview
A company's constitution (mainly its articles) operates as a statutory contract between the company and its members and among the members (s 33). Articles can be altered by special resolution (s 21), subject to equitable constraints that the alteration be exercised bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole (*Allen v Gold Reefs*). The company's capacity is unlimited in practice (s 39), and third parties dealing in good faith are protected against constitutional limits on directors' authority (s 40); but where a transaction that depends on s 40 is with a director or a person connected with a director, it is voidable by the company (s 41). "Connected persons" (ss 252-254) defines who is caught by many of the Act's conflict and approval rules: the family list is narrower than many assume.

## The law
**The constitution.** s 17: the constitution includes the articles and any resolutions and agreements affecting the company's constitution (s 17, s 29). Model articles apply as default for companies incorporated after 1 October 2009 (s 20). The articles bind the company and its members to the same extent as if they were covenants made by each member (s 33(1)). Money payable by a member under the articles is a debt (s 33(2)).

**Contractual effect of articles: the rules.**
- The contract is between the company and its members *as members*. Rights given to members in their capacity as members (voting, dividends, pre-emption) are enforceable.
- Rights given to outsiders, or to a member in another capacity (solicitor, director, promoter), are not enforceable as constitutional rights: *Eley v Positive Government Security Life Assurance Co* (1876) 1 Ex D 88; *Hickman v Kent or Romney Marsh Sheepbreeders' Association* [1915] 1 Ch 881 (the articles' arbitration clause covered disputes between the company and a member in his capacity as member, and the member's right was limited to that capacity); *Beattie v E & F Beattie Ltd* [1938] Ch 708 (CA: a director-member was not bound to arbitrate a dispute in his capacity as a director).
- The contract can be enforced by members against the company, by the company against members and (in some cases) by members against each other (*Rayfield v Hands* [1960] Ch 1), though a member's claim for internal irregularities faces the proper claimant rule and the court's reluctance to interfere in matters of internal management.

**Alteration of articles (ss 21-25).** A company may amend its articles by special resolution (s 21(1)), subject to entrenched provisions (s 22; amendment requires more than a special resolution, or satisfaction of conditions, and notification to the registrar). A member is not bound by an alteration made after he became a member that requires him to take more shares or increases his liability to the company unless he agrees in writing (s 25). The member-protective limit developed by courts is that the power must be exercised bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole.

**Capacity and authority (ss 39-42).**
- s 39(1): the validity of an act done by a company cannot be called into question on the ground of lack of capacity by reason of anything in the company's constitution (s 39(2): subject to s 42 for charities).
- s 40(1): in favour of a person dealing with a company in good faith, the power of the directors to bind the company, or authorise others to do so, is deemed to be free of any limitation under the company's constitution. Such a person need not enquire about limits on the directors' powers, is presumed to act in good faith unless the contrary is proved, and is not in bad faith merely because they knew an act was beyond the directors' constitutional powers (s 40(2)). Limitations include those from a resolution of the company or a class of members, or from any agreement between members (s 40(3)). Members may bring proceedings to restrain an act that is beyond the directors' powers, but not if it is to be done in fulfilment of a legal obligation arising from a previous act of the company (s 40(4)); and directors' liability to the company for exceeding their powers is not affected (s 40(5)).
- s 41 applies only to a transaction whose validity depends on s 40, that is, one in which the board exceeded a limitation on its powers under the constitution (s 41(1)). Where such a transaction's parties include a director of the company (or its holding company), or a person connected with such a director, the transaction is voidable at the instance of the company (s 41(2)). Whether or not it is avoided, the director or connected person and any director who authorised it is liable to account for any gain and to indemnify the company for any loss (s 41(3)). Avoidance is barred if restitution is impossible, the company has been indemnified, a bona fide third party for value without notice would be affected, or the company affirms (s 41(4)); a connected non-director who did not know the directors were exceeding their powers escapes s 41(3) liability (s 41(5)).
- s 42: s 40 and s 41 modified for charitable companies.
- s 40 deals only with limitations under the constitution. It does not validate a transaction that is within the directors' constitutional powers but entered into in breach of their duties (for example for an improper purpose or in a conflict). Whether such a transaction binds the company depends on ordinary agency principles (*Criterion*), and a recipient of company property may face liability for knowing receipt or, if dishonest, dishonest assistance.
- Common law agency rules remain: actual and apparent authority and the indoor management rule (*Royal British Bank v Turquand* (1856) 6 E & B 327).

**Connected persons (ss 252-254).** Many Part 10 provisions (substantial property transactions, loans, s 239 ratification, s 41) apply to a person "connected" with a director.
- s 252(2): the following persons (and only those): (a) members of the director's family (s 253); (b) a body corporate with which the director is connected (s 254); (c) a person acting as trustee of a trust of which the director or a connected person under (a) or (b) is a beneficiary, or whose terms confer a power on the trustees exercisable for their benefit, other than a trust for an employees' share scheme or pension scheme; (d) a person acting as partner of the director or of a person connected under (a), (b) or (c); (e) a firm that is a legal person and in which the director, or a connected person, is a partner. s 252(3): a person who is himself a director of the company is not "connected" for these purposes (disapplied for s 239 ratification: s 239(5)(d)).
- s 253(2), the family: (a) spouse or civil partner; (b) any other person (of any sex) with whom the director lives as partner in an enduring family relationship; (c) the director's children or step-children; (d) children or step-children of that partner who live with the director and are under 18; (e) the director's parents. s 253(3): the partner under (b) does not count if the other person is the director's grandparent or grandchild, sister, brother, aunt or uncle, or nephew or niece.
- **Not connected** (unless caught by another limb): the director's siblings, grandparents, grandchildren, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, cousins and in-laws; a former spouse; a friend or business associate who is not a partner. A sibling's company could still be connected under s 254 if the director and connected persons hold at least 20% of its equity.
- s 254(2): a director is connected with a body corporate if he and persons connected with him together (a) are interested in shares comprising at least 20% of the equity share capital by nominal value, or (b) are entitled to exercise or control more than 20% of the voting power at any general meeting. Treasury shares are disregarded (s 254(5)); control through a body corporate the director controls counts (s 254(4)); the interests rules in Schedule 1 apply (s 254(3)). s 254(6) contains anti-circularity rules so that a body corporate connected with a director is not itself used to create further connections in the wrong way; read it before applying the test to a chain of companies.

## Key authorities
- *Allen v Gold Reefs of West Africa Ltd* [1900] 1 Ch 656 (CA): an alteration extending the company's lien to fully paid shares, so that it caught the shares of a deceased member who owed the company money for other shares, was upheld even though in practice it affected only that member; the power to alter must be exercised "bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole" (Lindley MR).
- *Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas Ltd* [1951] Ch 286 (CA): the "company as a whole" means the corporators as a general body; the question is whether those voting honestly thought the alteration was for the benefit of an individual hypothetical member; an alteration may be impeached if it discriminates between majority and minority so as to give the majority an advantage of which the minority is deprived. On the facts, relaxing a pre-emption provision was valid.
- *Sidebottom v Kershaw, Leese & Co Ltd* [1920] 1 Ch 154 (CA): an alteration allowing the directors to compel a competing member to sell his shares was valid, as it was for the company's benefit.
- *Shuttleworth v Cox Bros & Co (Maidenhead) Ltd* [1927] 2 KB 9 (CA): an alteration providing that a director would vacate office if requested in writing by the other directors, adopted to remove a director who had failed to account for money, was valid; the test is whether the members acted bona fide for the company's benefit, and the court will not substitute its own view unless no reasonable person could consider the alteration beneficial.
- *Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd* [1964] 2 QB 480 (CA): apparent authority of a managing director acting with the board's acquiescence.
- *Criterion Properties plc v Stratford UK Properties LLC* [2004] UKHL 28, [2004] 1 WLR 1846: where directors allegedly entered an agreement for an improper purpose, whether it binds the company is a question of actual or apparent authority, not of knowing receipt or "unconscionability"; a counterparty who knows or has reason to believe the directors are not acting in good faith in the company's interests cannot rely on apparent authority. Remitted for trial.
- *Phonogram Ltd v Lane* [1982] QB 938 (CA): s 51 CA 2006 (pre-incorporation contracts): the person who purports to contract for a company not yet formed is personally liable, unless otherwise agreed.

## Applying it
1. Constitutional disputes: identify the capacity in which the claimant sues; only rights held as a member are enforceable under s 33.
2. For amendment of the articles: check the majority (special resolution; any entrenchment, s 22), whether the alteration breaches the company's contract with someone else (damages but no injunction), whether it increases a member's liability (s 25), and then apply the *Allen*/*Greenhalgh* bona fide test. Check also unfair prejudice if the alteration harms a minority.
3. For limits on directors' authority: did the board exceed a constitutional limitation? If so, did the counterparty deal in good faith (s 40)? If the counterparty is a director or connected person, the company can avoid under s 41, subject to the s 41(4) bars. Otherwise (no board act at all, or a breach of duty within the board's powers) apply agency principles.
4. Connected persons: list the family members (s 253), then companies at the 20% thresholds (s 254), then trusts and partnerships.
5. Common traps: assuming siblings, grandparents or in-laws are connected; treating the 20% test as 20% or more of votes (the voting limb is *more than* 20%, while the equity limb is *at least* 20%); forgetting that a person who is himself a director of the company is not a "connected person" (s 252(3)), except for s 239 ratification; assuming s 40 protects a party in bad faith, cures a breach of duty, or helps where there was no act of the board at all; applying s 41 to an ordinary transaction with a director that did not exceed any constitutional limitation (that is governed by ss 175, 177 and Chapter 4, not s 41).
Everyday version: a small company's articles are binding as a contract between the owners; the owners can change them by a 75% vote, but the majority cannot use that power in bad faith simply to squeeze out a minority owner. Someone who contracts with a company in good faith can generally rely on the director's apparent power to sign.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the *Allen* test differs meaningfully from a proper purposes test or the unfair prejudice jurisdiction; "the company as a whole" is criticised as vague.
- The "connected person" definition is criticised as formulaic and under-inclusive: it excludes siblings and in-laws but includes parents and step-children.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Model articles are in the Companies (Model Articles) Regulations 2008 (SI 2008/3229); companies formed before 1 October 2009 may still have articles based on Table A.
- Entrenched provisions (s 22) must be respected before altering.
- The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 changed Companies House filing requirements from 4 March 2024: every company must have an appropriate registered email address and an appropriate registered office address, and must state on incorporation and in each confirmation statement that its intended future activities are lawful. Director identity verification followed from 18 November 2025.

## Common errors to avoid

- Siblings fall outside CA 2006 s 253(2): a director's brother is not connected through family (only via another s 252 head, e.g. business partner). Section 254 (at least 20% of equity, or over 20% of votes) decides whether a body corporate is connected. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#1]
- Do not say s 41 makes all director/connected-person transactions voidable. CA 2006 s 41 applies only where validity depends on s 40, i.e. the board exceeded a constitutional limitation; then the transaction is voidable at the company's instance. Contrast ss 190 and 195 (substantial property transactions). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#2]
- Section 40 covers constitutional limits on directors' powers, not breach of duty; good faith is presumed and knowing of the excess is not alone bad faith (s 40(2)(b)). For breach of duty ask authority (Criterion Properties v Stratford [2004] UKHL 28) and notice. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#3]
- Registration is not just memorandum, articles and IN01. CA 2006 s.9 requires the memorandum, statement of capital (s.10) or guarantee (s.11), proposed officers (s.12), PSC statement (s.12A), registered office address and statement of compliance (s.13). Articles only if model articles are not adopted (ss.9(5)(b), 20). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q06#1]
- The s 252/253 connected-person test (which excludes siblings) governs Part 10 transaction rules (eg s 190), not s 177. Section 177(1) catches a director 'in any way, directly or indirectly, interested', so a contract with a sibling's company can be an indirect interest. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#2]
- Knowing the directors lack constitutional power is not itself bad faith (s 40(2)(b)(iii)); s 40 may not cover a lone director acting without board authority (Smith v Henniker-Major [2002] EWCA Civ 762). Use actual or ostensible authority (Freeman & Lockyer [1964] 2 QB 480). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#8]
- Error: treating s 40 like apparent authority. s 40(2)(b): no duty to enquire; good faith presumed; knowing an act exceeds directors' powers is not itself bad faith. Real limit: a lone director acting without any board decision (Smith v Henniker-Major [2002] EWCA Civ 762). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1746/q05#0]
