# Directors' duty to creditors and the zone of insolvency (BTI v Sequana; s 172(3))

## Overview
Directors owe their general duties to the company, but where the company is insolvent or close to it, the company's interests are no longer simply those of the shareholders; the interests of creditors have to be taken into account. The Supreme Court in *BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA* [2022] UKSC 25 confirmed that this "creditor duty" exists as a rule of law saved by s 172(3) of the Companies Act 2006, set out when it is engaged (insolvent, bordering on insolvency, or insolvent liquidation or administration probable) and rejected a lower "real risk of insolvency" trigger. It is an aspect of the duty to the company, not a separate duty owed to creditors.

## The law
**Section 172(3).** The duty to promote the success of the company "has effect subject to any enactment or rule of law requiring directors, in certain circumstances, to consider or act in the interests of creditors of the company". The section itself does not describe the rule: it is a gateway to the common law line of authority.

**Sources of the creditor duty.** Before *Sequana*, the Australian case *Kinsela v Russell Kinsela Pty Ltd* (1986) and *West Mercia Safetyware Ltd v Dodd* [1988] BCLC 250 (CA) were widely cited as the origin of the principle that the interests of the company, where it is insolvent, are in substance the interests of its creditors; *Re HLC Environmental Projects Ltd* [2013] EWHC 2876 (Ch) and *Colin Gwyer & Associates Ltd v London Wharf (Limehouse) Ltd* [2002] EWHC 2748 (Ch) applied it.

**What the Supreme Court decided in *Sequana*** (in May 2009 AWA paid a dividend of EUR 135 million to its sole shareholder, Sequana, extinguishing most of a debt Sequana owed it; the dividend complied with Part 23 and AWA was then balance-sheet and cash-flow solvent, but it had long-term contingent pollution liabilities of uncertain amount, so there was a real risk, though not a probability, of future insolvency; AWA went into insolvent administration in October 2018 and BTI, as assignee of its claims, sued the directors):
1. The creditor duty exists and forms part of English law. It is not a freestanding duty owed to creditors; it is part of the duty to act in the interests of the company, and it is a duty to consider creditors' interests in the company's decision making.
2. It can apply to a decision to pay an otherwise lawful dividend: Part 23 is subject to any rule of law to the contrary (s 851(1)), and a balance-sheet test of distributable profits cannot make it proper for a cash-flow insolvent company to pay a dividend.
3. It is engaged when the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable. A "real and not remote risk" of insolvency is not enough: the Court unanimously rejected that lower threshold. The majority (Lord Briggs, Lord Kitchin, Lord Hodge) added that the directors must know or ought to know of that state of affairs; Lord Reed and Lady Arden left open whether such knowledge is essential.
4. Content: where the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency but insolvent liquidation or administration is not inevitable, the directors must consider creditors' interests and balance them against shareholders' interests where they conflict, giving creditors' interests more weight the greater the company's financial difficulties. The Court laid down no fixed weighting (no rule that creditors' interests must count at least equally). Where insolvent liquidation or administration is inevitable, creditors' interests become paramount, because shareholders no longer retain any valuable interest; Lord Briggs said this coincides with the point at which s 214 is engaged. The relevant interests are those of creditors as a general body, not particular creditors. Lady Arden added that the duty is not only to consider creditors' interests but also not to harm them.
5. On the facts the duty was not engaged: when the dividend was paid the company was neither insolvent nor bordering on insolvency, and insolvent liquidation or administration was not probable. The claim therefore failed.
The Court was unanimous on the result. Lord Briggs gave the majority judgment (Lord Kitchin agreeing), Lord Hodge concurred, and Lord Reed and Lady Arden concurred for broadly similar reasons. Lord Briggs and Lord Hodge said s 172(3) affirms the duty; Lord Reed and Lady Arden said it preserves its possible existence. Lord Reed and Lady Arden prefer the label "the rule in *West Mercia*".

**Related statutory regimes.**
- *Wrongful trading* (Insolvency Act 1986 s 214; s 246ZB in administration): after the company has gone into insolvent liquidation (or insolvent administration), a director who knew or ought to have concluded that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding that outcome can be ordered to contribute, unless he took every step to minimise potential loss to creditors. The creditor duty operates earlier and is relevant to claims for breach of duty by the company or liquidator; see the separate note on insolvency-related liability.
- *Transactions defrauding creditors* (s 423) and *transactions at an undervalue and preferences* (ss 238-239): additional routes for the office-holder.
- *Disqualification* (Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986) for unfitness where directors have disregarded creditors.
- *Part 23*: dividends and other distributions must be made out of distributable profits (s 830); a distribution that complies is not automatically safe from the creditor duty.

**Consequences of breach.** The remedy is that of an ordinary breach of fiduciary duty by a director: the company (acting through an administrator or liquidator) may seek compensation or equitable relief; where the creditor duty is engaged, the shareholders cannot authorise or ratify a transaction in breach of it (*Sequana*, following *West Mercia*).

## Key authorities
- *BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA* [2022] UKSC 25, [2024] AC 211 (5 October 2022): above. The claim failed; the Court dismissed the appeal and fixed the trigger as stated above.
- *West Mercia Safetyware Ltd v Dodd* [1988] BCLC 250 (CA): directors who paid a debt due to a related company when their own company was insolvent breached their duty to the company (in substance, to creditors); shareholders cannot ratify this.
- *Kinsela v Russell Kinsela Pty Ltd* (1986) 4 NSWLR 722 (NSW CA): where a company is insolvent, directors must consider creditors' interests; cited approvingly in English cases.
- *Re Produce Marketing Consortium Ltd (No 2)* [1989] BCLC 520: wrongful trading standard; the director's conduct is measured against the s 214(4) objective-subjective test.
- *Re Continental Assurance Co of London plc* [2001] BPIR 733: wrongful trading requires showing that the director should have concluded there was no reasonable prospect, assessed with foresight, and not by hindsight.
- *Re Brian D Pierson (Contractors) Ltd* [2001] 1 BCLC 275: wrongful trading and the reasonably diligent director; also the relevance of continuing to trade where the director has a reasonable basis for hope.

## Applying it
1. Establish the date on which creditors' interests mattered: look at balance sheet and cash-flow insolvency, overdue creditors, covenant defaults, going-concern warnings. Ask whether insolvency was actual, near ("bordering"), or whether insolvent liquidation or administration was probable.
2. If not at that stage, the creditor duty is not engaged (*Sequana*) and the directors' duty is the ordinary s 172 duty to members, even if there is a risk of future insolvency.
3. If engaged, ask what the directors actually did. Did they consider creditors' interests? A dividend, intra-group transfer, payment to an associate, or continuing to incur credit are the usual transactions.
4. Weigh the position on the sliding scale. At the margin, a rescue plan with a genuine prospect of success may justify risk; where insolvency is inevitable, creditors come first.
5. Add the statutory claims (s 214, s 238, s 239, s 423) and disqualification risk. These have different elements and limitation periods.
6. Common traps: treating the creditor duty as owed directly to creditors (it is owed to the company); treating a lawful dividend as safe; applying a "real risk" trigger; forgetting that breach cannot be ratified by shareholders where the company is insolvent.
Everyday version: a director whose company is in serious financial trouble must not carry on paying dividends or favouring friends and family, and should take advice; if the company cannot be saved they should stop trading in a way that takes creditors' interests into account.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the duty is better framed as a rule of fiduciary law (the *Sequana* approach) or as a statutory duty to creditors (as the Insolvency Act wrongful trading model might suggest); and whether the "probable" trigger gives directors enough certainty.
- Whether the "bordering on insolvency" formulation adds anything to "probable" insolvency, and whether the directors' knowledge should be part of the trigger (affirmed by the majority, left open by Lord Reed and Lady Arden).
- The overlap with s 214: a duty to consider creditors' interests earlier does not carry statutory contribution consequences unless wrongful trading is shown.
- Policy: rescue culture versus early creditor protection; the 2020 Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act temporarily suspended wrongful trading liability for a period during the pandemic but did not alter the creditor duty.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The decision is from 5 October 2022; there is no legislative codification of the creditor duty, and lower courts are still working out the "bordering on insolvency" limb.
- Wrongful trading claims can be brought by liquidators (s 214) and, since 1 October 2015, administrators (s 246ZB).

## Common errors to avoid

- No 'at least equal' rule: creditors' interests are weighed, gaining weight as difficulties deepen, and are paramount only once insolvent liquidation or administration is inevitable; a mere real risk of insolvency does not engage the duty (BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA [2022] UKSC 25). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#5]
- Do not say actual insolvency may fail to engage the creditor duty: under Sequana [2022] UKSC 25 it is engaged where the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or insolvent liquidation or administration is probable; only the weight given to creditors varies. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#6]
- The creditor duty operates within s 172, a subjective good-faith test (Regentcrest plc v Cohen [2001] BCC 494). The objective 'intelligent and honest director' test (Charterbridge Corp v Lloyds Bank [1970] Ch 62) applies only where directors failed to consider the relevant interests at all. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#7]
- The creditor duty is not triggered by a 'real and not remote risk' of insolvency (rejected in BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA [2022] UKSC 25). It arises when the company is insolvent or bordering on it, or insolvent liquidation or administration is probable. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#4]
