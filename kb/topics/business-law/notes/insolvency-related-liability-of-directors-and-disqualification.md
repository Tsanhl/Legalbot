# Insolvency-related liability: wrongful and fraudulent trading, antecedent transactions, phoenix names, disqualification

## Overview
When a company fails, directors and others face personal exposure under the Insolvency Act 1986 (IA 1986) and the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 (CDDA). Wrongful trading (s 214) is a civil liability for contributions that does not require dishonesty; fraudulent trading (s 213) requires intent to defraud. The office-holder can unwind transactions at an undervalue (s 238) and preferences (s 239) within set look-back periods, and s 423 reaches transactions defrauding creditors with no statutory look-back period. Sections 216-217 restrict "phoenix" re-use of a failed company's name, and the CDDA allows disqualification of unfit directors for 2 to 15 years.

## The law
**Fraudulent trading (s 213).** Where in a winding up any business of the company has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors (of the company or anyone else) or for any fraudulent purpose, the court may, on the liquidator's application, order anyone (not only directors) who was knowingly party to it to contribute to the company's assets (s 213). Dishonesty is required ("real moral blame"); defrauding a single creditor in a single transaction can suffice (*Re Gerald Cooper Chemicals Ltd* [1978] Ch 262), but not every fraud committed in the course of business means the business was carried on with intent to defraud (*Morphitis*). Parallel criminal offence in Companies Act 2006 s 993. Applies in administration as well (s 246ZA).

**Wrongful trading (s 214).** On the liquidator's (or administrator's) application, the court may order a director (including a shadow director, s 214(7)) to contribute to the company's assets where (s 214(2)): (a) the company has gone into insolvent liquidation (the administration equivalent is s 246ZB); (b) at some time before the winding up the person knew or ought to have concluded that there was no reasonable prospect that the company would avoid going into insolvent liquidation or entering insolvent administration (words added from 1 October 2015); and (c) he was a director at that time. The court shall not make an order if the director satisfies it that after the relevant time he took every step with a view to minimising the potential loss to the company's creditors that he ought to have taken (s 214(3)). What the director ought to have known, concluded and done is judged by a reasonably diligent person with both the general knowledge, skill and experience reasonably expected of someone carrying out his functions and those he actually has (s 214(4)). The contribution is compensatory, usually measured by the increase in the net deficiency after the date the director should have stopped trading (*Re Produce Marketing Consortium Ltd (No 2)* [1989] BCLC 520), and is held for the creditors generally (*Re Oasis Merchandising Services Ltd* [1998] Ch 170 (CA): the proceeds are not caught by a floating charge). Since 1 October 2015 office-holders may assign ss 213, 214, 238, 239 and 423 claims, for example to litigation funders (s 246ZD). During the pandemic the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 s 12 (as extended) required the court to assume that a director was not responsible for any worsening of the company's or creditors' position during 1 March to 30 September 2020 and 26 November 2020 to 30 June 2021.

**Transactions at an undervalue (s 238).** Applies where a company enters administration or goes into liquidation. A transaction at an undervalue is a gift, a transaction for no consideration, or one for consideration worth significantly less, in money or money's worth, than the consideration provided by the company (s 238(4)). The court shall make such order as it thinks fit to restore the position (s 238(3)), unless the company entered the transaction in good faith for the purpose of carrying on its business and at the time there were reasonable grounds for believing it would benefit the company (s 238(5)). The relevant time (s 240(1)(a)) is two years ending with the onset of insolvency (administration application/filing of notice, or liquidation). The company must have been unable to pay its debts within s 123 at the time or become so because of the transaction (s 240(2)); for a transaction at an undervalue with a connected person (not connected only as an employee) this is presumed (s 240(2)).

**Preferences (s 239).** A company gives a preference to a person if that person is one of its creditors, or a surety or guarantor of its debts, and the company does anything which puts that person in a better position in the event of insolvent liquidation than he would otherwise have been (s 239(4)). The court may not make an order unless the company was influenced in giving the preference by a desire to produce that effect (s 239(5)); a desire to prefer is presumed if the person preferred was connected with the company (not merely an employee) (s 239(6)). Relevant time: two years for connected persons, six months otherwise (s 240(1)); the insolvency test in s 240(2) applies. The order is to restore the position as if the preference had not been given (s 239(3)); s 241 protects third parties who acquired in good faith and for value.

**Transactions defrauding creditors (s 423).** A transaction at an undervalue entered into for the purpose of putting assets beyond the reach of a person who is making or may make a claim, or otherwise prejudicing his interests. No statutory look-back period and no requirement of insolvency, though the Limitation Act 1980 applies to the claim (*Hill v Spread Trustee Co Ltd* [2006] EWCA Civ 542); the application can be made by the victim or the office-holder (s 424). The statutory purpose need not be the dominant purpose, but it must be a real and substantial purpose, not a mere by-product (*IRC v Hashmi* [2002] EWCA Civ 981, [2002] 2 BCLC 489).

**Phoenix companies (ss 216-217).** s 216: where a company goes into insolvent liquidation, a person who was a director or shadow director at any time in the 12 months before liquidation may not, for five years from the day of liquidation, without leave of the court or in prescribed circumstances, be a director of, or directly or indirectly be concerned in the promotion, formation or management of, a company known by a prohibited name, or be concerned in carrying on a business under such a name (s 216(3)). A prohibited name is a name by which the liquidating company was known at any time in the 12 months, or one so similar as to suggest an association with it (s 216(2)). Contravention is a criminal offence (s 216(4)) and s 217 imposes personal liability for the debts of the new company on the person involved and on those who act on his instructions knowing of the contravention. The Insolvency (England and Wales) Rules 2016, Part 22 provide three excepted cases: (1) where the whole or substantially the whole of the business is acquired under arrangements made by the office-holder and, before acting, the director gives notice to every known creditor and publishes it in the Gazette, no later than 28 days after completion of the arrangements (r 22.4, with the prescribed statement in r 22.5); (2) where the director applies for the court's permission within seven business days of liquidation, he may act for up to six weeks or until the application is decided (r 22.6); and (3) where the other company has been known by the name for the whole of the 12 months before the liquidation and was not dormant in that period (r 22.7).

**Disqualification (CDDA 1986).** Grounds include: conviction of an indictable offence in connection with the promotion, formation, management, liquidation or striking off of a company (s 2); persistent default under the Companies Acts (s 3); fraud in winding up (s 4); participation in wrongful or fraudulent trading (s 10); and **unfitness** (s 6). Under s 6(1) the court *shall* make a disqualification order where the person is or has been a director of a company that became insolvent (or, since 15 February 2022, has been dissolved without becoming insolvent: Rating (Coronavirus) and Directors Disqualification (Dissolved Companies) Act 2021) and the court is satisfied that conduct as a director makes him unfit to be concerned in the management of a company. "Becomes insolvent" includes insolvent liquidation, administration or administrative receivership (s 6(2)). The minimum period is two years and the maximum 15 years (s 6(4)). Without the court's permission, an application must be made within three years of the company becoming insolvent (s 7(2), extended from two years by SBEEA 2015). The Secretary of State may accept disqualification undertakings (s 1A, s 7(2A)), which are usual in practice. Conduct is assessed by reference to Schedule 1 matters. Since 1 October 2015 the court may also make a **compensation order** or accept a compensation undertaking against a disqualified director whose conduct has caused loss to creditors (s 15A, from the Small Business, Enterprise and Employment Act 2015). Acting while disqualified is an offence (s 13) with personal liability for the debts of the company (s 15).

## Key authorities
- *Re Produce Marketing Consortium Ltd (No 2)* [1989] BCLC 520 (Knox J): first major wrongful trading case; the amount is compensatory.
- *Re Continental Assurance Co of London plc* [2001] BPIR 733; *Re Brian D Pierson (Contractors) Ltd* [2001] 1 BCLC 275: wrongful trading assessed without hindsight; directors can continue to trade if they have a reasonable ground to believe the company would avoid insolvent liquidation.
- *Morphitis v Bernasconi* [2003] EWCA Civ 289, [2003] Ch 552 (Chadwick LJ): deceiving one creditor (a landlord) did not mean the business had been carried on with intent to defraud; any contribution must reflect loss caused by the fraudulent trading and has no punitive element.
- *R v Grantham* [1984] QB 675 (CA, Criminal Division): intent to defraud may be found where a person obtains credit knowing there is no good reason to think funds will be available to pay the debt when due or shortly after.
- *Bilta (UK) Ltd v Nazir (No 2)* [2015] UKSC 23, [2016] AC 1: in the company's (liquidators') claim against directors who used it for a fraud, their dishonesty is not attributed to the company, so illegality is no defence; s 213 can apply to persons outside the UK.
- *Re MC Bacon Ltd* [1990] BCLC 324 (Millett J): the company must positively wish to improve the creditor's position; granting a debenture to the bank in order to keep the overdraft facility and continue trading was not influenced by a desire to prefer. (Also held that granting security is not a transaction at an undervalue.)
- *Ricketts v Ad Valorem Factors Ltd* [2003] EWCA Civ 1706, [2004] 1 BCLC 1: a new company's name was held to be a prohibited name; "so similar as to suggest an association" is judged from the viewpoint of the public and creditors; liability is strict.
- *Re Sevenoaks Stationers (Retail) Ltd* [1991] Ch 164 (CA): guidelines on length of disqualification: over 10 years for particularly serious cases; 6-10 for serious cases not meriting the top bracket; 2-5 for less serious.
- *Re Lo-Line Electric Motors Ltd* [1988] Ch 477 (Browne-Wilkinson V-C): ordinary commercial misjudgment is not enough for unfitness; there must be a lack of commercial probity or, in an extreme case, gross negligence or total incompetence; disqualification protects the public.
- *Re Barings plc (No 5)* [1999] 1 BCLC 433 (Jonathan Parker J): directors' duty of supervision and the standard for unfitness; disqualification of directors for failure to supervise a subordinate.

## Applying it
1. Identify the insolvency process and who can sue (liquidator or administrator for ss 213, 214, 238, 239; Secretary of State for CDDA proceedings; any creditor for s 423).
2. **Wrongful trading**: fix the date by which the director knew or ought to have concluded there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation or administration (s 214(4) standard); consider the s 214(3) defence (taking advice, restricting new credit, a realistic rescue plan); assess the increase in the net deficiency since that date.
3. **Fraudulent trading**: show intention to defraud or a fraudulent purpose and knowing participation; dishonest incurring of debts with no prospect of payment is typical.
4. **Antecedent transactions**: check the relevant time (two years/six months), the insolvency test (and presumption if connected), whether the company got "significantly less" (TUV), the s 238(5) defence, or desire to prefer (preference).
5. **Phoenix**: check the new name, the 12-month window, and the exceptions (notice/leave).
6. **Disqualification**: unfitness conduct (Sch 1) and the three brackets of period; consider undertakings and compensation orders.
7. Common traps: confusing the s 214 "ought to have concluded" objective test with fraud; missing the three-year application period for s 6; forgetting the 12-month look-back for s 216; ignoring that creditors, not shareholders, benefit from s 214 recoveries.
Everyday version: a director who keeps taking credit after it is clear the company cannot survive may have to contribute to the company's assets for creditors, and may be disqualified.

## Debates and reform
- The practical weakness of s 214: few claims succeed and funding is difficult, though assignment (s 246ZD) has helped.
- The relationship between the s 214 standard and the creditor duty (see the *Sequana* note).
- Phoenixing remains a policy concern: HMRC can require security deposits for PAYE and VAT and can issue joint and several liability notices to individuals involved in repeated insolvencies (Finance Act 2020 Sch 13).

## Recent changes and points to check
- Section 214(2)(b) has included "or entering insolvent administration" since 1 October 2015; the 2020-2021 temporary suspension has expired.
- Small Business, Enterprise and Employment Act 2015 (from 1 October 2015): conduct as director of overseas companies counts, the s 7(2) period became three years, Schedule 1 was replaced and compensation orders (s 15A) were introduced. Directors of dissolved companies were brought within s 6 from 15 February 2022.

## Common errors to avoid

- Wrongful trading (IA 1986 s.214) is not trading while insolvent. It needs insolvent liquidation/administration (s.246ZB) and that the director knew or ought to have concluded there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding it. Defence: s.214(3). Remedy: contribution to company assets. Fraudulent trading: s.213, intent to defraud. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q06#0]
- A sibling is a 'relative', hence an associate, under IA 1986 s 435(2), (8); a company the sibling controls is the director's associate (s 435(7), (10)) and so 'connected' (s 249). This triggers ss 239(6) and 240(2) presumptions and the two-year preference look-back (s 240(1)(a)). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#6]
- Sections 238–239 IA 1986 are office-holder claims to restore the position (orders under s 241), typically against the counterparty; they are not the director's personal exposure (see ss 212–214). An overpriced contract with a supplier may itself be a transaction at an undervalue (s 238(4)(b)). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#7]
- Error: tying the two-year look-back for a transaction at an undervalue to connection. IA 1986 s 240(1)(a): two years regardless; connection only triggers the s 240(2) presumption of inability to pay debts. Connection extends s 239 preferences from six months to two years. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1746/q05#1]
