# Separate legal personality and piercing the corporate veil

## Overview
A registered company is a legal person distinct from its members and directors. Its debts are its own, and a shareholder's exposure is normally limited to the unpaid price of the shares (or the guarantee). The courts will "pierce" or "lift" the veil only in a narrow class of cases: the Supreme Court in *Prest v Petrodel* confined true piercing to cases where a person uses a company to evade an existing legal obligation or to frustrate its enforcement. Most attempts to reach a controller's pocket succeed by other routes (the controller's own tort, personal guarantees, statutory liability, agency, trusts).

## The law
**Incorporation.** On registration the company is incorporated (Companies Act 2006 s 16(2)); unless its articles restrict them its objects are unrestricted (s 31(1)), and the validity of its acts cannot be called into question on the ground of lack of capacity by reason of anything in its constitution (s 39). A company limited by shares has members whose liability is limited to any amount unpaid on their shares (s 3(2)); one limited by guarantee, to the amount they undertook to contribute on a winding up. One person may form a company.

**Consequences of separate personality.**
- The company owns its own property; members have no insurable or proprietary interest in company assets (*Macaura v Northern Assurance*).
- The company contracts and sues in its own name; a controlling shareholder who is also an employee can still be an employee of the company (*Lee v Lee's Air Farming*).
- The company, not the members, is the proper claimant for wrongs done to the company (the proper claimant rule, see the note on members' remedies).
- A parent is not, merely by owning all the shares, liable for the debts or torts of a subsidiary.

**Piercing the veil: the modern test.** *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] UKSC 34 (Lord Sumption giving the leading judgment; Lord Neuberger agreed with his analysis, while Lady Hale, Lord Wilson, Lord Mance and Lord Clarke reserved their positions on whether the categories are exhaustive) distinguished:
1. the **concealment principle**: looking behind a company to discover who really controls it or is party to a transaction does not pierce anything; it is a matter of evidence and fact-finding;
2. the **evasion principle**: the court may disregard the corporate façade where a person is under an existing legal liability or obligation, or subject to an existing legal restriction, which he deliberately evades or whose enforcement he deliberately frustrates by interposing a company under his control. The principle is a last resort, available only where all other, more conventional remedies have proved to be of no assistance. It is not engaged merely because a company is used to avoid *future* liabilities, or because of impropriety unconnected with an existing obligation.

In *Prest* itself the veil could not be pierced (the husband had not used the companies to evade an existing obligation, and the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 s 24 gives no power to transfer a company's property), but the properties were ordered to be transferred to the wife because, on the evidence and the inferences drawn from the husband's non-disclosure, the companies held them on resulting trust for him. Lady Hale (with Lord Wilson) doubted whether all the cases could be classified neatly as concealment or evasion, and Lords Mance and Clarke declined to rule out other exceptional cases; all agreed piercing would rarely be needed.

**Other routes to liability that do not pierce the veil.**
- *Agency*: the company acts as agent of its controller (rarely found; *Adams v Cape Industries plc* [1990] Ch 433 (CA) rejected single economic unit and agency arguments on the facts).
- *Personal tort liability of the director*: a director who personally assumes responsibility for advice or a service can be liable in negligence (*Williams v Natural Life Health Foods Ltd* [1998] 1 WLR 830, HL, where assumption of responsibility on the facts was not established); a director is also personally liable for torts he commits or directs even if done in the company's name.
- *Parent company liability in tort*: a parent may owe its own duty of care to those affected by a subsidiary's activities where it has taken over, or shared, management of the relevant activity or has held itself out as supervising it (*Chandler v Cape plc* [2012] EWCA Civ 525; *Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe* [2019] UKSC 20; *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* [2021] UKSC 3). *Vedanta* and *Okpabi* were jurisdiction decisions on whether there was a real issue to be tried, but they state the duty principles.
- *Statute*: wrongful trading (Insolvency Act 1986 s 214) and fraudulent trading (s 213) allow contribution orders against directors and others; see the note on insolvency-related liability.
- *Contract*: personal guarantees, which lenders routinely demand from owners of small companies.
- *Trust and constructive trust*: property held in a company's name that is in truth held on trust for the controller.

## Key authorities
- *Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd* [1897] AC 22 (HL): a one-man business sold to a company he controlled; the company is a separate person even though the "members" were in truth Salomon and his family; Salomon, as secured debenture holder, ranked ahead of unsecured creditors. Foundational; not doubted.
- *Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd* [1925] AC 619 (HL): sole shareholder had no insurable interest in timber owned by his company.
- *Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd* [1961] AC 12 (PC): controlling shareholder and managing director was also an employee for compensation purposes.
- *Gilford Motor Co Ltd v Horne* [1933] Ch 935 (CA): a company formed to evade a covenant against soliciting customers was enjoined; the company was "a mere cloak or sham".
- *Jones v Lipman* [1962] 1 WLR 832: a company used to evade a contract to sell land was subject to specific performance; the company was "a creature of the defendant, a device and a sham".
- *Adams v Cape Industries plc* [1990] Ch 433 (CA): refused to enforce a US judgment against a UK parent; no agency, no "single economic unit" exception, and the court will pierce only when it is a mere façade concealing the true facts. Lord Sumption in *Prest* treated the categories there as examples of the concealment and evasion principles.
- *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] UKSC 34, [2013] 2 AC 415: see above; the leading statement of the doctrine.
- *VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp* [2013] UKSC 5, [2013] 2 AC 337: the claimant bank sought to hold the alleged controller of its borrower jointly liable on the loan agreement by piercing the veil. The Supreme Court (Lord Neuberger giving the leading judgment) held that, even where piercing is available, it cannot make the controller a party to the company's contract: that would extend the doctrine beyond anything justified by authority and contradict the parties' intentions. Lord Neuberger left open whether a power to pierce exists at all (later resolved in *Prest*).
- *Creasey v Breachwood Motors Ltd* [1993] BCLC 480: lifted the veil to allow a judgment to be enforced against a successor company to which assets had been moved; overruled by the Court of Appeal in *Ord v Belhaven Pubs Ltd* [1998] BCC 607.
- *Lungowe v Vedanta Resources plc* [2019] UKSC 20, [2020] AC 1045: not a piercing case; a parent may owe a duty to those affected by a subsidiary's operations depending on the extent to which it took over, intervened in, controlled, supervised or advised the management of the relevant activity; parent liability is not a distinct category of negligence, and it was arguable on the facts (jurisdiction stage).
- *Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc* [2021] UKSC 3, [2021] 1 WLR 1294: followed *Vedanta*; the Court of Appeal had wrongly focused on formal "control" and on a mini-trial of the facts. What matters is the extent to which the parent took over or shared management of the relevant activity; group-wide policies can found a duty if they contain systemic errors or the parent takes active steps to implement them.

## Applying it
1. Identify who the claimant wants to reach and why (a creditor of an insolvent company, a spouse in matrimonial proceedings, a victim of the company's tort).
2. Check whether the claim can be brought against the individual directly (tort, guarantee, statute) before turning to the veil.
3. Only then ask if the facts fit the evasion principle: was there an *existing* legal obligation, and did the person deliberately use the company to evade or frustrate it? Mere under-capitalisation, group structures chosen for tax or limited liability, or a failed business are not enough.
4. If the property is in the company's name but was paid for by the controller, consider a resulting or constructive trust (*Prest*).
5. For parent liability for subsidiary harm, apply the *Chandler/Vedanta* factors: control over the relevant activity, the parent's superior knowledge, and whether it held itself out as supervising it.
Everyday version: a small trader who incorporates is usually safe from business debts, but lenders and landlords will normally have taken personal guarantees, and a director can still be personally liable for their own negligence, for wrongful trading or for dishonest conduct.

## Debates and reform
- Is veil piercing a distinct doctrine at all? After *Prest* and *VTB*, commentators argue that most cases are explained by existing doctrines (agency, trust, tort, contract construction), and that "piercing" survives, if at all, only as a residual evasion rule.
- The Law Commission has not proposed general reform; the discussion focuses on parent liability for overseas harms (mandatory due diligence regimes elsewhere in Europe) and on corporate groups in insolvency.
- Limited liability for small businesses: the real protection is thin where owners must provide personal guarantees, and attention has turned to director identity verification and transparency under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 as a way of making it harder to hide behind companies.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023: identity verification for directors and people with significant control became mandatory on 18 November 2025 for new appointments; existing directors must verify by their company's first confirmation statement after that date (a 12-month transition ending in November 2026). Companies House also has enhanced powers to query and remove information.
- Wrongful trading (s 214) applies in insolvent liquidation; since 1 October 2015 administrators can bring equivalent claims under ss 246ZA-246ZB (inserted by the Small Business, Enterprise and Employment Act 2015).
- No change to the *Prest* test; the Supreme Court has not revisited it.

## Common errors to avoid

- Ownership alone does not attribute a shareholder's knowledge to the company; attribution turns on his role (eg director/directing mind) and the rule's purpose (Meridian Global Funds Management Asia Ltd v Securities Commission [1995] 2 AC 500).
