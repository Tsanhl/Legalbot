# Directors' general duties (Companies Act 2006 ss 171-177), enforcement and exemptions

## Overview
Part 10 Chapter 2 of the Companies Act 2006 codifies seven general duties owed by each director to the company (not to members or creditors directly). Six are fiduciary in character; only s 174 (care, skill and diligence) is not. Breach has the same consequences as the old common law and equitable rules (s 178), the duties can be modified only in limited ways (ss 180, 232), and the company, usually through a derivative claim or a liquidator, enforces them.

## The law
**The duties.**
- **s 171 Act within powers**: act in accordance with the constitution, and exercise powers only for the purposes for which they were conferred (the proper purposes rule).
- **s 172 Promote the success of the company**: act in the way the director considers, in good faith, would be most likely to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, having regard (among other matters) to the likely long-term consequences of decisions, employees' interests, business relationships with suppliers and customers, the impact on community and environment, the desirability of a reputation for high standards of conduct, and the need to act fairly between members (s 172(1)(a)-(f)). s 172(2) requires a different purpose to be given priority where the company's purposes are not solely for the benefit of members; s 172(3) makes the duty subject to any rule of law requiring directors in certain circumstances to consider or act in the interests of creditors (see the separate note on *Sequana*). The test is subjective ("considers ... in good faith"); only where the director failed to consider the company's interests at all does the court ask whether an intelligent and honest director could reasonably have believed the transaction was for the company's benefit (*Charterbridge Corp Ltd v Lloyds Bank Ltd* [1970] Ch 62).
- **s 173 Independent judgment**: not to fetter discretion, but a director may act in accordance with an agreement duly entered into by the company that restricts discretion, or in a way authorised by the constitution (s 173(2)).
- **s 174 Reasonable care, skill and diligence**: the care, skill and diligence of a reasonably diligent person with (a) the general knowledge, skill and experience reasonably expected of someone carrying out the director's functions, and (b) the director's actual knowledge, skill and experience. It is a combined objective minimum and subjective upward test (the same approach as Insolvency Act 1986 s 214(4)).
- **s 175 Avoid conflicts of interest**: avoid a situation in which the director has, or can have, a direct or indirect interest that conflicts, or possibly may conflict, with the interests of the company (s 175(1)); applies in particular to exploiting property, information or opportunity, and it is immaterial whether the company could have taken advantage of it (s 175(2)). It does not apply to a transaction or arrangement with the company (s 175(3), which is dealt with by s 177). It is not infringed if the situation cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict, or if the matter has been authorised by the directors (s 175(4)). For a private company, authorisation is by the other directors proposing and approving the matter unless the constitution invalidates that; for a public company, only if the constitution enables it (s 175(5)). Authorisation is effective only if the quorum is met without counting the interested director and the matter was agreed without their vote or would have been agreed if their vote had not been counted (s 175(6)).
- **s 176 Not to accept benefits from third parties** conferred by reason of being a director or doing (or not doing) anything as director; not infringed if the benefit cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict (s 176(4)). Unlike s 175, the board cannot authorise the benefit; only the members (or the articles) can.
- **s 177 Declare interest in proposed transaction or arrangement** with the company: declare the nature and extent of the interest to the other directors before the transaction is entered into. s 182 imposes a parallel declaration duty (with criminal liability under s 183) for existing transactions.

**Consequences (s 178).** The consequences of breach (or threatened breach) of ss 171-177 are the same as would apply if the corresponding common law rule or equitable principle applied (s 178(1)); the duties other than s 174 are enforceable like any other fiduciary duty (s 178(2)). Remedies therefore include rescission of contracts, an account of profits, equitable compensation, constructive trust, and injunction. Section 174 attracts damages in the manner of negligence.

**Consent and approval (s 180).** Where s 175 is complied with by directors' authorisation, or s 177 by declaration, the transaction is not liable to be set aside for want of member approval under any common law or equitable rule, without prejudice to any statute or constitutional provision requiring approval (s 180(1)). Statutory member-approval requirements (for example for substantial property transactions and loans, Chapters 4 and 4A) apply in addition to the general duties (s 180(2)-(3)). The general duties have effect subject to any rule of law enabling the company to authorise what would otherwise be a breach, and are not infringed by acts done in accordance with constitutional conflict provisions (s 180(4)); otherwise they apply notwithstanding any enactment or rule of law (s 180(5)).

**Protection from liability (ss 232-235).** A provision purporting to exempt a director from liability for negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust is void (s 232(1)); so is a company indemnity of a director against such liability, except insurance (s 233), qualifying third-party indemnity provisions (s 234), and qualifying pension scheme indemnities (s 235) (s 232(2)). Court relief for honest and reasonable conduct is in s 1157. Ratification of breaches by member resolution is in s 239 (see the note on ratification).

**Who counts.** "Director" includes any person occupying the position (s 250), so de facto directors can be liable (*Holland v HMRC* [2010] UKSC 51; *Re Hydrodam (Corby) Ltd* [1994] 2 BCLC 180 distinguishing de facto from shadow directors; *Secretary of State for Trade and Industry v Deverell* [2001] Ch 340 (CA) on what makes someone a shadow director). Shadow directors are subject to the general duties to the extent the duties are capable of applying (s 170(5)).

## Key authorities
- *Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver* [1967] 2 AC 134n (HL, decided 1942): directors who took up shares in a subsidiary, using a company opportunity, had to account for their profit though the company could not itself have taken the shares and they acted honestly. Strict no-profit rule; the foundation of s 175(2). Section 175(2) preserves the immateriality of whether the company could have taken the opportunity.
- *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] 2 AC 46 (HL): fiduciary liable to account for profits gained from information acquired in the fiduciary position, with allowance for skill and effort.
- *Industrial Development Consultants Ltd v Cooley* [1972] 1 WLR 443: a managing director who was offered a contract in his personal capacity, obtained his release by pretending ill health and then took the contract, was liable to account, even though the client would not have given the work to the company; the information came to him while a director.
- *Bhullar v Bhullar* [2003] EWCA Civ 424, [2003] 2 BCLC 241: directors held to be in breach of the conflict rule when they bought a property next to the company's land; the test is whether a reasonable person would see a real sensible possibility of conflict; it was irrelevant that the company had not been looking for more property or might not have taken the opportunity, since the directors were obliged to tell the company about it.
- *Item Software (UK) Ltd v Fassihi* [2004] EWCA Civ 1244, [2004] BCC 994: a director who, while still a director, planned a competing venture and diverted a contract was in breach of fiduciary duty. The Court of Appeal (Arden LJ) held that he was also in breach by failing to disclose his own misconduct to the company: the duty to disclose arose as an aspect of the general duty to act in good faith in what he considered the company's best interests, not as a free-standing rule. Contrast an ordinary employee, who has no general duty to confess his own wrongdoing.
- *O'Donnell v Shanahan* [2009] EWCA Civ 751, [2009] 2 BCLC 666: reaffirms the strictness of the conflict rule: a director must not decide for himself that the company would not be interested in an opportunity and take it; whether the company could or would have taken it up is irrelevant to liability to account.
- *Re D'Jan of London Ltd* [1994] 1 BCLC 561 (Hoffmann LJ): applying the combined objective and subjective test to a director's care and skill (the later s 174 formulation); the director was held negligent (signing an insurance proposal without reading it) but, having acted honestly and reasonably, was partially relieved of liability under the predecessor of s 1157.
- *Re Barings plc (No 5)* [1999] 1 BCLC 433: directors must keep themselves informed and cannot delegate without supervision.
- *Eclairs Group Ltd v JKX Oil & Gas plc* [2015] UKSC 71, [2016] 1 BCLC 1: proper purposes; the court identifies the purpose for which the power was conferred and the actual purpose for which it was exercised. Directors who used a power under the articles to restrict shares (after defective disclosure-notice responses) in order to influence the outcome of a general meeting acted improperly. Lord Sumption (with Lord Hodge) favoured asking whether the improper purpose was decisive ("but for") where purposes are mixed; the other Justices left the test for mixed purposes open.
- *Howard Smith Ltd v Ampol Petroleum Ltd* [1974] AC 821 (PC): proper purposes; allotment to defeat a takeover bid improper.
- *Foster Bryant Surveying Ltd v Bryant* [2007] EWCA Civ 200: duties after resignation; liability turns on whether the opportunity was obtained by the exploitation of the position.

## Applying it
1. Establish the claimant (usually the company, or liquidator, or a member by derivative claim) and the defendant's status (de jure, de facto or shadow director).
2. Identify the facts that map to the duties. A diverted contract or opportunity is s 175 (and s 172). A deal where the director is on both sides is s 177 (declaration) and not s 175. An undisclosed commission from a supplier is s 176.
3. Check whether the director can show authorisation or declaration. s 175 authorisation by the board requires quorum and voting independent of the interested director; check also whether the articles prohibit it.
4. Consider whether the conduct could be ratified under s 239 (see ratification note) or authorised in advance by the members under the *Duomatic* principle, and whether any statutory approval requirement (loans, substantial property transactions) was missed.
5. Remedies: account of profits for conflict breaches regardless of loss (note allowance for skill and effort in the profit account); compensation for loss caused. For s 174, show causation and loss. Consider s 1157 relief.
6. Common traps: treating a post-resignation opportunity as automatically safe (check *Cooley* and *Foster Bryant*); forgetting that honesty is no defence to conflict breaches; forgetting that the s 172 duty runs to the company; forgetting that a director may not contract out of liability (s 232).
Everyday version: a director who takes a business opportunity belonging to the company, or secretly profits from a supplier, must account for it to the company, unless the other directors properly authorised it after full disclosure.

## Debates and reform
- Is s 172 "enlightened shareholder value" or does it genuinely force boards to weigh stakeholder interests? Reporting requirements (the s 172(1) statement for larger companies) have been criticised as boilerplate.
- Whether the s 175(5) authorisation machinery in private companies, which allows directors rather than members to sanction conflicts, provides too little protection for minority owners.
- Statutory codification has been criticised for leaving s 178 to borrow from case law, so the law on remedies remains judge-made.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduced director identity verification: mandatory from 18 November 2025 for new directors, with existing directors verifying by their company's next confirmation statement during a 12-month transition.
- Large companies must include a s 172(1) statement in the strategic report describing how the directors had regard to the s 172(1)(a)-(f) matters (s 414CZA).
- For directors approving distributions or trading while insolvent, see the separate notes on distributions and creditor duties.

## Common errors to avoid

- Attaching the s 183 offence to s 177: breach of s 177 has civil consequences only (s 178); s 183 makes failure to declare under s 182 (existing transactions) an offence. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#0]
- Citing s 177(1)-(2) for timing: the duty to declare before the company enters the transaction is s 177(4). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#1]
- Importing the s 252 connected-person test into s 177: 'in any way, directly or indirectly, interested' is wider, so a brother's company can give a director an indirect interest. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#2]
- Accessory (dishonest assistance) liability needs dishonesty, not mere knowledge (Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan [1995] 2 AC 378). Knowing receipt asks whether the recipient's knowledge makes retention unconscionable (BCCI (Overseas) Ltd v Akindele [2001] Ch 437). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q05#4]
- Breach of s 177 is not itself a criminal offence: its consequences are civil (s 178). The s 183 offence is failing to declare an interest in an existing transaction under s 182, which applies where no s 177 declaration was made (s 182(1)). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#0]
- Pin-cite s 177 correctly: s 177(1) duty to declare; (2) methods; (4) declaration before the company enters the transaction; (5) no declaration needed of an interest the director is unaware of (constructive awareness counts); (6) exceptions: no likely conflict, directors already aware, service contracts. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q05#1]
- Ignorance of a co-director's conduct is no automatic defence under s 174: directors must stay informed and supervise co-directors (Re Barings plc (No 5) [1999] 1 BCLC 433; Lexi Holdings plc v Luqman [2009] EWCA Civ 117), so failing to notice can itself be breach.
