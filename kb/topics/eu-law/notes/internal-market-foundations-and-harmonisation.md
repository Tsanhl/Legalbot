# The EU internal market, positive and negative integration, and harmonisation

## Overview
This is the opening topic of the module (Lectures 1-2). It asks what an internal market is, why the EU built one, and the two tools used to build it: **negative integration** (the Treaty free movement provisions strike down national obstacles) and **positive integration** (EU legislation, chiefly harmonisation under Article 114 TFEU, replaces divergent national rules). The topic matters for every later topic because the first question in any free movement problem is whether a national rule is judged against the Treaty or against EU secondary legislation, and that depends on whether, how far and how intensely the area has been harmonised. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. The Treaty framework of the internal market
1. **Article 3 TEU**: "The Union shall establish an internal market".
2. **Articles 3-4 TFEU**: set the EU's competences in the internal market (the internal market is a shared competence; the customs union is exclusive, per Art 3 TFEU).
3. **Article 26 TFEU**: the internal market is an area without internal frontiers in which the free movement of goods, persons, services and capital is ensured (quoted in Weatherill's comparative article and the handouts).
4. **Article 28 TFEU**: the customs union (internal and external dimension; see `customs-duties-and-internal-taxation.md`).
5. **Article 114 TFEU**: the legal basis for harmonisation measures to improve the establishment and functioning of the internal market. Art 114(2) excludes fiscal provisions, free movement of persons and the rights and interests of employed persons; those need Art 115 TFEU (special legislative procedure, unanimity in Council) (secondary source: *Introduction to EU internal market law*).

The lectures distinguish a **free trade area** (no tariffs between members), a **customs union** (plus a common external tariff), and an **internal market** (plus free movement of the factors of production and removal of non-tariff barriers). The EU is unusual because it also has a strong court, direct effect and supremacy, and because the market has moved beyond economics into citizenship.

### 2. The four (five) freedoms and their Treaty bases
- Goods: Article 34 TFEU (plus Arts 30 and 110 for fiscal measures).
- Workers: Article 45 TFEU; non-economically active citizens: Articles 20-21 TFEU and Directive 2004/38.
- Establishment: Article 49 TFEU.
- Services: Article 56 TFEU and the Services Directive 2006/123.
- Capital: Article 63 TFEU.

None is absolute. Member States can restrict free movement if they have a ground of justification: **Treaty derogations** (e.g. Art 36 for goods) or the Court-made, open-ended category of **mandatory requirements / objective justifications / public interest requirements** (from *Cassis de Dijon*). See `article-36-justifications-and-proportionality.md`.

### 3. Negative integration
- The free movement provisions tell Member States what they **cannot** do. The target is national legislation that obstructs free movement.
- The main actors are individuals and companies who challenge national rules in national courts (often via preliminary references), and the Commission through the infringement procedure.
- Negative integration preserves legal diversity: the national rule is either struck down or survives as justified, but no EU rule replaces it.

### 4. Positive integration (harmonisation)
- The EU legislature adopts common EU standards (directives or regulations) that replace national rules.
- The legal basis is Article 114 TFEU. the materials's examples of secondary law in this course: the Citizens' Rights Directive 2004/38 and the Services Directive 2006/123.
- Negative integration often "paves the way" for harmonisation: harmonisation tends to follow sectors with many free movement cases (Handout 1).

### 5. Which yardstick applies? Treaty or secondary law
1. **No harmonisation** in the sector: national rules are assessed under the Treaty provisions. This is the default.
2. **Harmonisation exists**: national rules are assessed under the relevant secondary law, and the Treaty provisions in principle drop out.
3. But you "return" to the Treaty in two situations, depending on the **intensity** and the **scope** of harmonisation:
   - **Intensity: minimum vs maximum (full) harmonisation.** Minimum harmonisation sets a floor: a Member State may not go below it (that is a breach of the directive) but may adopt stricter rules, and those stricter rules are then tested against the Treaty freedoms. Maximum harmonisation sets the exact standard: no going below or above, and national rules are tested only against the secondary law.
   - **Scope: partial vs exhaustive harmonisation.** Partial harmonisation covers only some aspects of a product or service; national rules on unharmonised aspects are tested under the Treaty. Exhaustive harmonisation covers the whole field; there is no room for national action and only the EU measure is the yardstick.
4. Handout 1 worked examples:
   - A minimum-harmonisation directive banning cigarette sales to under-18s. A German rule allowing sales to 16-year-olds breaches the directive; a German rule banning sales to under-21s is tested under Article 34 TFEU. If the directive were maximum harmonisation, Germany could only set 18.
   - EU tobacco directives say nothing about where tobacco can be sold. A Dutch ban on selling tobacco near supermarket entrances falls outside the harmonised field and is tested under Article 34 TFEU.

### 6. Limits on Article 114 TFEU: *Tobacco Advertising*
- *Germany v Parliament and Council (Tobacco Advertising I)* (C-376/98): the Court annulled Directive 98/43 (a near-total ban on tobacco advertising) for lack of a proper legal basis. A measure under Art 114 must genuinely contribute to improving the internal market, i.e. remove obstacles to free movement or appreciable distortions of competition (Handout 1; 2023-24 Handout 2).
- The 2023-24 handout explains the reasoning: there was hardly any cross-border trade in the advertising products concerned, and banning them outright did not improve the internal market but eliminated it. Because the directive's main aim was public health, Art 114 could not be used; the public health base, Art 168 TFEU, does not allow measures that replace national rules (this is a lack of competence, not misuse of powers).
- The secondary source *Introduction to EU internal market law* summarises the principles drawn from *Tobacco Advertising I*, *Tobacco Advertising II* (C-380/03) and *Swedish Match* (C-210/03): (a) Art 114 measures must contribute to removing barriers to trade or distortions of competition; (b) there is no de minimis threshold, but the obstacles or distortions must be "appreciable", otherwise Art 114 would give the EU unlimited power; (c) Art 114 may be used to prevent future obstacles, but their emergence must be likely and the measure designed to prevent them (*British American Tobacco* C-491/01, *Arnold André* C-434/02, *Vodafone* C-58/08). The legislature has discretion over the method of approximation, especially in technical fields (*Philip Morris* C-547/14; *UK v Parliament and Council* C-270/12), and may harmonise in stages.

### 7. Contexts and development of the internal market (Lectures 1-2, Handout 1)
- **Historical**: the Schuman Declaration (1950); coal and steel as an experiment to secure peace through interdependence ("neo-functionalism"). The market is an instrument for peace and citizens' well-being, never purely economic.
- **Economic**: efficiency, specialisation and comparative advantage; strained in a 27-State market with large wage gaps.
- **Social**: increased social interaction; Union citizenship (1992) extended rights to students and pensioners; EU regulation of employment, consumer and environmental protection.
- **Ethical**: questions of moral diversity, e.g. abortion services (*Grogan*), laser games (*Omega*), noble titles (*Sayn-Wittgenstein*), euthanasia.
- **Substantive** development: from coal and steel (1950) to the four freedoms (1957), then economic and monetary coordination and, with Schengen, asylum and immigration.
- **Territorial** development: 6 to 27 States; the 2004 enlargement and wage disparities (*Viking*, *Laval*); rule-of-law tensions in Poland and Hungary.
- **Personal** development: from economically active persons to all Union citizens (Maastricht 1992; Directive 2004/38). How far can States protect welfare systems from inactive citizens (*Dano* C-333/13)? The handout notes reliable statistics are scarce.

## Key authorities
- **Article 114 TFEU**: legal basis for internal market harmonisation; Art 114(2) carve-outs; Art 114(4)-(5) notification procedure for national derogations from full harmonisation (Klamert).
- **Article 115 TFEU**: unanimity route for matters excluded from Art 114.
- *Germany v Parliament and Council (Tobacco Advertising I)* (C-376/98): advertising-ban directive annulled; Art 114 requires a genuine contribution to the internal market; health as the main aim cannot be pursued through Art 114 alone.
- *Tobacco Advertising II* (C-380/03) and *Swedish Match* (C-210/03): confirm and refine the limits (as summarised in the secondary source).
- *Cassis de Dijon* (120/78): mutual recognition and mandatory requirements; the bridge from negative integration to the need for positive integration (see `article-34-dassonville-cassis-mutual-recognition.md`).
- Directive 2004/38 and Services Directive 2006/123: examples of secondary law that becomes the yardstick in place of the Treaty.

## Debate and critique
- **Weatherill, "The Limits of Legislative Harmonisation Ten Years After Tobacco Advertising" (2012) 12 German LJ 827**: after *Tobacco Advertising I*, almost every later annulment challenge failed. The Treaty defines the limits of competence only loosely, so the legislature has wide discretion. The case law has become a "drafting guide": the Court states a formula and the legislature drafts recitals to fit it, which makes the process circular. Weatherill argues that subsidiarity and proportionality are better seen as frameworks for political debate than as tests courts can apply, and calls for a wider institutional culture that confronts the EU's structural bias towards centralisation, with more procedural openness about why a competence is used. In *What is the EU's internal market?* he adds that the *Tobacco Advertising I* limit has been "largely drowned out" by later rulings, and that the thresholds (appreciable effect, likely divergence) are as slippery as those defining the free movement provisions.
- **Dougan, "Minimum Harmonisation and the Internal Market" (2000) 37 CML Rev 853**: the EU has moved away from the ideal of one uniform legal regime towards competing economic and social-welfare agendas, which requires more legal diversity. Minimum harmonisation is entrenched in the Treaty and accepted by the Court, but its implications have not been thought through: the Treaty does not say how far stricter national rules may go, and the Court has no systematic solution. So minimum harmonisation's potential to put citizens' welfare ahead of economic integration remains unresolved. He also warns of increasingly complex, differentiated regulatory patterns.
- **Klamert, "What We Talk About When We Talk About Harmonisation" (2017) 17 CYELS 360**: "harmonisation" covers very different things. There is a close but unclear link between minimum harmonisation and mutual recognition, and between full harmonisation and the country-of-origin principle (illustrated by the Tobacco Products and Services Directives). Years after *Tobacco Advertising I and II* it is still unclear how minimum harmonisation instruments must be designed to be lawful. He also maps procedural controls: Art 114(4)-(5) notification for derogations from full harmonisation; the standstill obligation in the Technical Standards Directive 98/34 (given direct effect by the Court); and the notification duty without standstill in Art 15(7) of the Services Directive.
- **de Witte, "Sex, Drugs and EU Law" (2013) 50 CML Rev 1545**: national choices on moral or ethical questions (e.g. Dutch cannabis coffee shops limited to residents) clash with integration. He sets an "argument from self-determination" (respect national choices because they reflect a particular time, place and people) against an "argument from containment" (EU law should impose substantive limits). He proposes **procedural proportionality**, focused on the coherence and consistency of the national moral policy and on general regulatory equivalence between domestic and foreign situations.
- **van Leeuwen, "Legal Empathy in the Internal Market" (2021) 46 EL Rev 625**: negative integration depends on differences between national laws, which free movement cases explore. This creates dialogues in which Member States must recognise and respond to each other's legal positions, a form of comparative law. A balance between harmonisation and negative integration is needed so that States keep engaging in this "legal empathy"; Brexit shows how hard empathy is without a commitment to communicate through free movement law.
- **Thematic tensions for essays**: market efficiency against regulatory autonomy and diversity; the court-led (negative) route against the legislature-led (positive) route; the democratic legitimacy of judicial market-making; whether the internal market is economic or also social and ethical. the materials's discussion question, whether "British jobs for British workers" is compatible with internal market membership, is a good way into these issues (direct nationality discrimination under Art 45 TFEU).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the sector and any EU legislation.** Is there a directive or regulation covering the product or activity?
2. **If there is none**: apply the Treaty provisions (scope, direct effect, restriction, justification, proportionality). See `structure-of-free-movement-law.md`.
3. **If there is harmonisation, ask about its scope**: does the measure cover the specific aspect regulated by the national rule? If not (partial harmonisation), go back to the Treaty for that aspect.
4. **Ask about intensity**: if minimum harmonisation and the national rule is stricter, test the stricter rule under the Treaty. If below the minimum, it breaches the directive. If maximum harmonisation, any deviation breaches the directive.
5. **If the validity of the EU measure itself is questioned** (e.g. an essay on the EU's power to ban a product), discuss Art 114 limits: genuine contribution to the internal market, appreciable obstacles, likely future obstacles, and *Tobacco Advertising*.
6. **Traps**: do not apply Art 34 when a maximum-harmonisation directive covers the field; do not assume all harmonisation is maximum. Remember that Art 114 cannot be used for fiscal provisions or free movement of persons (Art 114(2)).
