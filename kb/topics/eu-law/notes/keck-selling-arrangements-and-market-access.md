# Keck, selling arrangements, restrictions on use and market access under Article 34 TFEU

## Overview
In *Keck and Mithouard* (1993) the Court tried to limit the reach of *Dassonville* by holding that non-discriminatory "certain selling arrangements" are not MEEs at all. This made **classification** of the national rule decisive: product requirements are always MEEs; selling arrangements are outside Art 34 if they satisfy the two Keck provisos. Later, restrictions on **use** (*Commission v Italy (Trailers)*, *Mickelsson and Roos*) and price rules (*Scotch Whisky*, *Deutsche Parkinson*) pushed the Court towards a general **market access** test. Whether Keck survives, and whether market access is a coherent concept, are core essay questions. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. Keck and Mithouard (C-267/91 and C-268/91)
- Facts: French supermarket managers were prosecuted under a French law prohibiting **resale at a loss**. They argued it breached Art 30 EEC (now Art 34).
- Context (Handout 1; Lectures 4-5): traders had been using *Dassonville* to challenge rules unrelated to inter-State trade (the Sunday trading saga, *Torfaen*); Member States and AGs (Tesauro in *Hünermund*) pressed the Court to limit Art 34.
- Para 16 (quoted in Handout 1): "contrary to what has previously been decided", applying to imports national provisions **restricting or prohibiting certain selling arrangements** does not hinder trade within *Dassonville* "so long as those provisions apply to all relevant traders operating within the national territory and so long as they affect in the same manner, in law and in fact, the marketing of domestic products and of those from other Member States".
- Para 17: if so, such rules are not by nature such as to prevent access to the market or impede access any more than for domestic products; they fall outside Art 34.
- The Court expressly overruled earlier case law (rare). The aim was a regulatory "safe zone" for rules with insignificant impact on inter-State trade.

### 2. The Keck test
1. Is the rule a **product requirement** (content, composition, shape, size, packaging, labelling, presentation)? Always an MEE (*Cassis*) requiring justification.
2. Is it a **selling arrangement**: a rule on the circumstances in which a product is sold that does not touch its physical characteristics? Typical: where, when, how and by whom products may be sold; who may buy; where and how products may be advertised; price rules (Handout 1; Lectures 4-5).
3. If a selling arrangement, apply the **provisos**: (a) it applies to all relevant traders in the national territory; (b) it affects in the same manner, in law and in fact, domestic and imported products (no direct or indirect discrimination).
4. If both provisos are met, the rule is outside Art 34 and needs no justification. If either fails, it is automatically an MEE that must be justified (no further *Dassonville* test needed).

### 3. Classification: product requirement or selling arrangement?
- *Familiapress* (C-368/95): Austrian ban on selling periodicals containing prize competitions. Although aimed at a method of sales promotion, it bore on the actual content of the product, since the competitions formed an integral part of the magazine; it required foreign publishers to alter content, impairing market access: an MEE in principle, not a selling arrangement. (Justification: maintaining press diversity, which must be reconciled with freedom of expression; for the national court.)
- Examples of selling arrangements (Handout 1): *Punto Casa* (C-69/93) (opening hours, Sunday trading); *De Agostini* (C-34/95) (advertising aimed at under-12s); *DocMorris* (C-322/01) (distance selling of medicines); *Ker-Optika* (C-108/09) (online sale of contact lenses); *DocMorris* (C-190/20) (promotional gifts for medicines). Spaventa's table also lists *Tankstation*, *Semeraro* (Sunday trading), *Commission v Greece* (milk sold only in pharmacies), *Hünermund* (advertising outside pharmacies), *Leclerc-Siplec* (TV advertising), *Banchero* (tobacco sold through authorised retailers) as selling arrangements not caught.

### 4. The second proviso is applied strictly (market access)
- *De Agostini* (C-34/95, C-35/95, C-36/95): Swedish ban on TV advertising aimed at children under 12. A ban on TV advertising in a sector is a selling arrangement (para 39, citing *Leclerc-Siplec*). The first proviso was met (para 41). As to the second, an outright ban on a type of promotion for a lawfully sold product might have a greater impact on imports (para 42); De Agostini argued that TV advertising was its only effective way to penetrate the Swedish market (para 43). So the ban is outside Art 34 unless it does not affect domestic and imported products in the same way (para 44); if it does not, the national court must assess justification (consumer protection, fair trading) and proportionality (paras 45-47).
- *DocMorris* (C-322/01): a ban on internet sales of medicines affects pharmacies outside Germany more, since the internet is a more significant way for them to reach German consumers (as recalled in *Deutsche Parkinson* para 23).
- Handout 1: the focus of the second proviso is market access; rules preventing or making it harder for imports to reach the market are indirectly discriminatory. Because of this strictness, it is unclear whether Keck is still a safe zone.

### 5. AG Jacobs in Leclerc-Siplec (C-412/93)
- Leclerc-Siplec, a fuel distributor, wanted to advertise its petrol stations on TV; French rules barred the distribution sector from TV advertising (the Court treated this as a selling arrangement under Keck, as recalled in *De Agostini* para 39).
- AG Jacobs criticised Keck: (i) some selling arrangements, e.g. a total advertising ban, can be almost as restrictive as an import ban, so a single test based on the purpose of Art 34 is better (para 38); (ii) the "same manner" proviso introduces a discrimination test, which is inappropriate: an obstacle to inter-State trade does not cease to exist because the same obstacle affects domestic trade, and a discrimination test would fragment the market by making traders accept whatever local selling rules exist (paras 39-40).
- Guiding principle: all undertakings engaged in legitimate economic activity in one State should have **unfettered access to the whole Community market** unless there is a valid reason (para 41). Test: is there a **substantial restriction** on that access? This would introduce a de minimis test for indistinctly applicable measures only; overtly discriminatory measures remain prohibited per se however slight their effect (paras 42-44). Rules prohibiting the sale of goods lawful elsewhere, or requiring goods to be modified, are by nature a substantial barrier.
- The Court has never explicitly followed him.

### 6. Restrictions on use: a separate test
- *Commission v Portugal* (C-265/06): ban on affixing tinted film to car windows. Consumers knowing they cannot affix the film have practically no interest in buying it; the ban was an MEE and disproportionate because a wide range of films exists, some transparent enough for visual inspection of vehicles.
- *Commission v Italy (Trailers)* (C-110/05): Italian ban on motorcycles towing trailers. The Court restated three categories (paras 33-37): (i) measures with the object or effect of treating imports less favourably; (ii) product requirements on goods lawfully made and marketed elsewhere, even if applying to all products alike (*Cassis*, *Familiapress*); (iii) and, by contrast, selling arrangements within Keck. Then: "Any other measure which hinders access of products originating in other Member States to the market of a Member State is also covered" (para 37). A prohibition on use has a considerable influence on consumer behaviour which affects market access (para 56); consumers have practically no interest in buying a trailer they cannot use (para 57); MEE (para 58). But justified by **road safety**: Member States may choose their level of protection and have a margin of appreciation; the fact another State is less strict does not make the rule disproportionate (para 65); the State need not prove that no other conceivable measure could achieve the aim (para 66); States may use general and simple rules easily understood and enforced (para 67). Action dismissed (para 70).
- *Mickelsson and Roos* (C-142/05): Swedish rules confined personal watercraft (jet-skis) to general navigable waterways, whose use was dangerous and marginal (para 25). Even without aim or effect of less favourable treatment, a use restriction may, depending on its scope, considerably influence consumer behaviour and so market access (para 26); consumers have limited interest in buying a product whose permitted use is very limited (para 27). Where rules prevent use "for the specific and inherent purposes for which they were intended" or "greatly restrict their use" (for the national court), they hinder market access and are MEEs unless justified (para 28). AG Kokott had argued use restrictions should be treated like selling arrangements; the Court did not follow her (Lectures 4-5).

### 7. After Trailers: four tests depending on the type of measure (Handout 1; Lectures 4-5)
1. **Product requirements**: always an MEE.
2. **Selling arrangements**: the Keck provisos.
3. **Restrictions on use**: MEE if they prevent or greatly restrict the use of the product.
4. **All other measures**: the general *Dassonville* formula (e.g. restrictions on parallel imports in *Kohlpharma* (C-602/19)).
So classification still largely determines the test.

### 8. Price rules: categorisation and complication
- *Scotch Whisky Association* (C-333/14): Scottish minimum price per unit of alcohol (MPU, GBP 0.50, formula MPU x S x V x 100). The Court did not apply Keck; it restated *Dassonville* (para 31) and held that, because the rule prevented the lower cost price of imported products being reflected in the retail price, it was by itself capable of hindering access to the UK market for drinks lawfully marketed elsewhere: an MEE (para 32). (Justification: see `article-36-justifications-and-proportionality.md`.)
- *Colruyt* (C-221/15): ban on selling cigarettes below the price indicated by manufacturer or importer (listed in Handout 1).
- *Deutsche Parkinson Vereinigung* (C-148/15): German fixed prices for prescription-only medicines. Citing *DocMorris*, the Court reasoned that traditional pharmacies are better placed to give individual advice and emergency supply, so price competition matters more for mail-order pharmacies, and mail order is a more important, perhaps the only, way for pharmacies in other States to reach German patients (paras 23-25). The fixed-price system has a greater impact on pharmacies in other Member States, impeding market access more than for domestic products: an MEE (paras 26-27). Price rules are classic selling arrangements, so this is effectively a failure of the second Keck proviso, though framed in market access terms.

## Key authorities
- *Keck and Mithouard* (C-267/91, C-268/91): selling arrangements outside Art 34 if the provisos are met.
- *Familiapress* (C-368/95): content-related promotion rule = product requirement.
- *Punto Casa* (C-69/93); *Leclerc-Siplec* (C-412/93); *Hünermund* (C-292/92): selling arrangements not caught.
- AG Jacobs in *Leclerc-Siplec*: substantial restriction on market access test.
- *De Agostini* (C-34/95): advertising ban; second proviso and effective means of market penetration.
- *DocMorris* (C-322/01); *Ker-Optika* (C-108/09); *DocMorris* (C-190/20): distance/online selling and promotional rules.
- *Commission v Portugal* (C-265/06): tinted film use ban.
- *Commission v Italy (Trailers)* (C-110/05): "any other measure which hinders access"; use restriction; road safety justification.
- *Mickelsson and Roos* (C-142/05): prevents or greatly restricts use test.
- *Kohlpharma* (C-602/19): residual Dassonville category.
- *Scotch Whisky* (C-333/14); *Colruyt* (C-221/15); *Deutsche Parkinson* (C-148/15): price rules.

## Debate and critique
- **Why Keck?** To relieve the Court of Sunday-trading-type litigation and restore State autonomy over market-structuring rules. Horsley sees selling arrangements as one of three "exclusionary rules" keeping "market circumstances rules" out of Art 34, and regrets the recent widening.
- **Spaventa, "Leaving Keck behind?" (2009) 34 EL Rev 914**: *Trailers* and *Mickelsson* confirm that the Keck distinction by type of rule is no longer relevant; what matters is the effect on market access. They realign goods with persons, so barriers to economic freedom ("freedom to trade") may now be caught. "Market access" is a somewhat deceiving term masking a move to a freedom-to-trade reading. This raises constitutional questions about national regulatory autonomy (the "Sunday trading trap" returns), so the Court must leave a wide margin of appreciation; but it is preferable to the confused "exception to the exception" approach.
- **Lianos, "In Memoriam Keck" (2015) 40 EL Rev 225**: Keck's categorisation approach gave States more policy space, but the settlement has eroded and the latest case law has returned to an overbroad MEE definition, replacing categorisation with balancing of interests. Causes include the difficulty of applying fuzzy categories and the external dimension: new trade and investment regimes (CETA, TTIP, investor-State dispute settlement) compete with Art 34 as fora for traders, which pressures interpretation.
- **Gormley, "Inconsistencies and misconceptions" (2015) 40 EL Rev 925**: the case law is riddled with inconsistent reasoning from a divided Court. *Dassonville* and, "sadly", to an extent Keck survive the use cases; there is not and should not be a de minimis rule; case-law justifications should not be assimilated with Art 36. Revisionist views favouring local interests are misconceived ("a wailing call to Ballygobackwards").
- **Schütze, "Of Types and Tests" (2016) 41 EL Rev 826**: post-Keck there are three lines following three market models: selling arrangements (international model, non-discrimination), product requirements (federal model, *Cassis*), and consumer-use restrictions after *Trailers* (close to a national market model, elevating market access). In *Ker-Optika* the Court rhetorically combined them in a unitary framework, but ambivalences remain.
- **Snell (2010)**: market access "obscures rather than illuminates"; it conceals the choice between anti-discrimination and economic freedom models; if there is no middle ground the term should be abandoned. Note the Court focuses on the significance of the impact but denies a de minimis rule.
- **AG Jacobs v the Court**: an impact-based test (substantial hindrance) instead of type-based classification is attractive, but it introduces a de minimis threshold the Court has rejected, and risks national courts excluding too much.
- **Exam essay structure**: (1) Dassonville breadth and its problems; (2) Keck's attempt; (3) erosion through strict second proviso; (4) use restrictions and "any other measure"; (5) price cases; (6) evaluate: is Keck dead (Lianos), alive (Gormley), merged into market access (Spaventa, Schütze), or is market access itself incoherent (Snell)?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify what the rule regulates: the product itself, the conditions of sale, the use of the product, or something else (prices, parallel imports).
2. **Product requirement** (including promotional content forming part of the product, *Familiapress*): MEE; go to justification.
3. **Selling arrangement**: check proviso 1 (all traders) and proviso 2 (same effect in law and fact). Look for facts suggesting imports are hit harder: newcomers who depend on the banned channel (advertising in *De Agostini*, internet sales in *DocMorris*, price competition in *Deutsche Parkinson*), outright bans rather than partial limits, rules favouring established local traders. If both provisos met: outside Art 34. If not: MEE, justify.
4. **Use restriction**: does it prevent use for the product's specific and inherent purpose or greatly restrict use (*Mickelsson*, *Trailers*, *Commission v Portugal*)? If so, MEE.
5. **Price rules**: note *Scotch Whisky* (MEE because import cost advantages cannot be reflected) and *Deutsche Parkinson*.
6. **Otherwise**: Dassonville.
7. Justification and proportionality; note the margin of appreciation in safety (*Trailers*) and that the State need not disprove every conceivable alternative.
Traps: assuming a selling arrangement is automatically safe; treating advertising rules as product requirements (they are usually selling arrangements, unless content is affected); forgetting that the national court decides the factual market-access question.
