# Direct effect of the freedoms - vertical, horizontal and the Article 34 exception

## Overview
The Treaty free movement provisions do not say whom they bind. The Court quickly held that they all have **vertical** direct effect (individuals can rely on them against the State). Whether they bind **private parties** (horizontal direct effect) has been answered differently per freedom: workers, establishment and services reach collective regulators and, for workers, even private employers, while **Article 34 TFEU (goods) has no horizontal direct effect**. The Court has softened this through attribution to the State and through the State's positive duty to prevent private obstacles. This is step 2 of the free movement structure. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. Vertical direct effect - all freedoms
- All free movement provisions can be relied on against the State in national courts (Handout 1).
- Goods: *Iannelli* (C-74/76).
- Workers: *Van Duyn v Home Office* (41/74) (Art 45 vertically directly effective) (Barnard textbook ch 11).
- Against which State? For workers, against the host State (direct, indirect and non-discriminatory barriers) and against the home State for exit restrictions and restrictions on return (Handout 3; *Singh*).

### 2. Horizontal effect - persons and services
Handout 3 sets out three levels for Art 45, which Handout 4 says apply equally to Arts 49 and 56:
1. **Vertical**: against Member States.
2. **"Semi-horizontal"**: rules regulating employment collectively, e.g. collective agreements or rules of professional or sporting bodies, in all cases (direct, indirect and non-discriminatory): *Walrave and Koch* (36/74) (UCI cycling rules); *Bosman* (C-415/93) (UEFA transfer rules). Rationale (textbook): in many States working conditions are set by collective agreements as well as law, so limiting the prohibition to public authorities would reduce its effect and create inequality in its application.
3. **Horizontal**: private parties may not impose directly or indirectly discriminatory conditions: *Angonese* (C-281/98) (a private bank in Bolzano required a bilingualism certificate obtainable only by an exam in that province; this disadvantaged other Member States' nationals; the non-discrimination prohibition applied to private employers). Cf Art 7(4) Regulation 492/2011.
- Establishment / services: trade union action to stop a business relocating: *Viking* (C-438/05); also *Laval* (C-341/05).
- Handout 1: the general consensus is that every freedom except Art 34 now has horizontal direct effect, but the Court has only been explicit for Art 45 (*Angonese*). Some commentators call the sporting body and union cases "extended vertical direct effect" because those bodies had state-like powers.

### 3. Article 34 TFEU: no horizontal direct effect
- *Van de Haar* (C-177/82): Art 34 does not bind private parties. Rationale: private conduct is for competition law (Arts 101-102 TFEU, addressed to undertakings), State action is for the free movement rules (Lectures 4-5; Krenn).
- Krenn identifies *Sapod Audic* (C-159/00) as the clearest rejection: a contractual provision cannot be a barrier to trade under Art 34 since it was agreed between individuals, not imposed by a State.

### 4. Ways around the rule for goods
1. **Bodies controlled by the State**: *Commission v Ireland (Buy Irish)* (C-249/81): a campaign run by a body effectively controlled by the State was attributable to the State.
2. **Bodies to which the State delegates a task**: *Hennen Olie* (C-302/88).
3. **Private bodies with State-conferred regulatory power**: *Fra.bo* (C-171/11). DVGW was a non-profit private-law body, not State-financed, over which Germany had no decisive influence (para 24). But (i) German legislation deemed DVGW-certified products compliant with national law (para 27); (ii) DVGW was in practice the only body able to certify the copper fittings, the expert alternative being of little or no practical use (paras 28-29); and (iii) lack of DVGW certification considerably restricted marketing, as almost all consumers bought DVGW-certified fittings (para 30). DVGW therefore "in reality holds the power to regulate the entry into the German market" (para 31). Held: Art 34 applies to standardisation and certification activities of a private body where national legislation treats its certified products as compliant and this restricts marketing of uncertified products (para 32). Handout 1 asks whether this is horizontal or vertical effect (see van Leeuwen's 2013 EJRR note on *Fra.bo*, cited there).
4. **The State's positive duty to act against private obstacles** (Art 34 read with the duty of sincere cooperation, now Art 4(3) TEU):
   - *Commission v France (Spanish Strawberries)* (C-265/95): France breached Art 34 by failing to take adequate measures against French farmers' violent campaign against imported produce.
   - *Schmidberger* (C-112/00): Art 34 applies not only to State measures but also where a State abstains from adopting measures needed to deal with obstacles not caused by the State (para 57); inaction can obstruct trade as much as a positive act (para 58); States must take all necessary and appropriate measures to ensure free movement on their territory (para 59), including for goods merely in transit (paras 60-62), especially on a major route like the Brenner (para 63). Not banning a demonstration that closed the Brenner for almost 30 hours was capable of restricting trade and so an MEE in principle (para 64). But the restriction was justified by freedom of expression and assembly: the authorities have a wide margin of discretion (para 82); unlike *Spanish Strawberries*, the protest was authorised, on one route, on one occasion, for about 30 hours, not aimed at goods of a particular origin, accompanied by publicity and alternative routes, and did not create a general climate of insecurity (paras 84-88); an outright ban or stricter conditions could have been an unacceptable interference with the rights (paras 89-90); so the authorities were entitled to think the aim could not be achieved by less restrictive means (para 93). Not incompatible with Arts 30 and 34 read with Art 5 (now Art 4(3) TEU) (para 94).
   - The State has discretion in weighing interests, and the threshold for requiring State intervention is high (Lectures 4-5).

## Key authorities
- *Iannelli* (C-74/76): Art 34 vertical direct effect.
- *Van Duyn* (41/74): Art 45 vertical direct effect.
- *Walrave and Koch* (36/74); *Bosman* (C-415/93): collective regulation by private bodies caught.
- *Angonese* (C-281/98): Art 45 binds private employers (discrimination).
- *Viking* (C-438/05); *Laval* (C-341/05): trade union collective action within Arts 49/56.
- *Van de Haar* (C-177/82); *Sapod Audic* (C-159/00): no horizontal direct effect for Art 34.
- *Buy Irish* (C-249/81); *Hennen Olie* (C-302/88); *Fra.bo* (C-171/11): attribution to the State.
- *Commission v France (Spanish Strawberries)* (C-265/95); *Schmidberger* (C-112/00): positive duty to act.

## Debate and critique
- **Krenn, "A Missing Piece in the Horizontal Effect 'Jigsaw'" (2012) 49 CML Rev 177**: horizontal effect has developed in a piecemeal, context-specific way (*Bosman*, *Angonese*, *Viking*). He describes early "glimpses" of horizontal effect in the intellectual property cases (e.g. a "subjective test" of the rights holder's intention in *Terrapin* and *Centrafarm*) before the clear rejection in *Sapod Audic* and *Van de Haar*. He argues there are compelling reasons to give Art 34 horizontal direct effect, built on the *effet utile* reasoning the Court used for Arts 45, 49 and 56, subject to limits (respect the text, fit the Treaty system, go no further than necessary). He proposes a flexible **de minimis threshold** for private conduct, set lower for citizens' free movement than for goods, because citizens' free movement protects autonomous individual rights and not only the market.
- **van Leeuwen (2017)**: horizontal effect is one of the developments merging stages of the structure (direct effect and restriction); in deciding whether a private actor is bound, the Court looks at the impact of its conduct, which pushes the real decision into proportionality.
- **Arguments for horizontal effect of Art 34** (drawing on the materials): consistency with other freedoms; private actors (certification bodies, unions, protest groups) can block trade as effectively as States; *Fra.bo* shows the line between public and private regulation is blurred; State enforcement can be slow or ineffective (as in *Spanish Strawberries*).
- **Arguments against**: the text targets State "measures"; competition law already addresses private market conduct; extending Art 34 would impose heavy obligations on private parties and could restrict private autonomy; the State-duty route (*Schmidberger*) already gives protection while allowing balancing with fundamental rights.
- **Is *Fra.bo* horizontal or vertical?** One reading: vertical, because DVGW's power came from legislation (a status-based, "extended vertical" test). Another: a move towards an impact-based test, because DVGW was a genuinely private body and what mattered was its effect on market access.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the defendant: State organ, regional body, public body, State-controlled entity, private regulator, trade union, private company, protesters.
2. **Persons / services**: State or collective regulator, any type of restriction (*Walrave*, *Bosman*, *Viking*); private employer, at least discrimination (*Angonese*).
3. **Goods**: if the defendant is private, Art 34 cannot be relied on directly (*Van de Haar*, *Sapod Audic*). Then ask:
   - Is it controlled by the State (*Buy Irish*)?
   - Has the State delegated a task (*Hennen Olie*)?
   - Does national law give its decisions regulatory force so that it effectively controls market access (*Fra.bo* factors: legal presumption of conformity, monopoly in practice, alternative routes impractical, market dependence)?
   - If not, sue the **State** for failing to act (*Spanish Strawberries*); then the State can justify its inaction, e.g. by fundamental rights, and has wide discretion (*Schmidberger*). Compare the facts with paras 84-88 of *Schmidberger* (authorisation, duration, targeting of foreign goods, mitigation measures, violence).
4. Mention competition law (Arts 101-102 TFEU) as the alternative route against private undertakings.
5. Traps: assuming Art 34 binds a private certification body without analysing the *Fra.bo* factors; forgetting that a State's failure to act is itself the "measure".
