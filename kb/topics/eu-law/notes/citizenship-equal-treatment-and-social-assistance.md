# Equal treatment and social assistance for mobile citizens - Article 24 Directive 2004/38 from Grzelczyk to Dano and Alimanovic

## Overview
How far must a host State share its welfare system with other Member States' nationals who are not working? The early case law (*Grzelczyk*, *Trojani*) used citizenship and Article 18 TFEU to demand "a certain degree of financial solidarity". Directive 2004/38 then built a graduated scheme: Article 24(1) promises equal treatment, Article 24(2) withholds social assistance for the first three months and from job-seekers, and Article 7(1)(b) makes residence of the inactive depend on sufficient resources. *Brey*, *Dano* and *Alimanovic* tied equal treatment to lawful residence under the Directive, sharply limiting claims by the inactive poor. This is the most debated part of citizenship law. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. The provisions
- **Art 18 TFEU**: no discrimination on grounds of nationality within the scope of the Treaties, "without prejudice to any special provisions".
- **Arts 20(2) and 21(1) TFEU**: rights exercised subject to the conditions and limits in the Treaties and implementing measures.
- **Art 24(1) Dir 2004/38**: all citizens residing on the basis of the Directive enjoy equal treatment with nationals within the scope of the Treaty (extended to family members).
- **Art 24(2)**: the host State need not grant social assistance during the first three months of residence or, for job-seekers, the longer Art 14(4)(b) period; nor, before permanent residence, maintenance aid for studies (grants or loans) to persons other than workers, the self-employed, those retaining that status and their family members (*Dano* para 65).
- **Art 7(1)(b)**: inactive citizens need sufficient resources and comprehensive sickness insurance so as not to be an unreasonable burden.
- **Art 14(1), (3)**: residence lasts while not an unreasonable burden; recourse to social assistance must not automatically lead to expulsion.
- **Art 16 / Art 24**: permanent residents (five years) enjoy full equal treatment.
- **Reg 883/2004**: Art 4 (equal treatment) and Art 70 (special non-contributory cash benefits, paid in the State of residence).

### 2. The solidarity phase
- *Martínez Sala* (C-85/96): lawful residence allows reliance on non-discrimination in all matters within EU law's material scope.
- *Grzelczyk* (C-184/99): a French student in Belgium refused the minimex in his final year; discrimination solely on nationality (para 29); citizenship "destined to be the fundamental status" (para 31); recourse to social assistance can lead to loss of residence, but **never automatically** (paras 42-43); the residence directives accept "a certain degree of financial solidarity" especially for **temporary** difficulties; only an **"unreasonable"** burden matters (para 44).
- *Trojani* (C-456/02): a citizen not a worker may reside under Art 18(1) EC (now 21 TFEU) subject to proportionate conditions; once lawfully resident with a **residence permit**, he may rely on Art 12 EC (now 18 TFEU) to obtain the minimex.

### 3. The Directive's graduated scheme (the materials; Handout 2)
1. **First three months**: residence unconditional (Art 6) but no right to social assistance (Art 24(2)); residence can end if the person becomes an unreasonable burden.
2. **Three months to five years**: equal treatment applies, but inactive citizens must satisfy Art 7(1)(b); the host State may treat recourse to social assistance as an indication of insufficient resources (*Brey* C-140/12), though not automatically leading to expulsion (Art 14(3)): a **case-by-case assessment** (*Brey*).
3. **Five years**: permanent residence; full equal treatment including social assistance and maintenance aid.
4. **Economically active**: the Art 24(2) limits do not apply to workers, the self-employed and their family members: *García-Nieto* (C-299/14) (the materials; Handout 2).

### 4. Dano (C-333/13): lawful residence as precondition
- Facts: Ms Dano, a Romanian national born in 1989, lived in Leipzig with her son in her sister's flat; received child benefit and maintenance advances; three years' schooling, no qualifications, limited German literacy, no work in Germany or Romania and no sign of looking for work (paras 35-39). She claimed SGB II basic provision benefits (special non-contributory cash benefits).
- Reasoning:
  - Art 18 TFEU is given specific expression for mobile citizens in Art 24 Dir 2004/38 and Art 4 Reg 883/2004, so those provisions are interpreted (paras 60-62). Special non-contributory cash benefits are "social assistance" (para 63, citing *Brey* para 61).
  - Art 24(2) did not apply to her: she had been resident over three months and was not seeking work (para 66).
  - Under Art 24(1) only citizens residing "on the basis of the directive" enjoy equal treatment; so for inactive citizens resident between three months and five years, the question is whether they meet Art 7(1)(b), including sufficient resources (paras 68-73).
  - Allowing those without a right of residence to claim benefits would undermine the Directive's aim of preventing an unreasonable burden (recital 10) (para 74). Art 7(1)(b) "seeks to prevent economically inactive Union citizens from using the host Member State's welfare system to fund their means of subsistence" (para 76). Unequal treatment is "an inevitable consequence" of the Directive (para 77).
  - A State may refuse benefits to inactive citizens who move "solely in order to obtain another Member State's social assistance" without sufficient resources (para 78). The financial situation of each person must be examined specifically, without counting the benefits claimed (para 80).
  - She lacked sufficient resources, had no right of residence and could not invoke Art 24(1) (paras 81-82; same for Art 4 Reg 883/2004: para 83).
  - The Charter did not apply because, in setting conditions for these benefits, States are not implementing EU law (paras 90-91).
- the materials's summary: as an economically inactive citizen Ms Dano must satisfy Art 7(1)(b) to benefit from equal treatment under Art 24; she was not entitled.

### 5. Alimanovic (C-67/14) and after
- Job-seekers whose retained worker status had lapsed could be refused subsistence benefits (social assistance broadly defined) when residing only under Art 14(4)(b); no individual assessment required; the accumulation of claims would be an unreasonable burden (paras 44-46, 52-62). Full detail in `jobseekers-and-access-to-benefits.md`.
- *CG* (C-709/20): the Court applies the *Dano*/*Alimanovic* case law to EU citizens with pre-settled status under the UK Withdrawal Agreement (Handout 2; see `brexit-and-citizens-rights.md`).
- Handout 3: after *Lebon* and *Alimanovic*, "very few benefits" remain available to job-seekers.

### 6. Students and maintenance aid
- Art 24(2): no obligation to grant maintenance grants or loans before permanent residence, unless the student is a worker/self-employed (or family member) (Handout 2; the materials). Students reside under Art 7(1)(c) on a declaration of resources (as in *Grzelczyk* under Dir 93/96, where a student's declaration is assessed when made: para 45).
- *Brown* (197/86) once excluded maintenance assistance from the Treaty; *Grzelczyk* distinguished it after Maastricht (paras 34-35); Handout 3 asks how *Förster* fits.

- *Brown* (197/86): student maintenance.

## Debate and critique
- **Thym, "The Elusive Limits of Solidarity: Residence Rights of and Social Benefits for Economically Inactive Union Citizens" (2015) 52 CML Rev 17**: uncertainty stems from the legislature's indecision; the Directive contains internal discrepancies, leaving the Court to distil coherence. *Brey* and *Dano* separated free movement from social security coordination and held that those without a residence right cannot claim equal treatment. The "unreasonable burden" formula is the key test, but three ambiguities remain: (1) a **systemic** versus an **individual** assessment of burden (Thym argues the individual approach prevails as the line between lawful and unlawful residence); (2) access depends on the "real link/integration" formula, which the Court must develop; (3) rights are acquired and lost automatically, so flexible thresholds create legal uncertainty in borderline cases. Underlying are two visions: a "residence model" (territorial presence) and one promoting social cohesion/integration.
- **Kramer, "Earning Social Citizenship in the European Union" (2016) 18 CYELS 270**: *Dano* reflects "earned citizenship", a technique of neoliberal communitarianism requiring citizens to earn welfare access through individual responsibility and economic, social and cultural conditions; the idea existed in early case law but was reconstructed by the *Dano* line. He notes the Court's rulings gave Western European States (and the UK's renegotiation agenda) support.
- **O'Brien (2016)**: the retreat leaves a "moral vacuum"; class and income now determine who benefits from free movement; the work/inactivity boundary carries all the weight.
- **Nic Shuibhne (2015)**: secondary law has been given supremacy over primary law; *Dano* makes Art 24 an exhaustive expression of equal treatment, limiting Art 18 TFEU through Art 21, which "induces normative migraine"; the general principles filter (proportionality, Charter) was set aside without explanation. (*Dano* paras 90-91 found the Charter inapplicable.)
- **Defenders**: the Court respects the legislature's choices in the Directive, gives legal certainty (*Alimanovic* paras 60-61), and protects national welfare systems and political support for free movement (cf the Brexit context; Handout 2: free movement as a "political battle ground").
- **Handout 2 check-list**: What issues need balancing for inactive citizens? Has the Court struck the right balance? To what extent do they have equal treatment?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Classify the claimant**: worker/self-employed (or retained status) → full equal treatment, Art 24(2) limits do not apply (*García-Nieto*); job-seeker → see job-seekers note; student → maintenance aid limits; inactive → continue; permanent resident → full equal treatment.
2. **Length of residence**: under three months → no social assistance (Art 24(2)); three months to five years → step 3; over five years lawful residence → full equal treatment.
3. **Lawful residence under the Directive?** For inactive citizens: sufficient resources and sickness insurance (Art 7(1)(b)), assessed individually without counting the benefit claimed (*Dano* para 80). If not met → no Art 24(1) equal treatment (*Dano*).
4. **Is the benefit "social assistance"?** Use the *Dano*/*Brey* definition and the "predominant function" test (*Alimanovic* para 45); benefits facilitating labour market access are different (*Vatsouras*).
5. **Expulsion?** Recourse to social assistance is not automatically a ground for expulsion (Art 14(3); *Grzelczyk*); and economic reasons cannot justify public policy expulsion (Art 27(1)).
6. **Earlier case law**: if the citizen holds a residence permit and is lawfully resident, *Trojani* suggests equal treatment applies; but after *Dano* the Directive conditions control.
7. **Essay**: frame as solidarity versus protection of welfare systems; deploy Thym, Kramer, O'Brien, Nic Shuibhne.
Traps: applying *Grzelczyk* without addressing *Dano*; forgetting the economically active are unaffected by Art 24(2).
