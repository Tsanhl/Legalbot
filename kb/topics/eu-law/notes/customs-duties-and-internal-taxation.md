# Fiscal barriers to goods - the customs union, Article 30 and Article 110 TFEU

## Overview
Money charged on goods is dealt with separately from non-fiscal obstacles. **Article 30 TFEU** absolutely prohibits customs duties and charges having equivalent effect (CEEs) on goods crossing frontiers; **Article 110 TFEU** allows Member States to tax products but prohibits discriminatory or protective **internal taxation**. Articles 30 and 110 are mutually exclusive with each other and with Article 34 TFEU, so classification is the key skill. Jurisdiction: EU law. (After Brexit, Northern Ireland must still comply with Arts 30 and 110 under the Protocol: see `brexit-and-free-movement-of-goods.md`.)

## The law

### 1. The customs union: Article 28 TFEU
- Two dimensions: **internal** (no customs duties on goods moving between Member States) and **external** (a common customs tariff on goods from third countries).
- Once third-country goods are in **free circulation** (duties paid and formalities completed), they are treated as EU goods and move freely.
- The common external tariff explains why the UK did not want to stay in the customs union after Brexit: it would have prevented its own trade deals.

### 2. Article 30 TFEU: customs duties and CEEs
1. **Customs duty**: a sum paid on goods entering or leaving a Member State. The two elements in *Sociaal Fonds voor de Diamantarbeiders* (Joined Cases 2 & 3/69): (a) a pecuniary charge (b) imposed because goods cross a frontier.
2. **CEE**: *Commission v Italy (Statistical Levy)* (24/68): "any pecuniary charge, however small and whatever its designation and mode of application, which is imposed unilaterally on domestic or foreign goods by reason of the fact that they cross a frontier, and which is not a customs duty in the strict sense". Italy's 10 lira levy on imports and exports to fund statistics was a CEE despite being small. A similar formula is in *Bresciani* (87/75) (cited as "C-87/85").
3. **Purpose and effect**: the purpose of the charge and the use of its revenue are irrelevant; it is the effect that matters (*Diamantarbeiders*: a levy on imported diamonds for a workers' social fund; *Statistical Levy*). The prohibition does not depend on whether the goods compete with domestic products.
4. **Internal frontiers**: charges on goods crossing regional borders inside one State are also CEEs: *Lancry* (C-363/93), *Carbonati* (C-72/03). The discussion asks whether this fits the wording of Art 30.
5. **No justification**: a CEE is a breach; it cannot be justified (*Diamantarbeiders*; *Statistical Levy*: a fundamental rule that "does not permit of any exceptions"; and recently *FENS* (C-305/17)).
6. **Two exceptions (the charge is then not a CEE at all; this is not justification)**:
   - **Consideration for a genuine service** to the individual importer or exporter. The bar is high: the service must directly benefit the trader. *Statistical Levy* (statistics benefited the economy generally, not individual traders); *Commission v Belgium (Belgian Warehousing)* (C-132/82). *Bresciani*: public health inspections imposed in the general interest are not a service to the importer, so a fee for them was a CEE even though proportionate to cost.
   - **Fee for an inspection required by EU law**, under the conditions in *Bauhuis* (46/76) and *Commission v Germany* (18/87): (a) the fee does not exceed the actual cost (no profit); (b) the inspection is obligatory and uniform for all products in the EU; (c) it is prescribed by EU law in the general EU interest; (d) it promotes free movement (e.g. by removing obstacles from unilateral national inspections).
7. **Repayment**: unlawful charges must be reimbursed, with conditions set by national law, but not where the trader has passed the cost on to consumers (no unjust enrichment): *Comateb* (C-192/95).

### 3. Article 110 TFEU: internal taxation
States keep the power to decide whether and how to tax products, subject to two prohibitions:
1. **Art 110(1)**: no internal taxation on imported products in excess of that on **similar** domestic products.
2. **Art 110(2)**: no internal taxation that gives **indirect protection** to other domestic products in **competition** with imports.
Always start with similarity; if products are not similar, ask if they compete.

**Similarity (Art 110(1))**
- An impressionistic test of objective characteristics (content, method of production, price, origin, taste/alcohol content) and whether they meet the same consumer needs: *John Walker* (243/84) (whisky and fruit liqueur wine had manifestly different characteristics, so not similar).
- Direct discrimination (higher tax on imports) is a breach and cannot be justified.
- Indirect discrimination: a neutral-seeming system with a heavier burden on imports. *Humblot* (112/84): French car tax with a progressive tax up to 16 hp and a flat tax almost five times higher above it; no French cars exceeded 16 hp; discriminatory/protectionist, breach of Art 110.
- Indirect discrimination can be justified by **objective criteria** pursuing legitimate economic policy aims compatible with EU law, provided no discrimination or protection results: *Chemial Farmaceutici* (140/79): Italy taxed synthetic ethyl alcohol (made from petroleum, not produced in Italy) at about six times the rate of fermented alcohol (from agricultural products); the products were chemically identical; the differentiation, aimed at favouring agricultural raw materials and saving petroleum for more important uses, was compatible with EU law.
- Remedy: equalise the tax on domestic and imported products.

**Competition (Art 110(2))**
- Broader than 110(1): products need not be similar, only in (actual or potential) competition, possibly assessed using economic tests such as cross-elasticity of demand.
- *Commission v UK (Wine and Beer / Excise Duties on Wine)* (170/78): UK taxed wine (mostly imported) much more heavily than beer (mostly domestic). The UK argued different production, strength, price and drinking habits. The Court found that at least cheaper, lighter wines competed with beer; consumer habits are not immutable and tax policy must not "crystallise" them; the tax stamped wine as a luxury and protected beer: breach of Art 110(2). It looked at potential future competition as well as current.
- Remedy: remove the protective effect. This is not the same as equalising the taxes.

### 4. Drawing the line: Article 30 vs Article 110
- Mutually exclusive: Art 30 for charges because goods cross a border; Art 110 for general internal taxation.
- **No domestic production**: a charge forming part of a general internal tax system still falls under Art 110 even if there is no domestic product (otherwise States could not tax exotic goods): *Co-Frutta* (193/85) (bananas).
- **Para-fiscal charges** (charges on all products whose revenue benefits domestic producers):
  - fully offset for domestic producers: CEE under Art 30: *Capolongo* (77/72);
  - partly offset: assess under Art 110 (discrimination to the extent of the partial refund): *Nygård* (C-234/99).

### 5. Fiscal vs non-fiscal: Articles 30/110 vs Article 34
- Arts 30 and 110 cover monetary charges; Art 34 covers non-fiscal measures. Example: a mandatory health inspection of imported meat is assessed under Art 34; the fee charged for it under Art 30 or 110.

## Key authorities
- *Diamantarbeiders* (2 & 3/69): elements of a customs duty; purpose irrelevant; no justification.
- *Commission v Italy (Statistical Levy)* (24/68): CEE definition; "however small"; service exception narrow.
- *Bresciani* (87/75): inspection in the general interest not a service.
- *Lancry* (C-363/93); *Carbonati* (C-72/03): regional internal frontiers.
- *Belgian Warehousing* (C-132/82): service must benefit the trader.
- *Bauhuis* (46/76); *Commission v Germany* (18/87): fees for EU-mandated inspections.
- *Comateb* (C-192/95): repayment; no unjust enrichment.
- *FENS* (C-305/17): CEEs cannot be justified.
- *John Walker* (243/84): similarity test.
- *Humblot* (112/84): indirect discrimination in car tax.
- *Chemial Farmaceutici* (140/79): objective criteria justification.
- *Commission v UK (Wine and Beer)* (170/78): competing products; indirect protection.
- *Co-Frutta* (193/85): no domestic product, still Art 110.
- *Capolongo* (77/72); *Nygård* (C-234/99): para-fiscal charges.

## Debate and critique
- **Absolute v relative prohibitions**: Art 30 is absolute (no justification), Art 110 is a non-discrimination rule that leaves tax policy to States. This mirrors the "international market" model of non-discrimination rather than the broader obstacle model under Art 34 (compare Schütze's models in `keck-selling-arrangements-and-market-access.md` and Snell's note that the Court uses discrimination analysis for national tax rules, in `structure-of-free-movement-law.md`).
- **Internal frontiers**: extending Art 30 to regional charges (*Lancry*, *Carbonati*) sits awkwardly with the wording and with the purely internal rule; the discussion invites critique.
- **Similarity test**: criticised in the discussion as "quite impressionistic"; the Court mixes physical characteristics and consumer use, and in *Wine and Beer* relied on years of evidence-gathering.
- **Crystallising consumer habits**: the Court's refusal to let tax entrench national tastes shows the market-building purpose of Art 110(2), but raises questions about the Court second-guessing fiscal sovereignty.
- **Unjust enrichment**: limits on repayment protect State budgets but can undercut effective remedies for traders.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the measure a **charge** (money) or a non-fiscal rule? If non-fiscal, go to Art 34.
2. Is the charge imposed **because goods cross a frontier** (including regional borders)? If yes, it is a customs duty/CEE under Art 30 regardless of name, size or purpose.
   - Check the two exceptions: a genuine, specific service to the trader; or a fee for an EU-mandated inspection meeting the *Bauhuis*/*Commission v Germany* conditions (cost only, obligatory and uniform, required by EU law, promotes trade).
   - If neither applies: unlawful, no justification; repayment subject to unjust enrichment (*Comateb*).
3. Is it part of a general system of internal taxation applying to domestic and imported goods alike? Then Art 110.
   - Is the revenue returned to domestic producers? Fully: Art 30 (*Capolongo*); partly: Art 110 (*Nygård*).
   - No domestic production? Still Art 110 (*Co-Frutta*).
4. Under Art 110, are products **similar** (*John Walker*)? If yes: direct discrimination (unlawful) or indirect discrimination (*Humblot*) which may be justified by objective criteria (*Chemial*). Remedy: equalise.
5. If not similar, are they in **competition** (*Wine and Beer*)? If yes, does the tax protect the domestic product? Remedy: remove the protective effect.
Traps: trying to justify a CEE; equalising taxes under Art 110(2); assessing an inspection's existence under Art 30 (that is Art 34).
