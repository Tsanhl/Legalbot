# Justifying restrictions on goods - Article 36 TFEU, mandatory requirements, fundamental rights and proportionality

## Overview
Once a national measure restricts Article 34 TFEU, the Member State may justify it on a **Treaty derogation** in Article 36 TFEU or on a **mandatory requirement** recognised by the Court since *Cassis de Dijon*, and must then show the measure is **proportionate**. Most free movement cases are ultimately decided at the proportionality stage (Handout 1; the materials), which makes this note essential for both essays and problems. The same structure (with different labels) applies to persons and services. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. Article 36 TFEU: Treaty derogations
Art 36 permits restrictions justified on grounds of:
1. public morality;
2. public policy;
3. public security;
4. protection of health and life of humans, animals or plants;
5. protection of national treasures possessing artistic, historic or archaeological value;
6. protection of industrial and commercial property.
**Proviso (second sentence)**: restrictions must not constitute a means of arbitrary discrimination or a disguised restriction on trade between Member States.

Leading cases by ground:
- **Public morality**: *R v Henn and Darby* (34/79): UK import ban on pornographic material was a QR but justified; each State determines its own standards of public morality. *Conegate* (121/85; listed in the Handout as C-34/79 and in the slides as C-121/85): UK seized inflatable sex dolls; a State cannot rely on public morality to ban imports when it has no prohibition on the manufacture or marketing of the same goods domestically (no penal or other serious and effective measures against domestic goods) - this would be arbitrary discrimination / disguised restriction.
- **Public policy**: almost no successful cases (the materials). *Cullet* (231/83): French minimum petrol prices; the threat of civil unrest from price competition was not accepted (Barnard). Public policy now includes protection of **fundamental rights** (*Schmidberger*).
- **Public security**: *Campus Oil* (72/83): Ireland required importers to buy a proportion of oil from the State refinery; interruption of oil supplies could seriously threaten public security, so justified.
- **Health and life**: *Rosengren* (C-170/04): Swedish ban on private imports of alcohol irrespective of age went "manifestly beyond what is necessary" to protect young people; *Bluhme* (C-67/97): ban on keeping bees other than the Læsø brown bee on a Danish island justified as preserving biodiversity of an indigenous animal population; *BS and CA* (C-663/18) (cannabidiol; listed for study).
- **Scientific uncertainty / precaution**: *Sandoz* (174/82): Dutch ban on muesli bars with added vitamins; since science could not determine the critical amount of vitamin intake, Member States may decide the appropriate degree of health protection, subject to proportionality, and must authorise marketing where adding vitamins meets a technical or nutritional need.

### 2. No purely economic justifications
- Purely economic aims (protecting domestic industry, revenue) are excluded. But the Court is more flexible where an economic aim is linked to a non-economic interest (Handout 1): *Decker* (C-120/95): Luxembourg required prior authorisation to buy medical products (spectacles) abroad for reimbursement; health arguments were rejected (mutual recognition of qualifications; prescription guaranteed health protection), but the Court accepted in principle that risk of seriously undermining the **financial balance of the social security system** could be an overriding reason.

### 3. Mandatory requirements (Cassis de Dijon)
- Created in *Cassis* (120/78, para 8): obstacles from disparities between national laws must be accepted in so far as necessary to satisfy mandatory requirements, in particular fiscal supervision, public health, fairness of commercial transactions and consumer defence.
- Rationale (Handout 1): the Art 36 list is exhaustive and dates from 1957; the Court needed to allow for social and technological change. It is an **open, non-exhaustive** category: States can always argue for a new interest.
- Main examples: **consumer protection** (*Cassis*; *Mars* C-470/93); **environmental protection** (*Commission v Denmark (Danish Bottles)* 302/86: recognised, but the Danish reusable-container scheme failed proportionality; *PreussenElektra* C-379/98; *Essent Belgium* C-204-208/12); **financial balance of social security** (*Decker*). Barnard lists others: protection of children, road safety (*Commission v Italy (Trailers)*), public order, fiscal supervision, fundamental rights, prevention of crime and fraud; press diversity (*Familiapress*); socio-cultural characteristics (*Torfaen*).
- **Orthodox rule**: mandatory requirements justify only indistinctly applicable measures; distinctly applicable (directly discriminatory) measures can only be justified under Art 36 (*Cassis*; Handout 1).
- **Flexibility**: in *PreussenElektra* (German law requiring electricity suppliers to buy all renewable electricity produced in their area) the Court accepted an environmental justification for a measure that appeared to discriminate. Explanations: environmental protection is so important that it should also cover distinctly applicable measures; or, more formally, it can be "upgraded" into Art 36 health and life protection (Handout 1; Barnard says the reasoning is unclear).

### 4. Fundamental rights
Two directions (the materials; Handout 1):
1. **As a justification**: protection of fundamental rights is a legitimate interest with the status of a Treaty derogation under "public policy". *Schmidberger* (C-112/00): freedom of expression and assembly justified not banning the Brenner motorway protest; the authorities have a wide margin of discretion; a "fair balance" must be struck (paras 81-82); see `horizontal-direct-effect-of-free-movement.md` for the detailed balancing. *Omega* (C-36/02) (human dignity; laser games) is cited for the margin of discretion in sensitive areas.
2. **As a limit on justifications**: when restricting free movement, States must respect fundamental rights; any justification must be compatible with them. *Familiapress* (C-368/95): the press-diversity justification had to be reconciled with freedom of expression.

### 5. Proportionality
The decisive stage; where balancing happens (the materials, "the centralisation of proportionality").
1. **Suitability**: is there a causal link between the measure and the aim? The measure need not be 100% effective, but must be applied in a **consistent and coherent** way, ideally based on (scientific) evidence (*Hartlauer* C-169/07). Inconsistency undermines suitability (compare *Conegate*).
2. **Necessity**: are there less restrictive, equally effective alternatives? Has the State considered them? Labelling instead of a ban (*Cassis* paras 12-13). Tends to be intrusive, with a bias towards finding alternatives, so the Court often leaves it to the national court while listing relevant factors. But the State need not prove that no other conceivable measure could achieve the aim, and may adopt simple, general, enforceable rules (*Trailers* paras 66-67); States choose their level of protection, and another State's laxer rule does not make a stricter one disproportionate (*Trailers* para 65). A total ban on all tinted film was excessive because some films permit the visual inspection sought (*Commission v Portugal* C-265/06).
3. **Proportionality stricto sensu**: occasionally, a final balancing of costs against benefits (*Danish Bottles*).
- **Intensity** varies: marginal review and a broad margin in sensitive fields (*Omega*); strict review where protectionism is suspected.
- **Who decides**: the Court often "delegates" proportionality to the national court in factually complex or sensitive cases; where the answer is obvious it decides itself.

**Worked examples**
- *Humanplasma* (C-421/09): Austria allowed import only of blood from donors who received no compensation at all (not even travel costs or refreshments). Aims: blood quality and safety; encouraging voluntary donations. Held disproportionate: since every donation is tested, the total ban on compensation was not necessary for quality and safety; the Court drew on other States' practice and the directive accepting small tokens, refreshments and travel reimbursement as compatible with voluntary donation (Barnard).
- *Scotch Whisky Association* (C-333/14): minimum price per unit of alcohol (MPU) to protect health. The Court: (i) Arts 34 and 36 preclude choosing MPU and rejecting a measure such as **increased excise duties** that may be less restrictive; for the referring court to decide on a detailed analysis; the fact that MPU may bring extra benefits and be a broader response to alcohol misuse cannot itself justify rejecting the tax alternative; (ii) the national court must examine objectively whether the evidence shows the means are appropriate and whether less restrictive measures exist; (iii) proportionality is judged on the evidence available to the national court on the date of its ruling, not only that available to the legislature. On return, the **UK Supreme Court** ([2017] UKSC 76, Lord Mance) dismissed the appeal: it identified a particular concern with excessive drinking in deprived communities, accepted that higher taxes would burden moderate drinkers, and treated the unmeasured effect on inter-State trade as part of stricto sensu proportionality, rejecting the petitioners' arguments; MPU came in from May 2018 (Dunne).
- *Deutsche Parkinson Vereinigung* (C-148/15): fixed medicine prices; Handout 1 asks students to analyse its proportionality reasoning (the Court found an MEE; see Keck note).

## Debate and critique
- **Spaventa, "On discrimination and the theory of mandatory requirements" (2001) 3 CYELS 457**: traditionally discriminatory measures could be justified only under the Treaty derogations, while indistinctly applicable ones could also use mandatory requirements; economic considerations may be taken into account (though not exclusively) under mandatory requirements but not Treaty derogations. She argues *Decker* is not inconsistent with earlier case law and the environmental cases could be solved by a generous reading of the Art 36 derogations. Treating mandatory requirements as a judicial extension of Art 36 would pose legitimacy problems; treating them as objective justification for indirect discrimination assumes Art 34 only prohibits discrimination. Her preferred theory: mandatory requirements are **internal to the definition of an MEE** (a rule justified by a mandatory requirement is not an MEE at all), which fits the outcomes and the Court's wish to keep the categories separate, and can extend to other freedoms.
- **Gormley (2015)**: the case-law justifications and Art 36 should not be assimilated.
- **van Leeuwen (2017)**: the assimilation of justifications is one of the developments that, with market access and horizontal effect, has centralised proportionality as the main decision tool; he argues this does not improve the internal market and the other pillars should be developed.
- **Nic Shuibhne and Maci, "Proving Public Interest" (2013) 50 CML Rev 965**: evidence and proof have become increasingly important; the Court expects States to rationalise public interest choices rigorously, not merely assert them ("States really do have no one else to blame"). But there are no detailed procedural rules on evidence and national approaches vary; proof factors can determine outcomes, blurring procedure and substance; and it is misleading to give the language of proof a certainty it cannot deliver when public interest choices are complex, contestable and value-laden, in a system with a "thin culture of proof".
- **Dunne, "Minimum Alcohol Pricing: Balancing the 'Essentially Incomparable' in Scotch Whisky" (2018) 81 MLR 890**: the CJEU's intervention was "excessively doctrinaire and ultimately ill-advised", though the Scottish Government also defended the measure on an unnecessary legal fiction. Proportionality is flexible or ambiguous: despite the CJEU's technical framing it involves an inherent judgment call, and the EU and domestic courts weighed values differently; Lord Mance doubted that such essentially incomparable values can be reconciled. The case raises the question at what level such decisions should be made, and whether heavy reliance on such an elusive criterion suits a coherent internal market now that proportionality is the cornerstone of free movement after the market access turn.
- **de Witte (2013)**: in morally sensitive areas, review should be procedural (coherence, consistency, regulatory equivalence) rather than substantive.
- **Public health without EU competence**: Handout 1 asks whether the Court gave enough discretion to States in public health, a field where the EU cannot harmonise; a key essay theme (deference, Zglinski's "Europe's Passive Virtues" is listed as further reading).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Classify the restriction** first: distinctly applicable (direct discrimination) or indistinctly applicable / obstacle. This determines available justifications.
2. **Identify the State's aim** from the facts. Reject purely economic aims (protect local producers, raise revenue), but consider whether an economic aim is tied to social security balance (*Decker*).
3. **Match the aim** to Art 36 (any measure) or a mandatory requirement (indistinctly applicable only, orthodox; mention *PreussenElektra* flexibility for environment).
4. **Check the Art 36 proviso**: is there arbitrary discrimination or a disguised restriction (e.g. domestic equivalents tolerated: *Conegate*)?
5. **Fundamental rights**: can the State invoke rights (expression, assembly, dignity) as justification (*Schmidberger*, *Omega*)? Does the restriction itself infringe rights (*Familiapress*)?
6. **Proportionality**:
   - Suitability: evidence of causal link; consistency of the State's policy (exceptions, tolerance of similar domestic products).
   - Necessity: list concrete alternatives (labelling, information, taxation, partial bans, age limits, licensing, testing), and say whether they are equally effective. Remember the State's right to choose its level of protection and to use simple rules (*Trailers*).
   - Stricto sensu if appropriate.
   - Evidence: the State bears the burden to show suitability and necessity with evidence (*Scotch Whisky*; Nic Shuibhne and Maci); national court assesses on current evidence.
7. **Conclude** that the final decision is likely for the national court, giving the factors it should weigh.
Traps: relying on mandatory requirements for an overtly discriminatory rule without discussion; ignoring evidence; forgetting that a Treaty derogation can justify any type of restriction.
