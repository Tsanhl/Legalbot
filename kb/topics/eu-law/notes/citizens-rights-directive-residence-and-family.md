# Directive 2004/38 - beneficiaries, family members and rights of residence

## Overview
Directive 2004/38 (the Citizens' Rights Directive) is the main piece of secondary law giving effect to Article 21 TFEU. It replaced the earlier fragmented instruments (including the three 1990s residence directives) and codified much of the Court's citizenship case law. It sets out who benefits (Union citizens who move and their family members), the graduated rights of residence (up to three months, more than three months, permanent), equal treatment (Art 24), and the derogations (Arts 27-33). This note covers scope, family members and residence; equal treatment is in `citizenship-equal-treatment-and-social-assistance.md` and expulsion in `citizenship-derogations-and-abuse.md`. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. Personal scope: Article 3
1. The Directive applies to Union citizens who **move to or reside in a Member State other than that of their nationality** (the host State) and to their family members who accompany or join them, whatever the family members' nationality (Art 3(1)).
2. **Union citizens**: anyone holding a Member State nationality; nationality is for national law (*Chen* C-200/02). In *Chen*, Catherine was born in Belfast to a Chinese mother, acquiring Irish nationality; mother and daughter moved to Wales; the UK could not restrict the effects of Ireland's grant of nationality, and the reasons for moving are immaterial.
3. **No purely internal application**: the Directive does not apply to citizens in their own State (*Ruiz Zambrano* para 39). But:
   - **Returning citizens ("circular situations")**: *O and B* (C-456/12): where a citizen created or strengthened family life with a third-country national during genuine residence in another State, the Directive applies **by analogy** on return to the home State; conditions for the family member's derived right should not be stricter than in the Directive. (the discussion asks why the Court used Art 21 TFEU.) Earlier: *Singh* (C-370/90).
   - **Dual nationals who naturalise in the host State**: *Lounes* (C-165/16): Ms Ormazabal, Spanish, moved to the UK in 1996, worked there, naturalised British in 2009 keeping Spanish nationality, then married Mr Lounes, an Algerian overstayer, in 2014. Held: he has no derived right under the Directive (she now lives in a State of which she is a national), but he is eligible for a derived right under **Art 21(1) TFEU**, on conditions no stricter than the Directive's.

### 2. Family members
**Core family members (Art 2(2))** - automatic derived rights:
1. **Spouse**. *Metock* (C-127/08): the Directive precludes a rule requiring a third-country national spouse to have been lawfully resident in another Member State before arriving; it applies irrespective of when and where the marriage took place and how the spouse entered the host State. The Court expressly "reconsidered" *Akrich* (C-109/01) on prior lawful residence (para 58). The spouse's immigration status at the time of marriage is irrelevant.
2. **Registered partner**, if the host State treats registered partnerships as equivalent to marriage (cf *Netherlands v Reed* 59/85).
3. **Direct descendants under 21 or dependants**, including those of the spouse or partner.
4. **Dependent direct relatives in the ascending line**, including those of the spouse or partner. *Jia* (C-1/05): a Chinese mother-in-law of a German self-employed person in Sweden; dependency is determined by individual assessment; the need for material support must exist in the State of origin or from which the relative comes; proof by "appropriate means"; national law may be more generous.

**Other family members (Art 3(2))** - the host State "shall facilitate" entry and residence:
- other family members who are dependants or members of the household of the citizen, or who need the citizen's personal care on serious health grounds;
- a partner in a duly attested durable relationship.
- No right as such; the State must examine personal circumstances and justify any refusal.

**Same-sex spouses**: *Coman* (C-673/16): Mr Coman (Romanian) and Mr Hamilton (US) married lawfully in another Member State where Mr Coman had genuinely resided. On return to Romania, **Art 21(1) TFEU** precluded refusing Mr Hamilton residence on the ground that Romanian law does not recognise same-sex marriage; he has a derived right of residence for more than three months on conditions no stricter than Art 7. Recognition for the sole purpose of a derived residence right does not undermine the institution of marriage (defined by national law), national identity (Art 4(2) TEU) or public policy, which is interpreted strictly (genuine and sufficiently serious threat) (paras 43-46); restrictions must also respect the Charter (para 47).

### 3. Rights of exit and entry (Arts 4-5)
- Exit and entry on a valid ID card or passport; third-country family members may need a visa (Art 5; see also Art 5(4)). Lack of documents or visa does not justify turning someone back at the frontier (*MRAX* C-459/99).

### 4. Residence up to three months (Art 6)
- Only a valid ID card or passport needed; unconditional for citizens and family members, as long as they do not become an **unreasonable burden** on social assistance (Art 14(1)). No obligation to grant social assistance (Art 24(2)).

### 5. Residence for more than three months (Art 7)
Citizens qualify if they are:
1. **Workers or self-employed** (Art 7(1)(a)): no further conditions. **Job-seekers** have residence rights under **Art 14(4)(b)** if they provide evidence that they continue to seek work and have a genuine chance of being engaged. Retention of worker status: Art 7(3) (see `workers-concept-and-status.md`).
2. **Economically independent** (Art 7(1)(b)): sufficient resources for themselves and family so as not to be an unreasonable burden, and comprehensive sickness insurance in the host State. *Dano* (C-333/13) treats meeting these conditions (at least sufficient resources) as a precondition for the Directive's protection.
3. **Students**: enrolled in an educational establishment, comprehensive sickness insurance, and sufficient resources (a declaration suffices).
4. **Family members** of these citizens, whatever their nationality; they may reside and take up economic activity. For students, only spouse, registered partner and dependent children (Art 7(4)).

**Retained rights of family members**
- **Death or departure of the citizen (Art 12)**: family members may retain residence, e.g. third-country nationals who have resided at least a year before the death; on departure, if they are themselves citizens or if children in education remain with the parent who has custody (cf *Baumbast and R*, the "R" part).
- **Divorce or termination of marriage/partnership (Art 13)**: third-country spouses retain rights only if conditions are met (marriage lasted three years including one in the host State; custody or access rights to children; or particularly difficult circumstances such as domestic violence). Family members who retain rights must then meet the Art 7 conditions themselves.

**Administrative formalities**
- Citizens may be required to register for stays over three months (Art 8); a registration certificate is issued immediately on proof of Art 7 conditions. Failure to register can attract proportionate, non-discriminatory sanctions but never expulsion (*Royer* 48/75).
- Third-country family members receive a residence card (Art 9); absences up to six months a year or for important reasons do not affect it (Art 11). The State has no discretion: refusal only on public policy/security grounds or if the marriage is not genuine.
- **Free movement within the host State (Art 22)**: territorial restrictions only if equally imposed on own nationals (cf *Rutili* 36/75; *Olazabal* C-100/01).

### 6. Permanent residence (Arts 16-21)
- After **five years' continuous lawful residence** citizens and family members (including third-country nationals) acquire permanent residence (Art 16). Lost only through absence of more than **two consecutive years** (Art 16(4)).
- Retired or incapacitated economically active persons can acquire it sooner (Art 17).
- Benefits: residence becomes unconditional (no Art 7 conditions); full equal treatment including social assistance and maintenance aid; stronger protection against expulsion ("serious grounds", Art 28(2)).
- Documents: Arts 19-20. "Good behaviour" can matter for continuity: *M.G.* (C-400/12); *Onuekwere* (C-378/12) (cases mentioned but not covered).
- The discussion notes that over 92,000 permanent residence applications were reported in the UK in 2016 after the referendum.

### 7. Relationship between the Directive and the Treaty
- The Directive implements Art 21 but cannot reduce primary rights; where it does not apply, Art 21 may fill the gap (*Lounes*, *Coman*, *O and B*), usually by applying the Directive's conditions by analogy.
- In the social assistance cases the Court now treats the Directive's conditions as decisive (*Dano*), a development criticised by Nic Shuibhne as giving secondary law "supremacy".

## Debate and critique
- **Graduated residence and the financial independence split**: the Directive maintains the 1990s distinction between financially independent and dependent citizens, so citizenship rights are effectively conditioned on resources. O'Brien and Kramer see this as class-based or "earned" citizenship (see `union-citizenship-articles-20-21.md`).
- **Family reunification and immigration control**: *Metock* prioritised the effectiveness of free movement over national immigration policy (prior lawful residence no longer required), which was politically controversial; Member States retain only fraud/abuse (Art 35) and public policy tools. *Coman* shows EU law requiring recognition of status for free movement purposes while respecting national competence over marriage.
- **Use of Art 21 when the Directive does not apply**: *O and B*, *Lounes* and *Coman* use primary law to fill gaps, applying the Directive by analogy. This raises the question of the relationship between the Directive and Arts 20-21 and why the Court relies on the Treaty in some cases but subordinates the Treaty to the Directive in *Dano* (Nic Shuibhne's critique).
- **Should the Union be more or less generous to family members?** (the discussion check-list). Consider the "facilitate" duty for durable partners and extended family under Art 3(2) as a compromise.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Who is the citizen, and are they in a host State?** If in their own State, consider whether they previously moved (*O and B*), are dual nationals (*Lounes*), or fall within *Zambrano*; otherwise purely internal.
2. **Is the claimant a family member?** Core (Art 2(2)) or extended (Art 3(2))? For ascendants/descendants over 21, check **dependency** (*Jia*). For spouses, irrelevance of prior lawful residence and of where/when the marriage took place (*Metock*); same-sex spouses (*Coman*). Watch for marriages of convenience (Art 35).
3. **Which residence period?** Under three months (Art 6); over three months (Art 7: worker/self-employed, job-seeker under Art 14(4)(b), self-sufficient, student); five years plus (Art 16).
4. **Do the conditions hold?** Resources, sickness insurance (apply proportionality as in *Baumbast*), registration formalities (no expulsion for failure: *Royer*).
5. **Retained rights** on death, departure, divorce (Arts 12-13); children in education (*Baumbast and R*).
6. **Consequences**: equal treatment (Art 24; see social assistance note) and protection against expulsion (Arts 27-33).
Traps: applying the Directive to a citizen's own State; requiring a TCN spouse to have prior lawful residence; forgetting the narrower family category for students.
