# Union citizenship - Articles 18, 20 and 21 TFEU and the Court's case law

## Overview
Union citizenship, created by the Maastricht Treaty (1992), extended free movement beyond economically active persons. Article 20 TFEU makes every Member State national a Union citizen; Article 21 TFEU gives a directly effective right to move and reside, "subject to the limitations and conditions" in the Treaties and secondary law (now Directive 2004/38). The Court's early case law (*Martínez Sala*, *Grzelczyk*, *Baumbast*) treated citizenship as "destined to be the fundamental status" of nationals and used it with Article 18 TFEU (non-discrimination) to give inactive citizens rights; *Rottmann* and *Ruiz Zambrano* extended it to otherwise internal situations. Since around 2013 (*Brey*, *Dano*, *Alimanovic*) the Court has retreated, giving priority to the Directive's conditions. That arc is the core essay theme of Lectures 8-10. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. From economic actors to citizens (Handout 2)
1. **Economic freedoms only (Treaty of Rome)**: workers, establishment, services gave extensive equal treatment (access to employment, tax and social advantages under Art 7(2) Reg 492/2011, things connected with receiving a service). But those who did not move (reverse discrimination) or were not economically active were excluded.
2. **The 1990 residence directives**: Dir 90/364 ("playboy" directive: general right of residence for nationals with sufficient resources and comprehensive health insurance so as not to be an unreasonable burden), Dir 90/365 (pensioners), Dir 93/96 (students, who need only assure the authorities they have resources). They introduced the split between the financially independent (protected) and the dependent (not protected), a split still present in Directive 2004/38. All are now repealed and subsumed in Directive 2004/38.
3. **Maastricht (1992)**: Art 17 EC (now Art 20 TFEU) and Art 18 EC (now Art 21 TFEU).

### 2. The Treaty provisions
- **Art 20(1) TFEU**: "Citizenship of the Union is hereby established. Every person holding the nationality of a Member State shall be a citizen of the Union. Citizenship of the Union shall be additional to and not replace national citizenship." (Lisbon changed "complement" to "additional to".)
- **Art 20(2) TFEU**: citizens "shall enjoy the rights and be subject to the duties provided for in the Treaties", including the right to move and reside; to vote and stand in EP and municipal elections in the State of residence; diplomatic and consular protection in third countries where their own State is unrepresented; to petition the EP; the citizens' initiative (see also Arts 22-24 TFEU). the materials asks: partial rights with few duties?
- **Art 21(1) TFEU**: "Every citizen of the Union shall have the right to move and reside freely within the territory of the Member States, subject to the limitations and conditions laid down in the Treaties and by the measures adopted to give them effect." Given flesh by Directive 2004/38.
- **Art 18 TFEU**: no discrimination on grounds of nationality within the scope of the Treaties.
- **Nationality is for the Member States**, but must be exercised with due regard to EU law (*Rottmann* para 45; *Chen* C-200/02: a host State cannot restrict the effects of another State's grant of nationality).

### 3. The road from Martínez Sala to Baumbast
- *Martínez Sala* (C-85/96): a citizen lawfully resident in a host State can rely on the non-discrimination principle in all situations within the material scope of EU law (as recalled in *Grzelczyk* para 32).
- *Grzelczyk* (C-184/99): a French student in Belgium, who had supported himself in earlier years, applied in his final year for the minimex (minimum subsistence allowance). Belgian nationals in the same situation would have received it; nationality was the only bar, so it was discrimination solely on grounds of nationality (para 29). Para 31: "Union citizenship is destined to be the fundamental status of nationals of the Member States, enabling those who find themselves in the same situation to enjoy the same treatment in law irrespective of their nationality, subject to such exceptions as are expressly provided for." *Brown* (197/86), which excluded maintenance assistance from the Treaty, was distinguished because citizenship and the education chapter had since been added (paras 34-35). A State may treat recourse to social assistance as meaning the student no longer meets the residence conditions and withdraw or not renew the permit, but this can never be the **automatic** consequence (paras 42-43). The residence directives accept "a certain degree of financial solidarity" between host nationals and other Member States' nationals, particularly where difficulties are temporary; beneficiaries must not become an "unreasonable" burden (para 44). Result: Arts 6 and 8 EC (now 18 and 21 TFEU) precluded making the minimex conditional on falling within Reg 1612/68 (para 46).
- *Baumbast and R* (C-413/99): Mr Baumbast, a German national, had worked in the UK, then worked outside the EU for German companies; his Colombian wife and children lived in the UK. He had sufficient resources and German sickness insurance, but it arguably did not cover emergency treatment in the UK. Held: the Treaty does not require citizens to pursue an economic activity to enjoy citizenship rights, and a former worker does not lose them when activity ends (para 83). Art 18(1) EC (now Art 21(1) TFEU) confers the right of residence "directly on every citizen of the Union by a clear and precise provision" (para 84): it is **directly effective**. The limitations and conditions are subject to judicial review (para 86) and must be applied in accordance with general principles, in particular **proportionality**: measures must be "necessary and appropriate" (para 91). Given his resources, years of lawful residence, family residence, no burden on public finances and comprehensive insurance in another Member State, refusing residence because his insurance did not cover emergency treatment in the UK was a "disproportionate interference" (paras 92-93). (The "R" part concerned children in education and a primary carer's right of residence; see the Directive note.)
- After *Baumbast*, much of the case law was codified in Directive 2004/38 (Handout 2).

### 4. Beyond movement: the "substance of rights" line
- Normally an intra-Union link (movement or intention to move) is needed; static citizens are protected by their own State, which can produce **reverse discrimination** (Handout 2).
- *Rottmann* (C-135/08): an Austrian-born national moved to Munich while under investigation for serious fraud in Graz, obtained German nationality without disclosing the proceedings, and thereby lost Austrian nationality under Austrian law; Germany then sought to withdraw the naturalisation, which would leave him stateless (paras 22-27). A decision placing a citizen in a position capable of causing him to lose citizenship status falls "by reason of its nature and its consequences" within EU law (para 42). Withdrawing nationality obtained by deception is legitimate in principle (it is not contrary to EU law) provided it observes **proportionality** (para 59): the national court must consider the consequences for him and his family, the gravity of the offence, the time elapsed since naturalisation and whether he can recover his original nationality (paras 55-56); the State need not refrain merely because the original nationality has not been recovered (para 57), but a reasonable period to try to recover it may be required (para 58). the materials: "EU law matters!"
- *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09): Colombian parents whose asylum claims in Belgium were refused had two children born in Belgium who acquired Belgian nationality (Colombian law not granting nationality without specific steps). The Directive did not apply (no movement: para 39). The children were Union citizens (para 40). Para 42: "Article 20 TFEU precludes national measures which have the effect of depriving citizens of the Union of the genuine enjoyment of the substance of the rights conferred by virtue of their status as citizens of the Union." Refusing the third-country parent of dependent minor citizen children a right of residence and a work permit has that effect (para 43), since the children would have to leave the territory of the Union to accompany their parents, or the family would lack resources (para 44).
- **Narrowness**: Handout 2 stresses *Ruiz Zambrano* is an exception that the Court has not applied again in the same way: *Dereci* (C-256/11). Lenaerts describes a "stone-by-stone" approach.
- **Art 21 against the home State**: Art 21 protects citizens against their own State once movement (even potential) is established: residence conditions for benefits (*Pusa* C-224/02; justifiable per *De Cuyper* C-406/04); matters outside EU competence such as names (*Garcia Avello* C-148/02) and war victims' benefits (*Tas-Hagen* C-192/05); *D'Hoop* (C-224/98). Handout 2 asks whether having moved is enough to fall within the Treaty, contrasting *D'Hoop* with *Dano*.

### 5. The changing mood (Handout 2; the materials)
- *Brey* (C-140/12), *Dano* (C-333/13), *Alimanovic* (C-67/14), and *CG* (C-709/20) (applying the *Dano*/*Alimanovic* line to EU citizens with pre-settled status under the Withdrawal Agreement).
- *Dano*: a Romanian national in Leipzig with her son, no qualifications, no work history, not looking for work, claimed German basic subsistence benefits. Because economically inactive citizens resident for more than three months but less than five years must meet Art 7(1)(b) of the Directive (sufficient resources), and she did not, she had no right of residence under the Directive and so could not invoke equal treatment under Art 24(1) (paras 73-82). Unequal treatment is "an inevitable consequence" of the Directive (para 77); States may refuse benefits to inactive citizens who move "solely in order to obtain another Member State's social assistance" (para 78). Detail in `citizenship-equal-treatment-and-social-assistance.md`.

## Key authorities
- Arts 18, 20, 21, 22-24 TFEU.
- *Martínez Sala* (C-85/96): equal treatment for lawfully resident citizens.
- *Grzelczyk* (C-184/99): "fundamental status"; financial solidarity; no automatic expulsion.
- *Baumbast and R* (C-413/99): Art 21 directly effective; conditions subject to proportionality.
- *Chen* (C-200/02): nationality and reasons for moving immaterial.
- *Rottmann* (C-135/08): loss of nationality within EU law; proportionality.
- *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09): genuine enjoyment of the substance of rights.
- *Dereci* (C-256/11): narrowness of *Zambrano*.
- *Pusa* (C-224/02); *De Cuyper* (C-406/04); *Garcia Avello* (C-148/02); *Tas-Hagen* (C-192/05); *D'Hoop* (C-224/98): Art 21 and the home State.
- *Brey* (C-140/12); *Dano* (C-333/13); *Alimanovic* (C-67/14); *CG* (C-709/20): the retreat.

## Debate and critique
- **Maximalist vs minimalist** (Garner, "The Existential Crisis of Citizenship of the European Union: The Argument for an Autonomous Status", in the materials folder): "maximalists" celebrate the judicial construction of citizenship as the fundamental status; "minimalists" say it should stay "additional to" nationality with supplementary rights. Garner proposes making EU citizenship an **autonomous status** that individuals could choose, detached from Member State nationality, as a possible new constitutional settlement.
- **Spaventa, "Seeing the Wood Despite the Trees?" (2008) 45 CML Rev 13**: citizenship has broadened both personal and material scope; every citizen is within the Treaty's personal scope regardless of economic or cross-border link; Art 21 lets citizens challenge home-State rules affecting movement and host-State rules that limit residence or discriminate. She argues the right not to be discriminated against (Art 18) should be a citizenship right in itself, so static citizens comparable to migrants would have equal treatment from EU law. Citizenship sits uncomfortably with differentiation based solely on crossing a border; the *Baumbast* proportionality approach has constitutional effects, empowering individuals; "there should be no space for an Orwellian approach whereby some citizens are more equal than others".
- **Nic Shuibhne, "Limits Rising, Duties Ascending" (2015) 52 CML Rev 889**: a generational shift towards conditions, limits and (implicitly) duties in citizenship law, calling into question the existence, not just the exercise, of primary rights. She criticises a "hegemonic attribution of supremacy to secondary law": the Directive now dictates the content of Treaty rights, the reverse of the usual hierarchy. The explanation that Art 24 of the Directive limits Art 18 TFEU via Art 21 "induces normative migraine" and undercuts decades of equal treatment as an autonomous principle. Restrictions must remain subject to primary law, the Charter and general principles; when the Court sets aside that filter, especially when reversing earlier case law without saying so, it owes a careful explanation.
- **O'Brien, "Civis Capitalist Sum: Class as the New Guiding Principle of EU Free Movement Rights" (2016) 53 CML Rev**: as citizenship fades, the boundary between work and inactivity becomes critical. The Court's growing tolerance of nationality discrimination creates a "moral vacuum" exploited by States to impose their own definitions of work; low-income and insecure workers (zero-hours contracts) risk exclusion from equal treatment; the benefits of free movement are reserved for the better resourced. Domestic distortions then feed back into EU law.
- **Kramer, "Earning Social Citizenship in the European Union" (2016) 18 CYELS 270**: "earned citizenship" as a technique of neoliberal communitarianism: citizens must earn access to welfare by showing individual responsibility and meeting economic, social and cultural conditions. Visible since the early case law, reconstructed in the *Dano* line.
- **Thym (2015)**: the case law fluctuates between a vision based on territorial presence and one based on social cohesion/integration (see the social assistance note).
- **Handout 2 questions**: Has the Court struck the right balance between inactive citizens' rights and Member States' welfare systems? Should EU law be more generous to family members? What is the relationship between the Directive and Arts 20-21?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the claimant a Union citizen (nationality of a Member State; *Chen*; *Rottmann* for loss of nationality)?
2. Has the citizen moved (or returned)? If they are in a host State, start with **Directive 2004/38** (see `citizens-rights-directive-residence-and-family.md`); the Directive codifies most citizenship case law.
3. If the Directive does not apply (citizen in own State, dual national, returnee), consider **Art 21 TFEU** directly (*Baumbast* direct effect; *O and B*; *Lounes*; *Coman*) and **Art 20** for the *Zambrano* "substance of rights" test (would a citizen, usually a minor child, be forced to leave the EU?).
4. If the issue is a benefit, check lawful residence (Art 7) and equal treatment under Art 24 and *Dano*/*Alimanovic* (see `citizenship-equal-treatment-and-social-assistance.md`).
5. If the issue is expulsion, go to Arts 27-33 (see `citizenship-derogations-and-abuse.md`).
6. Apply **proportionality** to any condition or limitation (*Baumbast*; *Rottmann*; *Grzelczyk*: no automatic consequences).
Traps: assuming citizenship gives an unconditional right to reside or to benefits; forgetting that *Zambrano* is exceptional; ignoring Art 21 when the Directive does not apply.
