# Company mobility - Daily Mail, Centros, Inspire Art, Uberseering, SEVIC, Cartesio, VALE, Polbud

## Overview
Arts 49 and 54 TFEU give companies formed under the law of a Member State, with a registered office, central administration or principal place of business in the Union, the right of establishment. Lectures 16 and 17 ask how far a company can move, or set up elsewhere, to take advantage of a more attractive company law. The point is framed as **regulatory competition / arbitrage**. Positive integration replaces national laws with EU legislation; negative integration strikes down barriers while leaving legal diversity. How the Court applies the establishment rules therefore determines whether firms can choose among national company laws. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law
### 1. Two scenarios
1. **Re-incorporation**: a company in one State becomes a company of another (the materials's example: a German AG becoming an Irish plc).
2. **Split of incorporation and activity**: a company incorporated in one Member State but with its real place of business in another. Most case law concerns this scenario, and it shows an evolution in the Court's thinking.

The connecting factor adopted by Member States matters: the **incorporation theory** (law of the State of incorporation governs) versus the **real seat theory** (law of the State of the central administration governs). *Cartesio* refers to the "real seat" (siege reel) as the place of central administration.

### 2. Cases, in order
1. **Daily Mail (C-81/87)**: Daily Mail wished to move its central management and control from the UK to the Netherlands while remaining incorporated in the UK. The Court said the differences in national legislation on the connecting factor for companies, and whether and how the registered office or real head office of a national company may be moved to another Member State, were problems not resolved by the establishment rules but to be dealt with by future legislation or conventions. So a home State could restrict emigration of its own companies.
2. **Centros (C-212/97)**: Danish nationals formed a company in the UK (apparently to avoid Danish company law, including capital rules) and set up a branch in Denmark. The Court held that forming a company in the State whose company law seems least restrictive and setting up branches elsewhere cannot in itself be an abuse of the right of establishment; the right is inherent in the single market. (Quoted in the materials.) Impact: the materials cites Becht, Mayer and Wagner (2008) 14 J Corp Finance 241 that between 2003 and 2006 over 67,000 new private limited companies were established in the UK from other Member States, with an average of 146 incorporations per country-year before *Centros* rising to 671 afterwards; incorporation costs and minimum capital requirements explained the flows.
3. **Inspire Art (C-167/01)**: a company formed in the UK with a branch in the Netherlands where all trading took place. Dutch rules imposed minimum capital and directors' liability requirements on "formally foreign companies". The Court held it contrary to the right of establishment to impose on secondary establishment by a company formed in another Member State conditions from domestic company formation law relating to minimum capital and directors' liability; the reasons for the choice of State and the fact that it operates exclusively or almost exclusively in the State of establishment do not deprive it of the right to rely on the freedom, save where abuse is established case by case. The operative part also holds the disclosure obligations not provided for by the Eleventh Company Law Directive 89/666/EEC (Art 2) cannot be imposed on the branch.
4. **Uberseering (C-208/00)**: a Dutch company's actual centre of administration moved to Germany after all its shares were transferred to German residents. the materials quotes the Court noting that in *Daily Mail* it had not ruled on whether a host State (B) could refuse to recognise the legal personality a company enjoys under its State of incorporation (A) after it moves its centre of administration to B. (Only the framing of the question is in the sources used here.)
5. **SEVIC Systems (C-411-03)**: the Court held Arts 43 and 48 EC preclude a Member State generally refusing to register a merger (dissolution without liquidation and transfer of all assets to another company) where one of the companies is established in another Member State, when registration is possible where both companies are established in the first Member State. Cross-border mergers thus fall under establishment.
6. **Cartesio (C-210/06)**: a Hungarian company wanted to transfer its actual centre of administration to Italy. The Court held that a Member State has the power to define the connecting factor needed for a company to count as incorporated under its law and capable of enjoying the right of establishment, and the factor needed to keep that status; this includes the power to refuse to let a company governed by its law keep that status if it reorganises in another Member State by moving its seat and so breaking the connecting factor. As Community law then stood, Arts 43 and 48 EC did not preclude legislation under which a company incorporated under that State's law cannot transfer its seat to another Member State while retaining its status as a company of the State of incorporation. (The Court also decided references-procedure questions on the commercial register court.)
7. **VALE (C-378/10)**: a company wished to convert from Italy to Hungary (cross-border conversion). The Court held Arts 49 and 54 TFEU preclude national legislation that lets domestic companies convert but does not generally allow companies of another Member State to convert into a company governed by national law. It read the *Cartesio* phrase "to the extent that it is permitted under that law" not as removing host-State conversion law from the Treaty, but as reflecting that a company exists only through national law. The host State may determine the applicable national law and apply its conversion rules (for example asset and liability lists and inventories), but the principles of **equivalence and effectiveness** prevent it from refusing to record the converting company as predecessor if that is done for domestic conversions, or from refusing to take account of documents from the State of origin.
8. **Polbud (C-106/16)**: a Polish company wanted to convert into a Luxembourg company while keeping its real seat in Poland; Polish law required liquidation. The Court held that freedom of establishment applies to the transfer of the registered office of a company to another Member State for conversion into a company of that State, under that State's conditions, where there is **no change of the real head office**; and that Arts 49 and 54 preclude legislation making such a transfer conditional on liquidation of the first company.

### 3. Synthesis
- **Outbound** (moving out of the State of incorporation): Member States keep some freedom to define the connecting factor and refuse to let a company keep its national status if it moves its seat (*Daily Mail*, *Cartesio*), but may not block a conversion of the registered office into a company of the host State on the ground only of liquidation (*Polbud*), and must apply equivalence and effectiveness (*VALE*).
- **Inbound** (host State dealing with a foreign company): the host State cannot impose domestic company formation rules on a foreign-incorporated company with a branch (*Centros*, *Inspire Art*), and mergers and conversions must be treated like domestic ones (*SEVIC*, *VALE*).
- **Abuse** must be shown case by case; choosing a lenient law is not in itself abuse.
- The four-part *Gebhard* justification test applies to restrictions (see freedom-of-establishment.md; the *Centros* judgment applies the same four conditions).

## Key authorities
- *Daily Mail* (C-81/87); *Centros* (C-212/97); *Uberseering* (C-208/00); *Inspire Art* (C-167/01); *SEVIC* (C-411/03); *Cartesio* (C-210/06); *VALE* (C-378/10); *Polbud* (C-106/16).
- Arts 49 and 54 TFEU; Eleventh Company Law Directive 89/666/EEC (disclosure by branches).

## Debate and critique
- Regulatory competition: *Centros* is associated with a surge in UK incorporations (Becht, Mayer and Wagner); small differences in set-up costs and capital requirements had large effects. Supporters see competition between company laws; critics fear a race to the bottom, particularly for creditors and workers (the same concern as "social dumping" in *Viking* and *Laval*, discussed in freedom-of-establishment.md).
- Coherence: *Daily Mail* and *Cartesio* leave emigration control to the home State, while *Centros* and *Inspire Art* free immigration; *VALE* and *Polbud* narrow the home-State power by treating conversions and registered-office transfers as within the Treaty. The result is an asymmetry which the lectures describe as case law that is "somewhat complex" and evolving.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the move: change of incorporation, move of real seat only, branch in another State, merger, or conversion.
2. Is the restriction imposed by the home State (leaving) or host State (receiving)?
3. Host State and foreign company with branch: *Centros* / *Inspire Art*: can capital, directors' liability or disclosure rules be imposed? Only on a justified, proportionate basis; abuse case by case.
4. Home State and seat transfer retaining status: *Cartesio* (permitted to refuse); conversion into host-State form: *VALE*, *Polbud* (cannot demand liquidation or discriminate against foreign companies; equivalence and effectiveness).
5. Cross-border merger: *SEVIC*.
6. Justification: imperative requirements in the general interest, suitability, necessity.
