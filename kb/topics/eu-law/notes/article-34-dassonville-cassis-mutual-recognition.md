# Article 34 TFEU - scope, Dassonville, Cassis de Dijon and mutual recognition (the road to Keck)

## Overview
Article 34 TFEU prohibits quantitative restrictions on imports and all measures having equivalent effect (MEEs) between Member States. The Court defined MEEs very broadly in *Dassonville* (1974) and added the principle of **mutual recognition** and the open category of **mandatory requirements** in *Cassis de Dijon* (1979). The breadth of these tests let traders challenge almost any national rule (culminating in the Sunday trading cases, *Torfaen*), which led to *Keck* (1993). This note covers the law up to *Keck*; the post-Keck law is in `keck-selling-arrangements-and-market-access.md`. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. The text and the categories
- Art 34 TFEU: "Quantitative restrictions on imports and all measures having equivalent effect shall be prohibited between Member States." (Art 35 is the parallel provision for exports.)
- **Quantitative restrictions (QRs)**: import bans or quotas.
- **MEEs**: measures that do not directly ban or limit imports but have the same effect.
- Arts 34 and 30/110 are mutually exclusive: Art 34 covers non-fiscal measures (see `customs-duties-and-internal-taxation.md`).

### 2. Scope and direct effect (summary)
- Cross-border element required; rules whose effect is "too uncertain and indirect" are outside Art 34 (*Krantz* C-69/88); but potential impact suffices (*Pistre*; *Mickelsson and Roos* C-142/05). See `purely-internal-situations.md`.
- Vertical direct effect (*Iannelli* C-74/76); no horizontal direct effect (*Van de Haar* C-177/82), with the *Buy Irish* / *Fra.bo* / *Schmidberger* qualifications. See `horizontal-direct-effect-of-free-movement.md`.

### 3. The Dassonville formula
- *Procureur du Roi v Dassonville* (8/74): traders bought Scotch whisky in free circulation in France and imported it into Belgium without the certificate of origin from British customs required by Belgian rules; such a certificate could be obtained only with great difficulty by a parallel importer, unlike a direct importer (paras 3-4).
- Para 5: "All trading rules enacted by Member States which are capable of hindering, directly or indirectly, actually or potentially, intra-Community trade are to be considered as measures having an effect equivalent to quantitative restrictions."
- Absent an EU system guaranteeing designations of origin, States may act against unfair practices, but the measures must be **reasonable** and the means of proof must not hinder trade and must be accessible to all EU nationals (para 6); they must not be arbitrary discrimination or a disguised restriction (para 7). The certificate requirement, harder for parallel importers to meet, was an MEE (paras 8-9).
- Consequences: the test focuses on **effect**, not intent; no de minimis threshold; it catches discriminatory and non-discriminatory rules. Traders welcomed it; Member States resented it because they had to justify a huge range of rules and prove proportionality, which cut into their regulatory autonomy.

### 4. Cassis de Dijon: mutual recognition and mandatory requirements
- *Rewe-Zentral v Bundesmonopolverwaltung für Branntwein* (120/78): German law fixed a minimum alcohol content (25%) for fruit liqueurs, preventing marketing of French Cassis (about 15-20%).
- Para 8: in the absence of common rules, States regulate production and marketing on their territory. Obstacles resulting from disparities between national laws must be accepted in so far as they are **necessary to satisfy mandatory requirements**, in particular "the effectiveness of fiscal supervision, the protection of public health, the fairness of commercial transactions and the defence of the consumer".
- Germany's health argument (low-alcohol drinks induce tolerance) failed: consumers can buy a wide range of weak drinks and strong drinks are often diluted (paras 10-11). Its consumer protection/fairness argument failed on necessity: suitable information could be given by labelling the origin and alcohol content (paras 12-13). The rules mainly promoted high-alcohol drinks by excluding other States' products (para 14).
- **Mutual recognition** (para 14): there is "no valid reason why, provided that they have been lawfully produced and marketed in one of the Member States, alcoholic beverages should not be introduced into any other Member State". Products complying with home-State rules enjoy a presumption of regulatory equivalence; requiring compliance with a second set of rules (the **dual burden**) is a restriction that must be justified.
- The mandatory requirements (now an open list) may justify only indistinctly applicable measures (orthodox rule). See `article-36-justifications-and-proportionality.md`.

### 5. Distinctly and indistinctly applicable MEEs
After *Cassis* the Court distinguished:
1. **Distinctly applicable MEEs** (directly discriminatory): rules that distinguish between domestic and imported goods.
   - *Commission v Ireland (Buy Irish)* (C-249/81): a State-funded campaign to promote Irish goods.
   - *Commission v Ireland (Irish Souvenirs)* (113/80): imported souvenirs suggesting Ireland had to bear an origin mark or the word "Foreign", while domestic ones did not: "indisputably" discriminatory and an MEE.
   - Other examples: country-of-origin labels, inspections of imports only.
2. **Indistinctly applicable MEEs**: apply to all goods but in effect make imports harder. Most common: **product requirements** (rules on content, form, size, packaging, labelling, presentation).
   - *Walter Rau v de Smedt* (261/81): Belgian rule requiring all margarine to be sold in cube-shaped packaging; not an absolute barrier but makes marketing of imports more difficult or costly; MEE.
   - *Mars* (C-470/93): packaging/presentation rule (consumer protection; see justification note).

### 6. The road to Keck
- Traders used *Dassonville* to attack rules unconnected with inter-State trade. AG Tesauro in *Hünermund* (C-292/92) asked whether the aim of Art 34 is to liberalise trade in general or only to regulate trade between Member States.
- **Sunday trading**: *Torfaen BC v B&Q* (145/88). The UK Shops Act 1950 banned most Sunday trading. B&Q argued that the ban reduced its total sales, about 10% of its goods came from other Member States, and imports would fall accordingly. The Court accepted that rules on working, delivery and sale hours reflect "political and economic choices" to arrange hours according to "national or regional socio-cultural characteristics", a matter for the States, and are "not designed to govern the patterns of trade between Member States". It then asked whether their effects exceeded what was necessary, referring to Art 3 of Directive 70/50 (measures whose restrictive effect "exceeds the effects intrinsic to trade rules"), and left this to the national court. The result: such rules were within Art 34 but justifiable, which produced inconsistent national rulings and pressure on the Court.
- By contrast, in *Oebel* (155/80), a ban on night work in bakeries, the Court treated a rule on conditions of sale/production applying equally as outside the scope.
- The pressure from Member States and AGs produced *Keck* (C-267/91 and C-268/91) in 1993. See `keck-selling-arrangements-and-market-access.md`.

## Key authorities
- *Dassonville* (8/74): MEE definition; reasonableness of origin certificate requirements.
- *Cassis de Dijon* (120/78): mutual recognition; mandatory requirements; minimum alcohol rule unjustified, labelling the less restrictive alternative.
- *Buy Irish* (C-249/81); *Irish Souvenirs* (113/80): distinctly applicable MEEs.
- *Rau* (261/81); *Mars* (C-470/93): product requirements as indistinctly applicable MEEs.
- *Torfaen* (145/88): Sunday trading within Art 34 but justifiable on socio-cultural grounds; proportionality for the national court.
- *Oebel* (155/80): bakery night-work ban.
- *Hünermund* (C-292/92), AG Tesauro: purpose of Art 34.
- *Krantz* (C-69/88): too uncertain and indirect.
- *Noria Distribution* (C-672/15): food supplements lawful elsewhere but over French nutrient limits (Weatherill).
- Regulation 764/2008: the mutual recognition procedure (Weatherill).

## Debate and critique
- **Pros of a broad MEE definition**: effective removal of hidden barriers; the burden on States to justify; protects traders and consumer choice; mutual recognition promotes market integration without legislation. **Cons**: erodes regulatory autonomy, catches rules with no protectionist effect, floods courts, makes the Court an arbiter of ordinary social and economic policy (*Torfaen*), and produces uncertainty.
- **Schütze, "'Re-reading' Dassonville" (2018) 24 ELJ 376 (EUI WP version in sources)**: textually the formula suggests a radical "national market" reading, but historically that is implausible: the legal vocabulary of the time did not let the Court think in such terms. Read in context, *Dassonville* was about third-country goods in the customs union and "mixed situations" where private parties used national laws to restrict parallel trade. Its purpose was a doctrinal bridge with competition law (a "pattern of trade" test covering both public laws and private agreements affecting imports), and confirmation of the Commission's GATT-consistent definition. The orthodox "hyper-liberal" reading is, in his words, historical nonsense that later reception gave the formula.
- **Schütze's market models** (see also "Of Types and Tests"): international (non-discrimination), federal (mutual recognition, *Cassis*) and national (any obstacle) models of the internal market; Article 34 law moves between them.
- **Weatherill, "The principle of mutual recognition: it doesn't work because it doesn't exist" (2018) 43 EL Rev 224**: discussing *Noria*, he argues there is no absolute "principle" of mutual recognition; what exists is a conditional, managed mutual recognition because States may always try to justify insisting on local rules. The scope for justification is both a laudable expression of admitted regulatory diversity and a serious obstacle to confident cross-border trade, especially against bureaucratic intransigence. He calls for reform of Regulation 764/2008 (more precise scope, better procedures, more robust challenges, publicity for notifications on the model of the Notification Directive), for the Court to extend the *CIA Security* approach, and for non-litigation tools such as SOLVIT; the internal market "will never be finalised".
- **Horsley, "Unearthing Buried Treasure: Article 34 TFEU and the Exclusionary Rules" (2012) 37 EL Rev 734**: three exclusionary rules, "too uncertain and indirect" (*Krantz*), selling arrangements (*Keck*) and insignificant effects (*Burmanjer*), share a function: keeping "market circumstances rules" (genuinely non-discriminatory rules that simply structure national markets) out of Art 34. He criticises recent case law for again scrutinising such rules, giving the Court power to review non-discriminatory policies without proof of a specific effect on trade, in tension with subsidiarity. Litigants should have to adduce credible evidence that a measure blocks imports or favours the domestic market.
- **Gormley, "Inconsistencies and misconceptions in the free movement of goods" (2015) 40 EL Rev 925**: asks whether *Dassonville* remains good law (it does) and whether *Keck* survives; discusses whether there is a de minimis threshold under Arts 34-35 and whether case-law justifications should be assimilated to Art 36.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Fiscal or non-fiscal?** If a charge, use Arts 30/110.
2. **Harmonisation?** If a directive covers the field exhaustively, apply it instead.
3. **Scope and defendant**: cross-border element (potential is enough); State measure or attributable conduct.
4. **Classify the measure**:
   - QR (ban/quota) or distinctly applicable MEE (treats imports differently): restriction; only Art 36 (orthodox view).
   - Product requirement (content, shape, packaging, labelling): MEE under *Cassis*/*Rau*; dual burden.
   - Selling arrangement or use restriction: go to the Keck note.
   - Other: apply *Dassonville* (capable of hindering, directly or indirectly, actually or potentially).
5. **Justify**: Art 36 or mandatory requirements; then proportionality, especially whether labelling or information would be a less restrictive alternative (*Cassis*).
6. **Mutual recognition point**: if the product is lawfully made and sold in another State, stress the presumption of equivalence and the State's burden to show its local rule adds necessary protection.
Traps: requiring proof of actual reduction in imports (potential suffices); forgetting that intent is irrelevant; treating *Torfaen* as still good law on Sunday trading (after *Keck* such rules are selling arrangements).
