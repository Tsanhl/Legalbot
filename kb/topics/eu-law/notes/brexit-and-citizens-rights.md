# Brexit and citizens' rights - Part Two of the Withdrawal Agreement and "former EU citizens"

## Overview
The discussion schedule lists the discussion as "EU citizenship and Brexit", and the rights of EU citizens in the UK and UK citizens in the EU were one of the two main free movement issues in the Article 50 TEU negotiations. The Withdrawal Agreement (WA) protects those who had moved before the end of the transition period (31 December 2020), largely by importing the conditions of Directive 2004/38. Free movement for new movers ended. The debate is whether UK nationals lost everything with Union citizenship or retain a protected status as "former EU citizens". The sources on this topic are thinner than on others; this note sets out what they say. Jurisdiction: EU law and the WA (an international agreement with EU-law-like effects in the UK).

## The law

### 1. The Withdrawal Agreement framework
1. UK membership ended on 31 January 2020; the WA entered into force on 1 February 2020, concluded by the Council with the European Parliament's consent; the UK was excluded from European Council/Council Brexit meetings (Art 50(4) TEU). The first draft (November 2018) was rejected by the UK Parliament; a revised WA (autumn 2019, changing the Northern Ireland Protocol) was implemented by the European Union (Withdrawal Agreement) Act 2020.
2. **Part Two (described as Part II) on citizens' rights** covers EU citizens (and family members) who moved to the UK, and UK citizens (and family members) who moved to an EU State, **before the end of the transition period**; it applies beyond 31 December 2020.
3. Free movement rights for people moving after that date ended; the TCA does not restore them (it covers social security coordination, but not free movement: textbook).

### 2. Legal effects in the UK (Art 4 WA)
- Same legal effects as EU law in Member States: **primacy** over conflicting UK law and **direct effect** where the EU-law conditions are met (Art 4(1)).
- EU law concepts interpreted in conformity with CJEU case law handed down **before** the end of the transition period (Art 4(4)); "due regard" to later case law (Art 4(5)).

### 3. Institutions and enforcement for citizens' rights
- **Preliminary references**: UK courts may refer questions on Part Two to the CJEU for **eight years** after the end of transition, with binding rulings (Art 158 WA). The discussion notes this had not yet been used by UK courts.
- **Independent Monitoring Authority**: receives complaints from private parties and monitors implementation; its powers are limited to citizens' rights (Art 159(1) WA).
- Joint Committee (Art 164) and arbitration with CJEU rulings on EU-law questions (Art 174).

### 4. Substance: Directive 2004/38 conditions carried over
- The WA's residence rights mirror the Directive's conditions (economic activity, self-sufficiency, permanent residence after five years). Spaventa explains that inclusion of the Directive's conditions may be justified by legal and political constraints: it would have been difficult to treat former citizens better than current EU citizens.
- In the UK, protected EU citizens obtained **settled** or **pre-settled status**.
- *CG* (C-709/20): on the interpretation of Part Two of the WA; the Court applied the *Dano*/*Alimanovic* line to EU citizens with pre-settled status in the UK. See `citizenship-equal-treatment-and-social-assistance.md`.
- Before Brexit, applications by EU citizens in the UK to confirm permanent residence rose sharply after the referendum (over 92,000 reported in 2016).

### 5. Political context
- Free movement of persons was a "major political battleground" in the 2016 referendum. The discussion asks whether "British jobs for British workers" is compatible with internal market membership (it is not, under Art 45 TFEU's ban on nationality discrimination), and the discussion notes debates about workers from newer Member States.
- The EU insisted on the indivisibility of the four freedoms, so the UK could not keep the goods market while ending free movement of persons (Political Declaration).

## Key authorities
- Art 50 TEU; WA Arts 4, 158, 159, 164, 174 and Part Two.
- European Union (Withdrawal Agreement) Act 2020.
- Directive 2004/38 (conditions imported into the WA).
- *CG* (C-709/20): pre-settled status and *Dano*/*Alimanovic*.
- *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09): the "fundamental status" narrative Spaventa relies on.

## Debate and critique
- **Spaventa, "Mice or horses? British citizens in the EU 27 after Brexit as 'former EU citizens'" (2019) 44 EL Rev 589** (written before the WA was ratified, against the risk of no deal):
  - UK and EU citizens were treated badly: all sides promised rights would be preserved, but they were not. The EU's refusal to give free movement rights to British citizens can only be explained by using citizens as **bargaining chips** for the trade deal.
  - Citizens asked for the citizens' rights part to be ring-fenced from the rest of the WA; a "lack of imagination" left no safety net for a no-deal exit.
  - Treating former EU citizens as ordinary third-country nationals would "hammer a big nail in the coffin" of EU citizenship: it would show it is not a fundamental status and undermine cases like *Ruiz Zambrano* that rely on that narrative.
  - She proposes a constitutional category of **"former EU citizens"**: British citizens who moved were exercising Treaty rights and have a legitimate expectation (*Altun*) that residence remains protected, at least equivalently to former family members of citizens (applying Directive 2004/38 by analogy). Even if the WA applies (which protects them better), recognising this category ensures that denial of residence must be shown to be **proportionate**, with the burden on national authorities, since the status cannot be regained once lost.
- **Positivist vs constitutional view of citizenship** (Spaventa's structure): if citizenship is merely a derivative of nationality (Art 20: "additional to"), it disappears when a State leaves; if it is a constitutional status, residual protection is owed. Connect to Garner's argument for an autonomous EU citizenship (see `union-citizenship-articles-20-21.md`).
- **Equal treatment after Brexit**: *CG* shows that the restrictive *Dano* approach to social assistance carries over to WA beneficiaries with pre-settled status, which raises the same critiques as in the domestic case law (Nic Shuibhne, O'Brien).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. When did the person move? Before the end of transition (31 December 2020): WA Part Two may apply. After: no free movement; national immigration law (UK) or EU third-country rules.
2. Which status? Pre-settled (under five years) or settled (five years); map onto Directive 2004/38 conditions (Art 7, Art 16).
3. Benefits claims: apply *Dano*/*Alimanovic* as extended by *CG*.
4. Expulsion: WA imports Directive-style public policy protections; reason by analogy with Arts 27-33 (see `citizenship-derogations-and-abuse.md`).
5. Remedies: direct effect and primacy in UK courts (Art 4 WA); preliminary references within eight years (Art 158); Independent Monitoring Authority complaints (Art 159).
6. Essay: is EU citizenship a fundamental status if it can be lost by a State's withdrawal (Spaventa)?
Traps: assuming the TCA gives free movement; assuming the WA protects people who moved after 2020.
