# Free movement of services - Articles 56-57 TFEU

## Overview
Art 56 TFEU prohibits restrictions on the freedom to provide services within the Union in respect of nationals of Member States established in a State other than that of the person for whom the services are intended. It completes the picture alongside workers (Art 45) and establishment (Art 49): services covers a provider who is temporarily active in another State, or a recipient who travels, or a service that crosses a border alone. The Court has interpreted it extremely broadly, moving from a discrimination ban to a market access approach. Jurisdiction: EU law (the handout notes that Services Directive 2006/123 is to be treated separately).

## The law
### 1. Treaty text
- **Art 56**: restrictions on freedom to provide services within the Union are prohibited for nationals of Member States established in a State other than that of the person for whom the services are intended.
- **Art 57**: services are "services" under the Treaties where normally provided for remuneration, so far as not governed by the provisions on goods, capital and persons. They include activities of an industrial or commercial character, craftsmen and the professions. Without prejudice to the establishment chapter, the provider may temporarily pursue the activity in the host State on the same conditions as that State imposes on its own nationals.
- **Art 62**: Arts 51 (official authority) and 52 (public policy, security, health) apply to services.

### 2. Scope
- **Remuneration**: "consideration for the service provided". State-funded education within the national system lacks the characteristic (*Humbel*), but private education is within scope (*Schwarz*). The recipient need not pay (*Bond van Adverteerders*). Where there is no economic element, Art 56 does not apply (*Grogan*: information on an economic activity not distributed on behalf of an economic operator is a manifestation of freedom of expression).
- **Cross-border element** (three situations): the provider moves (even if the recipient is from the same State: *Commission v France* tourist guide); the recipient moves (for example medical treatment abroad); neither moves but the service does (a lawyer advising by phone, fax or internet: *Alpine*). A potential recipient or provider in another State suffices (*Gourmet*, *Freskot*). The line between establishment and services (stable and continuous versus temporary) must be remembered (see freedom-of-establishment.md).
- **Immoral or illegal services** fall within Art 56 even if illegal in some States (*Grogan* on abortion information; *Schindler* on gambling), but see *Josemans* (Dutch "coffee shops") for a limit.
- Purely internal situations are excluded, though the scope is so broad that it is increasingly easy to find a cross-border link.

### 3. Rights of provider and recipient
- Rights to leave, enter and move; the right to stay is by need: Directive 2004/38 mentions only the self-employed, and there is no retention of status since the provider is only temporarily present. Recipients are mostly "temporary residents" under Art 6 of Directive 2004/38 (three months), with equal treatment except social assistance (Art 24); beyond three months the case law applies.
- **Direct discrimination**: only Art 51/52 grounds (official authority, public policy, security, health), narrowly construed.
- **Indirect discrimination**: broadly construed. The host State can impose less than in workers/establishment because the provider already complies with the State of establishment's rules (double regulatory burden: *van Wesemael*). Even professional requirements with a legitimate aim cannot be imposed if similar requirements are already met in the home State. **Residence requirements** are "the very negation" of the freedom (*van Binsbergen*; *Commission v Germany* [1986]; for recipients *Ciola*).
- **Recipients**: may challenge discriminatory rules where the service is received if they affect receipt, even indirectly (museum admission conditions: *Commission v Spain*; compensation for victims of crime: *Cowan*; right to be tried in another language: *Bickel and Franz*). They may challenge origin-State barriers to receiving services abroad (*Luisi and Carbone*; *Van Schaik*: territorial rule requiring a road-worthiness test in the home State). Social and tax advantages linked to the service are covered; now Art 24 of Directive 2004/38 (subject to Art 24(2)).

### 4. Non-discriminatory barriers
- *Sager* (C-76/90): Art 56 requires abolition of any restriction, even one applying without distinction, "liable to prohibit or otherwise impede" the activities of a provider established in another State where it lawfully provides similar services.
- Applies both to the destination State's rules and to the establishment State's rules that affect access to a foreign market (*Alpine*), and to a provider challenging origin-State rules even where the service is for foreign recipients (*Gourmet*, advertising restriction) or discouraging moving (*Carpenter*, link between rule and obstacle can be weak).
- Rules that **prohibit** a service must be justified (imperative requirements) although non-discriminatory (*Schindler*): a lawful provider elsewhere should in principle have access to any market.
- Advertising is always a service. The handout asks readers to compare the scope of Art 34 (goods) and the persons provisions, especially *Alpine*.

### 5. Summary of who can invoke Art 56 against which rules
- Provider against destination State: direct or indirect discrimination; prohibitions (*Schindler*); access restrictions (*Sager*).
- Provider against State of establishment: access to foreign market (*Alpine*); services to foreign recipients (*Gourmet*); discouraging moves (*Carpenter*).
- Recipient against State of origin: restrictions on receiving services abroad; less favourable treatment for services abroad. Against State of reception: discrimination affecting receipt, duties owed to those present (*Cowan*), or unjustified differences (*Bickel and Franz*).

## Key authorities
- *Sager* (C-76/90); *Schindler* (C-275/92); *Alpine Investments* (C-384/93); *Gourmet International* (C-405/98); *Carpenter* (as cited in the handout).
- *van Binsbergen* (33/74); *van Wesemael* (110 and 111/78); *Luisi and Carbone* (286/82 and 26/83); *Cowan* (186/87); *Humbel* (263/86); *Schwarz* (C-76/05); *Bond van Adverteerders* (352/85); *Grogan* (C-159/90); *Josemans* (C-137/09).

## Debate and critique
- Whether the Court was right to "step beyond discrimination" in establishment and services (the materials theme; Spaventa, "From Gebhard to Carpenter", discussed in freedom-of-establishment.md): the broad market access test catches almost any regulation and raises legitimacy questions.
- The handout stresses the breadth of Art 56 and how a weak link to cross-border activity can suffice (*Carpenter*).
- Social dumping: the materials introduces posting of workers and collective action (*Viking*, *Laval*) at the establishment/services boundary.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the activity an economic service for remuneration (not a state-funded public service; consider *Grogan* for non-economic information)?
2. Identify the cross-border element: provider moves, recipient moves, or service moves; separate from establishment (duration, regularity, continuity).
3. Who is relying on Art 56 and against which State's rule (destination, establishment, origin)?
4. Classify the rule: directly discriminatory (Arts 51/52 only), indirectly discriminatory, residence requirement, non-discriminatory access barrier (*Sager*), outright prohibition (*Schindler*).
5. Justification: imperative requirements, suitability and proportionality; consider home-State compliance and the double burden (*van Wesemael*).
6. Check the Services Directive and Directive 2004/38 for recipients.
