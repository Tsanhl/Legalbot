# Scope of the freedoms - the cross-border element and purely internal situations

## Overview
The free movement provisions protect movement **between** Member States. A situation whose elements are all confined to one Member State is "purely" or "wholly" internal and falls outside the freedoms (the "purely internal rule", introduced in 1979). The rule protects national regulatory autonomy, but it produces **reverse discrimination** (a State's own static nationals can be treated worse than migrants) and the Court has carved out many exceptions. This is step 1 of the free movement structure, and problem questions often plant a scope issue. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. The basic rule
1. The freedoms apply only where there is a cross-border element; wholly internal situations are outside them: *Saunders* (C-175/78) (a case involving England and Northern Ireland, both within the UK) and *Walloon Government* (C-212/06) (Flanders and Wallonia).
2. Article 34 TFEU says "between Member States", so a cross-border element is needed.
3. Workers: Art 45 does not apply to purely internal situations, i.e. where the national has not exercised free movement: *Morson and Jhanjan* (35 and 36/82).
4. Establishment: Art 49 needs an intra-Union element.
5. Citizenship: an intra-Union link (movement or intention to move) is generally needed to trigger Arts 20-21 TFEU, since Union citizenship does not replace national citizenship and static citizens are protected by their own State. Directive 2004/38 by Art 3 applies only to citizens who move to or reside in a State other than their own.

### 2. "Too uncertain and indirect"
- A national rule whose effect on inter-State movement is too uncertain and indirect falls outside the freedoms. Used for all freedoms: *Krantz* (C-69/88) (goods), *Kremzow* (C-299/95) (workers), *Commission v Spain* (C-211/08) (services).

### 3. Flexibility: finding a cross-border element
- **Goods**: a potential impact on cross-border trade is enough: *Pistre* (cited as C-321/94 and as C-159/91); see also *Mickelsson and Roos* (C-142/05).
- **Returning nationals**: a national who returns to her own State after exercising free movement rights is treated like a migrant: *Singh* (C-370/90) (returning worker can be accompanied by family members as a migrant would); *Kraus* (C-19/92) (German national with a UK LLM; German rules requiring authorisation to use the title fell within Art 49). Directive 2004/38 applies by analogy to such "circular" situations: *O and B* (C-456/12) (the discussion asks why the Court relied on Art 21 TFEU).
- **Dual nationals**: a citizen who moves and later naturalises in the host State: *Lounes* (C-165/16) (see `citizens-rights-directive-residence-and-family.md`).
- **Citizenship and family life**: *Carpenter* (C-60/00), *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09). In *Rottmann* (C-135/08) and *Ruiz Zambrano*, citizens in what would otherwise be a purely internal situation fell within the Treaty where denying protection would affect the very essence of their citizenship rights. The discussion stresses that *Ruiz Zambrano* is exceptional and that the Court has not applied it again in the same way (*Dereci* C-256/11). See `union-citizenship-articles-20-21.md`.

### 4. Arena's taxonomy of the case law
Arena identifies three approaches that developed in parallel:
1. **Traditional** (from 1979): absolute rule. No inter-State element means outside the freedoms, and preliminary questions on the freedoms are inadmissible.
2. **Expansive** (from 1988): six exceptions extending the freedoms or the Court's jurisdiction to situations with tenuous or potential cross-border elements:
   - "potential cross-borderness" (*Jersey Produce*);
   - "genuine enjoyment of Union citizenship" (*Zambrano*);
   - "certain cross-border interest" (*Coname*);
   - "renvoi to EU law" (*Dzodzi*), where national law refers to EU law;
   - "corresponding cross-border situation" (*Guimont*);
   - "annulment of indistinctly applicable measures" (*Libert*).
3. **Reflective** (from 1995): correctives to five of the six exceptions requiring a concrete evaluation of whether the ruling could be useful to the referring court.
4. **Codification**: *Ullens de Schooten* (November 2016) restated the rule, referred to four exceptions and required referring courts to supply concrete factors; *Fremoluc* continued the process for the "annulment" and "certain cross-border interest" exceptions.

## Key authorities
- *Saunders* (C-175/78): origin of the purely internal rule; internal UK movement outside the freedoms.
- *Walloon Government* (C-212/06): regional rules within Belgium; wholly internal situations outside the freedoms.
- *Krantz* (C-69/88); *Kremzow* (C-299/95); *Commission v Spain* (C-211/08): too uncertain and indirect.
- *Pistre*; *Mickelsson and Roos* (C-142/05): potential cross-border impact suffices for goods.
- *Morson and Jhanjan* (35 and 36/82): no Art 45 rights for nationals who have not moved.
- *Singh* (C-370/90): returning workers and family.
- *Kraus* (C-19/92): returning national, foreign qualification, Art 49.
- *O and B* (C-456/12): Directive applied by analogy to returning citizens.
- *Rottmann* (C-135/08); *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09); *Dereci* (C-256/11): essence-of-rights exception and its narrowness.
- *Ullens de Schooten* (2016); *Fremoluc*: codification (per Arena).

## Debate and critique
- **Rationale (Arena, "The Wall Around EU Fundamental Freedoms" (2019) 38 YEL 153)**: when the Court moved from a discrimination-based model (international market) to an "obstacles to trade" model in the 1970s, it needed a "wall" to stop the freedoms reaching into every area of national competence; the purely internal rule limited the freedoms to measures liable to affect inter-State trade (federal market model).
- **False negatives and false positives**: the traditional approach risks excluding domestic measures with appreciable cross-border effects and is the root cause of reverse discrimination. The expansive exceptions produce false positives (bringing in measures with negligible inter-State impact) and preliminary rulings on fictitious cross-border situations of little use to referring courts, and more references.
- **Calls for abolition**: critics say the rule clashes with an area "without internal frontiers", with the ethos of citizenship (citizens should enjoy rights wherever they are), and is an unreliable guide to scope. Arena warns that abolition would make the freedoms apply everywhere: because they catch any rule that hinders or makes less attractive free movement (*Säger*; *Gebhard*), they would turn the EU from a federal market into an EU-wide national market, resembling the discredited US doctrine of "economic due process" (*Lochner*). His examples: challenging domestic railway rules, pharmacy closing times or municipal taxi licences.
- **Spaventa, "Seeing the Wood Despite the Trees?" (2008) CML Rev****: on the reverse discrimination problem in citizenship; the case law is varied and its reasoning often unclear, leaving a picture like "an impressionist painting". (See `union-citizenship-articles-20-21.md`.)
- **Essay angles**: Is reverse discrimination a matter for national (constitutional) law rather than EU law? Is the Court's flexibility principled (family life, essence of citizenship) or result-driven? Does the rule fit an internal market ideal?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. List every connecting factor: nationality of the claimant, where goods come from or go to, where the service provider and recipient are, whether anyone has moved, foreign qualifications, family members' nationality.
2. If all factors are in one State, say the situation is prima facie purely internal (*Saunders*; *Morson*).
3. Then test the exceptions:
   - Goods: could the rule potentially affect imports (*Pistre*)? Even if the claimant is domestic, imported products may be affected.
   - Persons: has the claimant previously exercised free movement and returned (*Singh*, *Kraus*, *O and B*)? Is a foreign qualification involved?
   - Citizenship: would refusal deprive a citizen of the genuine enjoyment of the substance of citizenship rights, e.g. forcing a minor citizen child to leave the EU (*Ruiz Zambrano*)? Remember it is narrow (*Dereci*).
   - Dual nationality and naturalisation (*Lounes*).
4. Consider whether the effect is "too uncertain and indirect" (*Krantz*).
5. If truly internal, note the outcome (no EU law protection) and that reverse discrimination is a matter for national law.
Trap: assuming a national suing her own State is always internal; check her history of movement.
