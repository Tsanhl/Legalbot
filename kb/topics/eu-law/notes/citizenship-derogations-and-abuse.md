# Restricting free movement of persons - public policy, security and health (Directive 2004/38 Arts 27-33) and abuse of rights (Art 35)

## Overview
Member States may restrict the entry and residence of Union citizens and their family members on grounds of **public policy, public security and public health**. The Treaty bases are Art 45(3) TFEU (workers), Art 52 TFEU (establishment, applied to services by Art 62) and the "limitations and conditions" in Art 21 TFEU; Chapter VI of Directive 2004/38 (Arts 27-33) now governs the detail for all categories and codifies earlier case law. It builds a **graduated system of protection against expulsion** tied to length of residence. Separately, Art 35 allows States to act against **fraud and abuse**, such as marriages of convenience. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. General principle: Article 27
1. Restrictions on free movement and residence of citizens and family members, whatever their nationality, are permitted on grounds of public policy, public security or public health (Art 27(1)).
2. These grounds **cannot be invoked to serve economic ends** (e.g. high unemployment) (Art 27(1)). Note that the Directive's separate conditions on residence (Art 7) and the "unreasonable burden" rule (Art 14) are different tools (see the social assistance note).
3. Derogations are exceptions to a fundamental freedom and are **strictly construed** (*Bonsignore*, textbook); their scope cannot be determined unilaterally by each State without EU control (*Coman* para 44).

### 2. Public policy and public security: personal conduct (Art 27(2))
1. Measures must comply with **proportionality** and be based **exclusively on the personal conduct** of the individual.
2. **General prevention is excluded**: *Bonsignore* (67/74): an Italian worker in Germany was fined for unlawful possession of a firearm, which had accidentally killed his brother; the deportation could be justified only by general deterrence of immigrant circles; this was impermissible because deportation must be based on personal conduct.
3. The conduct must represent a **"genuine, present and sufficiently serious threat affecting one of the fundamental interests of society"** (Art 27(2)):
   - **Sufficiently serious**: *Adoui and Cornuaille* (115 & 116/81): French women working in a Belgian bar "suspect from the point of view of morals" (prostitution) were refused residence. Belgium did not prohibit prostitution as such (only exploitation and incitement), so the conduct was not sufficiently serious: a State cannot rely on conduct against migrants that it does not repress (through genuine and effective measures) when committed by its own nationals.
   - **Genuine**: not a presumed threat.
   - **Present**: not merely a past threat.
4. **Previous criminal convictions** are not in themselves enough; they count only insofar as the circumstances are evidence of personal conduct constituting a present threat (Art 27(2); *Bouchereau* 30/77, where a French worker in the UK had a drugs conviction; the textbook explains that a present threat generally implies a propensity to act the same way in future). (The textbook gives the case number as 34/77; the discussion as 30/77.)
5. **Present association with an organisation** can be personal conduct: *Van Duyn* (41/74): a Dutch national refused entry to the UK to work for the Church of Scientology; conduct need not be unlawful for public policy to apply, and present association reflects participation in and identification with the organisation; past association does not suffice.
6. **No automatic expulsion**: deportation can never be the automatic consequence of a custodial sentence (Art 33). *Calfa* (C-348/96): an Italian on holiday in Greece convicted of drug offences was automatically expelled for life under Greek law; not permitted, because personal conduct and the threat it poses must be assessed.
7. **No permanent bans**: exclusion orders cannot be permanent; the person may apply for review after a reasonable period and in any event after three years (Art 32).

The illustration: two sisters serve two-year robbery sentences. Sister A (first conviction, good behaviour, now employed) is unlikely to be a present threat; Sister B (fourth, increasingly serious conviction, poor behaviour, caught planning another robbery two weeks after release) may be.

### 3. Graduated protection against expulsion (Art 28)
1. **All cases (Art 28(1))**: before expelling on public policy or security grounds, the host State must consider length of residence, age, state of health, family and economic situation, social and cultural integration, and links with the country of origin.
2. **Permanent residents** (five years: Art 16): only on **serious grounds** of public policy or public security (Art 28(2)).
3. **Residents for the previous ten years, and minors**: only on **imperative grounds of public security** (Art 28(3)).
   - *Tsakouridis* (C-145/09): organised drug dealing can fall within imperative grounds of public security.
   - *PI* (C-348/09): the referring court asked whether "imperative grounds of public security" covers only threats to the State's existence, institutions, survival of the population, foreign relations and peaceful coexistence; the Court accepted that sexual exploitation of a child can be covered. The discussion callss this "surprisingly broad".

### 4. Public health (Art 29)
1. Only diseases with **epidemic potential** as defined by the WHO, and other infectious or contagious parasitic diseases that are also the subject of protective provisions for the host State's own nationals.
2. Diseases occurring **more than three months after arrival** do not justify expulsion.
3. Where there are serious indications it is necessary, a host State may require a medical examination within three months of arrival, but **not as a matter of routine** (Art 29(3)).
4. *Nordic Info* (C-128/22): Arts 27 and 29, read with Arts 4-5, do not preclude general legislation on COVID-19 public health grounds that (i) bans non-essential travel to Member States classified as high-risk and (ii) requires non-nationals entering from them to undergo testing and quarantine, provided the safeguards in Arts 30-32, the Charter (especially non-discrimination) and proportionality are respected.

### 5. Procedural safeguards (Arts 30-31)
- Notification of decisions, and a right of appeal or review (Arts 30 and 31). These safeguards also condition measures under Art 35 and public health measures (*Nordic Info*).

### 6. Abuse of rights and fraud (Art 35)
- "Member States may adopt the necessary measures to refuse, terminate or withdraw any right conferred by this Directive in the case of abuse of rights or fraud, such as marriages of convenience. Any such measure shall be proportionate and subject to the procedural safeguards..." (Art 35).
- **Fraud** (Commission guidance): deliberate deception or contrivance to obtain free movement and residence rights, e.g. forged documents or false representation of a material fact.
- **Abuse**: artificial conduct entered into solely to obtain free movement and residence rights which, although formally meeting the conditions, does not comply with the purpose of the rules; e.g. **marriages of convenience**, defined in Recital 28 as marriages contracted "for the sole purpose of enjoying the right of free movement and residence".
- Consequences: rights refused, terminated or withdrawn, subject to proportionality and Arts 30-31.
- Compare *Rottmann* (withdrawal of nationality acquired by deception, subject to proportionality) and the corporate abuse case law in `company-mobility.md`.

### 7. Same grounds for economic actors
- Workers: Art 45(3) TFEU; establishment: Art 52 TFEU; services: Art 62 TFEU applying Art 52. The Directive's Chapter VI applies to all Union citizens, economically active or not.
- Public policy as an EU concept: in *Coman* the Court required a genuine and sufficiently serious threat to a fundamental interest of society even where national identity (Art 4(2) TEU) was invoked, and required compliance with the Charter (paras 43-47).

## Key authorities
- Directive 2004/38 Arts 27-33, 35; Recital 28.
- *Van Duyn* (41/74): present association as personal conduct.
- *Bonsignore* (67/74): no general prevention.
- *Bouchereau* (30/77): previous convictions; present threat.
- *Adoui and Cornuaille* (115 & 116/81): no reliance on conduct tolerated for nationals.
- *Calfa* (C-348/96): no automatic lifelong expulsion.
- *Tsakouridis* (C-145/09); *PI* (C-348/09): imperative grounds of public security.
- *Nordic Info* (C-128/22): COVID-19 restrictions.
- *Coman* (C-673/16): strict construction of public policy; national identity.

## Debate and critique
- **Codification and evolution**: Chapter VI is "based on earlier ECJ case law", but the graduated scheme in Art 28 is a legislative innovation linking protection to integration, consistent with the idea (Kramer, Thym) that rights strengthen the more a person is integrated.
- **Breadth of "imperative grounds"**: *PI* and *Tsakouridis* read "public security" expansively to include serious crime, which arguably dilutes the strongest tier of protection; the discussion flags *PI* as surprisingly broad. Some see a shift from security of the State to protection of society's fundamental values.
- **Consistency principle**: *Adoui* reflects the same logic as *Conegate* in goods: a State cannot treat migrants' conduct as a threat when it tolerates the same conduct by its own nationals.
- **Economic ends**: excluding economic grounds from Art 27 protects citizens from expulsion for being poor, yet the Directive allows loss of residence for those who become an unreasonable burden (Art 14; *Dano*). Students may ask whether this undermines Art 27's protection (Spaventa's work on expulsion: "Once a foreigner, always a foreigner").
- **Abuse**: the "sole purpose" test for marriages of convenience balances immigration control against the presumption of genuine family life; proportionality and procedural safeguards prevent blanket suspicion.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the measure (refusal of entry, expulsion, exclusion order, travel ban, quarantine) and the ground relied on.
2. Reject any **economic** motivation (unemployment, cost) under Art 27(1); if it is really about resources, go to Art 7/Art 14 instead.
3. **Public policy/security**:
   - Is it based on the individual's **personal conduct** (not general deterrence: *Bonsignore*)?
   - Is the threat **genuine, present and sufficiently serious** affecting a fundamental interest of society? Assess past convictions as evidence of propensity (*Bouchereau*); check whether the State tolerates the same conduct by nationals (*Adoui*); present association with organisations (*Van Duyn*).
   - Is the decision **automatic** following conviction (Art 33; *Calfa*)? Is a ban **permanent** (Art 32)?
4. **Level of protection**: count lawful residence. Under five years: Art 28(1) factors. Permanent residence: serious grounds. Ten years or a minor: imperative grounds of public security (*PI*, *Tsakouridis*).
5. **Public health**: listed disease? Arose within three months? Medical examination routine or justified? (Art 29; *Nordic Info*.)
6. **Procedure**: notification and remedies (Arts 30-31).
7. **Proportionality**: weigh Art 28(1) factors, family life (Charter), alternatives.
8. **Abuse/fraud**: is there evidence the marriage was contracted solely to gain residence (Art 35, Recital 28)? Is the response proportionate and procedurally fair?
Traps: treating a criminal conviction as automatically sufficient; forgetting the ten-year tier; using public policy to cover welfare costs.
