# Job-seekers - residence and access to benefits (Antonissen, Collins, Vatsouras, Alimanovic)

## Overview
People who move to look for their first job in another Member State fall within Article 45 TFEU, but have only a "semi-status": a right to stay for a reasonable period while genuinely seeking work, and limited equal treatment. The big question is access to benefits. *Collins* (2004) used citizenship to bring job-seekers' allowances within equal treatment, subject to a "genuine link" with the labour market; Directive 2004/38 then excluded job-seekers from social assistance (Art 24(2)); *Vatsouras* (2009) read that exclusion narrowly; *Alimanovic* (2015) read "social assistance" broadly, leaving very little for job-seekers. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law

### 1. Job-seekers are within Article 45
- Art 45 covers those who move to seek work; otherwise the Treaty rights would be of limited use: *Royer* (48/75). Art 45(3) is therefore a non-exhaustive list.
- They are "first-time job-seekers", distinct from people who retain worker status under Art 7(3) Dir 2004/38.

### 2. Right to stay to look for work
- *Antonissen* (C-292/89): a State must give job-seekers a **reasonable period** to find work. It is not contrary to EU law for national legislation to require a job-seeker to leave (subject to appeal) if he has not found work after **six months**, unless he provides evidence that he is **continuing to seek employment** and has **genuine chances of being engaged**.
- *Commission v Belgium* (C-344/95): even after a reasonable period, job-seekers cannot be required to leave if they prove they are still looking and have genuine chances.
- Codified in **Art 14(4)(b) Dir 2004/38**: citizens who entered to seek work may not be expelled as long as they provide evidence of continuing search and a genuine chance of being engaged.
- If the conditions are not met, the job-seeker may be expelled **without** the State needing to rely on public policy/security.
- Job-seekers do not have a right to reside as such, only a right to "stay" to look for a job.

### 3. Equal treatment before citizenship: Lebon
- *Lebon* (316/85): Art 7(2) Reg 1612/68 (now Reg 492/2011) on social and tax advantages does not apply to nationals who move in search of work; job-seekers could not claim equal treatment in social advantages. See also *Commission v Belgium* (C-278/94), cited in *Collins* para 63 alongside *Lebon* as the earlier restrictive approach (note: the raw file under this name in this collection actually contains *D'Hoop* (C-224/98)).

### 4. Collins: citizenship updates the law
- Facts: Mr Collins, dual Irish/US national, had studied briefly and worked casually in the UK in 1980-81, then lived in the US and Africa; he returned to the UK in May 1998 to seek work and claimed jobseeker's allowance, refused because he was not "habitually resident" (paras 18-19).
- Held: (1) he was not a "worker" for Title II of Reg 1612/68 (no recent work link); (2) he had no right of residence solely under Dir 68/360; (3) but "in view of the establishment of citizenship of the Union ... it is no longer possible to exclude from the scope of Article 48(2) [now Art 45(2) TFEU] ... a benefit of a financial nature intended to facilitate access to employment in the labour market of a Member State" (para 63), updating *Lebon* and *Commission v Belgium*.
- **Indirect discrimination and justification**: the habitual residence requirement is more easily met by nationals, so it disadvantages migrant job-seekers (para 65, citing *O'Flynn*). It can be justified only by objective considerations independent of nationality and proportionate (para 66). It is legitimate to grant such an allowance only once a **genuine link** with the State's employment market is established (para 69; cf *D'Hoop* para 38 on tideover allowances), e.g. by showing that the person has genuinely sought work there for a reasonable period (para 70). A residence requirement is in principle appropriate, but to be proportionate it must rest on clear criteria known in advance, allow judicial redress, and any residence period must not exceed what is necessary to verify a genuine search (para 72).

### 5. Directive 2004/38 Art 24(2)
- Host States need not grant **social assistance** during the first three months or, for job-seekers, for the longer period covered by Art 14(4)(b).
- This seemed to conflict with *Collins*.

### 6. Vatsouras: reconciling Collins and Art 24(2)
- Joined Cases C-22/08 and C-23/08 *Vatsouras and Koupatantze*: Greek nationals in Germany who had worked briefly claimed basic benefits for job-seekers (SGB II).
- Job-seekers enjoy equal treatment under Art 45(2) (para 36); following *Collins*, financial benefits facilitating access to employment cannot be excluded (para 37); States may require a **real link** with the labour market (para 38), e.g. genuine search for a reasonable period (para 39); national authorities assess the link and the benefit's purpose and conditions (paras 40-41), judged by its **results, not its formal structure** (para 42); a condition that the claimant be capable of earning a living could indicate the benefit aims to facilitate access to employment (para 43).
- Art 24(2) must be interpreted in accordance with Art 45(2) (para 44): benefits of a financial nature intended to facilitate access to the labour market **are not "social assistance"** within Art 24(2) (para 45). So nothing affected the validity of Art 24(2) (para 46). (Also: Art 12 EC does not preclude rules excluding EU nationals from social assistance granted to third-country nationals.)
- Also (per *Alimanovic* para 54): those retaining worker status under Art 7(3)(c) are entitled to social assistance for at least six months.

### 7. Alimanovic: social assistance read broadly
- Facts: Ms Alimanovic and her daughter (Swedish nationals) had worked in Germany in short jobs; after their worker status (retained for six months under Art 7(3)(c)) lapsed, they were refused SGB II subsistence benefits.
- **Definition of social assistance** (para 44, citing *Dano* para 63): "all assistance schemes established by the public authorities, whether at national, regional or local level, to which recourse may be had by an individual who does not have resources sufficient to meet his own basic needs and those of his family and who by reason of that fact may, during his period of residence, become a burden on the public finances of the host Member State..."
- The benefits' **predominant function** was to cover minimum subsistence costs for a life in keeping with human dignity, so they were social assistance, not benefits facilitating access to the labour market (paras 45-46).
- Only Art 7(3)(c) and Art 14(4)(b) could give them residence (para 52). Their retained worker status had expired (para 55). Under Art 14(4)(b) they might reside as job-seekers, but the host State may rely on Art 24(2) to refuse **any** social assistance to someone whose residence is based solely on Art 14(4)(b) (paras 57-58).
- **No individual assessment required** (contrast *Brey*): the Directive's gradual system for retaining worker status already takes individual circumstances into account; the six-month criterion gives legal certainty and transparency and complies with proportionality (paras 59-61). While a single claim could hardly be an "unreasonable burden", the accumulation of all claims would be (para 62).
- Held (para 63): Art 24 Dir 2004/38 and Art 4 Reg 883/2004 do not preclude excluding Art 14(4)(b) job-seekers from special non-contributory cash benefits that are also social assistance.
- The Court "de facto narrowed the significance of *Vatsouras* and *Collins* to marginal situations by defining social assistance broadly"; this "could cover most, if not all, cash benefits for workseekers"; after *Lebon* and *Alimanovic* "there are very few benefits that would be available to jobseekers".

## Key authorities
- Art 45 TFEU; Dir 2004/38 Arts 7(3)(c), 14(4)(b), 24(2); Reg 492/2011 Art 7(2); Reg 883/2004 Arts 4, 70.
- *Royer* (48/75): job-seekers within Art 45.
- *Antonissen* (C-292/89): reasonable period; six months acceptable with exception.
- *Commission v Belgium* (C-344/95): no expulsion if genuine chances.
- *Lebon* (316/85): no Art 7(2) for job-seekers.
- *D'Hoop* (C-224/98): tideover allowance; genuine/real link.
- *Collins* (C-138/02): citizenship brings job-seeker allowances within Art 45(2); genuine link; residence test proportionality.
- *Vatsouras* (C-22/08, C-23/08): labour-market access benefits are not social assistance.
- *Alimanovic* (C-67/14): broad definition of social assistance; no individual assessment.
- *Dano* (C-333/13); *Brey* (C-140/12): background on inactive citizens.

## Debate and critique
- **Semi-status**: The conclusion: part-timers have full rights, while job-seekers have a semi-status, and denial of social advantages "might be justified". Is it right that the most vulnerable movers get the least?
- **Citizenship as a bridge and then a wall**: *Collins* used citizenship to expand Art 45 equal treatment; *Alimanovic* uses the Directive to shrink it. Nic Shuibhne (2015) criticises the "hegemonic" supremacy given to secondary law and the abandonment of individual proportionality review without explanation; Davies (cited by her) notes the Treaty says nothing about job-seekers.
- **Legal certainty vs individual justice**: *Alimanovic* replaces *Brey*'s case-by-case assessment with bright-line rules, defended on certainty and transparency (paras 60-61). The "accumulation" reasoning (para 62) is criticised for treating individuals as a class; Kramer sees the *Dano* line as reconstructed "earned citizenship".
- **Genuine/real link**: is it a proportionate integration test, or a disguised residence requirement? *Collins* para 72 requires clear criteria, judicial redress and a period no longer than necessary.
- **Questions**: "Discuss the Court's reasoning in *Collins*, as applied to work seekers."

## Applying it to a problem question
The problem 2 (Germany: six months to find a job or deportation; social assistance only for the economically active and permanent residents) is a model:
1. **Status**: is the person a first-time job-seeker, a former worker retaining status (Art 7(3)), or inactive?
2. **Residence**: a six-month limit is lawful in principle (*Antonissen*), but expulsion is not allowed if the person shows continuing search and genuine chances (Art 14(4)(b); *Commission v Belgium*). Automatic deportation after six months without that exception breaches EU law. A "terrorist threat" cannot be used as a general justification; public policy needs personal conduct (Art 27(2), see `citizenship-derogations-and-abuse.md`).
3. **Benefits**: classify the benefit by its predominant function (*Vatsouras* para 42; *Alimanovic* para 45):
   - facilitates access to the labour market: within Art 45(2) equal treatment; State may require a genuine link (*Collins*, *Vatsouras*), proportionately;
   - covers basic subsistence: social assistance; may be refused to job-seekers residing only under Art 14(4)(b) (*Alimanovic*) and during the first three months (Art 24(2)).
4. **Retained workers**: under Art 7(3)(c) they keep equal treatment for at least six months (*Vatsouras* para 32; *Alimanovic* paras 53-54); over one year's work plus registration keeps status longer (Art 7(3)(b)).
5. **Permanent residents**: full equal treatment (Art 16, Art 24).
Traps: treating all job-seeker benefits as social assistance without analysing function; forgetting Art 14(4)(b)'s evidence-based exception to removal.
