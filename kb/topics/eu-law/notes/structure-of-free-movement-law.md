# The structure of free movement law (the five-step framework)

## Overview
Most cases in this module concern negative integration: a national rule is tested against a Treaty free movement provision. Across goods, workers, establishment, services and citizenship, the Court of Justice reasons through broadly the same sequence of steps. Learning that sequence gives you the template for every problem question, and the way the Court has blurred the steps (market access, horizontal effect, assimilation of justifications) is a major essay theme. Jurisdiction: EU law.

## The law
Before the steps: decide whether the case is governed by the **Treaty** or by **secondary legislation** (harmonisation); see `internal-market-foundations-and-harmonisation.md`. Then the five structural elements (Handout 1; Lectures 1-2):

### Step 1: Scope - is there a cross-border element?
- The free movement provisions apply only where there is movement between Member States. Wholly internal situations are outside them: *Saunders* (C-175/78) (England and Northern Ireland, i.e. within one State) and *Walloon Government* (C-212/06) (Flanders and Wallonia).
- A related formula: rules whose effect on inter-State movement is "too uncertain and indirect" fall outside the freedoms. Used across freedoms: *Krantz* (C-69/88) (goods), *Kremzow* (C-299/95) (workers), *Commission v Spain* (C-211/08) (services).
- The Court is flexible in finding a cross-border element, especially in citizenship cases, often to protect family life: *Carpenter* (C-60/00), *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09). For goods, a potential impact suffices: *Pistre*.
- Detail in `purely-internal-situations.md`.

### Step 2: Direct effect - can the claimant rely on the provision against this defendant?
- All free movement provisions have **vertical** direct effect (against the State).
- Most also have some **horizontal** effect: explicitly for Art 45 in *Angonese* (C-281/98); applied to sporting bodies (*Walrave and Koch* C-36/74, UCI; *Bosman* C-415/93, UEFA) and trade unions (*Viking* C-438/05, *Laval* C-341/05). Some call these "extended vertical direct effect" because the bodies had state-like regulatory power.
- **Article 34 TFEU is the exception**: no horizontal direct effect, but it catches State-controlled bodies (*Buy Irish*), bodies with delegated tasks (*Hennen Olie*) or special powers (*Fra.bo*), and the State must act against private obstacles (*Spanish Strawberries*, *Schmidberger*).
- Detail in `horizontal-direct-effect-of-free-movement.md`.

### Step 3: Restriction - is there a prima facie breach?
Three main methods (used across freedoms; goods differs a little because the Court rarely uses discrimination language there, except in the second *Keck* proviso):
1. **Direct discrimination** (distinctly applicable measures): the rule openly distinguishes by nationality or origin. Examples: nationality requirement, country-of-origin label, health inspection of imports only.
2. **Indirect discrimination** (indistinctly applicable measures): neutral on its face but harder for non-nationals or imports to satisfy. Examples: language or residence requirements, product requirements.
3. **Obstacle / restriction**: genuinely non-discriminatory but still hinders or makes less attractive the exercise of free movement. Examples: exam or licence requirements. The general formula comes from *Gebhard* (C-55/94): national measures "liable to hinder or make less attractive the exercise of fundamental freedoms".
Goods-specific tests (Dassonville, Keck, use restrictions) are in `article-34-dassonville-cassis-mutual-recognition.md` and `keck-selling-arrangements-and-market-access.md`.

### Step 4: Justification
- **Treaty derogations**: each freedom has a list. The core three are public policy, public security and public health (Art 36 TFEU for goods is longer). They can justify any kind of restriction.
- **Mandatory requirements / public interest requirements / objective justifications**: Court-made, open-ended, from *Cassis de Dijon* (120/78). The orthodox rule is that they are available only for indirectly discriminatory or non-discriminatory measures; directly discriminatory measures need a Treaty derogation. The Court has become more flexible, particularly for environmental protection (*PreussenElektra* C-379/98).
- Purely economic aims are not accepted, though the Court is more flexible when an economic aim is tied to a non-economic interest (*Decker* C-120/95).
- Detail in `article-36-justifications-and-proportionality.md`.

### Step 5: Proportionality
1. **Suitability**: is the measure capable of achieving the aim? There must be a causal link; the measure need not be 100% effective, but it must be applied consistently and coherently (*Hartlauer* C-169/07).
2. **Necessity**: could a less restrictive, equally effective measure achieve the aim? Has the State properly considered alternatives (*Cassis de Dijon*)? This test can be intrusive, so the Court often leaves it to the national court, which knows the facts better.
3. **Proportionality stricto sensu** (sometimes): does the burden on those affected outweigh the benefits? *Danish Bottles* (C-302/86). Used less often because it is intrusive.
- The intensity of review varies: more deference in sensitive areas (*Omega* C-36/02), stricter where protectionism is suspected. The Court may decide proportionality itself or "delegate" it to the national court.

### How the Court actually uses the steps
- The Court need not go through every step if a step is not in dispute; most judgments focus on steps 3-5 (Handout 1).
- Steps 1 and 2 are usually uncontested, but problem questions often plant issues there (a purely internal situation; a private defendant under Art 34).

## Key authorities
- *Saunders* (C-175/78); *Walloon Government* (C-212/06): wholly internal situations outside the freedoms.
- *Krantz* (C-69/88); *Kremzow* (C-299/95); *Commission v Spain* (C-211/08): "too uncertain and indirect".
- *Carpenter* (C-60/00); *Ruiz Zambrano* (C-34/09): flexible cross-border link.
- *Angonese* (C-281/98); *Walrave and Koch* (C-36/74); *Bosman* (C-415/93); *Viking* (C-438/05); *Laval* (C-341/05): horizontal or "extended vertical" effect.
- *Buy Irish* (C-249/81); *Fra.bo* (C-171/11); *Schmidberger* (C-112/00); *Commission v France (Spanish Strawberries)* (C-265/95): goods and private parties.
- *Gebhard* (C-55/94): "hinder or make less attractive" restriction formula.
- *Cassis de Dijon* (120/78): mandatory requirements; necessity and alternatives.
- *PreussenElektra* (C-379/98): flexibility on justifying distinctly applicable measures.
- *Decker* (C-120/95): economic aims.
- *Hartlauer* (C-169/07): consistency and coherence.
- *Danish Bottles* (C-302/86): stricto sensu balancing.
- *Omega* (C-36/02): margin of discretion in sensitive areas.

## Debate and critique
- **van Leeuwen, "Rethinking the Structure of Free Movement Law: The Centralisation of Proportionality in the Internal Market" (2017) 10 EJLS 235**: three developments, **market access**, **horizontal direct effect** and **assimilation of justifications**, each come from structural changes. The Court uses "backwards reasoning" (it no longer follows the steps in order) and merges stages that used to be distinct (market access merges scope and restriction; horizontal effect merges direct effect and restriction; assimilation merges restriction and justification). The result is that proportionality has become the main tool for deciding cases. He explains this by the Court's aim of ensuring the *effet utile* of the freedoms, but argues that near-exclusive reliance on proportionality does not improve the internal market, and that the Court should develop the other pillars again.
- **Snell, "The Notion of Market Access: A Concept or a Slogan?" (2010) CML Rev 437**: market access appears in key rulings (*Keck*, *Alpine Investments*, *Bosman*, *Graf*, *Gourmet*, *Commission v Italy (Trailers)*) and Opinions (Jacobs in *Leclerc-Siplec*, Tizzano in *CaixaBank*, Kokott in *Mickelsson and Roos*), but it "obscures rather than illuminates". The real choice is between a discrimination / anti-protectionism model (comparative test of disparate impact) and an economic freedom model (absolute test without comparison). Market access promises a third way, but 15+ years after *Keck* the middle ground has not been located; if none exists, the term should be dropped as an unhelpful slogan.
- **Spaventa, "From Gebhard to Carpenter: Towards a (Non-)Economic European Constitution" (2004) 41 CML Rev 743**: after *Keck* the scope of goods was partly curtailed while the persons provisions were broadened, moving to a non-discrimination-free "obstacle" approach. This raises questions about how far the freedoms reach into national regulatory choices that are not about market integration (see `freedom-of-establishment.md`).
- **van Leeuwen, "Legal Empathy" (2021)**: each pillar of the structure has a role in structuring "comparative dialogues" between Member States' laws.
- **Essay angles**: Is the structure a real constraint or a post-hoc description? Does the shift from discrimination to restriction enlarge judicial power over national policy? Is it coherent that goods (Keck) and persons (Gebhard) use different restriction tests? Who should apply proportionality, the Court or national courts?

## Applying it to a problem question
Use this skeleton for every freedom:
1. **Treaty or secondary law?** Check harmonisation.
2. **Which freedom?** Goods (Art 34/30/110), workers (Art 45), establishment (Art 49), services (Art 56), citizenship (Art 21 and Dir 2004/38). Classification matters (e.g. establishment vs services depends on temporary vs stable presence: *Gebhard*).
3. **Scope**: is there a real cross-border element? If the facts are all in one State, consider purely internal rule and its exceptions.
4. **Direct effect**: who is the defendant? State, emanation of the State, regulatory private body, purely private actor? For goods, private actors are out unless *Buy Irish*/*Fra.bo* applies or you reframe against the State's failure to act.
5. **Restriction**: classify as direct discrimination, indirect discrimination or obstacle (or for goods, product requirement / selling arrangement / use restriction / other).
6. **Justification**: pick Treaty derogations and/or mandatory requirements, checking the discrimination rule; reject purely economic aims.
7. **Proportionality**: suitability (consistency), necessity (name realistic less restrictive alternatives), and if relevant stricto sensu. Say whether the national court would decide.
8. **Remedy / conclusion**: is the rule disapplied, and what should the claimant argue?
Typical traps: skipping scope when facts are internal; applying mandatory requirements to direct discrimination without noting the orthodox rule; treating proportionality as a single step.
