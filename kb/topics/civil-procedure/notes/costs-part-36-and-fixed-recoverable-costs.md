# Costs, Part 36 offers, fixed recoverable costs and QOCS

## Overview
The general rule is that the unsuccessful party pays the successful party's costs, but the court has a wide discretion, and conduct (including the failure to engage with ADR or to follow pre-action steps) can change the outcome. Part 36 offers to settle carry specific, mandatory consequences when they are beaten at trial. Since 1 October 2023 fixed recoverable costs apply to most fast-track and intermediate-track cases, and in personal injury claims qualified one-way costs shifting (QOCS) protects claimants from adverse costs orders except in defined situations such as fundamental dishonesty.

## The law
**Discretion and general rule (CPR 44.2).** Costs are in the discretion of the court (Senior Courts Act 1981 s 51). The general rule is that the unsuccessful party will be ordered to pay the costs of the successful party, but the court may make a different order (r 44.2(2)). In deciding what order to make, it must consider all the circumstances, including the conduct of all the parties, whether a party has succeeded on part of its case even if not wholly successful, and any admissible offer to settle that is not a Part 36 offer (r 44.2(4)). Conduct includes conduct before and during the proceedings, the extent to which a party followed the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct or any relevant protocol, whether it was reasonable to raise, pursue or contest a particular allegation or issue, the manner in which a party pursued or defended the case or a particular allegation or issue, whether a claimant who has succeeded in the claim, in whole or in part, exaggerated its claim, and, since 1 October 2024, whether a party failed to comply with an order for ADR or unreasonably failed to engage in ADR (r 44.2(5)). The court may order a proportion of costs, costs from or until a date, costs relating to a distinct part of the proceedings, or interest on costs.

**Basis of assessment.** Costs are assessed on the standard basis (only costs that are proportionate and reasonably incurred and reasonable in amount; doubts resolved in favour of the paying party) or on the indemnity basis (doubts resolved in favour of the receiving party, and no requirement of proportionality) (r 44.3). Indemnity costs may be ordered where the conduct is out of the norm. Summary assessment is common at the end of hearings of a day or less; detailed assessment is used for larger bills. On the multi-track, costs budgeting (CPR 3.12-3.18; PD 3E) caps recoverable costs for most cases where the claim is below £10 million, and the court will not depart from an approved budget without good reason.

**Part 36 offers.** Part 36 is a self-contained code. An offer must be in writing, state that it is made pursuant to Part 36, specify a period of not less than 21 days within which the defendant (or claimant) will be liable for the claimant's (or defendant's) costs if the offer is accepted (the "relevant period"), state whether it relates to the whole claim or part or an issue, and state whether it takes into account any counterclaim (r 36.5). It can be made at any time, including before proceedings, and it can be withdrawn or changed before it is accepted (r 36.9, 36.10). It is not an admission and is not disclosed to the judge until the case has been decided on liability and quantum (r 36.16).
- *Acceptance*: a Part 36 offer may be accepted at any time unless withdrawn; if accepted within the relevant period, the claimant gets their costs up to the date of acceptance (r 36.13); late acceptance makes costs a matter for agreement or the court (usually the offeree pays the offeror's costs after the relevant period expired) (r 36.13(4)-(5)).
- *Claimant's offers beaten*: where judgment against the defendant is at least as advantageous to the claimant as the claimant's offer, the court must (unless it considers it unjust) order: (a) interest on the whole or part of any sum awarded (other than interest) at up to 10% above base rate for some or all of the period from the end of the relevant period; (b) costs, including recoverable pre-action costs, on the indemnity basis from the end of the relevant period; (c) interest on those costs at up to 10% above base rate; and (d) an additional amount of 10% of the amount awarded up to £500,000, plus 5% of any amount above £500,000, capped at £75,000 in total (r 36.17(4)). Where interest is awarded under another power for the same sum and period, the total rate must not exceed 10% above base rate (r 36.17(6)).
- *Defendant's offers beaten*: where the claimant fails to obtain a judgment more advantageous than the defendant's offer, the court must (unless it considers it unjust) order the defendant to pay costs from the expiry of the relevant period and the claimant to pay the defendant's costs from that date, with interest on those costs (r 36.17(3)). For a money claim, "more advantageous" or "at least as advantageous" is judged in money terms by any amount, however small (r 36.17(2)).
- *"Unjust"*: in deciding whether the consequences would be unjust the court considers all the circumstances, including the terms of the offer, when it was made, the information available to the parties, the conduct of the parties regarding information, and whether the offer was a genuine attempt to settle the proceedings (r 36.17(5)). The burden is on the party who says it would be unjust, and the test is demanding.
- *Fixed costs cases*: r 36.17 is subject to r 36.24 where fixed costs under Part 45 apply. A claimant who beats its own offer then recovers fixed costs plus, instead of indemnity costs, an amount equal to 35% of the difference between the fixed costs for the stage reached when the relevant period expired and the fixed costs for the stage at judgment; where the claimant fails to beat a defendant's offer, the claimant gets fixed costs only up to the stage when the relevant period expired and pays the defendant's fixed costs after that.
- Part 36 offers differ from "Calderbank" offers (without prejudice save as to costs), which can be taken into account under r 44.2(4)(c) but have no automatic consequences.

**Fixed recoverable costs (FRC).** For claims started on or after 1 October 2023 (with transitional provisions for some claim types), Part 45 and the associated practice direction fix the costs recoverable by a successful party in most fast-track cases and all intermediate-track cases (the intermediate track, for claims between £25,000 and £100,000, was introduced at the same time). Fixed costs are tied to complexity bands (four bands in each track) and tables that set the costs for each stage. A party may seek more than fixed costs only in exceptional circumstances or in specified situations, such as a Part 36 consequence or unreasonable conduct. Some categories of case are excluded (for instance, clinical negligence claims, some complex personal injury claims and certain claims against public bodies where allocated to the multi-track); the existing portal and predictable fixed costs for road traffic accident claims continue within the new structure. The tables and exclusions are in Part 45 and PD 45. The scheme reflects Sir Rupert Jackson's 2017 supplemental report on fixed recoverable costs.

**Qualified one-way costs shifting (QOCS) (CPR 44.13-44.17).** In a claim for damages for personal injury (including Fatal Accidents Act 1976 claims and claims for the benefit of a deceased's estate under the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934), costs orders made against the claimant may be enforced without permission only up to the aggregate amount of any orders for, or agreements to pay or settle the claim for, damages, interest and costs in the claimant's favour (r 44.14(1), as substituted from 6 April 2023), unless an exception applies. Since that change a defendant can set its costs off against the claimant's costs and against sums agreed in a settlement (including a Part 36 acceptance). A claimant is not protected where: (a) the claim is struck out on the grounds that it discloses no reasonable grounds or is an abuse of process or likely to obstruct the just disposal (r 44.15), in which case costs may be enforced without permission; or (b) with permission, where the claim is found on the balance of probabilities to be fundamentally dishonest, in which case the costs order may be enforced in full (r 44.16(1)); or where the claim includes an element for someone else's financial benefit (r 44.16(2)). Fundamental dishonesty under s 57 of the Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015 also allows a court to dismiss a personal injury claim altogether where the claimant has been fundamentally dishonest about a primary or related claim, unless the claimant would suffer substantial injustice.

**Small claims.** On the small claims track, costs recovery is limited to fixed amounts (issue fee, fixed costs for the claim form, witness expenses, expert's fees up to a limit, loss of earnings and unreasonable behaviour costs) (r 27.14); Part 36 does not apply to small claims. A litigant in person may recover limited costs (r 46.5).

## Key authorities
- *Re Elgindata Ltd (No 2)* [1992] 1 WLR 1207 (CA): general statement of costs principles, including that costs normally follow the event and that the court should distinguish issues on which a party has failed; the successful party who unreasonably raises issues may be deprived of costs.
- *Excelsior Commercial & Industrial Holdings Ltd v Salisbury Hammer Aspden & Johnson (a firm)* [2002] EWCA Civ 879 (CA, Lord Woolf CJ): indemnity costs require some conduct or circumstance taking the case "out of the norm"; lack of moral probity is not required. Unreasonable conduct must be unreasonable to a high degree, not merely wrong or misguided in hindsight (*Kiam v MGN Ltd (No 2)* [2002] EWCA Civ 66).
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576, [2004] 1 WLR 3002 (CA): an unreasonable refusal to engage in ADR may attract costs sanctions even against a successful party (see the ADR note).
- *Summers v Fairclough Homes Ltd* [2012] UKSC 26, [2012] 1 WLR 2004 (UK Supreme Court): the court has the power to strike out a personal injury claim after trial where the claimant has been fundamentally dishonest, but it will be exercised only in very rare cases where it would be a proportionate response; now overlaid by s 57 of the 2015 Act.
- *Denton v TH White Ltd* [2014] EWCA Civ 906 (CA): costs consequences for unreasonably resisting applications for relief from sanctions.
- *Carver v BAA plc* [2008] EWCA Civ 412 (CA): an earlier decision on when a judgment is "more advantageous" than an offer; the point is now addressed by the express wording of r 36.17(2) (better in money terms by any amount, however small).

## Applying it
1. Who won (identify the successful party on the issues or the claim as a whole) and are there reasons to depart from the general rule (partial success, issue-based approach, conduct, offers)?
2. Part 36: find any offers; check they comply with r 36.5 (form), the relevant period and timing, whether they were withdrawn; compare the judgment with the offer in money terms (including interest to the date of the offer's expiry as relevant); list the r 36.17 consequences and whether it would be unjust.
3. Other offers and conduct: consider Calderbank offers, ADR failures (*Halsey*; r 44.2(5)(e)), non-compliance with protocols, exaggeration; consider an indemnity costs order for exceptionally unreasonable behaviour.
4. Assessment: standard or indemnity basis; summary or detailed; in an FRC case, the applicable band and table, and whether an exception allows more.
5. PI claims: does QOCS apply? If so, is there any exception (strike out, fundamental dishonesty, claim for another's benefit)? Remember QOCS caps enforcement at the damages, interest and costs awarded or agreed in the claimant's favour; it does not protect against the claimant's own solicitor's costs.
6. Small claims and litigants in person: limited recovery.
7. Common traps: applying pre-October-2023 costs rules to new claims; overlooking that Part 36 is subject to r 36.24 in FRC cases; treating a Part 36 offer as automatically giving indemnity costs without checking the "unjust" test; forgetting that a claimant's Part 36 offer must be "at least as advantageous".
8. Everyday question: "If I lose a small claim, will I pay the other side's legal bills?" Answer: not generally; the small claims track limits recoverable costs to small fixed items (and the court fee), unless there has been unreasonable behaviour.

## Debates and reform
- Fixed costs bring predictability and proportionality, but critics fear they deter lawyers from lower-value claims and find the bands and tables complex.
- Part 36 rewards well-pitched offers, but the "additional amount" is criticised as penal.
- Funding: in *R (PACCAR Inc) v Competition Appeal Tribunal* [2023] UKSC 28 the Supreme Court held that litigation funding agreements paying the funder a share of damages are damages-based agreements, unenforceable unless they meet the DBA rules. The Civil Justice Council (2025) recommended reversing this, and the government said in December 2025 that it would legislate, prospectively, when parliamentary time allows; no such legislation had been passed by November 2026.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 6 April 2023: QOCS enforcement cap widened to include costs orders and settlement sums in the claimant's favour (r 44.14(1)).
- 1 October 2023: fixed recoverable costs extended; intermediate track introduced; Parts 45 and 36 amended.
- 1 October 2024: ADR conduct added to r 44.2(5)(e).
- Fixed costs figures and guideline hourly rates are not reproduced here; use the current PD 45 tables and published rates.

## Common errors to avoid

- Don't say small claims costs follow only unreasonable conduct: CPR 27.14(2) still lets the winner recover fixed issue costs, court fees, reasonable travel/subsistence, capped loss of earnings and capped expert fees (limits in PD 27); r 27.14(2)(g) adds costs for unreasonable behaviour.
