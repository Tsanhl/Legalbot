# Starting a claim and service of the claim form (CPR Parts 6 and 7)

## Overview
A claim is started by issuing a claim form (Part 7, or the alternative Part 8 procedure) and must then be served on the defendant in time and in the manner the rules prescribe. The claim form is valid for service for four months after issue (six months if served outside the jurisdiction). Service errors can be fatal, especially when limitation has expired, and the courts have been strict: alternative service under r 6.15 needs a "good reason", and extensions under r 7.6 are tightly controlled.

## The law
**Issue.** A claim is started when the court issues a claim form at the claimant's request (r 7.2); for limitation purposes, however, the claim is "brought" on the date the court office receives the request to issue (PD 7A para 5.1), which may be earlier than the issue date. The claim form (N1) must state the remedy, value (for allocation and fees), and be verified by a statement of truth. Particulars of claim must be contained in or served with the claim form, or served within 14 days after service of the claim form and no later than the latest time for serving it (r 7.4). A Part 7 claim is used where facts are likely to be disputed; a Part 8 claim is used where there is no substantial dispute of fact or where a rule requires it. Court fees are payable on issue (the usual percentage scale up to a cap).

**Service within the jurisdiction (Part 6, Section II).** Methods for the claim form (r 6.3, supplemented by PD 6A): personal service on an individual or company (r 6.5), first class post or another service that provides for delivery on the next business day, leaving it at the specified place, through a document exchange, by fax or other electronic means (subject to prior written indication of willingness and the PD 6A conditions: a party who has not indicated that they accept service by email cannot be served by email), or any other method authorised by the court. Where a defendant has given an address for service, or has a solicitor authorised to accept service and who has notified the claimant, service must follow that arrangement (r 6.7, r 6.23). Contractual service clauses are recognised (r 6.11). Place of service for an individual: usual or last known residence; for a sole trader: place of business or residence; for a company: its principal office or any place of business with a real connection to the claim, or, under the Companies Act 2006 s 1139, its registered office (r 6.9, PD 6A).

**Time (r 7.5).** The claimant must complete the required step before 12 midnight on the calendar day four months after the date of issue (for service within the jurisdiction): for instance, posting the claim form, leaving it at the address, or sending an email. The step must be completed within four months, though the claim form is deemed served (r 6.14) on the second business day after the step is completed. Where the claim form is to be served out of the jurisdiction, the period is six months (r 7.5(2) as quoted in *Abela*).

**Extending time (r 7.6).** The claimant may apply to extend time for compliance with r 7.5. If the application is made within the period or the period in a previous extension order, the general rule is that the court may extend (the court takes into account all the circumstances, including the reasons for delay and whether the claim is time-barred). If the application is made after the period has ended, the court may extend only if (a) it has failed to serve the claim form, or (b) the claimant has taken all reasonable steps to comply with r 7.5 but has been unable to do so, and (c) in either case the claimant has acted promptly in making the application (r 7.6(3)). The application can be made without notice (r 7.6(4)(b)) but must be supported by evidence.

**Alternative methods and validation (r 6.15).** Where it appears to the court that there is a good reason to authorise service by a method or at a place not otherwise permitted by Part 6, it may make an order permitting service by an alternative method or place (r 6.15(1)). On an application the court may order that steps already taken to bring the claim form to the attention of the defendant by an alternative method or at an alternative place is good service (r 6.15(2)). The application may be made without notice and must be supported by evidence (r 6.15(3)); the order must specify the date of deemed service and the time to file an acknowledgement or defence (r 6.15(4)). Rule 6.16 allows the court to dispense with service of the claim form in exceptional circumstances.

**Service out of the jurisdiction.** Covered in the separate jurisdiction note: permission is generally needed unless a rule dispenses with it; for service out the claim form is served under Part 6 Section IV (r 6.40 and 6.41-6.44); the period is six months.

**Consequences of defective service.** If the claim form is not served in time and the court does not extend time or validate alternative service, the claim is liable to be struck out, and if the limitation period has expired the claim is lost. A defendant who wishes to dispute jurisdiction or service must apply under Part 11 within 14 days after filing an acknowledgement of service, otherwise they are treated as accepting the court's jurisdiction (r 11(5)). A defendant who applies for summary judgment or otherwise acts as if the court has jurisdiction may lose the objection.

## Key authorities
- *Barton v Wright Hassall LLP* [2018] UKSC 12 (UK Supreme Court): a litigant in person, with limitation expiring, emailed the claim form to the defendant's solicitors' email address on their letterhead, without the firm indicating willingness to accept email service (as PD 6A para 4.1 requires). The email reached the solicitors on the last day, and the claimant applied later for validation under r 6.15(2). Held (3-2, Lord Sumption for the majority; Lord Briggs and Lady Hale dissenting): validation refused. Receipt of the claim form by the defendant is not enough on its own to constitute a good reason; the question is whether, in all the circumstances, there was a good reason; the court will consider whether the claimant took reasonable steps to serve by a permitted method and the importance of the rules; litigants in person do not get a lower standard; the loss of a limitation defence is a relevant factor (the majority treated it as a factor against the claimant; Lord Briggs disagreed that it should be). Lord Briggs suggested that the Rule Committee should consider the rules on email service.
- *Abela v Baadarani* [2013] UKSC 44, [2013] 1 WLR 2043 (UK Supreme Court): a claim form for a claim concerning a Lebanese defendant had not been served in compliance with the rules (steps taken to serve out of the jurisdiction); the judge declared under r 6.15(2) and r 6.37(5)(b) that the steps taken amounted to good service and the Court of Appeal reversed. Held: the Supreme Court allowed the appeal and restored the declaration. Rule 6.15(2) can be used retrospectively for service out; the fact that the defendant learnt of the contents of the claim form is the critical factor in assessing whether there is a good reason, although it is not necessarily sufficient; the court must consider all circumstances; the court should consider whether the claimant took reasonable steps to serve properly.
- *Hashtroodi v Hancock* [2004] EWCA Civ 652 (Court of Appeal): the claim form was not served in time because of serious errors by the claimant's solicitor, for which no explanation was offered. Held: an extension under r 7.6 was refused; the complete absence of any reason for the failure was decisive on those facts, and granting an extension would mean that the rule in r 7.5 would cease to be the general rule. The decision stresses the importance of the reason for the failure and of observing time limits.
- *Hoddinott v Persimmon Homes (Wessex) Ltd* [2007] EWCA Civ 1203, [2008] 1 WLR 806 (Court of Appeal): a without-notice extension under r 7.6(2) was granted for a claim form issued in May 2006 where the claimants wished first to fully particularise a substantial claim; the defendant later applied to set it aside. Held: even where there is no good reason for failing to serve within four months, the court may exceptionally exercise the discretion; here the unusual facts (the claim was clearly not yet time-barred, a copy of the claim form had been sent to the defendant within the four months, and full particulars were then served) justified an extension. The decision also turned on r 11(5) (the defendant, having not applied under Part 11, was treated as accepting the court's jurisdiction). The decision does not undermine *Hashtroodi*.

## Applying it
1. Identify the issue date and calculate the four-month (or six-month for service out) deadline; check the date and step actually completed (for post: the date posted).
2. Check the method used: is it permitted by r 6.3 and PD 6A? For email: was there prior written indication of willingness to accept it? For solicitors: were they authorised and notified? Which address (r 6.9, r 6.23)?
3. If service was defective or late: assess the position under r 6.15(2) (good reason; any steps to bring the form to the defendant's attention; whether the defendant had knowledge; reasonableness of the claimant's attempts; prejudice; limitation) and r 7.6 (made in time or late; r 7.6(3) conditions). If neither works, the claimant may have to issue a new claim if limitation allows or, if not, pursue the solicitor.
4. For a defendant: if served late or incorrectly, apply under Part 11 within 14 days of acknowledging service, or you risk being treated as accepting jurisdiction; consider striking out; do not delay.
5. Check whether limitation expired in the meantime (see the limitation note) and whether the claimant can start fresh proceedings.
6. Common traps: relying on email service without consent; counting four months from receipt rather than issue; waiting until after expiry to seek an extension; treating the defendant's knowledge as enough; forgetting that deemed service is the second business day after the step.
7. Everyday question: "I issued my small claim but forgot to serve the defendant; is it too late?" Answer: if within the four months, serve it now; if outside, apply promptly to extend time or re-issue if the limitation period allows.

## Debates and reform
- Critics (including Lord Briggs in *Barton*) argue that the formal rules on email service are a trap for unrepresented parties and out of step with modern practice; the Rule Committee has been urged to simplify the rules and clarify the rules on email service.
- The relationship between strict compliance and access to justice, particularly where the defendant has actual notice; the balance struck in *Barton* contrasts with the more flexible approach in *Abela* for service out.
- Digital transformation of court processes (online issue and service through court-managed systems for represented parties) may reduce errors but is not yet universal.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Email service still depends on the recipient (or its solicitor) having indicated in writing that it will accept service by email (PD 6A para 4.1); a solicitor's email address on letterhead is not enough on its own.
- Court fees and the electronic issue systems (CE-File, Money Claim Online and the online civil money claims service) change from time to time and are outside the CPR.
