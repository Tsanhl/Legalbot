# Interim remedies - American Cyanamid, freezing injunctions, search orders and security for costs (CPR 25)

## Overview
Interim remedies preserve the position, assets or evidence before trial. The ordinary interim injunction is governed by *American Cyanamid*: a serious question to be tried, adequacy of damages, and the balance of convenience. A freezing injunction stops a defendant dissipating assets and needs a good arguable case and a real risk of dissipation; a search order protects evidence and is granted only in extreme cases. Security for costs lets a defendant require a claimant to put up money for the defendant's costs where the conditions in CPR 25.27 are met. Part 25 was rewritten and renumbered from 6 April 2025 (SI 2025/106), without intended change of substance, so older cases cite different rule numbers. Without-notice applications carry a duty of full and frank disclosure, and a failure often leads to discharge.

## The law
**The range of remedies (CPR 25.1).** The list in r 25.1(1)(a)-(q) includes interim injunctions and declarations; orders to preserve, inspect or sell relevant property; freezing injunctions and asset-information orders; search orders (Civil Procedure Act 1997 s 7); imaging orders; pre-action and non-party disclosure (Senior Courts Act 1981 ss 33-34; County Courts Act 1984 ss 52-53); interim payments; payment of a disputed fund into court; and security for costs. The court may grant an interim remedy before a claim is started if the matter is urgent or it is otherwise desirable in the interests of justice (r 25.2). An order may be made without notice only if there are good reasons for not giving notice, which the evidence must state (r 25.3(2)-(3)). The High Court's power to grant interim injunctions is in the Senior Courts Act 1981 s 37 ("just and convenient"). Section 25 of the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 allows interim relief in support of proceedings abroad.

**Interim (prohibitory) injunctions: the American Cyanamid guidelines.** The claimant must show: (1) a serious question to be tried (not frivolous or vexatious; no need for a prima facie case); (2) whether damages would adequately compensate the claimant if the injunction is refused, or the defendant (under the claimant's cross-undertaking, if the claimant can pay) if it is granted; (3) the balance of convenience, preserving the status quo where factors are even, with the relative strength of the cases a last resort where the evidence on the merits is clear. The claimant normally must give a cross-undertaking in damages (exceptions exist, for example in some public law cases). Where the remedy sought would in effect decide the case, or is mandatory, the court is generally more reluctant and may require a high degree of assurance that the claimant would succeed at trial; but the underlying question is which course carries the lower risk of injustice if it turns out to be wrong, and the prohibitory/mandatory label is not decisive (*National Commercial Bank Jamaica Ltd v Olint Corp Ltd* [2009] UKPC 16). Where the claim concerns freedom of expression, the Human Rights Act 1998 s 12(3) requires that the applicant is "likely" to establish at trial that publication should not be allowed, which means more than a real prospect, in general more than 50% (*Cream Holdings Ltd v Banerjee* [2004] UKHL 44).

**Freezing injunctions.** A freezing injunction (originally known as a Mareva injunction) restrains a defendant from disposing of or dealing with assets, so that a future judgment is not frustrated. Requirements:
1. A cause of action justiciable in England or in foreign proceedings supported under s 25 of the 1982 Act.
2. A good arguable case on the merits (a case that is more than barely capable of serious argument, and not necessarily one with more than a 50% chance).
3. The defendant has assets (within or outside the jurisdiction; the claimant must have grounds for believing so).
4. A real risk that the defendant will dissipate assets to frustrate enforcement; evidence of dishonesty may support the risk but is not necessary, and the court looks at all circumstances (the "real risk" test rather than a general fear).
5. It is just and convenient to grant the order (the injunction must be proportionate, and the court considers the effect on the defendant and third parties, limits the order to the amount of the claim plus costs and allows normal living expenses, legal fees and ordinary business dealings).
6. The claimant gives a cross-undertaking in damages and may be required to give security; the application is usually made without notice (CPR 25.3, 25.8; freezing injunctions rr 25.12-25.14) and the court expects full and frank disclosure of all material facts, including facts against the application; failure can be a ground for discharge even if the claimant had good grounds for the order. Since 6 April 2025 the former standard form in PD 25A has been replaced by a published model freezing order.
Worldwide freezing orders include safeguards (the "Babanaft proviso" protects third parties abroad and the claimant undertakes not to use the order abroad without the court's permission). The order may be accompanied by an order for asset disclosure (r 25.1(1)(g)); a non-party bank is bound once it has notice.

**Search orders and imaging orders.** A search order (CPA 1997 s 7; CPR 25.1(1)(h); rr 25.15-25.19) requires the defendant to admit the claimant's representatives to premises to search for, copy and remove documents or items. The classic conditions (from *Anton Piller KG v Manufacturing Processes Ltd* [1976] Ch 55) are an extremely strong prima facie case, very serious actual or potential damage to the applicant, clear evidence that the defendant has incriminating documents or items, and a real possibility that the defendant would destroy them if warned. The order is executed under the supervision of an independent supervising solicitor, with a safeguard for privilege and for the privilege against self-incrimination in certain circumstances. Imaging orders (CPR 25 Section IV, introduced in the 2025 rewrite to deal with electronic data) allow an independent IT expert to copy data on devices or accounts; they are less intrusive and may be used instead of a search order.

**Security for costs (CPR Part 25 Section VI, rr 25.26-25.29).** On the defendant's application the court may order a claimant to give security for the defendant's costs if (a) it is satisfied, having regard to all the circumstances, that it is just to make the order, and (b) an enactment permits it or one of the r 25.27(b) conditions applies: (i) the claimant is resident out of the jurisdiction (the current rule contains no Hague Convention or other carve-out); (ii) the claimant is a company or other body and there is reason to believe it will be unable to pay the defendant's costs if ordered to do so; (iii) the claimant has changed address since the claim was started with a view to evading the consequences of the litigation; (iv) the claimant failed to give an address in the claim form or gave an incorrect address; (v) the claimant is a nominal claimant (other than a Part 19 representative claimant) and there is reason to believe it will be unable to pay costs; or (vi) the claimant has taken steps in relation to its assets that would make it difficult to enforce an order for costs. Security may also be sought from someone other than the claimant, such as a funder (r 25.28), and for the costs of an appeal (r 25.29). Against a foreign-resident claimant, security must be justified by real obstacles to or extra burden of enforcement, not residence as such (*Nasser*). Security is not ordered if it would stifle a genuine claim, but the claimant must show on the balance of probabilities that it cannot raise the money, including from those who would be expected to support it (*Goldtrail Travel Ltd v Aydin* [2017] UKSC 57); the court also asks whether the claimant's lack of means was caused by the defendant's conduct. The amount is a fair estimate of the costs the defendant is likely to recover, adjusted by a discount for uncertainty.

## Key authorities
- *American Cyanamid Co v Ethicon Ltd* [1975] AC 396 (HL, Lord Diplock): the leading guidelines for interim injunctions as above; no need to find a prima facie case; the court should not try to resolve conflicts of evidence on affidavits; the relative strength of each party's case is a last-resort factor.
- *National Commercial Bank Jamaica Ltd v Olint Corp Ltd* [2009] UKPC 16, [2009] 1 WLR 1405 (PC, Lord Hoffmann): the principle in an interim case is to take the course that appears to carry the lower risk of injustice if wrong; in a case which in effect decides the issue, the court should assess the merits to a greater extent.
- *Cream Holdings Ltd v Banerjee* [2004] UKHL 44, [2005] 1 AC 253 (HL): for interim relief against publication, "likely" in s 12(3) of the Human Rights Act means the applicant must show that success at trial is more likely than not, with some exceptions where the risk is grave.
- *Mareva Compania Naviera SA v International Bulkcarriers SA* [1975] 2 Lloyd's Rep 509 (CA) and *Nippon Yusen Kaisha v Karageorgis* [1975] 1 WLR 1093 (CA): the first freezing injunctions in England, granted against foreign defendants with assets in the jurisdiction.
- *Derby & Co Ltd v Weldon (Nos 3 and 4)* [1990] Ch 65 (CA): the court may grant worldwide freezing orders, with safeguards.
- *Motorola Credit Corp v Uzan (No 2)* [2003] EWCA Civ 752, [2004] 1 WLR 113 (CA): freezing orders in aid of a foreign (US) judgment under s 25 CJJA 1982: the court must consider whether it is inexpedient (considerations include interference with the primary court's management of the case, the risk of conflicting orders and the connection with England); the worldwide orders were set aside against two defendants with no residence or assets in England and upheld against the others.
- *Fundo Soberano de Angola v dos Santos* [2018] EWHC 2199 (Comm) (Commercial Court): a US$3 billion worldwide freezing order was discharged at the return date for serious material non-disclosure and not re-granted, despite the seriousness of the underlying allegations.
- *Anton Piller KG v Manufacturing Processes Ltd* [1976] Ch 55 (CA): the origin of search orders and the preconditions listed above (stated by Ormrod LJ).
- *Keary Developments Ltd v Tarmac Construction Ltd* [1995] 3 All ER 534 (CA): principles for security for costs, including that the court has a discretion, no presumption of security merely because the claimant is a foreign resident or an impecunious company, and the need to balance the risk of stifling a claim.
- *Nasser v United Bank of Kuwait* [2001] EWCA Civ 556, [2002] 1 WLR 1868 (CA): because of Art 14 ECHR, security against a claimant resident abroad must rest on objectively justified grounds relating to obstacles to, or the burden of, enforcing a costs order there, and the amount should be tailored to that risk.
- *Goldtrail Travel Ltd v Aydin* [2017] UKSC 57, [2017] 1 WLR 3014 (UK Supreme Court): a party alleging that a financial condition would stifle its case must show that it cannot raise the money from its own resources or from those (such as its owner) who could reasonably be expected to provide it.

## Applying it
1. Identify the interim remedy needed (preserve a status quo, stop a threatened act, freeze assets, secure evidence, require payment, security for costs).
2. Prohibitory injunction: apply *American Cyanamid* in order (serious question; adequacy of damages for each side; balance of convenience; cross-undertaking); note any higher threshold (effectively final order, mandatory order, free speech); consider delay and the terms of the order.
3. Freezing injunction: work through the requirements (cause of action, good arguable case, assets, risk of dissipation, just and convenient), the safeguards, and full and frank disclosure (*Fundo*). For foreign proceedings use s 25 and inexpediency (*Motorola*).
4. Search order: would a lesser remedy (preservation or imaging order) do? Then the *Anton Piller* conditions and safeguards.
5. Security for costs: identify the r 25.27(b) condition, then the "just" assessment (merits, stifling, delay, enforcement difficulty, the amount).
6. Remedies if wrongly granted: discharge, damages under the cross-undertaking, costs.
7. Common traps: applying the *Cyanamid* test to a case that is effectively final; omitting the cross-undertaking; assuming that a freezing order needs proof of dishonesty; forgetting disclosure duties in without-notice applications; treating foreign residence as an automatic ground for security.
8. Everyday question: "My former business partner is moving money abroad before I can sue; can the court stop him?" Answer: possibly, by a freezing injunction sought urgently without notice, if you have a good arguable claim and evidence of a real risk of dissipation; you must undertake to compensate him if the order proves wrong.

## Debates and reform
- The severity of discharge for non-disclosure in large worldwide freezing order cases (a technical non-disclosure need not lead to discharge, but the sanction deters).
- Whether *Cyanamid* is too rigid where the interim stage decides the matter in practice (*Olint* stresses the lower risk of injustice).
- Search orders in the digital era (imaging orders and Norwich Pharmacal orders as alternatives).

## Recent changes and points to check
- 6 April 2025: Part 25 substituted (SI 2025/106); PD 25A and 25B revoked; model orders replace the old standard forms. Pre-2025 cases use the old numbers (security for costs was rr 25.12-25.15, conditions in r 25.13(2)).
- The current residence condition for security (r 25.27(b)(i)) is simply "resident out of the jurisdiction"; the former carve-out for residents of Hague 2005 Convention states is not in the new rule.
