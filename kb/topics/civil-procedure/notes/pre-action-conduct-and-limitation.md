# Pre-action conduct and limitation periods

## Overview
Before issuing a claim, parties are expected to follow the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols (PD-PAC) or any specific protocol: set out the claim, exchange key information and documents, and consider settlement and ADR. Non-compliance can lead to costs sanctions, stays and adverse inferences. Separately, the Limitation Act 1980 bars most claims brought outside fixed periods (usually six years for contract and tort, three years for personal injury), with extensions for knowledge, disability, fraud, concealment and mistake, and a discretion for personal injury claims.

## The law
**Pre-action conduct.** The PD-PAC applies where no specific pre-action protocol applies. Its objectives are to enable parties to understand each other's positions, make informed decisions about settlement and ADR, try to settle without proceedings, support efficient management if proceedings are needed, and reduce costs. Steps, in outline: (a) the claimant writes to the defendant with concise details of the claim, the basis, what is claimed and, if relevant, how it is calculated; (b) the defendant responds within a reasonable time (14 days in a straightforward case, and no more than three months in a very complex one), stating whether the claim is accepted, and if disputed giving reasons and its own version, identifying key documents; (c) the parties disclose key documents; (d) the parties consider whether the claim can be settled by negotiation or some form of ADR, and a party's silence or unreasonable refusal to participate may be penalised in costs (see the ADR note); (e) a party may need to make an offer or provide evidence that ADR has been considered, and proceedings should be a last resort. Specific protocols exist for personal injury, clinical disputes, construction and engineering, professional negligence, defamation, disease and illness, housing disrepair, judicial review, debt claims, and others (some, such as the personal injury protocol, are supplemented by the Claims Portal and fixed costs).

**Compliance.** The court may take non-compliance into account when giving directions for management and when making orders about costs (PD-PAC paras on compliance and sanctions; CPR 44.2(5)(a), conduct includes the extent to which parties followed the PD-PAC or a relevant protocol). Sanctions include: staying the proceedings pending compliance, ordering the non-compliant party to pay costs (including indemnity costs), depriving a successful party of its costs, ordering that interest be reduced or increased (for example a claimant's interest on damages being reduced, or a defendant's increased up to 10% above base rate), and ordering a party to pay costs of proceedings in the meantime. Parties may not rely on the protocol to delay when limitation is about to expire; they may issue protectively and apply for a stay.

**Limitation Act 1980: main periods.**
- *Contract and tort* (ss 2 and 5): six years from the date the cause of action accrued (the breach for contract; damage for negligence). A claim in contract under a deed (specialty) has twelve years (s 8). A claim to recover a sum recoverable by statute is six years (s 9). A claim for contribution is two years (s 10).
- *Personal injury* (s 11): three years from the date the cause of action accrued or, if later, the date of knowledge of the injured person (s 11(4)). Death claims: three years from death or date of knowledge of the personal representative (s 12).
- *Defamation and malicious falsehood* (s 4A): one year from accrual.
- *Land and equity:* recovery of land 12 years (s 15); certain trust claims are subject to s 21.
- *Latent damage not involving personal injury* (s 14A): in negligence, an alternative period of three years from the date the claimant had the required knowledge (knowledge of material facts about the damage, that it is attributable to the defendant's act or omission, and the defendant's identity), if later than the six-year period; a long-stop of 15 years from the date of the act or omission causing damage (s 14B) applies unless there is fraud, concealment or mistake.
- *Date of knowledge* (s 14): for personal injury, knowledge that the injury was significant, that it was attributable in whole or in part to an act or omission alleged to be negligence or breach of duty, and the defendant's identity. Knowledge includes what the claimant might reasonably have been expected to acquire with the help of expert advice (s 14(3)). Knowledge that an act or omission was or was not negligent as a matter of law is not required (s 14(1) last sentence).
- *Disability* (s 28): where the claimant is a minor or lacks capacity (a person who, by reason of mental disorder, is incapable of managing their property or affairs) at the time the cause of action accrues, time runs from the end of the disability (six years for the main contract and tort period; three years for personal injury, from the end of disability). Time does not run against a person who lacks capacity while the incapacity continues; a minor's time starts at 18.
- *Fraud, concealment and mistake* (s 32): time does not run until the claimant discovered or could with reasonable diligence have discovered the fraud, deliberate concealment of a fact relevant to the right of action, or mistake. Section 32(2) treats a deliberate breach of duty in circumstances where the breach is unlikely to be discovered for some time as deliberate concealment. A purchaser for value without notice is protected (s 32(3)).
- *Discretion in personal injury and fatal claims* (s 33): the court may disapply the primary period if it would be equitable to allow the action to proceed having regard to the degree to which the provisions of s 11 prejudice the claimant and any decision to disapply would prejudice the defendant. Section 33(3) lists factors: length and reasons for the delay, the effect on the cogency of the evidence, the conduct of the defendant, the duration of any disability, the extent to which the claimant acted promptly and reasonably once aware that the act or omission might be actionable, and the steps taken to obtain medical, legal or other expert advice.

**Procedural points.** An action is "brought" when the request to issue the claim form is received by the court office (PD 7A para 5.1), not when the form is served (but the claim form must then be served within four months of issue under CPR 7.5). New claims added in pending proceedings are dealt with in s 35. An acknowledgment or part payment (ss 29-31) can restart time for debts. Limitation is a defence that must be pleaded by the defendant; the court does not take the point of its own motion. Standstill (tolling) agreements are used to prevent claims expiring during negotiations.

## Key authorities
- *Cain v Francis; McKay v Hamlani* [2008] EWCA Civ 1451, [2009] QB 754 (Court of Appeal, Smith LJ): two road traffic claims in which the claimant's solicitors or the claimant missed the three-year period (by one day in one case; by about a year after failure to serve in the other). Held: in considering s 33 prejudice, the loss of the limitation defence is not itself prejudice to the defendant, since the court assumes s 11 is disapplied; the focus is on whether the delay has caused evidential or other prejudice, balanced against the prejudice to the claimant of being denied a trial (including the claimant's ability to sue her solicitor, which is relevant but not decisive). The Court allowed the claimant's appeal in the one-day case and dismissed the defendant's appeal in the other.
- *A v Hoare* [2008] UKHL 6, [2008] 1 AC 844 (HL): s 33 applies to deliberate assaults and sexual abuse claims; the earlier *Stubbings* approach (excluding intentional torts from s 11) was departed from; a claimant's late claim against an abuser can be allowed under s 33.
- *Adams v Bracknell Forest BC* [2004] UKHL 29, [2005] 1 AC 76 (HL): the constructive-knowledge test in s 14(3) is objective: what the claimant might reasonably have been expected to discover, judged by a reasonable person in the claimant's position; the claimant's own characteristics (there, dyslexia that had gone unrecognised) did not turn it into a subjective test, and the claim was out of time.
- *Haward v Fawcetts* [2006] UKHL 9, [2006] 1 WLR 682 (HL): under s 14A, knowledge is knowledge of facts rather than of the law (s 14A(9)); time starts when the claimant knows enough to justify starting to investigate, and certainty of negligence is not required.
- *Canada Square Operations Ltd v Potter* [2023] UKSC 41 (UK Supreme Court): for s 32(1)(b), a fact is "deliberately concealed" if the defendant deliberately withheld or hid it, whether or not there was a duty to disclose it; for s 32(2), a "deliberate" breach of duty requires that the defendant knew it was committing a breach (recklessness is not enough).
- *Barton v Wright Hassall LLP* [2018] UKSC 12 (UK Supreme Court): a claim form not validly served within four months, so the claim was lost to limitation; retrospective validation under r 6.15(2) refused (see service note). *Hashtroodi v Hancock* [2004] EWCA Civ 652 and *Hoddinott v Persimmon Homes (Wessex) Ltd* [2007] EWCA Civ 1203 deal with the interaction of limitation with extension of time for service.

## Applying it
1. Identify the cause of action and the relevant period (tort/contract six years; PI three years; defamation one year; specialty twelve years; latent damage s 14A).
2. Work out accrual: for contract, the date of breach; for negligence, the date damage was suffered (a recoverable loss, not a mere risk); for professional negligence, when the claimant's position was worse (this is often later than the negligent advice).
3. Calculate expiry (the day of accrual is excluded; the last day is the anniversary); check when the court received the request to issue (PD 7A para 5.1), which is the date that counts for limitation.
4. Check extensions: date of knowledge (ss 11(4), 14, 14A), disability (s 28), fraud/concealment/mistake (s 32), acknowledgment (s 29), standstill agreement.
5. For late personal injury claims: apply s 33 by running through its factors and considering prejudice to both sides (Cain).
6. Pre-action: what steps were taken; are there protocols; compliance and sanctions; consider the effect on limitation (for example issue and apply for a stay if time is about to run out).
7. Common traps: using the date of the negligent act rather than damage; assuming a claim form issued is the same as served (service within four months); forgetting that the personal injury three-year period runs from the date of knowledge if later.
8. Everyday question: "How long do I have to sue for a car accident injury?" Answer: normally three years from the accident (or from when you first knew the injury was significant), and children's time starts at 18; late claims may be allowed in exceptional cases but are uncertain.

## Debates and reform
- The Law Commission's 2001 report (Limitation of Actions, Law Com No 270) recommended a core regime of three years from knowledge with a 10-year long stop; it has not been enacted, and the current law is a patchwork of periods and exceptions.
- Debate about whether the long-stop and the personal injury discretion are fair to victims of historic abuse and to defendants; the *A v Hoare* approach is generally seen as claimant-friendly.
- Whether the pre-action regime delivers early settlement or adds cost for low-value claims; the Civil Justice Council's work on pre-action protocols and ADR.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 October 2024: ADR duties in the CPR have raised the profile of pre-action settlement efforts.
- The main Limitation Act periods are unchanged by recent legislation.
- *Canada Square v Potter* [2023] UKSC 41 narrowed the s 32(2) "deliberate breach" route and clarified s 32(1)(b); older Court of Appeal authority on recklessness under s 32(2) should not be relied on.
