# Jurisdiction and service out of the jurisdiction - CPR 6.36, gateways and forum conveniens

## Overview
An English court has jurisdiction as of right over a defendant served with the claim form in England and Wales. To sue a defendant abroad the claimant usually needs the court's permission to serve out of the jurisdiction (CPR 6.36), which depends on three things: a serious issue to be tried, a good arguable case that the claim falls within a gateway in Practice Direction 6B, and that England and Wales is clearly the appropriate forum (*Spiliada*). Since 1 January 2021 the Brussels regime no longer applies, so English common-law rules govern most cases, supplemented by the Hague Convention on Choice of Court Agreements 2005 and, from 1 July 2025, the Hague Judgments Convention 2019 for recognition and enforcement.

## The law
**Jurisdiction as of right.** If the defendant is present in England and Wales and validly served with the claim form (even briefly), the court has jurisdiction, although it may stay the claim on forum non conveniens grounds (under *Spiliada*, the defendant must show that another available forum is clearly or distinctly more appropriate).

**Service out: permission.** Under CPR 6.36, the claimant may serve a claim form out of the jurisdiction with the court's permission if any of the grounds in PD 6B para 3.1 (the "gateways") applies. Since 6 April 2021, permission is not needed in some cases: under r 6.33(2B) service out is permitted without permission where the claim is in respect of a contract containing a term that the English court is to have jurisdiction (an exclusive or non-exclusive English jurisdiction clause), with conditions. The application is made under r 6.37 and must set out which ground in PD 6B para 3.1 is relied on, that the claimant believes the claim has a reasonable prospect of success, and the defendant's address or where the defendant may be found (r 6.37(1)); in practice the evidence must also show why the claim falls within the gateway. The court will not give permission unless satisfied that England and Wales is the proper place in which to bring the claim (r 6.37(3)). If the court gives permission, it may give directions on the method of service (r 6.37(5)(b)).

**Gateways (PD 6B para 3.1)** (summary, not the full list): a claim for a remedy against a person domiciled in the jurisdiction; a claim for an injunction ordering the defendant to do or refrain from doing something within the jurisdiction; a claim where the defendant is a necessary or proper party to a claim against another defendant who has been or will be served; a claim in contract where the contract was made within the jurisdiction, was made by or through an agent trading or residing within it, is governed by English law (gateway 6(c)), or contains a term that the court has jurisdiction; claims about breach of contract in the jurisdiction; claims in tort where damage was sustained, or resulted from an act committed, within the jurisdiction; claims in respect of property within the jurisdiction; claims about trusts, estates and under certain statutes (gateway 20); and others (for example, claims for information orders or to enforce judgments). New gateways were added in 2022 (for instance in relation to claims for disclosure to identify wrongdoers and certain claims connected to the jurisdiction). Different gateways have different evidential requirements.

**The three-limb test for permission.**
1. *Serious issue to be tried on the merits:* the claimant must show a real prospect of success, the same test as summary judgment (*AK Investment CJSC v Kyrgyz Mobil Tel Ltd* [2011] UKPC 7).
2. *Good arguable case that the claim falls within a gateway:* the claimant must have the better of the argument on the gateway, or a plausible (albeit contested) evidential basis for it that the court considers has a sufficient prospect of being established (*Brownlie v Four Seasons Holdings Inc* [2017] UKSC 80, building on *Canada Trust Co v Stolzenberg (No 2)* [2002] 1 AC 1, and approved in *FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Brownlie* [2021] UKSC 45). The "damage" in the tort gateway (para 3.1(9)(a)) is not limited to the direct damage that completes the cause of action: significant damage, including indirect or consequential damage such as continuing pain or financial loss, sustained in England suffices (*FS Cairo v Brownlie* [2021], by a majority, Lord Leggatt dissenting).
3. *England is clearly or distinctly the appropriate forum* (forum conveniens) to try the case in the interests of all the parties and the ends of justice, applying the *Spiliada* principles: the burden is on the claimant to show that England is clearly the appropriate forum (the reverse of the stay position for cases served in the jurisdiction); the court looks for the forum with which the case has the most real and substantial connection, considering the personal and factual connections (for example, availability of witnesses, language, location of documents, applicable law, place of the parties); if the foreign forum is more appropriate the court looks at whether the claimant would be deprived of a legitimate personal or juridical advantage and whether substantial justice would be done abroad. A real risk that the claimant cannot obtain justice abroad may outweigh other factors (*Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe* [2019] UKSC 20).

**Stays and anti-suit injunctions.** Where the defendant is served in the jurisdiction, the court may stay proceedings on the basis that another forum is clearly more appropriate (*Spiliada*), but an exclusive jurisdiction clause in favour of another court will usually lead to a stay unless there are strong reasons; an exclusive English clause supports an anti-suit injunction against foreign proceedings (*Donohue v Armco Inc* [2001] UKHL 64). Anti-suit relief is also available to enforce an arbitration agreement governed by English law even where the seat is abroad (*UniCredit Bank GmbH v RusChemAlliance LLC* [2024] UKSC 30: claim form served out under the contract gateway; England was the proper place because the seat's courts could not give effective relief).

**Methods of service abroad (Part 6 Section IV).** Rule 6.40 sets the general provisions: service out of the UK may be effected through the Hague Service Convention 1965 central authority channel, through foreign governments or British consular authorities (r 6.42), by any method permitted by a civil procedure convention or treaty, or by any method permitted by the law of the country of service; nothing may require anything contrary to that country's law (r 6.40(4)). The EU Service Regulation route (r 6.41) no longer applies after Brexit. The court can authorise alternative methods or validate steps already taken (r 6.15, r 6.37(5)(b); see *Abela v Baadarani*). The claim form must be served within six months of issue (r 7.5(2)), and r 7.6 allows extension.

**Challenging jurisdiction (CPR Part 11).** A defendant who disputes the court's jurisdiction must file an acknowledgement of service and then apply within 14 days; failing to do so is treated as accepting that the court has jurisdiction (r 11(5)). In a Part 11 challenge to service out, the claimant must show that permission was properly given; the court decides afresh, on the evidence, and may set aside permission.

**Post-Brexit position.** The Brussels I (Recast) Regulation and the Lugano Convention no longer apply to new proceedings, so Articles 4, 7, 25 and similar rules no longer govern jurisdiction and recognition of EU judgments. The UK applied to accede to Lugano in 2020 but the EU refused consent in 2021. The UK is party to the Hague 2005 Choice of Court Convention (applies to exclusive jurisdiction clauses; judgments of designated courts recognised across contracting states; in the UK since 1 January 2021 as a party in its own right) and the Hague 2019 Judgments Convention, which entered into force for the UK on 1 July 2025 (it has applied to the EU since 1 September 2023) and applies only where the proceedings in the court of origin began after it was in force for both the state of origin and the UK (the two conventions are implemented by the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020 and regulations). Recognition of foreign judgments otherwise is under the common law (judgments in personam by a court of competent jurisdiction according to English rules, final and conclusive) and the Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933.

## Key authorities
- *Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd (The Spiliada)* [1987] AC 460 (HL, Lord Goff): the leading statement of the forum conveniens test for stays and service out; the question is which forum is the more suitable for the trial of the action, with the burden on the party asserting that England is not (stay) or that England is (service out).
- *AK Investment CJSC v Kyrgyz Mobil Tel Ltd* [2011] UKPC 7 (Privy Council, Lord Collins): summary of the principles on service out (including the three limbs and the real prospect standard).
- *Brownlie v Four Seasons Holdings Inc* [2017] UKSC 80, [2018] 1 WLR 192 (UK Supreme Court): restated "good arguable case": (i) the claimant must supply a plausible evidential basis for the gateway; (ii) the court should try to resolve disputed issues of fact or law where it reliably can; (iii) if it cannot, the question is whether there is a plausible (albeit contested) evidential basis. Remarks there that the tort gateway requires direct damage were obiter and were rejected in *FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Brownlie* [2021] UKSC 45, [2022] AC 995.
- *Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe* [2019] UKSC 20, [2020] AC 1045 (UK Supreme Court): jurisdiction against an English-domiciled parent and a foreign subsidiary; the foreign subsidiary was a necessary or proper party; the existence of an English anchor defendant did not by itself make England the appropriate forum, but the real risk of no access to substantive justice in the foreign forum justified service out.
- *Abela v Baadarani* [2013] UKSC 44, [2013] 1 WLR 2043 (UK Supreme Court): on retrospective validation of service steps by the court (r 6.15(2)); see the service note.
- *Motorola Credit Corp v Uzan (No 2)* [2003] EWCA Civ 752, [2004] 1 WLR 113 (Court of Appeal): s 25 Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 permits interim relief in support of foreign proceedings but the court will consider whether it is "inexpedient", looking at the lack of connection with England, the risk of conflicting orders and the interference with the primary court; worldwide freezing orders were set aside against two defendants with no substantial connection to England.
- *Donohue v Armco Inc* [2001] UKHL 64, [2002] 1 All ER 749 (HL): a party suing abroad in breach of an exclusive English jurisdiction clause will normally be restrained by an anti-suit injunction unless there are strong reasons not to; on the facts strong reasons existed (parties not bound by the clause and a risk of fragmented proceedings), so the injunction was refused.

## Applying it
1. Where is the defendant, and is service in the jurisdiction possible? If so, jurisdiction exists as of right, and the defendant's remedy is a stay for forum non conveniens.
2. If service is abroad: is there a jurisdiction clause (permission-free route under r 6.33(2B)), a Hague or other treaty, or a statute? If not, identify a PD 6B gateway and supporting evidence.
3. Work through the three limbs: (a) serious issue (merits); (b) good arguable case on the gateway (explain each element with evidence, for instance where a contract was made or what damage occurred in England); (c) forum conveniens, listing connecting factors for England and the alternative, personal advantages, and any risk of no fair trial abroad.
4. Choose the service method and time (six months), plus whether translation is needed and whether the State of service has objected to methods (for instance postal service).
5. For the defendant: consider a Part 11 application within 14 days, with arguments on the gateway, the merits and the forum; consider an anti-suit injunction if there is an exclusive foreign clause; consider stay.
6. Consider enforcement: where are the assets, and will the judgment be recognised there (Hague 2005 or 2019, bilateral treaties, local law)?
7. Common traps: assuming Brussels I still applies; forgetting the claimant's burden at the forum stage for service out; mixing the "serious issue" test (a low merits threshold) with the gateway standard; omitting full and frank disclosure in a without-notice application; missing the six-month deadline.
8. Everyday question: "Can I sue a company in England when it is based abroad?" Answer: only if you can serve it and the English court will accept the case: usually, the contract needs an English clause or a real link to England, and a judge must agree that England is the proper place.

## Debates and reform
- Loss of the Brussels regime: criticised for uncertainty, parallel proceedings and cost; defended for flexibility and the protection of English jurisdiction clauses.
- Whether the *Spiliada* test needs adjusting to reflect the Hague Conventions and modern litigation (for instance, group actions against multinationals, as in *Vedanta*).
- Whether the UK should seek accession to Lugano again, or rely on the 2005 and 2019 Hague Conventions; the 2019 Convention has a more limited scope (excludes some matters and requires the indirect jurisdiction filter).

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 January 2021: end of the Brussels regime for new proceedings; Hague 2005 in force for the UK as a contracting party; gateways and r 6.33(2B) amendments from 6 April 2021.
- 1 October 2022: PD 6B gateways expanded (including a gateway for information orders against non-parties to identify wrongdoers or trace assets).
- 2021: *FS Cairo v Brownlie* settled that indirect damage in England satisfies the tort gateway.
- 1 July 2025: Hague 2019 Judgments Convention in force for the UK.
