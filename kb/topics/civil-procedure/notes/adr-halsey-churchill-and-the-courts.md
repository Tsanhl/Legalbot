# ADR and the courts - Halsey, Churchill, the October 2024 CPR amendments and small claims mediation

## Overview
For two decades *Halsey* (2004) was read as meaning that English courts could encourage but not compel parties to use mediation and similar processes, while penalising an unreasonable refusal in costs. In *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil CBC* [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 the Court of Appeal held that the court can lawfully stay proceedings for, or order the parties to engage in, non-court-based dispute resolution, provided the order does not impair the very essence of the right of access to court and is proportionate. From 1 October 2024 the CPR were amended to reflect that: ADR is part of the overriding objective, the court has an express power to order ADR, and failure to engage or to comply is a costs factor.

## The law
**Before Churchill: encouragement and costs sanctions.** The overriding objective and case management duties required the court to encourage parties to use ADR (r 1.4(2)(e)); a party could request a stay for settlement attempts on the directions questionnaire (r 26.5 at the time of *Churchill*); the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct (para 8 onwards) states that litigation should be a last resort and that parties should consider ADR, that the court may require evidence that ADR was considered, and that silence or refusal may be considered unreasonable and lead to a costs order. Costs rules allowed the court to take unreasonable refusal of ADR into account as conduct (r 44.2(4), (5)).

**The Halsey guidance.** In *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576, [2004] 1 WLR 3002 (Dyson LJ), the Court of Appeal held that a successful party who unreasonably refuses to engage in ADR may be deprived of some or all of its costs, but the burden is on the unsuccessful party to show that the refusal was unreasonable (the successful party is not required to show that ADR would have been futile). The court should consider all circumstances including: (a) the nature of the dispute; (b) the merits of the case; (c) the extent to which other settlement methods have been attempted; (d) whether the costs of ADR would be disproportionately high; (e) whether any delay in setting up and attending ADR would have been prejudicial; and (f) whether ADR had a reasonable prospect of success. The court also said, in passages at [9]-[10], that to oblige truly unwilling parties to refer disputes to mediation would be an unacceptable obstruction of the right of access to the court, citing the Strasbourg case of *Deweer*.

**Later development.** *Dunnett v Railtrack plc* [2002] EWCA Civ 303 (CA) had earlier deprived a successful party of costs for refusing mediation; *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 (CA) held that refusing mediation in a small building dispute was unreasonable and warned that a proper request to mediate cannot be ignored, although, because the refusal pre-dated *Dunnett* and *Halsey*, the defendants escaped an explicit costs sanction for it; *PGF II SA v OMFS Co 1 Ltd* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288 (CA) held that a failure to respond at all to an invitation to participate in ADR is, as a general rule, itself unreasonable, regardless of whether ADR would have succeeded.

**Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil CBC [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 (Sir Geoffrey Vos MR).** A homeowner sued the council over Japanese knotweed spreading from adjoining council land, and the council applied for a stay so the parties could use the council's internal complaints procedure. The district judge, holding that he was bound by *Halsey* at [9]-[10], refused. The Court of Appeal decided:
1. Paragraphs [9]-[10] of *Halsey* were obiter (the decision concerned costs sanctions, not an order to mediate), so the judge was not bound to dismiss the application.
2. The court can lawfully stay existing proceedings for, or order the parties to engage in, a non-court-based dispute resolution process (including negotiation, mediation and early neutral evaluation), provided the order (a) does not impair the very essence of the claimant's right to proceed to a judicial hearing, and (b) is proportionate to achieving the legitimate aim of settling the dispute fairly, quickly and at reasonable cost. This conclusion drew on Article 6 case law, EU case law on mandatory settlement procedures and the Civil Justice Council's June 2021 report on compulsory ADR.
3. The court declined to set out fixed principles or a checklist of when to order ADR, but said factors will include (at [61]-[66]) the form of ADR, whether the parties are represented, whether it is likely to be effective, delay and limitation, costs, the prospects of resolution, resource imbalance, the reasons given for not wishing to take part, and the proportionality of any sanction. These echo the *Halsey* factors.
4. On the facts the court made no stay. The judge had found the claimant unreasonable in refusing the complaints procedure (a finding not appealed) and would have stayed the claim but for *Halsey*, but things had moved on: the claimant had refused to let the council treat the knotweed, a one-month stay would achieve nothing, and the court could not order him to allow treatment because no such mandatory order had been sought. The merits of the council's procedure were left for another occasion, and the parties were encouraged to consider a temporary stay for mediation.

**The October 2024 amendments (SI 2024/839, Civil Procedure (Amendment No. 3) Rules 2024).** In force from 1 October 2024; they amend Part 1, Part 3, Part 28 and Part 44 (and consequential rules):
- *Overriding objective*: r 1.1(2)(f) now includes "promoting or using alternative dispute resolution", and enforcement of compliance becomes (g). Active case management (r 1.4) covers ordering or encouraging ADR.
- *Case management powers*: r 3.1(2)(o) enables the court to order the parties to engage in ADR; commentary notes that this includes early neutral evaluation and that the rules do not specify the type of ADR or timing.
- *Directions*: provisions in Part 28 (fast track and intermediate track directions) refer to ADR in the standard directions and allow the court to consider ADR at case management.
- *Costs*: r 44.2(5)(e) adds that the conduct a court may consider includes whether a party failed to comply with an order for ADR or unreasonably failed to engage in ADR.
Together, these amendments codify *Churchill* and align costs consequences with the mandatory approach.

**Pre-action position.** The PD-PAC continues to say that litigation should be a last resort and that parties should consider ADR; non-compliance is a factor under r 44.2(5)(a). Some specific protocols (for instance, the housing disrepair, professional negligence and clinical disputes protocols) include their own ADR provisions.

**Small claims mediation.** Since 22 May 2024 the CPR have required parties to defended money claims of £10,000 or less to attend a free, one-hour telephone mediation appointment with the HMCTS Small Claims Mediation Service before the case goes to a hearing; the scheme began with claims for a specified sum, as the first step of a planned wider roll-out to other small claims. For claims over £10,000 the court may offer mediation, which goes ahead only if both sides agree. If a party fails to attend without good reason, the judge can impose sanctions, including striking out the claim or defence or ordering that party to pay costs even if it wins (see also r 44.2(5)(e) and r 27.14 on unreasonable behaviour).

## Key authorities
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 (CA): costs sanctions for unreasonable refusal to mediate; six factors; paragraphs [9]-[10] on compulsion held obiter in *Churchill*.
- *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council* [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 (CA, Sir Geoffrey Vos MR): the court can stay proceedings for or order ADR, with Article 6 safeguards; no checklist; no stay on the facts.
- *PGF II SA v OMFS Co 1 Ltd* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288 (CA): silence in face of an ADR invitation is generally unreasonable.
- *Dunnett v Railtrack plc* [2002] EWCA Civ 303 (CA): refusal of ADR led to the winner being deprived of costs.
- *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 (CA): refusal to mediate sanctioned in a low-value building dispute where mediation had a reasonable prospect.
- *Lomax v Lomax* [2019] EWCA Civ 1467 (CA): the court can order early neutral evaluation without the consent of the parties, a case cited in *Churchill* in support of the court's power.

## Applying it
1. Identify the stage: pre-action (PD-PAC), after issue (ADR order or stay), or at the end (costs).
2. Pre-action: has each side proposed, responded to, or evaluated ADR? A failure to answer at all is almost always unreasonable (*PGF II*); record offers (including without prejudice save as to costs) and reasons for refusal.
3. After issue: can the claimant or defendant seek a stay or an order under r 3.1(2)(o)? Apply the *Churchill* conditions (essence of the right to a hearing is intact; proportionate), and consider the factors in [61]-[66]: kind of ADR, cost, delay, limitation, representation, prospects, imbalance of power, reasons for refusal.
4. Costs: use r 44.2(5)(e) and the *Halsey* factors; identify the unsuccessful party's burden to show that the refusal was unreasonable; consider both deprivation of costs and an adverse order (including indemnity costs where the conduct is exceptional).
5. Small claims: check whether the case is within the mediation service, whether a mediation appointment has been arranged, and what happens if one party fails to attend.
6. Common traps: relying on *Halsey* [9]-[10] for the proposition that courts cannot order ADR; assuming ADR is compulsory in all cases (it is not; the safeguards in *Churchill* must be met); ignoring the old factors that remain relevant; forgetting that "unreasonable" is judged by the *Halsey* factors, not by whether ADR would have worked.
7. Everyday question: "The other side refuses to mediate; can the judge make us?" Answer: since *Churchill* and the October 2024 rule changes the court can order the parties to take part in ADR, and unreasonable refusal can cost you money even if you win; but courts will not make an order that effectively blocks your day in court.

## Debates and reform
- Whether mandatory mediation undermines access to justice or is a cost-effective way to reduce backlog; the Civil Justice Council's 2021 report favoured compulsion in appropriate cases, the Ministry of Justice consulted in 2022 on increasing the use of mediation, and the 2024 rule changes adopt a judge-led approach rather than a blanket requirement.
- Questions about the quality, regulation and cost of mediation, the effect on litigants in person, and the role of ADR where there is an imbalance of power or a need for a binding precedent (public law and regulatory cases).
- The definition of ADR (is an internal complaints procedure ADR?) was described as academic in *Churchill*, but the court said the merits of each process matter.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 October 2024: SI 2024/839 amendments (rules 1.1(2)(f), 3.1(2)(o) and 44.2(5)(e)).
- 22 May 2024: required mediation for defended small money claims of £10,000 or less.
- Case law applying r 3.1(2)(o) and r 44.2(5)(e) is still developing at first instance; *Churchill* remains the leading appellate authority.

## Common errors to avoid

- Don't call small claims mediation optional: for specified money claims issued from 22 May 2024 and defended on the small claims track, referral to the free HMCTS Small Claims Mediation Service (one-hour phone session) is automatic; unreasonable non-attendance risks costs sanctions or strike-out.
