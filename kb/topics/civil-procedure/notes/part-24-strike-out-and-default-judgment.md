# Summary judgment, strike out and default judgment (CPR 24, 3.4, 12 and 13)

## Overview
Three procedural tools allow cases to end without a full trial: summary judgment (a party has no real prospect of success and there is no other compelling reason for a trial), strike out (a statement of case discloses no reasonable grounds, is an abuse of process, or a party has failed to comply with a rule or order), and default judgment (the defendant has not responded in time). Default judgments can be set aside, either as of right (if wrongly entered) or at the court's discretion (real prospect of defending or another good reason, applied for promptly). The tests ask whether a trial is realistically needed, not who is more likely to win.

## The law
**Summary judgment (CPR Part 24).** Part 24 was substituted with effect from 1 October 2023 (Civil Procedure (Amendment No. 3) Rules 2023, SI 2023/788) and Practice Direction 24 was revoked, with its main provisions moved into the rules. Under r 24.3 the court may give summary judgment against a claimant or defendant on the whole of a claim or on an issue if it considers that (a) that party has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim, defence or issue, and (b) there is no other compelling reason why the case or issue should be disposed of at a trial. The same test applies to claimants and defendants, and the substance of the pre-2023 test was kept. Procedure (rr 24.4-24.6):
- A claimant may not apply until the defendant has filed an acknowledgement of service or a defence, unless the court gives permission or a rule provides otherwise (r 24.4(1)). If the application is made before the defence is filed, the defendant need not file a defence before the hearing.
- The respondent must be given at least 14 days' notice of the hearing and of the issues to be decided; the respondent's written evidence must be filed and served at least 7 days before the hearing, and any reply evidence at least 3 days before (rr 24.4-24.5).
- Outcomes include judgment on the claim or issue, dismissal of the application, an order subject to conditions under r 3.1(3) (for instance, to pay money into court), and case management directions (r 24.6). Costs normally follow the event.

**Principles on "real prospect".** The test is not whether the case is likely to succeed but whether the prospect is realistic as opposed to fanciful. The court should not conduct a mini-trial on documents, but need not accept assertions that are contradicted by contemporaneous documents or are inherently implausible. It takes into account evidence that can reasonably be expected to be available at trial. Where there is a short point of law or construction and the court has all the material it needs, it should decide it. A "compelling reason" for a trial may exist, for instance, where the facts are complex or the law is in a state of development.

**Strike out (CPR 3.4).** The court may strike out a statement of case (or part of it) if it appears (a) that it discloses no reasonable grounds for bringing or defending the claim; (b) that it is an abuse of the court's process or is otherwise likely to obstruct the just disposal of the proceedings; or (c) that there has been a failure to comply with a rule, practice direction or court order (r 3.4(2)). Where strike out is based on a breach of a rule or order, an application for relief is approached through r 3.9 and *Denton* (see the CPR framework note). Abuse includes a collateral attack on an earlier final decision (*Hunter*) and raising points that should have been raised in earlier proceedings (the rule in *Henderson v Henderson* as explained in *Johnson v Gore Wood*, which calls for a broad merits-based judgment).

**Default judgment (CPR Part 12).** A claimant may obtain judgment in default if the defendant has failed to file an acknowledgement of service within the time allowed or has failed to file a defence (r 12.3), provided that no application to strike out or for summary judgment, and no Part 11 jurisdiction challenge, is pending and the claim has not been satisfied. For a money claim the claimant can request judgment for a specified sum, or for an amount to be decided by the court. For some claims (for instance, where a remedy other than money is sought, or against certain parties) an application to the court is needed.

**Setting aside (CPR Part 13).**
- *Mandatory* (r 13.2): the court must set aside a default judgment that was wrongly entered because the conditions in r 12.3 were not satisfied (for instance, the time for filing had not expired, or the defendant had already filed a defence or made a relevant application).
- *Discretionary* (r 13.3): the court may set aside or vary a default judgment if the defendant has a real prospect of successfully defending the claim, or it appears that there is some other good reason why the judgment should be set aside or varied or the defendant should be allowed to defend. In deciding, the court must have regard to whether the person seeking to set aside made the application promptly (r 13.3(2)). Because a default judgment is a sanction for failing to file on time, the court also applies the *Denton* relief-from-sanctions approach when exercising the r 13.3 discretion (*Gentry v Miller* [2016] EWCA Civ 141).
- Conditions may be attached (for example, payment into court), and the defendant usually pays the costs of the application.

## Key authorities
- *Three Rivers District Council v Governor and Company of the Bank of England (No 3)* [2001] UKHL 16, [2003] 2 AC 1 (HL, Lord Hope): "real prospect" means a prospect that is realistic rather than fanciful; the court should be wary of deciding contested factual issues without trial.
- *Easyair Ltd v Opal Telecom Ltd* [2009] EWHC 339 (Ch) (Lewison J): widely adopted statement of principles, including that the court should not conduct a mini-trial, may take into account evidence reasonably expected to be available at trial, and should decide short points of law or construction where it has the material to do so.
- *Swain v Hillman* [2001] 1 All ER 91 (CA, Lord Woolf MR): the word "real" distinguishes fanciful from realistic prospects of success; summary judgment saves expense and serves the overriding objective.
- *ED & F Man Liquid Products Ltd v Patel* [2003] EWCA Civ 472 (CA): in considering whether a defendant has a real prospect of defending (in the context of setting aside default judgment), the same "realistic rather than fanciful" approach applies; a mini-trial is to be avoided.
- *Barrett v Enfield London Borough Council* [2001] 2 AC 550 (HL): strike out is inappropriate where the law is in a state of development and the facts need to be found, for instance novel claims against public authorities in negligence.
- *Hunter v Chief Constable of the West Midlands Police* [1982] AC 529 (HL): a collateral attack on a final decision of a court of competent jurisdiction in subsequent civil proceedings is an abuse of process.
- *Johnson v Gore Wood & Co (a firm)* [2002] 2 AC 1 (HL): *Henderson v Henderson* abuse requires a broad, merits-based judgment taking into account the public and private interests involved and all the facts.
- *Denton v TH White Ltd* [2014] EWCA Civ 906 (CA): the approach to relief from sanctions, relevant where strike out arises under r 3.4(2)(c) and, by *Gentry v Miller* [2016] EWCA Civ 141, on applications to set aside default judgment.

## Applying it
1. Identify the application type and the stage: is the defendant yet to respond (default), is the claim bad in law on its pleaded facts (strike out under 3.4(2)(a)), is it an abuse (3.4(2)(b)), or does the evidence show no real prospect (summary judgment)?
2. Summary judgment: identify each element of the claim or defence; point to documents that contradict the other side's account; ask whether further evidence is likely at trial; consider whether the point is purely one of law or construction; consider whether there is a compelling reason for a trial (complexity, public importance, developing law).
3. Strike out: for r 3.4(2)(a) look at the pleading alone and assume the facts pleaded are true; where the law is developing, hesitate (*Barrett*). For abuse, apply *Johnson v Gore Wood* and *Hunter*.
4. Default judgment: check that the r 12.3 conditions are satisfied (service, deemed service dates, time for acknowledgement of service and defence, no pending applications).
5. Setting aside: if wrongly entered, r 13.2 applies. Otherwise r 13.3: a real prospect of defending (with a draft defence and evidence) or another good reason, and promptness. Explain the reason for any delay.
6. Outcomes: summary judgment (with costs and possibly an interim payment), conditional order, dismissal; strike out and its effect on limitation; default judgment and enforcement.
7. Common traps: confusing "probably will lose" with "no real prospect"; ignoring the "compelling reason" limb; forgetting that a claimant may need permission to apply before the defence is filed; treating promptness as optional; not producing a draft defence on a set-aside application.
8. Everyday question: "A default judgment was entered against me because I missed the deadline. Can I undo it?" Answer: apply at once to set it aside, with a draft defence showing a real prospect of success and an explanation for the delay.

## Debates and reform
- The 2023 restructuring of Part 24 was meant to simplify while keeping the test; commentators ask whether courts should use summary judgment more readily for cases that turn on documents or points of law, and whether the "mini-trial" warning is applied too restrictively.
- Strike out and novel claims: the "developing law" caution in *Barrett* sits uneasily with the judicial wish to control cost and delay.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 October 2023: Part 24 substituted (rr 24.1-24.6) and PD 24 revoked; the test in r 24.3 is unchanged in substance.
- Many money claims now proceed online (Money Claim Online and the online civil money claims service), where default judgment is requested through the online system rather than by paper form.
