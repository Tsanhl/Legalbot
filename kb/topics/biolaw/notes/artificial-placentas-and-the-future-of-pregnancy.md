# Artificial Placentas, Choice and the Future of Pregnancy

## Overview
This topic tests two claims made for artificial placentas: that they give people a **choice about how to gestate**, and that they are necessary to end **sex/gender oppression** by partially "de-gendering" reproduction. Romanis shows that, legally, ending a pregnancy early to transfer a fetus to an artificial placenta may itself be a crime under the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 unless an Abortion Act 1967 ground applies; and, socially, the technology could deepen inequality and be used to control pregnant people. This is a major essay area combining criminal law, reproductive autonomy and feminist theory.

## The law

### 1. Choices about gestation (Romanis 2020)
- Preterm delivery is common in obstetrics, but NIC is not an *alternative* to pregnancy. AAPT may "frame out" some risks of preterm delivery and make reasons for it weigh more, shifting perceptions of tolerable risk - so preterm delivery may become an earlier response to more complications (Romanis, "Artificial Womb Technology and the Choice to Gestate Ex Utero: Is Partial Ectogenesis the Business of the Criminal Law?" (2020) 28 Med L Rev 342).
- **Semi- or non-medical reasons**: less urgent physical symptoms; Kendal (2015) - to avoid social stigma of pregnancy. Is a "right to opt out of gestation" workable or helpful? (Romanis 2025).
- **Access obstacles**:
  - No right to demand particular treatment: ***R (Burke) v GMC*** [2005] 3 FCR 169.
  - Possible criminal liability for unlawful miscarriage (OAPA 1861 s 58) and limited 1967 Act defences.
  - Unequal access by class, socio-economic status and race; social pressure to make certain choices; non-emergency use likely limited to private care.

### 2. Is fetal extraction for AAPT a "miscarriage"?
- "Miscarriage" is not defined in the OAPA 1861 or the Abortion Act 1967; the statutes assumed termination usually caused fetal death.
- ***R (Smeaton) v Secretary of State for Health*** [2002] EWHC 610 (Admin), Munby J: miscarriage means "the termination of an established pregnancy" - broad and silent on outcome. Two readings (Romanis 2020): (i) cessation of pregnancy; (ii) cessation of pregnancy intended to result in fetal loss.
- **Broad reading**: miscarriage is not outcome-dependent (unlike child destruction); the offence covers "any method whatsoever", not only violent means; it is in the OAPA - arguably a crime against the woman's body; the absence of any reference to the fetus makes it counterintuitive to read fetal death into it; the 1967 Act's defences were drafted to cover premature delivery situations.
- **Narrow reading**: premature delivery did not exist in 1861; the Act refers to "poison or other noxious things" and instruments (Alghrani and Brazier 2011); the principle of **updating construction** (*R v Ireland* [1998]; *Smeaton*); the word "unlawful" in the actus reus may matter - or may simply refer to the terms of the 1967 Act.
- **Defences in context** (Romanis 2020):
  - Before 24 weeks, s 1(1)(a) would cover extraction only if continuing the pregnancy is riskier than *transfer* - complex to prove.
  - s 1(1)(b) "grave permanent injury" - semi/non-medical reasons would not qualify; some people might have to wait to become more unwell.
  - s 1(1)(c) "risk to life" - would an argument that all pregnancy risks life succeed?
- **Consequence**: on the broad reading there may be a legal obligation to remain pregnant even with a viable technological alternative, sending a powerful message about the role of people who can become pregnant and the need to justify deviation. Romanis's conclusion: the offence is too uncertain and broad and the defences too restrictive - **decriminalise**, or if gestation is to remain the criminal law's business, reform specifically.

### 3. Social narratives and control
- People who opt out of vaginal birth face criticism ("too posh to push").
- AAPT could be used to "control" behaviour of **"sub-standard gestators"** (Cavaliere 2020; Romanis et al 2021), hitting socio-economically disadvantaged people hardest.
- **Romanis, Begovic, Brazier and Mullock, "Reviewing the Womb"** (JME): the womb has historically been seen as dangerous and something husbands, doctors and the state could control; as technology makes the womb more accessible, similar arguments for controlling women recur. An era of "womb technology" dawns.

## Key authorities
- OAPA 1861 s 58 ("unlawfully" procuring miscarriage); Abortion Act 1967 s 1(1)(a)-(c).
- *R (Smeaton) v SS for Health* [2002] EWHC 610 (Admin) - meaning of miscarriage; updating construction.
- *R v Ireland* [1998] - updating (always-speaking) construction.
- *R (Burke) v GMC* [2005] 3 FCR 169 - no right to demand treatment.
- Hooton and Romanis (2022) JLB - AWT, pregnancy and EU employment rights (pressure to use technology).

## Debate and critique

### Ectogestation as liberation
- **De Beauvoir (1949)**: woman's "enslavement to the generative function" doomed her to domestic work. **Firestone (1970)**: the heart of women's oppression is childbearing and childrearing; women cannot be free until released from the "tyranny of reproduction".
- **Smajdor, "The Moral Imperative for Ectogenesis" (2007) 16 CQHE 336**: ectogenesis is an imperative to let women reproduce as men do, without risking health, economic and social wellbeing or bodily integrity.
- **MacKay, "The 'tyranny of reproduction'" (2020) 34 Bioethics 346**: building on Firestone, full ectogenesis could sever the tie between the identity "woman" and reproductive capacity; it is ethically preferable to gestational surrogacy and uterine transplantation.

### Critiques
- **Cavaliere, "Ectogenesis and gender-based oppression: Resisting the ideal of assimilation" (2020) 34 Bioethics**: the link between gestational capacity and oppression is weaker than MacKay suggests; ectogenesis pursues Iris Marion Young's "ideal of assimilation" (women becoming like men) which should be resisted; problems with surrogacy/transplants persist; the focus on biology overlooks systemic causes.
- **Horn and Romanis (2020)**: locating inequality in female bodies rather than social and legal responses "shifts the blame"; do women have to be like men to be treated equally? Pressure to use technologies or be held responsible for disadvantage (work, health outcomes) (Hooton and Romanis 2022). Arguments assume wide availability.
- **Jackson, "Degendering Reproduction?" (2008) 16 Med L Rev 346**: without equal distribution of household labour and childcare after birth, eliminating pregnancy would not end gender inequality. Is the real problem the social devaluing of care labour? (Horn 2022; Horn and Romanis 2020; Romanis 2025).
- **Binary and nuclear assumptions**: equality arguments assume two distinct sexes and nuclear families; trans men, transmasculine and non-binary people already become pregnant (Horn and Romanis 2020). Access to reproductive technologies is often delayed for LGBTQ+ people (Romanis 2023).
- **Reproductive justice**: technologies have liberated white women more than others - birth control used to "control" Indigenous populations, forced sterilisation of Black women in the US (Horn 2023); in the UK Black women are five times more likely to die in childbirth than white women (Five X More). Firestone herself warned technology may reinforce exploitation. Without social change, ectogestation may deepen divides of race, class and gender.
- **Lee, Di Nucci and Bidoli, "Does ectogestation have oppressive potential?" (2023) J Soc Phil** - examines oppressive potential of full ectogestation.
- **Equitable access**: Kimberly, Sutter and Quinn (2020) 34 Bioethics: if full ectogenesis becomes safe and is offered to cisgender heterosexual people, social justice requires equal access for sexual and gender minorities. Romanis (2023) 37 Bioethics 637 - empirical interviews with reproductive rights advocates on the benefits and challenges of uterus transplants and artificial placentas.
- **Romanis and Horn (2020)**: the ectogenesis debate overstates current technology and presumes stability of reproductive rights.

## Applying it to a problem question
Scenario: an HR manager in 2064 asked whether to offer complete ectogestation as an employee benefit.
- **For**: relieves physical risks and career penalties; inclusive for those who cannot gestate (single people, same-sex couples, trans people, people without a uterus); may promote equality at work.
- **Against**: implicitly pressures employees to avoid pregnancy (coercion, "sub-standard gestator" narratives); locates the problem in bodies rather than workplace culture and care labour; discrimination risks for those who choose pregnancy; privacy; equality law and employment rights (Hooton and Romanis); safety and evidence; unequal access.
- **Recommendation**: frame as one option among robust parental leave and anti-discrimination measures, never as an expectation.

For a partial ectogestation question ("Can a person end a pregnancy at 20 weeks to use an artificial placenta?"):
1. Is extraction a "miscarriage" under s 58 OAPA (broad v narrow reading; *Smeaton*)?
2. If yes, is there a 1967 Act ground? (a) requires risk comparison with transfer; (b)/(c) need serious medical grounds.
3. Can she demand the treatment? No (*Burke*); access and cost.
4. Evaluate: decriminalisation v specific reform (Romanis).
