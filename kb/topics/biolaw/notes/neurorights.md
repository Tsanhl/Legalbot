# Neurorights

## Overview
Neurotechnologies (brain-computer interfaces, neural implants, neurostimulation, neuroimaging, cognitive enhancers) can increasingly read from and write to the brain. the materials of Topic 1 asks whether existing human rights (privacy, freedom of thought, integrity of the person) are enough to protect the mind, or whether a new family of "neurorights" is needed. The debate is between proponents of new, neuro-specific rights (Ienca and Andorno; Yuste and the Neurorights Foundation) and critics who say existing rights should be interpreted and developed instead (Bublitz; to a degree Ligthart et al and the UN Human Rights Council Advisory Committee). This is core essay material: you need to know the proposed catalogues, the legal anchors in existing law, and the arguments about rights inflation.

## The law
There is no binding international instrument creating "neurorights". The relevant law is (a) existing human rights that may cover the mind, (b) a small number of national measures, and (c) soft-law proposals.

1. **Existing rights that may already protect the mind** (as mapped by Ienca and Andorno, Ligthart et al and the UN Advisory Committee):
   - *Freedom of thought*, ICCPR art 18. It protects the *forum internum* (the inner realm where thoughts form) and its inner dimension is treated as **absolute** (UN report, citing Human Rights Committee General Comments 22 and 29). Individuals have a right not to reveal their thoughts.
   - *Right to respect for private life*, ECHR art 8. The ECtHR treats mental (also "moral" or "psychological") integrity as part of private life alongside bodily integrity; it has recognised a generic right to self-determination and informational self-determination (Ligthart et al). Art 8 is a qualified right: art 8(2) allows restrictions for e.g. prevention of crime or protection of health (Ienca and Andorno).
   - *Right to integrity of the person*, EU Charter of Fundamental Rights art 3(1): "everyone has the right to respect for his or her physical and mental integrity." The Charter (adopted 2000) says nothing specific about neurotechnology; its biomedical requirements focus on free and informed consent, non-commercialisation of the body, and bans on eugenics and reproductive cloning (Ienca and Andorno). The EU Network of Independent Experts treats art 3 CFR as "fairly broad", covering forced psychiatric interventions, mandatory psychoactive drugs and "brainwashing" (Ligthart et al).
   - *Other instruments*: CRPD art 17 (physical and mental integrity of persons with disabilities); American Convention on Human Rights art 5(1) (physical, mental and moral integrity); UDHR art 1 (cited by the UN report for personal integrity); Oviedo Convention art 1 (dignity and identity) (Bublitz).
   - *Privilege against self-incrimination / right to silence* (see [neuroimaging-lie-detection-and-mind-reading.md](neuroimaging-lie-detection-and-mind-reading.md)): *Blunt v Park Lane Hotel* [1942] 2 KB 253; exceptions in *Murray v UK* and *Saunders v UK* (1997) 23 EHRR 313 (the materials).
2. **National measures** (detail in [mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md](mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md)): Chile's 2021 constitutional amendment (Law No 21.383 amending art 19(1)) and the 2023 Supreme Court ruling against Emotiv; California SB 1223 (2024) amending the CCPA; Colorado HB24-1058; Mexico (2023 Charter of Digital Rights amendment; 2024 General Law on Neurorights and Neurotechnologies Bill); Brazil (Rio Grande do Sul constitutional amendment 2023); Spain's non-binding Digital Rights Charter; France's bioethics law (the materials; UN report).
3. **Soft law / proposals**: OECD Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology (Yuste, Genser and Herrmann); the UN Human Rights Council Advisory Committee report A/HRC/57/61 (2024) recommending guiding principles and a soft-law instrument; Council of Europe Bioethics Committee Strategic Action Plan 2020-2025 (Ligthart et al).

## The proposed catalogues of neurorights

### Ienca and Andorno (2017): four new rights
Ienca and Andorno, "Towards New Human Rights in the Age of Neuroscience and Neurotechnology" (2017) 13 Life Sciences, Society and Policy 5, propose:
1. **Cognitive liberty** - drawing on Bublitz (2013), "mental self-determination": (a) the right to use neurotechnologies and (b) protection from coercive, unconsented use. Bublitz's phrase: "the right to alter one's mental states with the help of neurotools as well as to refuse to do so." Bublitz identifies three dimensions: liberty to change one's mind; protection against interventions into other minds; and an obligation to promote cognitive liberty. It has negative and positive aspects in Berlin's sense. Sententia calls it the "necessary substrate for just about every other freedom". Ienca and Andorno focus on the negative form - the right to refuse coercive uses.
2. **Mental privacy** - protects any brain information recorded by a neurodevice and shared across the digital ecosystem; covers brainwaves as data *and* as data generators; covers data not under conscious control. Privacy normally depends on our ability to filter what we disclose, and thought is that filter; brain-reading bypasses it. Violations can occur without direct intrusion (e.g. access to stored EEG databases or cloud data from consumer BCIs).
3. **Mental integrity** - reconceptualised beyond a right to mental health. Prompted by "malicious brain-hacking" (Ienca and Haselager 2016), e.g. hijacking a BCI-controlled wheelchair. A threat to mental integrity must (i) involve direct access to and manipulation of neural signalling, (ii) be unauthorised (no informed consent), and (iii) result in physical and/or psychological harm.
4. **Psychological continuity** - protects personal identity, i.e. experiencing oneself as the same person over time. Evidence: deep brain stimulation patients reporting "I do not feel like myself anymore"; increased impulsivity; memory engineering. Threats more likely outside clinics (military, intelligence, marketing, indoctrination).

They argue these rights pass justificatory tests for new rights (Nickel; Alston's criteria: fundamental social value, consistent with but not repetitive of existing law, capable of international consensus, sufficiently precise) and so do not cause "rights inflation". They also accept that most are relative rights; whether compulsory brain-based interrogation is ever allowed is open. Wolpe argues for "the skull" as a domain of absolute privacy, even against court orders.

### Yuste et al / the Neurorights Foundation: five rights
Yuste and others, "Four Ethical Priorities for Neurotechnologies and AI" (2017) 551 Nature 159, identify four concerns: **privacy and consent; agency and identity; augmentation; bias**. Recommendations include opt-out as the default for sharing neural data (treating neural data like organs or tissue, requiring explicit opt-in), adding neurorights clauses to treaties such as the UDHR and an international convention on prohibited uses, international guidelines on augmentation, and stringent regulation of military uses.

Yuste, Genser and Herrmann, "It's Time for Neuro-Rights" (2021) 18 Horizons 154, list five rights:
1. Right to **identity** (control of physical and mental integrity);
2. Right to **agency** (freedom of thought and free will to choose one's actions);
3. Right to **mental privacy** (keep thoughts protected against disclosure);
4. Right to **fair access to mental augmentation**;
5. Right to **protection from algorithmic bias**.
They note no international consensus yet, Chile as the first mover, and Spain's Digital Rights Charter incorporating all five.

### Lecture framing
the materials groups the core challenges as personal identity, cognitive liberty and mental privacy, and the ethical dilemmas as privacy v public safety, fairness in enhancement, informed consent, and misuse of brain data in hiring, education and insurance. the materials adds mental integrity (art 3 CFR) and cognitive liberty in the courtroom context. Future pressure points: workplace neuro-monitoring and criminal justice (brain data as evidence, predictive justice).

## Key authorities
- **ICCPR art 18** - freedom of thought; inner dimension absolute (UN A/HRC/57/61).
- **ECHR art 8** - private life, includes mental integrity and informational self-determination; qualified by art 8(2).
- **EU Charter art 3(1)** - physical and mental integrity.
- **CRPD art 17; ACHR art 5(1)** - integrity rights.
- **Chile Law No 21.383 (2021)** - constitutional amendment protecting brain activity and information from it.
- **Girardi Lavin v Emotiv Inc** (Chilean Supreme Court, rol 1.080-2020, 9 August 2023) - first judgment on neurodata (see [mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md](mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md)).
- **California SB 1223 (2024)** - neural data as sensitive personal information under the CCPA.
- **UN Human Rights Council Advisory Committee, A/HRC/57/61 (2024)** - surveys risks; recommends guiding principles, a soft-law document, general comments on art 18 ICCPR and art 17 CRPD, consent that is "prior, free, informed" and never assumed, and consideration of moratoria in military, law enforcement and criminal justice.

## Debate and critique

**1. New rights or adapted existing rights?** the materials frames this as the central question. Extending existing rights (e.g. GDPR, privacy) is quicker and less disruptive; a new category may give fuller protection because brain data is unique.

**2. Bublitz, "Novel Neurorights: From Nonsense to Substance" (2022) 15 Neuroethics 7** - the leading critique of the Neurorights Initiative's proposal:
- It promotes **rights inflationism**: human rights are few for good reason; proliferation dilutes them and turns them symbolic. Human rights also limit democratic decision-making, so new ones need strong justification.
- It is tainted by **neuroexceptionalism and neuroessentialism** and lacks grounding in human rights scholarship.
- None of the proposed rights passes the **Alston criteria**. Personal identity is ambiguous (diachronic identity, personality, group identity) and the law already identifies people over time; a right to **free will** imports an endlessly contested philosophical concept ("Precision is a virtue of lawmaking") - better to develop doctrines on manipulation and undue influence; **mental privacy** is already implied by the general right to privacy (Warren and Brandeis 1890 spoke of thoughts, sentiments and emotions) and proponents must show why the mind differs from the bedroom.
- The set is too broad (free will), too specific (algorithmic bias), over-inclusive (augmentation), and under-inclusive (omits workplace, non-digital life, and - ironically - the most promising rights, mental integrity and freedom of thought/cognitive liberty).
- Conclusion: stop lobbying; do bottom-up, detailed work refining existing rights.

**3. Ligthart et al, "Minding Rights" (2023) 32 CQHE 461** - seeks a minimal common understanding of mental privacy, mental integrity and cognitive liberty. They show each already has anchors in established law (art 8 ECHR, art 3 CFR, art 17 CRPD, art 5 ACHR; freedom of thought for brainwashing) but scope is unclear for non-medical neurotech. They take no position on whether new rights are needed. They note disagreement on whether cognitive liberty equals freedom of thought: Lavazza says freedom of thought suffices; Ienca says freedom of thought historically protected conscience and religion, not cognitive processes; Farahany says cognitive liberty is broader (self-access, self-alteration, consent to brain changes, mental privacy).

**4. The UN Advisory Committee (2024)** - takes a middle route: human rights instruments can evolve; "cognitive freedom, mental privacy, mental integrity and psychological continuity" are four strands for progressive adaptation; recommends a soft-law document first, warning that new standards must not dilute existing protection. It flags national security and law-enforcement exceptions in AI instruments (including the EU AI Act) as a weakness, and warns of the "criminalization of mere thought".

**5. Absolute or relative?** Freedom of thought is absolute; privacy is qualified. If mental privacy is a strand of freedom of thought it may be absolute (Wolpe, Stanley); if part of art 8 it can be balanced (Ienca and Andorno lean to relative). Similar questions arise for psychological continuity - could personality-altering interventions be justified for persistent violent offenders?

**6. Practical criticisms** of the California law: insufficient workplace protection (the materials). Global governance is needed to avoid inconsistent protection and to address equal access (UN concerns about wealthy nations monopolising enhancement).

## Applying it to a problem question
Seminar scenarios (workplace EEG monitoring; direct-to-consumer neural implants; youth offender brain monitoring; neurocognitive university admissions; neuroimaging in police questioning) are typical.
1. **Identify the technology and what it does** - reading (monitoring, decoding) or writing (stimulation, modulation)? Invasive or wearable? Medical or consumer?
2. **Identify the interest at stake** - access to the mind (mental privacy), interference with the mind (mental integrity), control over one's own mind (cognitive liberty), sense of self (psychological continuity/identity), fairness (augmentation, bias).
3. **Map to existing law first** - ECHR art 8 (qualified: legitimate aim, necessity, proportionality), ICCPR art 18 (absolute inner dimension), art 3 CFR, GDPR (see [mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md](mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md)), privilege against self-incrimination.
4. **Consent** - is it genuinely free? Power imbalances in employment, custody, education and with minors undermine consent (seminar guide; UN recommendation that consent be never assumed).
5. **Find the gap** - is there something existing law does not cover (e.g. inferred mental states, non-health brain data, private actors)? This is where to argue for or against neurorights, using Ienca and Andorno/Yuste v Bublitz.
6. **Recommend** - adapting existing rights, soft-law guidance, specific legislation (Chile/California model), or a moratorium.
Traps: overstating what technology can do now (current neuroimaging cannot truly read thoughts - the materials); treating "neurorights" as existing binding law in the UK (they are not); forgetting that most rights are qualified.
