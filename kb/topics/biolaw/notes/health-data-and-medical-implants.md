# Health Data, Medical Implants and Data Protection

## Overview
Connected medical devices continuously generate health data - a pacemaker transmits heart rhythm for remote monitoring; an insulin pump logs glucose. Topic 2 lists the GDPR as reading for the materials and, for the materials, Lindstad and Ludvigsen's article on when processing data from ICT implantable medical devices (ICTIMDs) is lawful. The key issues are which legal ground supports processing, whether consent can be genuinely free and informed, how the ePrivacy rules overlap, and what happens in emergencies. The note also connects to mental data in Topic 1 ([mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md](mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md)).

## The law

### 1. Personal data and special category data
- Data stored on or generated by an ICTIMD is personal data where linked to the patient; true anonymisation is hard in healthcare (Lindstad and Ludvigsen).
- Implant data are "data concerning health", a special category under **GDPR art 9(1)**, generally prohibited unless an art 9(2) exception applies.
- **Cumulative application**: mainstream doctrine requires both an art 6(1) lawful basis and an art 9(2) exception, plus the general principles (art 5); otherwise special category data could receive less protection than ordinary data.

### 2. Relevant art 9(2) exceptions for treatment purposes
1. **Explicit consent - art 9(2)(a)** (with arts 4(11), 6(1)(a), 7): consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and for special categories "explicit"; for one or more specified purposes (art 5(1)(b)); withdrawable (art 7(3)).
2. **Vital interests - art 9(2)(c)** with art 6(1)(d): where the data subject is physically or legally incapable of consenting, processing necessary to protect interests essential to life (recital 46) - a high threshold centred on life and death.
3. **Healthcare - art 9(2)(h)**: necessary for preventive or occupational medicine, diagnosis, provision of health or social care or treatment, based on Union/Member State law or a contract with a health professional, subject to art 9(3) (processing by or under the responsibility of a professional bound by secrecy). Clinicians commonly rely on this rather than consent within a "treatment relationship" (WP29). Processing by an external commercial actor such as the manufacturer is typically **not** "under the responsibility" of the physician (Kuner and Georgieva, as cited).
- Contract (art 6(1)(b)) can be relevant to services, but art 9(2)(h) restricts how far it can be stretched for health data.
- Further processing must be compatible with the original purpose (arts 5(1)(b), 6(4)) - a high threshold for implant data.
- **Security of processing - art 32(1)(c)**: ability to restore availability of and access to data in a timely manner after an incident.
- **Automated decision-making - art 22**; information duties arts 13-14 (including "meaningful information" about the logic involved, art 13(2)(f)).

### 3. ePrivacy rules
- The ePrivacy Directive (PECD, 2002/58/EC) - especially **art 5(3)** on storing or accessing information in users' terminal equipment - applies where the implant system uses public electronic communications networks; a proposed ePrivacy Regulation (EPR) would replace it (its January 2021 text adds a ground for security updates, art 8(1)(e), and for locating terminal equipment in emergency communications).
- **Gap**: the PECD has **no vital interest exception**, and the EPR only adds one for metadata - Lindstad and Ludvigsen argue a vital interest exception for terminal-equipment information is necessary (supported by WP29 and the EDPS).
- Conclusion of the article: only a **cumulative application of the GDPR and ePrivacy rules** adequately protects implant data.

### 4. Fundamental rights frame
EU Charter art 7 (private life), art 8 (protection of personal data - informational self-determination), art 3(2) (free and informed consent in medicine); also the right to life and right to health, which may require processing without consent (Lindstad and Ludvigsen). Rights may be limited if the essence is respected, limits are necessary and proportionate (art 52(1) Charter).

## Key authorities
- GDPR arts 4(11), 5(1)(b), 6(1)(a)/(b)/(d), 6(4), 7, 9(1), 9(2)(a)/(c)/(h)/(j), 9(3), 13-14, 22, 32; recitals 32, 39, 42-43, 46.
- ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC art 5(3); proposed ePrivacy Regulation.
- *Planet49* C-673/17 (2019) - informed consent must be clearly comprehensible and sufficiently detailed (cited by Lindstad and Ludvigsen).
- *Schrems II* C-311/18 (2020) - confidentiality of health data as fundamental right; limits under art 52(1) (as cited).

## Debate and critique
- **Consent is fragile in healthcare** (Lindstad and Ludvigsen): power and knowledge imbalances between patient and doctor or manufacturer; if processing is an unavoidable consequence of treatment it is misleading to base it on consent (WP29); complex multi-actor systems make "informed" consent hard - the Norwegian Consumer Council found terms of use for blood glucose meter and blood pressure apps averaging 6,653 words; vague purposes ("improving user experience") fail specificity; big data favours broad, discovery-driven purposes.
- **Weakening consent?** GDPR does not prioritise consent; some see this as shifting protection to controllers' accountability, others as eroding informational self-determination (losing rights to condition, revoke or refuse consent).
- **Emergencies**: a patient arriving unconscious needs clinicians to access the implant quickly, without advanced authorisation - mirroring the security trade-off between normal and emergency modes (Camara et al; see [cybersecurity-of-medical-devices.md](cybersecurity-of-medical-devices.md)).
- **Predictability**: healthcare providers and others in the value chain need clear, accessible rules; the overlapping GDPR/ePrivacy framework is complex.
- **Further regulation**: the European Health Data Space, Data Act and AI Act add requirements for connected medical devices; Ludvigsen argues for "genuine privacy" and security by design.
- **Link to neural data**: Ienca and Malgieri show that brain or mental data falls under art 9 only when it reveals health, beliefs or sexuality, which leaves consumer neurotech data less protected than implant health data.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the data (health? biometric? communications content/metadata?), the controller(s) (hospital, clinician, manufacturer, cloud provider) and purposes (treatment, monitoring, maintenance, R&D, commercial).
2. For each purpose pick an art 6 basis and an art 9(2) exception: (h) for clinicians in a treatment relationship; explicit consent (a) likely needed for manufacturer secondary uses; (c) in emergencies; (j) for research.
3. Test consent validity: freely given (imbalance), informed (complexity), specific (purposes), explicit.
4. Check ePrivacy art 5(3) if data pass through public networks; note the lack of a vital interest exception.
5. Security duties (art 32; MDR Annex I s 17; NIS2 for hospitals).
6. Automated decisions (art 22) for AI-driven implants (e.g. AI pacemaker adjusting therapy).
Trap: assuming patient consent is always the right basis for clinical processing.
