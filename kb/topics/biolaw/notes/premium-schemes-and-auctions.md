# Premium Schemes and Competitive Allocation (Auctions)

## Overview
the materials of Topic 4 examines **premium schemes** (feed-in premiums, FIPs), which pay generators a top-up on electricity they sell in the market, and **competitive allocation** through tenders and auctions. Driven by EU state aid policy since 2014, these have replaced FITs across Europe. They aim to keep consumer costs down and expose generators to market signals, but they reintroduce price, route-to-market and allocation risks. Germany's move from FIT to premiums to auctions is the main case study; the UK CfD is a floating-premium scheme allocated by auction (see [contracts-for-difference.md](contracts-for-difference.md)).

## The law

### 1. Terminology (the materials)
- Do not confuse premiums with FITs: under a FIT an actor must **buy** electricity at a set rate; under a FIP an actor must **pay a premium** for every unit the generator sells in markets, but there is **no purchase obligation** - the generator must find a buyer.
- Do not confuse premiums with auctions: an **auction is not a subsidy type** but an allocation method usable with any scheme type.

### 2. Common design features
- **Fixed premium**: the law sets a fixed premium per unit fed into the grid.
- **Floating premium**: varies with the market price - typically the difference between a **strike price** (cost recovery per unit needed for development costs plus reasonable profit) and a **market reference rate** (e.g. average price over days/weeks). The strike price may be **contractual** (put forward by the developer - negotiated or bid) or **administrative** (government sets the maximum rate per technology).
- **Repayment**: generators may have to repay when the market reference rate exceeds the strike price (floating) or a legally specified price (fixed) - to avoid "windfall profits" perceptions and support political sustainability.

### 3. Claimed benefits
Confidence in cost recovery (sale price + premium = cost + profit) with a lower burden on consumers; market exposure hoped to drive cost reductions (contested - higher risk may raise borrowing costs and delay cost reductions for newer technologies); more politically sustainable than FITs; repayment reinforces a positive image.

### 4. Concerns for investors
- **Price risk**: market revenue plus premium may not cover costs. Higher under **fixed** premiums when market prices are low (deterring smaller generators and costlier technologies; incumbents may accept risk hoping for high-price profits, but repayment can deter them). Lower under **floating** premiums, which are more open to new entrants and technologies.
- **Additional price risk for smaller generators**: unable to bear market transaction costs, they sell to wholesalers/aggregators at a discount, but the premium is calculated against the average market price - leaving a revenue gap.
- **Route-to-market risk**: no purchase obligation, so no guarantee of a buyer - see the UK's **Buyer/Offtaker of Last Resort**.

### 5. Germany's premium schemes (case study)
- Three stages since 2014: (1) a voluntary option to choose premiums over FITs (under the EEG 2012 direct marketing was optional, per Lang and Lang); (2) the **direct marketing premium scheme of 2014** - TSOs pay a premium on top of each unit sold, premium = difference between monthly average market price and strike price; (3) premiums allocated through **auctions** - generators bid the strike price they need; lowest bids win.
- **EEG 2014** (Lang and Lang (2015) 33 JENRL 131): direct marketing becomes the rule (s 2(2)); market premium (s 34) = "reference value" (*anzulegender Wert*) minus monthly market value; FITs only for small plants (under 500 kW if commissioned before 1 January 2016; under 100 kW after 31 December 2015) and a fallback remuneration at 80% if the direct marketer fails; support to be set by auctions by 2017 at the latest, to meet the **EU Guidelines on State aid for environmental protection and energy 2014-2020 (EEAG)**; pilot auctions for ground-mounted solar from 2015 (bids 100 kW-10 MW); **no support during negative prices** - reference value falls to zero if spot prices are negative for at least six consecutive hours (s 24); growth targets (40-45% by 2025, 55-60% by 2035, 80% by 2050) and expansion **corridors** with **"breathing caps"** (e.g. solar 2,500 MW per year; onshore wind up to 2,500 MW net).
- Outcome: concerns that market exposure would deter investment proved unfounded - renewables grew from **25.8% (2014) to 42.1% (2019)** (attributed by the IEA to falling risks as onshore wind, solar PV and offshore wind became cost competitive) and reached **62.7% in 2024**; the latest iteration is the **EEG 2023**, aiming at 80% renewable electricity by 2030.
- Lessons: FIT political sustainability problems (consumer cost burden, the Commission's view that market exposure always minimises cost); and market-exposure subsidies can work once a market is well established through FITs.

### 6. Competitive allocation - tenders and auctions
- Governments offer support (power purchase agreement, subsidy for a guaranteed period) via tender or auction on terms they set: eligibility conditions, assessment criteria; targeting a specified capacity or what a set budget can buy. Bidders state the price needed.
- **Tender**: government chooses by criteria. **Auction** ("Dutch" method in the materials): sealed bids ranked from lowest; accepted until capacity or budget is reached; **all winners receive the premium/strike price of the highest successful bidder** (uniform, "pay-as-clear" pricing - Welisch and Poudineh).
- **EU State aid guidelines**: competitive processes are the default for support to RE projects of **1 MW+** from **January 2017** for schemes seeking state aid approval.
- **Why use auctions?** Government control over how much, which technology, with what characteristics (contrast schemes available as of right to all eligible generators, which in Germany and Spain produced heavy demand and high costs); pressure to bid low cuts public cost; combining auctions with market exposure may cut costs faster than FITs.

### 7. Risks associated with auctions
- **Availability**: periodic rounds for specified technologies replace open access - momentum and cost reduction for less-supported technologies may be lost.
- **Administrative prices**: maximum strike prices set too low deter bids.
- **Allocation risk**: no guarantee of success.
- **Participation costs**: conditions to bid (environmental impact assessment, development consent, grid connection agreement, deposit) mean sunk costs before knowing the outcome.
- **Transaction costs**: complexity deters less experienced developers.
- **Deliverability**: because winners get the highest successful price, bidders may underbid hoping the clearing price will cover their costs - creating undeliverable projects.
- **Inflation**: marginal bids for delivery years ahead (e.g. 2028/29) may become uneconomic.
- Auctions favour established generators with revenue bases and staff; newer technologies can be helped by dedicated funding pots but remain deterred by allocation, transaction and participation costs.

## Key authorities
- German EEG 2000, 2012, 2014 (ss 2(2), 24, 34 per Lang and Lang), EEG 2023.
- EU Guidelines on State aid for environmental protection and energy 2014-2020 (EEAG).
- UK CfD as a floating premium allocated by auction.

## Debate and critique
- **Welisch and Poudineh**: the UK CfD auction (multi-unit, sealed-bid, uniform price) has achieved low offshore wind bids, but its design raises the probability of **speculative bidding**, and one-shot rounds prevent bidders learning. A stringent **non-delivery penalty** to induce truthful bids could improve deployment without raising support costs; **regular scheduled (e.g. annual) auctions** would feed cost information into bids, lowering uncertainty and support costs.
- **Fitch-Roy, Benson and Woodman**: the rapid dominance of auctions is explained by an activist interpretation of EU state aid law creating demand for the instrument and a ready supply of auction experts - positive feedback calling for critical engagement between policymakers and experts.
- **Couture and Gagnon**: premium-type (market-dependent) designs aid market integration and generation when needed most, but raise investment risk compared with fixed-price FITs and suit larger players.
- **Woolley**: limiting quantities and budgets through competitive allocation can reduce the regulatory risk of later retroactive cuts.
- **Does market exposure lower costs?** Germany shows growth continued, but only after FITs had matured the market; for newer technologies and small/community players, premiums and auctions add barriers.

## Applying it to a problem/briefing question
1. Identify fixed v floating premium; who pays; repayment rules; strike price setting (administrative v contractual).
2. Price risk: how does premium plus market revenue compare to costs? Effect of selling via aggregators at a discount.
3. Route to market: is there a buyer/offtaker of last resort?
4. Allocation: auction design (pay-as-clear), frequency (legally fixed timetable?), administrative prices, participation conditions, non-delivery penalties, inflation indexation.
5. Who is attracted (incumbents/established tech) and who is excluded (newer tech, small developers); use of ring-fenced pots.
6. Political sustainability and state aid/legal compliance.
