# Mental Privacy and the Regulation of Neural Data

## Overview
Consumer and medical neurotechnologies generate "neural data" (also called neurodata or brain data) that can reveal health conditions, attention, emotions and, increasingly with AI decoding, something approaching inner speech. The legal question is whether existing data protection law (in Europe, the GDPR; in Chile, Law 19.628; in the US, consumer privacy statutes) adequately protects this data, or whether specific rules are needed. The topic gives concrete examples of the neurorights debate in action: Chile's constitutional amendment and the *Emotiv* ruling, California's SB 1223, and the academic analysis of how the GDPR treats "mental data".

## The law

### 1. Chile
- **Law No 21.383 (promulgated 14 October 2021, published 25 October 2021)** adds a final paragraph to art 19(1) of the Chilean Constitution: scientific and technological development must serve people and respect "life and physical and mental integrity"; the law must regulate its use and "must especially protect brain activity, as well as the information from it". It is the first constitutional neurorights provision.
- A separate neuroprotection bill (Bulletin 13.828-19) was still going through Congress at the time of Cornejo-Plaza et al's article.
- Chile's general data law is Law 19.628 (1999) on the Protection of Private Life (arts 11 - due diligence; 13 - right to cancel or block), described as obsolete.

### 2. *Girardi Lavin v Emotiv Inc* (Chilean Supreme Court, rol N 1.080-2020, 9 August 2023)
(commentary calls it "Emotiv Inc. vs. Guido Lavin".)
- **Facts**: former senator Guido Girardi Lavin bought Emotiv's "Insight", a wireless EEG headset. He brought a constitutional protection action, claiming the device did not adequately protect his brain data; risks included reidentification, hacking, unauthorised reuse and commercialisation. Users had to accept an adhesion contract; without a paid "PRO" account the user could not export their own data, which stayed in Emotiv's cloud.
- **Emotiv's defence**: users gave explicit consent; data were pseudonymised/anonymised and used as statistics; the device was recreational, not therapeutic.
- **Other findings**: the health authority reported the device was marketed without relevant authorisations and had not been evaluated; customs certification was missing.
- **Outcome**: the Court ruled for protection of mental privacy. Per Cornejo-Plaza et al, the Court, citing the neuroprotection bill, treated neurodata as sensitive and biometric data; anonymisation and consent were not adequate safeguards because future technology may allow reidentification. It is described as the first judgment worldwide on neurodata.
- **Limits** (Cornejo-Plaza et al): the ruling does not address devices able to neurophysiologically identify an individual, nor transactions in neurodata.

### 3. United States
- **California SB 1223 (signed by Governor Newsom, 28 September 2024)** amends the California Consumer Privacy Act (s 1798.140 Civil Code) to add a consumer's "neural data" to "sensitive personal information". "Neural data" means information "generated by measuring the activity of the consumer's central or peripheral nervous system, and that is not inferred from nonneural information". Consumers gain the right to know what is collected and how used/shared, to delete, to opt out of sale/sharing, and to limit use and disclosure (Neurorights Foundation press release). California was the second US state after **Colorado** (HB24-1058 amending the Colorado Privacy Act).
- Rationale: medical neurodevices are licensed by the FDA and data covered by HIPAA, but consumer neurotech is "almost entirely unregulated". A Neurorights Foundation report on 30 consumer neurotech companies found 29 appeared to have unlimited access to users' neural data and could transfer it to third parties.
- Criticism: insufficient workplace protection; unique challenges not addressed.

### 4. European Union - GDPR
Ienca and Malgieri, "Mental data protection and the GDPR", define **mental data** as any data that can be organised and processed to make inferences about a person's mental states (cognitive, affective, conative). Their analysis:
1. **Personal data?** Neural data may identify a person (brain prints as biometrics), so they can be personal data.
2. **Special category (art 9(1))?** Art 9(1) covers data revealing racial/ethnic origin, political opinions, religious/philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data for uniquely identifying a person, health data, sex life/sexual orientation.
   - Data revealing mental health status are health data (art 4(15); recital 35), which they argue extends even to normal brain physiology.
   - But emotions, preferences, memories and thoughts are **not automatically** sensitive. Consumer BCIs measuring concentration or wellbeing are "unlikely" to be processing sensitive data unless they reveal health, beliefs or sexuality.
   - The gap: Rainey et al say art 9 is purpose-based; Ienca and Malgieri say sensitivity is contextual ("revealing"), and the real gap is that the list of categories is not comprehensive enough to capture emotions or thoughts.
3. **Lawful basis**: art 6 plus, for sensitive data, art 9(2). Commercial processing realistically needs explicit consent (art 9(2)(a)), which must be free and informed (art 7); consumer neurotech consent is often weak (unread terms, presumed consent). Healthcare: art 9(2)(h); research: art 9(2)(j) with Union/Member State law and proportionality. Purpose limitation (art 5(1)(b)) prevents moving from self-monitoring to commercial microtargeting.
4. **Conclusion**: the GDPR is broadly adequate, but interpreters should focus on processing characteristics rather than data category; they propose a **Mental Data Protection Impact Assessment (MDPIA)**.
- EU initiatives include expanding the GDPR to treat neural data as sensitive and Convention 108+; the EU and European Parliament are producing many reports.

### 5. Other jurisdictions
Mexico (2023 Charter of Digital Rights; 2024 General Law on Neurorights and Neurotechnologies Bill); Brazil (Rio Grande do Sul constitutional amendment 2023; national legislation on mental data and consent per the UN report); France (bioethics law prohibiting brain-modification activities posing serious danger); Spain (non-binding Digital Rights Charter); China (BCI research ethics guidelines). The UN report describes national responses as an "incoherent patchwork".

## Key authorities
- *Girardi Lavin v Emotiv Inc* (Chile SC, rol 1.080-2020, 9 Aug 2023): EEG headset; neurodata treated as sensitive; consent and anonymisation not sufficient safeguards.
- Chile Law No 21.383 (2021): art 19(1) Constitution protects brain activity and information from it.
- California SB 1223 (2024) / CCPA s 1798.140: neural data = sensitive personal information.
- Colorado HB24-1058: first US state to protect neural data.
- GDPR arts 4(15), 5(1)(b), 6, 7, 9(1), 9(2)(a), (h), (j); recital 35 and 51.
- ECHR art 8 (privacy, discussed as protection against "surveillance of your internal life").

## Debate and critique
- **Is neural data special?** Ienca and Andorno argue privacy normally relies on our ability to filter disclosure through thought; neurodevices bypass the filter and capture involuntary data, so a distinct right to mental privacy is needed. Yuste et al (2017) propose that opting out be the default and that neural data be treated like organs or tissue (explicit opt-in, restrictions on sale).
- **Wajnerman Paz** examines the Morningside Group's proposal to treat neural data like a body organ (bodily integrity) - which shaped the Chilean bill. He argues neural data are not organic so the organ analogy fails, but they are analogous to neurocognitive properties of the brain: natural semantic information exclusive to that person's neurocognitive architecture.
- **Brown, "Neurorights, Mental Privacy, and Mind Reading" (2024) 17 Neuroethics 34**: advocates overstate the link between neurotechnology and mind reading. Distinguishing natural, digital and neurotechnological mind reading shows similar privacy vulnerabilities across them, especially multimodal digital systems; real protection requires looking beyond brain-targeted devices. Current fMRI decoding has in-principle limits (temporal resolution, non-uniformity of brains).
- **Inference from non-neural data**: Yuste et al note typing patterns and smartphone mobility can reveal Parkinson's or Alzheimer's. the Naomi Oni example (acid attack victim accused of self-infliction because of browsing history) shows the danger of inferring thoughts from data: "Thoughts =/= Acts". The California definition excludes data "inferred from nonneural information" - a possible gap.
- **Consent is fragile**: cookie notice research (dark patterns study) shows notices are ignored and designs nudge consent; Ienca and Malgieri note weak consent regimes in consumer neurotech; *Emotiv* shows adhesion contracts.
- **Neuromarketing**: Stanton, Sinnott-Armstrong and Huettel argue the common concerns (autonomy, privacy, control) do not yet rise to meaningful ethical issues given current capabilities, but proprietary, opaque industry practice could create serious risks; the UN report by contrast warns neuromarketing can condition opinion formation.
- **Direct-to-consumer neurotech**: Ienca and Vayena distinguish consumer neurotech into neuromarketing and DTC devices for personal use; these sit outside medical regulation.
- **Fragmentation**: data protection laws do not distinguish data by informational content (Cornejo-Plaza et al); patchwork national responses (UN).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **What data and from what device?** Neural (directly measured) or inferred? Medical device (MDR/MHRA; see [medical-devices-regulation.md](medical-devices-regulation.md)) or consumer product?
2. **Jurisdiction**: UK/EU - GDPR; California/Colorado - consumer privacy laws; Chile - constitution.
3. **Under GDPR**: personal data? Special category (health; biometric for identification; revealing beliefs/sexuality)? Lawful basis under art 6 and art 9(2)? Is consent free (employment power imbalance, minors, custody)? Purpose limitation? Is a DPIA needed?
4. **Human rights overlay**: art 8 ECHR proportionality; freedom of thought; mental integrity.
5. **Gaps and reform**: argue whether a specific neural data regime (Chile/California) or neurorights is needed.
Scenario 2 (mandatory employee EEG for fatigue): duty of care v mental privacy; consent under power imbalance; real-time supervisor alerts; Emotiv's workplace use. Scenario 3 (DTC enhancement implant): "enhancement not treatment" argument; consumer protection; data security. Trap: assuming all brain data is automatically "sensitive" under GDPR - on Ienca and Malgieri's analysis it is not.
