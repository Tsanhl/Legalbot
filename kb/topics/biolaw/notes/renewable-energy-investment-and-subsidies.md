# Attracting Investment in Renewable Energy: Risks, Subsidies and Regulatory Risk

## Overview
The key question is how subsidy laws can be designed to attract the massive private investment needed to replace fossil fuels and reach net zero by 2050. Commentary explains what renewable energy is, why states are legally committed to it, why investors still see renewables as risky, and how subsidies (and their legal design) reduce that risk - or create new "regulatory risk" when governments change them. Every later scheme (FITs, obligation schemes, premiums, auctions, the UK CfD) is evaluated against the risk factors set out here.

## The law and policy framework

### 1. What renewable energy is
- Energy flows replenished at the rate they are used; no carbon emissions in production (biomass described as carbon neutral). Sources: solar, onshore and offshore wind, hydropower, wave and tidal, geothermal, biomass (crops, food waste).
- Some sources are **intermittent** (wind only when strong enough); storage (batteries, hydrogen from electrolysis) helps fill gaps.

### 2. Why switch - climate and legal commitments
- Fuel combustion for energy was the main source of global greenhouse gas emissions (76% in 2019); renewables are often the best-established lowest-cost low-carbon alternatives (onshore wind since the 1980s, offshore since the 1990s; solar PV rapidly since the 1970s).
- **Paris Agreement** (195 parties): keep warming well below 2°C (towards 1.5°C); collectively reach net zero by 2050; parties submit NDCs - by 11/2021, 182 of 195 included renewables. **COP28 (2023)**: pledge to triple global renewable capacity to 11,000 GW by 2030.
- **UK Climate Change Act 2008**: duty to reach 100% emissions reduction against 1990 by 2050; 77% reduction for 2033-2037; policy goal of 100% decarbonised electricity by 2030.
- **EU**: Renewable Energy Directive 2018 (revised 2023) - 42.5%-45% renewables in energy consumption by 2030; European Climate Law (Reg 2021/1119) - 55% GHG cut by 2030 against 1990.
- California: legally committed to 100% renewable electricity by 2045; China: largest destination for renewables investment.
- Other drivers: energy security (replacing imported fossil fuels), green economy (IEA 2024: clean energy 10% of global GDP growth in 2023; renewables the largest energy-sector recipient of investment in 2023, $623bn), cost (renewables often cheaper), health (air pollution).

### 3. How investment decisions work
- Many kinds of developer: householders with rooftop solar, landowners, community projects, new generators, incumbent generators.
- Key question: will electricity sales recover development costs **and** a sufficiently attractive profit margin (a buffer for unanticipated risks and inflation)?
- Most developers need finance - equity or, more commonly, loans. Lenders price risk: the higher the perceived risk of non-recovery, the higher the interest rate, and interest is part of development cost.

### 4. Risk factors for renewables
- **RF1 - Inability to compete in markets**: newer technologies (or new models of established ones) may not yet be cost competitive, so cannot find long-term purchasers - which stops them becoming cheaper through **learning by doing**.
- **RF2 - Scale**: smaller projects mean fewer economies of scale.
- **RF3 - Capital costs**: costs are mostly up-front, so investors need high confidence in recovery before committing (Woolley: fossil fuel businesses have more variable fuel costs and can hedge; renewables need stable minimum prices over long periods).
- **RF4 - Volume risk**: output depends on the resource and may not coincide with high demand/prices; high wind/sun lowers market prices; gas plants can switch on when prices are high.
- **Market failures** favour fossil fuels: environmental costs not priced in; fossil fuels are more heavily subsidised (record ~$1 trillion in 2022 per REN21).
- Woolley adds: incumbents' sunk investment (financial and psychological) in fossil fuel business models; project-by-project variation (resource quality, location, market experience) means global average cost figures can mislead (IRENA: 2010-2021 levelised costs fell 88% for utility solar, 67% onshore wind, 60% offshore wind).

### 5. The role of subsidies
- Public intervention is needed both to attract investment despite risk factors and to secure enough development fast enough for 2030/2050 goals.
- **Investment support**: grants, low-interest loans for early-stage technology - enabling development where private money would cost too much; lowers development costs.
- **Operating support**: for commercially ready technology - gives confidence that development costs plus profit will be recovered over the plant's operating life through electricity revenue plus subsidy; overcomes the perception of projects as "TOO RISKY".
- Main operating support types (Woolley ch 5): **feed-in tariffs**; **obligation and certificate schemes** (in the US, renewable portfolio standards); **premium schemes**. Auctions are an allocation method usable with any type (see [premium-schemes-and-auctions.md](premium-schemes-and-auctions.md)).

### 6. Controversy and regulatory (political) risk
- Operating subsidies conflict with policies for undistorted competition; the European Commission has treated national subsidies as a barrier to an EU-wide competitive internal energy market (state aid). Debate whether market exposure improves or harms efficiency. Note double standards on fossil fuel subsidies.
- Costs usually fall on energy consumers, producing public opposition that makes it politically hard to sustain support.
- **Regulatory risk** (Woolley): the possibility that governments alter support terms after investment. Examples: **Spain** cut rates and duration of solar FIT support already awarded in 2010 (growth exceeded what the system could absorb; distribution operators could not pass costs on, creating a deficit); the **UK** suddenly cut solar FIT rates, harming the emerging solar supply chain (and solar companies threatened to sue for £140m, Guardian 2013). Lenders respond by raising interest rates.
- **Legal tools to build confidence** (Woolley):
  1. Make change harder or penalised - **RES Directive 2018**: art 6(1) Member States must not revise support in a way that negatively affects rights conferred and undermines the economic viability of supported projects (breach exposes them to Commission proceedings); art 6(3) publish long-term schedules (five-year horizon: timing, frequency of tenders, capacity, budget/technology limits); art 6(4) assess effectiveness and distributional effects every five years. Legally binding national targets also signal continuity.
  2. **Investment treaties** - e.g. the **Energy Charter Treaty** (1994, in force 1998); many ECT claims allege failure to give **fair and equitable treatment** through ex post changes to renewables support.
  3. Better scheme design reducing the need for later intervention - limit supported quantities, finite budgets, maximum volumes for competitive allocation, restrict access to locations not needing major grid upgrades (Boute); and build in adjustment by **objective criteria established in the original design** (RES Directive 2018).
- Avoiding public opposition is a key consideration in legal design.

## Debate and critique
- **Subsidies v free markets** (Hart and Marcellino (2012) 3 Renewable Energy Law and Policy, cited by Woolley): does market exposure drive efficiency, or does risk raise financing costs and slow cost reduction?
- **Egli, "Renewable energy investment risk"**: interviews with 40 investors (Germany, Italy, UK) identify five main risk types - **curtailment, policy, price, resource and technology**. Risk premiums have fallen for solar PV and onshore wind thanks to cheaper, more reliable technology, better data and tools, and **credible and stable policies**. Policy and technology risks have become relatively less important; curtailment and price risks more important - implying subsidy design should shift focus.
- **Fitch-Roy, Benson and Woodman**: auctions rose to dominance through an activist interpretation of EU state aid law creating demand, met by a community of auction experts - a reminder that instrument choice is political, not neutral.
- **Political sustainability v investor confidence**: designs that keep consumer costs low (degression, repayment, auctions) may add investor risk; designs investors love (generous FITs) may become unsustainable and be cut, creating regulatory risk.
- **Context**: support must be judged against the 2030 decarbonised electricity and 2050 net zero goals.

## Applying it to a problem/briefing question
To evaluate any subsidy law:
1. **Which risk factors does the target technology face?** Maturity (RF1), scale (RF2), capital intensity (RF3), intermittency (RF4); and which investors (incumbents, newcomers, community)?
2. **What does the scheme guarantee?** Price (price risk), purchase (route to market risk), availability of support (availability/allocation risk), duration.
3. **How are costs funded?** Consumers? Exchequer? Effect on political sustainability.
4. **How stable is the law?** Can the government change terms? Are there legal protections (RES Directive art 6, investment treaties), objective adjustment criteria, budgets/caps?
5. **Distributional effects**: who is favoured - established technologies and incumbents, or new entrants and newer technologies?
6. **Conclude** on suitability for attracting investment at the scale and speed needed.
