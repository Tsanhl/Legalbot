# Artificial Placentas and Legal Parenthood

## Overview
English law identifies the legal mother as the person who gestates and gives birth (*mater semper certa est*). the materials of Topic 3 asks what happens when gestation is partly or wholly done by a machine. In partial ectogestation the formerly pregnant person remains the mother, but in complete ectogestation nobody gestates, and the law has no answer: should genetics, intention, or something else decide? The topic connects to a wider critique that the parenthood framework rests on cis-heteronormative assumptions about the nuclear family and "reproductive biosex" (Romanis and Brown).

## The law (England and Wales)

### 1. What legal parenthood is
- Distinct from social and genetic parenthood (though it may coincide); distinct from **parental responsibility** (Bainham 1999).
- English law uses a **binary, two-parent model** (Jackson 2006; Brown 2019).
- It matters: presumption of contact, easier acquisition of PR (Bainham), and identity - rules work "like a sieve" making some relationships visible and others "inexistent" (Swennen and Croce 2016).

### 2. Attribution rules
- **"Natural" conception**: presumptions, birth certificates, underlying genetic link. *The Ampthill Peerage* [1977] AC 547: motherhood, though a legal relationship, "is based on a fact, being proved demonstrably by parturition".
- **Assisted reproduction - Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008**:
  - **s 33(1)**: "The woman who is carrying or has carried a child as a result of the placing in her of an embryo or of sperm and eggs, and no other woman, is to be treated as the mother of the child." An egg donor (genetic mother) has no claim to motherhood.
  - **Father** (s 37 and related provisions, per the materials): husband/civil partner, genetic progenitor, or a man meeting the agreed fatherhood conditions. Consent is presumed for married men ("opt out") and must be shown for unmarried men ("opt in"); courts have upheld consent despite clinic paperwork errors (Romanis and Brown).
  - **Second female parent** (s 44, per the materials): wife/civil partner, or a woman meeting the agreed second female parent conditions.
  - Sperm donors under the statutory framework are not fathers.
  - **Surrogacy**: the surrogate is the legal mother at birth; transfer by **parental order** requires a genetic link to at least one intended parent (s 54(1)(b), s 54A(1)(b)). Theis J (cited by Romanis and Brown): parental orders are "a more honest order" reflecting intention and lineage.
- **Trans parents**: *R (McConnell and YY) v Registrar General* [2020] EWCA Civ 559: a trans man with a gender recognition certificate who gave birth must be registered as "mother"; at first instance Sir Andrew McFarlane P said "mother" is freestanding from legal gender - "there can be male mothers and female fathers".
- **Gestation is key**: the rule traces to Roman law (*mater semper certa est*; Justinian's Digest, Paulus: the mother is always certain, the father is he whom marriage indicates) and is entrenched worldwide.

### 3. Applying the rules to artificial placentas
- **Partial ectogestation**: the formerly pregnant person gestated (for a time) and "birthed", so she is the legal mother; a machine cannot be a legal parent (Romanis and Brown, citing Romanis's thesis and Alghrani 2018).
- **Complete ectogestation ("disembodied gestation")**: no one gestates. Options (the materials; Romanis 2025):
  - A machine cannot be a mother (Alghrani 2018; Hendricks).
  - Fall back on **genetics**? Inconsistent with the HFE Acts, where genetic connection is irrelevant to motherhood (egg donors are not mothers) - though genetics matters for parental orders.
  - Use **intention**? Intention has normative weight in some contexts but has never been determinative; what would it mean here?
  - Example (Romanis and Brown): A, single, with a family history of heritable disease, uses donor gametes and complete ectogestation. On biology alone A has no claim, but the donors are not parents either - so intention must matter.
- The UK has no anonymous birth; abandonment is investigated (OAPA 1861 offence in England and Wales; "cruelty" in Scotland, Children and Young Persons (Scotland) Act 1937) - showing the law always expects a birth-giver.

## Debate and critique
- **Defences of the gestation rule**: Hendricks (2011): at birth, the only existing human relationship the child can draw on is with the woman from whom she has just separated; Hendricks (2023) *Essentially a Mother*: law should value pregnancy and birth as creating the mother-child relationship. Mulligan (2023/24): egg donation and reciprocal egg donation in lesbian couples confirm the significance of gestation as mothering.
- **Critiques**: the Roman-law origins (Romanis 2025); the rule ignores the intentions of those who undertake pregnancy (Mahmoud and Romanis 2023 Med L Rev; Lima 2024 - focus on the surrogate's negative intention); a person can donate genetic material but not gestational work; biological determinism weighs most on people assigned female at birth.
- **Romanis and Brown, "Legal Parenthood, Novel Reproductive Practices, and the Disruption of Reproductive Biosex" (2024/2025) 88 MLR 155**: technologies on the horizon (artificial placentas, uterus transplants, in vitro gametogenesis, mitochondrial replacement) undermine reproductive biosex. The framework rests on cis-heteronormative assumptions about the idealised nuclear family. Three challenges: (1) biosexed reproductive roles may be deconstructed; (2) relatedness reimagined; (3) disembodied reproduction. The law must revisit the purpose, functions and basis of parenthood rules; is it time to abandon the binary, two-parent model?
- **Large, "Moral parenthood: not gestational" (2024) JME**: rejects the "Gestationalist View" (e.g. Gheaus) that gestation creates an intimate relationship grounding a moral right to parent one's biological child; two supporting arguments fail; normative reality is not "neat". (Published with commentaries - read for critique.) Note Large concerns *moral* parenthood, which Archard distinguishes from social and legal parenthood.
- **Kennedy, "Willing mothers: ectogenesis and the role of gestational motherhood" (2020) 46 JME 320**: ectogenesis should be pursued to address unintended pregnancy, but it disrupts the procreation-parenthood link; only the voluntarist account (obligations voluntarily undertaken) survives, but it presumes a patriarchal divide; she reframes gestational motherhood as voluntarily undertaken obligations - so all gestational mothers would be "willing mothers".
- **Empirical voice**: Romanis (2023) 10 JLB - reproductive rights advocates describe parenthood law as "very, very outdated", predating late-20th-century technologies, "a real mess", and note surrogacy reform has sidestepped parenthood.
- Further: Hammond-Browning (2018) on ectogenesis and reproductive choice; Adkins (2023) on the Warnock Report and partial ectogestation (the materials).

## Applying it to a problem question
Seminar scenario: Hannah, single, with no ovaries or uterus after cancer, uses donor egg, donor sperm and an artificial womb.
1. **Access**: would a clinic treat her? HFE Act welfare of the child considerations (the "welfare clause" mentioned by Romanis and Brown); fairness and reproductive justice for people unable to gestate.
2. **Motherhood**: s 33(1) requires that she carried the child - she did not. No genetic link either. Donors are not parents.
3. **Fatherhood/second parent**: none (single; donor sperm).
4. **Gap**: the child may have no legal parent at birth. Possible routes: adoption, or reform giving weight to **intention**.
5. **Evaluate**: gestation-based rule (Hendricks) v intention-based (Mahmoud and Romanis; Lima) v genetics; Romanis and Brown's call to rethink the foundations.
For partial ectogestation: the person who was pregnant remains mother (even if she did not intend to parent); her spouse/partner may be father or second female parent under the HFE Act 2008 rules (s 37, s 44 as cited in the materials); outside assisted conception a genetic progenitor may be the father.
Trap: assuming genetics decides motherhood in English law - it does not.
