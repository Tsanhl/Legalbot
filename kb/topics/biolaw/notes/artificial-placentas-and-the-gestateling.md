# Artificial Placentas and the Legal Status of the Gestateling

## Overview
Topic 3 of (The Future of Assisted Reproduction: Artificial Placenta Technology) concerns a device designed to continue gestation outside the body. In 2017 a US team (Partridge et al) sustained premature lambs in the "biobag" (EXTEND). The technology aims to overcome the limits of neonatal intensive care, where survival is possible only from around 22 weeks. The course looks only at **partial ectogestation** - taking over gestation of an already developing entity - not complete ectogestation. the materials asks what the subject of an artificial placenta *is*, and whether English law's "born alive" rule gives it legal personality. The answer shapes research regulation, abortion, parenthood and the rights of pregnant people.

## The law

### 1. The technology and terminology
- **Limits of conventional neonatal intensive care (NIC)**: neonates under 26 weeks are more likely to die than survive; about 50% of survivors have severe impairment. Main problems: mechanical ventilation, heart failure from circulatory aids, infection from nasogastric feeding (the materials).
- **EXTEND therapy** (Partridge et al, "An extra-uterine system to physiologically support the extreme premature lamb" (2017) 8 Nature Communications 15112): sealed bag with amniotic fluid, a pump-less oxygenator circuit and cannulas acting as an umbilical cord; it "sidesteps" NIC's limits; clinical target 22-24 weeks. Other teams: Japan/Western Australia (EVE platform), Canada, a Netherlands/European consortium (Perinatal Life Support).
- **Next steps**: the US FDA Pediatric Advisory Committee (2023) estimated readiness for human use within about 10 years; but trials have small samples, short durations, lamb physiology, and outcomes (e.g. EVE) need refinement (Romanis 2020).
- **Glossary terms**: *Artificial Amniotic Sac and Placenta Technology (AAPT)* / "artificial placenta" preferred over "artificial womb" (which is less precise and has normative implications for pregnant people). *Ectogestation* (Kingma and Finn 2020) preferred over "ectogenesis" (literally "outside development", which all babies undergo). *Complete* ectogestation (from fertilisation; science fiction for now) v *partial* ectogestation (transfer of a partially developed entity). Current devices cannot support **embryogenesis** (first 8 weeks); they depend on fetal physiology. *Gestateling* (Romanis) v *fetonate* (Philadelphia scientists) v *ectofetus*. *Viability*: often around 24 weeks, when about 50% of prematurely delivered fetuses survive.
- **Barriers to complete ectogestation** (the materials): design depends on fetal physiology; the **14-day rule** in the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 (s 3(3)(a), as amended in 2008, per Romanis); limits of science; and people will always have sex and become pregnant.

### 2. Pregnancy, gestation and birth (the materials; Romanis 2025)
- Treating pregnancy and gestation as interchangeable is a **category mistake**: pregnancy is a *state of being*; gestation is a *process* (Romanis and Adkins). Ectogestation = gestation without pregnancy; gestational trophoblastic disease or late fetal loss = pregnancy without gestation.
- Gestation (Romanis 2025): a series of events by which a conceptus becomes an entity with mature fetal physiology able to withstand the external environment.
- **Birthing and being born are distinct**: birthing is giving birth; being born is an emergence. NIC requires the neonate to carry some of the burden of sustaining itself (gas-based breathing); an artificial placenta continues fetal development ex utero.
- **Kingma and Finn (2020) 34 Bioethics 354**: fetuses and neonates differ physiologically and physically, not just in location; birth is a physiological, mereological and topological transformation as well as a change of location. Neonatal incubation supports **neonatal** physiology; ectogestation **preserves fetal** physiology. The 2017 system is genuine ectogestation, not improved incubation - but "artificial womb" is a term "better put to rest". Ethical discussion must always consider risks to and the rights of the pregnant person.

### 3. The born alive rule (England and Wales)
- **Legal personality starts at birth**: *Paton v British Pregnancy Advisory Service Trustees* [1979] QB 276 - a fetus has no rights until born with an existence separate from its mother (Sir George Baker P). The rule protects pregnant people's rights: *Re F (In Utero)* [1988] Fam 122 (cited as Fam 22 in Romanis 2020) refused to make a fetus a ward of court. Historically it also served as an evidential presumption in criminal cases.
- **Test** (the materials, Romanis 2020): **complete birth** (*R v Crutchley*; also *R v Poulton* (1832) 5 Car & P 328 - wholly come forth from the mother's body) **and born alive**.
- **"Alive"**:
  - *C v S* [1987] 1 All ER 1230 / [1987] 1 QB 135: "alive" should be read consistently with the Births and Deaths Registration Act (birth registrable if the child breathes or shows any sign of life); breathing "either naturally or with the aid of a ventilator".
  - *Rance v Mid-Downs Health Authority* [1991] 1 QB 587: a child breathing and living through its own lungs alone, not through any connection with its mother.
  - *R v Enoch* (1833) 5 Car & P 539: there must be independent circulation.
  - *R v Brain* (1834) 6 Car & P 350 (cited by Romanis on breathing and life).
- **Is the gestateling born alive?** Location (ex utero) raises less difficulty than "alive": legal evidence of life is built on the traditional newborn; the gestateling does not breathe; the primitive signs of life it shows are those of a fetus in utero; it is permanently non-self-sufficient and lacks "natality" (the materials; Romanis 2020).
- **Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929**: an offence to destroy a fetus "capable of being born alive" - so the meaning of "born alive" also affects abortion (see [artificial-placentas-and-abortion.md](artificial-placentas-and-abortion.md)).

## Key authorities
- *Paton v BPAS* [1979] QB 276 - no fetal personality before birth.
- *Re F (In Utero)* [1988] Fam 122 - no wardship of a fetus; protects pregnant woman's autonomy.
- *R v Crutchley*; *R v Poulton* (1832) - complete birth.
- *C v S* [1987] - "alive"/breathing, including with a ventilator; considered evidence on an 18-week fetus.
- *Rance v Mid-Downs HA* [1991] 1 QB 587 - breathing through own lungs.
- *R v Enoch* (1833); *R v Brain* (1834) - independent circulation/life.
- *Re MB (An adult: medical treatment)* [1997] EWCA Civ 3093 - cited by Romanis on the pregnant woman's autonomy.
- Births and Deaths Registration Act 1953 (as cited on the slide; *C v S* refers to the 1926 Act).
- Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929 s 1; HFE Act 1990 (14-day rule).

## Debate and critique
- **Romanis, "Artificial womb technology and the frontiers of human reproduction" (2018) 44 JME 751**: AWT is conceptually different from NIC, not an extension of it - it continues gestation; one reason is that the subjects are different. Potential uses beyond critical care.
- **Romanis, "...why gestatelings are not newborns (or fetuses)" (2019) 45 JME 728**: the gestateling is a unique human entity; moral status should not be treated as automatically decisive in AW debates.
- **Colgrove, "Subjects of ectogenesis: are 'gestatelings' fetuses, newborns or neither?" (2019) 45 JME 723**: subjects of partial ectogenesis *are* newborns (relying on definitions of "live birth" used by the WHO, EU, US law, the medical community - complete expulsion or extraction from the mother); subjects of complete ectogenesis share newborns' moral status; so gestatelings deserve newborns' protections, including research restrictions. In his reply ("Artificial wombs, birth and 'birth'", 2020) he answers Romanis's four objections (misconstruing birth; implying non-implanted embryos are "born"; failing to engage with distinctions; neglecting pregnant people).
- **Kingma, "In defence of gestatelings: response to Colgrove" (2021) 47 JME 355**: Colgrove equivocates between two meanings of "newborn" (a recognised infant with full protections v any entity extracted alive); the status questions stay open, and we should keep using "gestateling".
- **Romanis, "Challenging the 'Born Alive' Threshold" (2020) 28 Med L Rev 93**: the bright line no longer distinguishes the beings it was meant to; fetal surgery and AWT create entities novel to law. Options: (a) treat gestatelings as born alive - lowering the bar for personhood with knock-on effects for abortion; (b) exceptions; (c) a **"third status"** - legal existence before legal personality, a "partially born" category (as fetuses, IVF embryos and animals receive some protection without personhood; cf Hammack - personhood is too blunt). Concerns: precedent for different categories of human being. Also, a change to "born alive" would change the ILPA viability standard.
- **Why status matters** (the materials, slide 17-19): without personality, is the gestateling vulnerable to exploitation (e.g. research)? How would status affect people connected to it, especially pregnant people? Implications for experimental use, abortion, reproductive choice, parenthood and sex equality.
- **Clinical translation**: is first use "innovative treatment" or "medical research"? (Romanis 2020, Bioethics - cited in the materials).
- **Pregnancy loss**: Romanis and Adkins (2024) 50 JME 299 - artificial placentas make visible the difference between gestation and pregnancy; relevant to loss-sensitive care where a pregnancy ends but gestation continues.

## Applying it to a problem question
Seminar scenario: Ali, pregnant at 24 weeks with complications, is advised that premature delivery is needed - what is the status of an entity transferred to an artificial placenta?
1. **Describe the entity accurately**: partial ectogestation; gestateling (fetal physiology ex utero), not a neonate in an incubator.
2. **Complete birth?** It has been extracted from the body - likely satisfies separation.
3. **Born alive?** Apply *C v S*, *Rance*, *Enoch*: no breathing via lungs; fetal signs of life only - arguably not "alive" in law, so no personality.
4. **Consequences**: no civil/criminal protection as a person (e.g. homicide); but possibly protected by ILPA 1929/OAPA 1861 by analogy? Consider gaps (exploitation, research). Who decides about its treatment - the person from whom it was extracted?
5. **Reform arguments**: treat as newborn (Colgrove), third status (Romanis), or leave it to bespoke regulation.
6. **Keep the pregnant person central** (Kingma and Finn; Romanis): any change to the entity's status affects pregnant people's autonomy (*Re F*, *Re MB*).
Trap: calling it a "baby" or "artificial womb" uncritically - terminology carries normative weight.
