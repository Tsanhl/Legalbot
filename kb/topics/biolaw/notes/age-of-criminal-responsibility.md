# Developing Brains and the Minimum Age of Criminal Responsibility (MACR)

## Overview
In England and Wales a child aged 10 can be held criminally responsible on the same basis as an adult. The question is whether that fits what neuroscience shows about adolescent brain development (slow maturation of the prefrontal cortex, impulse-led behaviour) and the law's own premise that liability needs capacity to understand and choose. This is the clearest example of neuroscience being used to argue for legal reform, and also of the limits of doing so. It links children's rights (UN CRC), comparative law (Scotland, the Netherlands, the US) and the abolition of *doli incapax*.

## The law
1. **The MACR in England and Wales is 10**: Children and Young Persons Act 1963 (raising it from 8). History (Delmage; Arthur):
   - Laws of Ine (late 7th/8th century): children under 10 treated as lacking responsibility (Delmage: 10-year-olds could be held responsible).
   - Bracton (13th century): children lacked mens rea - "innocence of purpose".
   - Children Act 1908: MACR of 7.
   - Children and Young Persons Act 1933 s 50: MACR of 8.
   - Children and Young Persons Act 1963: raised to 10.
   - Below the MACR a child cannot be arrested or charged; above it they are presumed mature enough to stand trial and as accountable as adults (POST note).
2. **Doli incapax** - a rebuttable presumption that children aged 10-14 cannot tell right from wrong. To convict, the prosecution had to prove beyond reasonable doubt that the child knew the act was *seriously wrong*, not merely naughty or mischievous (Arthur). Commentary dates it as a defence from the 14th century, settled by *R v Smith* (1845).
   - *C (A Minor) v DPP* [1994] 3 All ER 190 (DC): Laws J held the presumption "no longer part of the law of England" because universal education meant children mature earlier. **Overruled** by the House of Lords, [1995] 1 AC 1: Lord Lowry accepted the presumption was illogical and criticised, Lord Jauncey called it "an affront to common sense", but abolition was for Parliament, not judges. Lord Lowry noted that better education does not guarantee children distinguish right from wrong.
   - After the killing of James Bulger (1993), the Home Office White Paper *No More Excuses* (Cm 3809, 1997) called doli incapax "contrary to common sense".
   - **Crime and Disorder Act 1998 s 34** abolished the presumption.
3. **Underlying principle of responsibility**: criminal responsibility requires actus reus, mens rea and capacity - understanding right and wrong and consequences, and autonomous choice. Law Commission, *Murder, Manslaughter and Infanticide* (Law Com No 304, 2006): if a person cannot choose between right and wrong, or cannot act on that knowledge, it is unfair to hold them criminally responsible. Ashworth (2003): liability only for those aware enough of what they do and its consequences to have chosen it. *R v G and R* [2003]: ignoring the special position of children "is not acceptable in a modern civil society".
4. **The Law Commission** recommended that "developmental immaturity" be included in diminished responsibility; this was **not** adopted in the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 (Arthur).
5. **International standards**: UN Committee on the Rights of the Child, General Comment No 10 (2007) CRC/C/GC/10 para 32: a MACR below 12 is "not to be internationally acceptable"; states should raise it to 12 as an absolute minimum and keep going (14 or 16 suggested, para 33). The 2018 draft **General Comment No 24** para 33 encourages at least 14 (Brown and Charles). The UN CRC has repeatedly criticised the UK (POST note).
6. **Comparative**:
   - **Scotland**: MACR raised from 8 to 12 by the Age of Criminal Responsibility (Scotland) Act 2019; the Edinburgh Study of Youth Transitions and Crime supports raising it to at least 15 (Scottish lessons paper).
   - **Netherlands**: since 1 April 2014 the adolescent criminal law lets judges sentence 18- to 22-year-olds (i.e. before turning 23) under the milder juvenile provisions (arts 77g-77hh Criminal Code) if the offender's personality or the circumstances justify it; juvenile law otherwise covers 12-17. The parliamentary justification expressly relied on brain development research (Kamerstuk 33 498 Nr 3) (Schleim). Note: Commentary summarises this as "MACR 18, 18-23 at judge's discretion"; Schleim's paper explains the 23 figure means up to a maximum of 22, and the juvenile system starts at 12. Neuroevidence in Dutch courts is always accompanied by behavioural assessment.
   - **United States**: *Roper v Simmons* (2005) - capital punishment unconstitutional for under-18s, relying on adolescent brain immaturity; *Graham v Florida* (2010) - life without parole unconstitutional for non-homicide juvenile offenders (a child's actions "not as morally reprehensible" as an adult's); *Miller v Alabama* (2012) - extended to homicide (mandatory life without parole). Many US states have no MACR; some set it as low as 7 (Brown and Charles).
   - Royal Society 2011: MACR 18 in Belgium, 16 in Spain, 15 in the Nordic states, 14 in Italy and Germany. England and Wales has the lowest MACR in Europe (POST).

## Key authorities
- Children and Young Persons Act 1963 - MACR 10.
- Crime and Disorder Act 1998 s 34 - abolished doli incapax.
- *C (A Minor) v DPP* [1995] 1 AC 1 (HL) - doli incapax survived until Parliament acted; Laws J's abolition overruled.
- *R v Smith* (1845) - established doli incapax.
- *R v G and R* [2003] - special position of children cannot be ignored.
- *R (P, G and W) v Secretary of State for the Home Department* (UKSC 2019) - recognised long-term harm of contact with the criminal justice system (Brown and Charles; not a MACR case).
- *Roper v Simmons*, 543 US 551 (2005); *Graham v Florida* (2010); *Miller v Alabama* (2012).
- UN CRC General Comment No 10 (2007) para 32; draft General Comment No 24 (2018) para 33.
- Age of Criminal Responsibility (Scotland) Act 2019.
- Dutch adolescent criminal law (2014).
- Dicta: A T H Smith (1994) 53 CLJ 426: a person is "completely irresponsible on the day before his tenth birthday and fully responsible" once the birthday "jelly and ice-cream have been cleared" away. Elliott (2011) 75 J Crim L 289: over-10s face "the full brunt of the criminal law" with no defence reflecting their status as children.

## The neuroscience
- **Imbalance model** (Royal Society): the amygdala (emotion, reward, instinct) develops fast in early adolescence; the frontal lobe/prefrontal cortex (judgement, decision-making, impulse control) matures slowly. The gap explains impulse-led, risky adolescent behaviour.
- White and grey matter growth: erratic signalling until properly distributed.
- Executive function and judgement mature in adulthood; abstract thinking (guilt, awareness of consequences) emerges mid-teens. Piaget: formal reasoning begins around 11.
- Royal Society: neural circuits keep developing to at least 20; huge individual variability, which could imply individual assessment; early adversity alters brain development.
- POST note: 10-year-olds are immature in moral and brain development; children in the system have high rates of learning disabilities (23-32%), communication difficulties (60-90%) and neurodevelopmental disorders, raising doubts about effective participation; criminalising children makes reoffending more likely.
- Delmage: most neuroimaging shows associations, not causation; civil responsibilities generally start at 16.

## Debate and critique
- **Inconsistency with civil ages**: under-18s cannot vote, sit on juries, buy alcohol, get tattoos; under-16s cannot leave school, consent to sex or buy lottery tickets - yet over-10s face adult criminal standards (POST; Delmage).
- **Does one fixed MACR make sense?** Schleim: law uses fixed thresholds while neuroscience finds a continuum, so any age looks arbitrary; studies variously suggest maturation is nearly complete by 16 (Casey et al) or continues to 30 (Paus et al), so an independent normative argument is still needed. Schleim also finds a mismatch between the cited studies and the Dutch justification, and raises knock-on effects for contract and medical consent.
- **Limits of neuroevidence**: association is not causation; little is known; ecological validity (lab brain v real brain); different rates of development; oversimplification and overreach of expert testimony that may sway juries; trials occur long after the offence, when the brain has changed. Morse: biological findings must be translated into legal criteria (capacity for rationality and self-control) - see [neuroscience-and-neurolaw-key-concepts.md](neuroscience-and-neurolaw-key-concepts.md).
- **Holistic approach** (Brown and Charles): MACR reform should not be an isolated legal change but part of rights-respecting interventions and diversion; Wales's child-support and diversion schemes could model this; the abolition of doli incapax "made childhood irrelevant to criminalisation" (Bandalli) and increased cautions/convictions of 10-14-year-olds by 29% in 1999 (Bateman). Westminster refuses reform.
- **Arthur**: the 2010 conviction of two 10-year-olds for attempted rape shows the problem; questions the assumption that children mature earlier; lower MACRs correlate with larger youth custody populations; compares Scotland, Ireland, Guernsey; Bandalli and Fionda ("almost stubborn blindness towards the incapacity of children") criticise s 34.
- **Arguments for keeping 10**: accountability and public protection (POST note); political "punitive narratives" (Scottish paper shows these are unfounded - fears of "soft options" driving up offending did not materialise).
- **Reform options**: raise the MACR (14 or 15 as a compromise); a new defence for ages 15-21; require proof the defendant probably knew the conduct was wrong (a return to doli incapax); neuroevidence of underdevelopment. Does this support calls for cognitive rights?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Age at the time of the offence**: under 10 - no liability; 10+ - full liability (no doli incapax since 1998).
2. **Capacity questions still available**: fitness to plead (effective participation), diminished responsibility for murder (developmental immaturity was not added), sentencing mitigation for youth and immaturity.
3. **Use of neuroevidence**: is it group-level or individual? Is it corroborated by behavioural/psychological assessment (Dutch model)? Is it reliable, and does it map onto a legal criterion?
4. **Rights**: UN CRC standards; special protection for minors' consent to scanning and data (scenario 4: brain monitoring of young offenders - prediction v potential for change; consent capacity; long-term data use).
5. **Policy evaluation**: compare Scotland (12, evidence for 15), Netherlands (extended juvenile sentencing to 22), US (Roper line), UN CRC (12 minimum, 14 proposed).
Traps: saying doli incapax still exists in England and Wales; overstating neuroscience as proving incapacity; ignoring individual variability.
