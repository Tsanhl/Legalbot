# Reading Minds: Neuroimaging, Lie Detection and Neuroevidence in Court

## Overview
This topic examines technologies that claim to "read minds" - prospectively (intentions, plans) or retrospectively (memories, whether someone is lying). The main tools are functional MRI (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG, including "brain fingerprinting" and BEOS profiling). Key questions: can such evidence meet the rules of admissibility; how does it differ from the polygraph; and what does compelled or covert brain-reading mean for the right to silence, the privilege against self-incrimination, privacy and mental integrity? Contrast this with the established, routine use of neuroimaging as medical evidence (brain injury, capacity), which English courts already accept.

## The law

### 1. Existing uses of neuroimaging as evidence (practical neurolaw)
- Catley and Claydon (2015) Journal of Law and the Biosciences 510 found **204 reported cases in England and Wales (2005-2012)** where defendants used neuroscientific evidence - about 1% of Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) cases, rising from 15 cases in 2005 to 45 in 2010. Uses: quashing convictions, convictions for lesser offences (especially diminished responsibility reducing murder to manslaughter), reduced sentences, resisting extradition, challenging bail, resisting unduly lenient sentence appeals, challenging cause of death/injury, attacking witness credibility, unfitness to plead, lack of mens rea.
- Examples: *Carr v Thomas* [2008] EWHC 2859 (testamentary capacity); *R v Mohammed Sharif* [2010] EWCA Crim 1709 (unfitness to plead); *Meah v McCreamer* [1985] 1 All ER 367 (personality change after brain damage); *R v Halling* [2012] EWCA Crim 1774 (brain injury); *R v X (Childhood amnesia)* [2005] EWCA Crim 1828 (reliability of testimony); *State v Hoskins* (2007), *State v Nelson* (2010) (US mitigation).

### 2. Lie detection today - the polygraph
- Measures heart rate, blood pressure, galvanic skin response, breathing. Its proxies are physiological, not neural, and of low accuracy (Ienca and Andorno).
- England and Wales do not use lie detectors pre-trial or at trial; polygraphs are used post-conviction (Johnston). **Offender Management Act 2007**: polygraph participation may be a licence condition for released sexual offenders, but statements made in the examination may not be used in criminal proceedings. Commentary adds trials under the **Domestic Abuse Act 2021** for released offenders and mandatory polygraphs under the **Counter-Terrorism Act 2021** for prior terror offenders; FoI requests show police use.
- Schauer: courts generally exclude polygraph evidence for three reasons - inaccuracy, countermeasures that can fool the machine, and (probably dominant) fear that jurors will overvalue it. *United States v Scheffer* (1998): an absolute ban on polygraph evidence, even for a defendant, is not unconstitutional; the "aura of infallibility" may lead jurors to abandon their duty to assess credibility.

### 3. fMRI and EEG cases
- ***United States v Semrau*, 693 F.3d 510 (6th Cir 2012)** (district court/magistrate report: 2010 WL 6845092). Dr Semrau, charged with healthcare (Medicare) fraud, sought to call Dr Steven Laken of Cephos Corp, who said fMRI scans showed Semrau was "generally truthful" in denying intent to defraud. Three scans: not deceptive, then deceptive (Semrau was fatigued; Laken's own report noted a positive result in someone claiming truth was accurate only 6% of the time), then not deceptive on a re-run. First fMRI lie detection admissibility ruling in any US jurisdiction.
  - **Held (Rule 702 / Daubert)**: exclusion not an abuse of discretion - technique not fully tested in "real world" settings; the test given differed from research protocols; unknown real-world error rates; no controlling standards; not generally accepted. Daubert factors: testability; peer review and publication; known or potential error rate and standards; general acceptance.
  - **Held (Rule 403)**: independently inadmissible - test obtained unilaterally without the prosecution's knowledge (Semrau risked nothing, as a failure would never be disclosed); using lie detection solely to bolster credibility is "highly prejudicial"; results showed only overall truthfulness, not truth on any specific statement.
- ***Wilson v Corestaff Services LP***, 28 Misc 3d 425, 900 NYS 2d 639 (NY Sup Ct 2010): workplace sexual harassment (civil). Party wanted fMRI evidence of a witness's truthfulness ("high probability" of truth). Inadmissible under the **Frye** standard; credibility is for the jury, and expert testimony must be beyond the ken of the average juror.
- **India - BEOS (Brain Electrical Oscillation Signature profiling)**: *State of Maharashtra v Sharma* (S.C. No 508/2007; Pune, 12 June 2008) - murder by poisoning; BEOS said to show "experiential knowledge"; convicted, life imprisonment; EEG evidence "supplementary". *Maharashtra v Bhoi* (S.C. No 422/2007) - recognition of murder weapon and circumstances; convicted; EEG "corroborative". BEOS accepted as corroborative under s 45 Indian Evidence Act. Later, in *Smt Selvi v State of Karnataka*, the Indian Supreme Court held brain mapping and polygraph techniques unconstitutional when compelled, rejecting the argument that results were "physical evidence" like blood - they resemble oral admissions (Johnston).
- **US EEG (P300 "brain fingerprinting")**: *Slaughter v State of Oklahoma*, 105 P.3d 832 (2005) - defence evidence that he did not recognise crime-scene images rejected: no real evidence it had been extensively tested; convicted. *Harrington v State of Iowa* PCCV 073247 (2001) - P300 showed no recognition of the scene but recognition of alibi details; accepted ("consensus ... that the P300 effect is valid") but ultimately "moot".

### 4. Rights engaged
- **Privilege against self-incrimination / right to silence**: *Blunt v Park Lane Hotel Ltd* [1942] 2 KB 253 - no one is bound to answer a question tending to expose them to a criminal charge. Not explicit in the ECHR but at the heart of a fair procedure under art 6 (Johnston). Not absolute: *Murray v UK* (1996) - no art 6(1) breach where adverse inferences were drawn from silence (defendant found in a house with an IRA kidnap victim); *Saunders v UK* (1997) 23 EHRR 313 (Johnston). PACE 1984 s 78 exclusion (*Newell*, per Johnston).
- **US Fifth Amendment**: Farahany criticises the *Schmerber* "physical v testimonial" dichotomy and proposes a spectrum: identifying, automatic, memorialised, uttered. Deception detection would produce "memorialised" evidence; brain structure "automatic" (Kraft and Giordano).
- **Privacy**: ECHR art 8 - mind reading as "surveillance of your internal life"; *Gillan and Quinton v UK* raises proportionality (Johnston). The brain is both source and subject of the information.
- **Cognitive liberty** (Bublitz 2013) and **mental integrity** (EU Charter art 3) - see [neurorights.md](neurorights.md).

## Key authorities
- *United States v Semrau* 693 F.3d 510 (6th Cir 2012) - fMRI excluded under Rules 702 and 403.
- *Daubert v Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals* 509 US 579 (1993) - judge's gatekeeping role; four factors; superseded *Frye*'s "general acceptance" as an absolute requirement (but acceptance remains relevant).
- *Wilson v Corestaff* (NY 2010) - Frye; credibility for jury.
- *United States v Scheffer* 523 US 303 (1998) - polygraph exclusion constitutional.
- *Maharashtra v Sharma*; *Maharashtra v Bhoi* - BEOS as supplementary/corroborative.
- *Selvi v State of Karnataka* (India SC) - compelled techniques unconstitutional.
- *Slaughter v Oklahoma* (2005); *Harrington v Iowa* (2001) - P300 evidence.
- *Blunt v Park Lane Hotel* [1942]; *Murray v UK*; *Saunders v UK* - self-incrimination.
- Offender Management Act 2007 - post-conviction polygraph for sex offenders.

## The science
- Neuroimaging (MRI/EEG/MEG/PET) aggregates signals from hundreds of thousands of neurons per data point.
- fMRI measures the BOLD signal in voxels. Nishimoto and Gallant reconstructed viewed video from brain activity; Shen et al (2019) deep image reconstruction; Takagi and Nishimoto (2023) used latent diffusion models; Tang et al (2023) semantically reconstructed continuous language from non-invasive recordings (paraphrases, not exact words).
- Detection of intentions up to about 1000 ms before action - raising "pre-crime" worries.
- fMRI lie detection: some areas are more active when formulating deception, but which matter? A sincere but mistaken belief would not show as a lie.
- EEG P300 wave indicates recognition - mixed results.
- Brown (2024): no device can decode spontaneous inner dialogue; fMRI has temporal and spatial limits and brains are non-uniform.

## Debate and critique
- **Reliability**: lab studies use young healthy volunteers told to lie, often with small incentives; real-world defendants are different (Semrau's "real world" point). Countermeasures. Memory is often inaccurate and coloured by hypotheses and prior memories; errors of inference.
- **Jury overvaluation / "neuroexuberance"**: images may seem more objective than they are; credibility is the jury's role (Wilson; Scheffer; Seaman: an accurate lie detector would call into question the jury's role).
- **Schauer** (Lie-Detection, Neuroscience, and the Law of Evidence): admissibility is not a purely scientific question; how accurate is "accurate enough" depends on the use. Evidence 80% reliable cannot convict but might create reasonable doubt for a defendant - so the live controversy concerns defence use. Courts already rely on unreliable demeanour-based credibility judgments; the right comparison is with existing methods ("compared to what?"). Neither scientists nor lawyers alone should decide.
- **Johnston**: England and Wales should resist using lie detection at pre-trial or trial stages; it would erode the privilege against self-incrimination and right to silence; but neuroscience has a role if parameters are set.
- **Kraft and Giordano**: law must not misconstrue the capabilities of technology; brain scans may reveal more than the legal question (incidental findings like tumours); classification of brain evidence under self-incrimination law is unsatisfying.
- **Is fMRI different from the polygraph?** It targets neural processes directly rather than proxies (Ienca and Andorno), which raises sharper privacy concerns; but legal problems (reliability, prejudice, credibility) are the same (Semrau magistrate: concerns are the same whatever the technology).
- **Thoughts are not acts**: the Naomi Oni case (acid attack victim suspected of self-infliction from browsing history) warns against inferring guilt from mental data; intention detection raises preventive detention concerns. UN Advisory Committee warns of "criminalization of mere thought".
- **Wolpe**: the skull should be a zone of absolute privacy, even against court orders; others accept limited use for serious crime.

## Applying it to a problem question
Scenario 1 (parliamentary committee on piloting neuroimaging in criminal investigations) is the model.
1. **What is the technology and its purpose?** fMRI/EEG; deception, recognition, or intention? Prosecution or defence use? Investigation, trial or post-conviction?
2. **Admissibility**: in England and Wales, expert evidence must be reliable and assist the tribunal; draw on US standards (Daubert factors, Frye general acceptance, Rule 403 prejudice) as persuasive frameworks; consider PACE s 78 fairness.
3. **Reliability problems**: real-world validation, error rates, protocol deviation, fatigue, countermeasures, sincere false beliefs, ecological validity, need for corroboration.
4. **Rights**: compelled use - privilege against self-incrimination (art 6; *Murray*, *Saunders* exceptions); privacy (art 8 proportionality); mental integrity; voluntariness in custody (consent under pressure); storage and later use of brain data ([mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md](mental-privacy-and-neural-data.md)).
5. **Jury influence** and the jury's role in credibility.
6. **Recommendation**: e.g. no use at investigation/trial (Johnston); limited defence use; strict standards; or new cognitive rights.
Traps: confusing established medical neuroimaging (admitted routinely) with deception detection (generally rejected); citing Semrau as a 2010 decision only - the appellate ruling is 2012; claiming fMRI can literally read thoughts.
