# The UK Contracts for Difference (CfD) Scheme

## Overview
The CfD is the UK government's main mechanism for supporting large-scale low-carbon (mainly renewable) electricity, and the syllabus names it as the central example for Topic 4. It is a **floating premium** scheme allocated through **auctions**: successful generators sign a private-law contract with the government-owned Low Carbon Contracts Company (LCCC), which pays them the difference between a **strike price** and a **market reference price** for 15 years - and requires them to pay back when market prices exceed the strike price. the materials assesses its success (rapid growth and falling prices for offshore wind) against the investment risks it leaves or creates (availability, allocation, route to market, repayment, undeliverability).

## The law

### 1. Legal and institutional framework
- Introduced as part of **Electricity Market Reform (EMR)**, legislated by the **Energy Act 2013** (royal assent December 2013) to create a long-term, stable and predictable market and revenue certainty; EMR's two mechanisms are the Capacity Market and CfDs (Watson and Bolton, House of Commons Library briefing 2024; Kozlov).
- Support from **October 2014**; ran alongside the **Renewables Obligation** until end March 2017; the RO closed to new large-scale renewables from **April 2017** (the materials).
- Applies to **Great Britain**.
- **LCCC**: private company owned by DESNZ; counterparty to the contracts; issues and manages them and makes payments. **National Grid ESO** is the delivery body running allocation; **Ofgem** hears certain appeals (Watson and Bolton).
- Pre-auction: "investment contracts" in 2014 (Final Investment Decision Enabling for Renewables - biomass and offshore wind, 4.6 GW); a separate CfD for **Hinkley Point C** (nuclear; key terms 2013, final agreement 2016).

### 2. Allocation rounds
- Six auctions since **AR1 (March 2015)** to **AR6 (September 2024)**; gaps from 1 year (AR5 9/2023 - AR6 9/2024) to 2 years 10 months (AR3 9/2019 - AR4 7/2022).
- Each round has a budget: AR6 largest (£1.56bn); AR1 and AR5 over £1bn; AR2 £295m, AR3 £65m, AR4 £285m. Regulations require the budget to be published in advance, but the government chooses the amount (Watson and Bolton).
- **Funding pots** by technology maturity: established (onshore wind, solar; offshore wind in AR5; remote island onshore wind in AR5-AR6) v less established (tidal stream, floating offshore wind, fixed offshore wind up to AR3, remote island wind up to AR4); technology-specific pots (fixed offshore wind in AR4, AR6); minima/maxima (e.g. minimum £15m for tidal stream, maximum £185m for onshore wind and solar in AR6); budgets split by delivery year.

### 3. Auction bidding
- Government sets **administrative strike prices** (maximum it will accept per technology).
- Sealed bids state strike price and delivery year; ranked cheapest upwards per technology/delivery year; accepted until budget exhausted or administrative price reached; successful bidders receive the **highest strike price awarded** for that technology and year (a "reverse auction", pay-as-clear). Example: wind farm 1 bids £45/MWh, wind farm 2 £50/MWh, both win - both get £50.
- **Non-delivery disincentive**: developers failing to deliver are barred from the next round.

### 4. The contract and payments
- Contract states strike price and delivery date; the **15-year term** runs from the end of the **commissioning window**, whether or not the plant is operating. Kozlov: failure to reach the start date by the end of the target commissioning window (other than force majeure or network delays) proportionately shortens support; milestone requirement within a year of signing (e.g. 10% of project costs spent).
- **Funding**: a levy on licensed electricity suppliers paid to the LCCC; costs recouped through consumer bills - around £100 added to bills April 2019-December 2024 (the materials, citing Watson and Bolton).
- **Premium calculation**: market reference price (MRP) = average electricity price at the relevant time. If strike price > MRP, the generator receives the difference on top of sales revenue. If MRP > strike price, the generator pays the excess to suppliers via the LCCC (offset against consumer costs) - to improve political sustainability. Kozlov: strike prices are CPI-indexed; MRP is calculated differently for baseload (season-ahead) and intermittent (hourly day-ahead) generators; payments may be adjusted for a qualifying change in law.
- Strike prices are expressed in 2012 prices and uprated by CPI (e.g. £50 in 2012 prices is almost £70 in 2024-25). Aggregate payments went negative (generators paying back) from October 2021 during high power prices; cumulative net payments to August 2024 were £8.9bn (Watson and Bolton).

### 5. Achievements
- Support for projects totalling **43 GW** (9 GW operating by September 2024; 13 GW approaching operation).
- Average strike price fell from **£102/MWh (AR1)** to about **£41 (AR3, AR4)**, then £52 (AR5) and **£58 (AR6)** (2012 prices).
- Offshore wind: from **£120 (AR1)** to about **£40 (AR4)**, then about £58 in AR6 (the materials conclusion: £120 to £59); onshore wind £80 to £50; solar £65 to £48.

## Investment risks under the CfD (the materials)
1. **Availability risk**: not all technologies supported in every round (no solar/onshore wind in AR2-AR3); **the law sets no timetable for rounds**, creating uncertainty for project planners and supply chains; the 2024 Labour government's policy of annual rounds is **not legally binding**. Too-low administrative strike prices make the scheme unattractive - **AR5 got no offshore wind bids**.
2. **Allocation risk**: fear of losing, heightened by **participation costs** (planning permission requiring environmental impact assessment, grid connection agreements) - deters actors with limited assets; **transaction costs** of a complex process deter less established bidders. Kozlov: milestone pressure means only robust projects can apply.
3. **Route-to-market risk**: premiums are paid only on electricity sold, so the generator must find a buyer; higher than under the RO, where suppliers were obliged to buy renewables. The government legislated for an **Offtaker of Last Resort**, deliberately unattractive (buys at a discount); never used to date.
4. **Price risk**: typically **very low** - sales + premium lift revenue to the strike price, which is the bidder's own statement of what it needs. Residual risk where electricity is sold to aggregators below the market average used to calculate premiums.
5. **Repayment**: valuable for political sustainability, but may deter smaller investors (cash-flow fears) and larger investors who, under the RO, were motivated by high-price profits.
6. **Undeliverability**: pressure to "bid low" in hope the clearing price rises; non-delivery bar more likely to deter marginal bidders than cause failures. **Inflation and supply chain costs**: premiums are CPI-indexed but offshore wind costs rose faster - AR4 offshore projects were terminated (e.g. **Vattenfall's Norfolk Boreas**); AR5's administrative strike price was too low (industry: high inflation, cost of capital, supply chain issues - Cornwall Insight; ScottishPower called it "a multi-billion pound lost opportunity"). **AR6** responded by raising the offshore wind administrative strike price to **£73/MWh** (Watson and Bolton: £75) and allowing AR4 projects "permitted reduction" bids; the resulting £58.87 offshore price was still low because of competition and budget.
7. **Bankability** (Kozlov, 2014): ranking of the CfD Counterparty relative to lenders and offtakers on default may make lenders uneasy; early concerns about Levy Control Framework budgets and EU state aid approval.

## Key authorities
- Energy Act 2013 (EMR; CfD framework).
- CfD allocation regulations (budget publication; non-delivery disincentive) as described by Watson and Bolton.
- Renewables Obligation (closed to new large-scale generation April 2017).
- Offtaker of Last Resort provisions.
- LCCC; National Grid ESO; Ofgem.

## Debate and critique
- **Overall assessment** (the materials): low price risk if the strike price is high enough, but other risks heightened or introduced; tends to favour **large incumbents with established technologies** over new entrants and less established technologies (hence minima for newer technologies). Clear success in rapidly growing UK offshore wind and cutting its cost - but **how much is due to auctions and how much to learning by doing and economies of scale?**
- **Welisch and Poudineh**: uniform-price, one-shot auctions encourage speculative bids; stronger non-delivery penalties and regular annual rounds would improve outcomes.
- **Kozlov**: allocation, milestone and bankability risks; dependence on available budgets (Levy Control Framework).
- **Woolley**: regulatory risk generally; budget-limited competitive allocation can reduce the need for retroactive changes but creates availability and allocation risks. The absence of a statutory round timetable contrasts with the EU RES Directive's requirement to publish long-term support schedules (art 6(3)).
- **Context**: must be judged against the targets of 100% decarbonised electricity by 2030 and net zero by 2050; government ambition of 50 GW offshore wind by 2030 (Watson and Bolton).

## Applying it to a problem/briefing question
Typical task: evaluate whether the CfD is effective at attracting investment, or advise a developer/government.
1. **Technology and developer**: established (offshore wind, solar) or emerging (tidal, floating wind)? Incumbent or small/community developer?
2. **Availability**: is there a pot and budget for this technology in the next round? Is the round timetable legally fixed (no)?
3. **Allocation**: costs of qualifying (consents, EIA, grid); chance of success; administrative strike price adequacy given inflation and supply chain costs.
4. **Revenue**: strike price v costs; CPI indexation; aggregator discount; repayment when prices are high.
5. **Route to market**: power purchase agreements; Offtaker of Last Resort.
6. **Delivery**: commissioning window, milestones, non-delivery bar.
7. **Reform options**: legally binding annual rounds, inflation/supply-chain adjustments, ring-fenced pots, adjusted administrative prices, stronger non-delivery rules (Welisch and Poudineh), longer terms.
Trap: describing the CfD as a FIT - it is a floating premium with no purchase obligation (though Kozlov calls it a "Feed-in-Tariff Contracts for Difference", reflecting its original name).
