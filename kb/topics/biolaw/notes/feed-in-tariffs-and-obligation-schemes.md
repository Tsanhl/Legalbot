# Feed-in Tariffs and Obligation/Certificate Schemes

## Overview
the materials of Topic 4 compares two classic operating-support designs. **Feed-in tariffs (FITs)** guarantee purchase of renewable electricity at a fixed price for a fixed period - very attractive to investors, but costly and politically fragile. **Obligation/certificate schemes** (in the UK, the Renewables Obligation; in the US, renewable portfolio standards) require suppliers to source a proportion of renewables, proved by tradeable certificates - leaving generators exposed to two markets. Germany's EEG FIT and the UK's Renewables Obligation are the case studies. The analysis applies the risk framework in [renewable-energy-investment-and-subsidies.md](renewable-energy-investment-and-subsidies.md).

## The law: feed-in tariffs

### 1. Common legal design features (the materials)
- **Purchase obligation**: a sectoral actor (often the transmission system operator) is legally required to buy the electricity - guaranteed sale, so **no market exposure**.
- **Fixed price** for a fixed period, unchanged throughout - high investor confidence provided the government sets the price high enough.
- **Degression**: the price for new entrants falls periodically to reflect technology improvements and lower costs - reassures the public that subsidies are not too generous and encourages quick development to get higher rates.
- **Cost recovery**: the obligated actor sells the electricity and passes unrecovered costs to consumers (e.g. higher use-of-system charges).
- Popular with investors: no route-to-market risk (guaranteed sales) and no price risk (guaranteed cost recovery).

### 2. Remuneration models (Couture and Gagnon, "An analysis of feed-in tariff remuneration models" (Energy Policy))
Seven ways to structure FIT payments: four **market-independent** (fixed-price; fixed with full or partial **inflation adjustment** - e.g. Ireland full, Ontario 20% portion, Spain full minus basis points under Royal Decree 661/2007; **front-end loaded** tariffs paying more in early years to repay loans faster or to gather site data; and the **spot market gap** model) and three **market-dependent** (premium-type) models. Fixed-price, market-independent models create greater investment security and lower-cost deployment; premium models encourage generation when most needed and aid market integration but suit larger players because of transaction costs. Germany targeted a 7% return; Spain 5-11% by technology.

### 3. Claimed benefits
Stable long-term support and investor confidence; strong support for new technologies; encourages new entrants; no route-to-market risk; can produce rapid sector growth; useful where electricity markets are immature and in developing countries (easy to run once the rate is set); low transaction costs help inexperienced developers; arguably cheaper overall because developers borrow at lower interest rates.

### 4. Concerns for investors
- Unattractive if the price is too low; hard to set the right price for rapidly evolving technologies.
- **Popularity undermines sustainability**: growing costs on bills, perception of FITs causing high prices and "windfall profits"; politicians cut payments or withdraw schemes - **regulatory/political risk**. Examples: **Spain FIT scheme 2013** (and retroactive cuts from 2010 per Woolley); the **UK FIT** - popularity led to rate drops and withdrawal in **2019**.

### 5. Criticisms of the type
- Seen as expensive for consumers (contested - low borrowing costs may make renewables cheaper).
- Said to lack incentives for innovation and cost cutting (contested - profit motive suffices).
- Disliked by advocates of undistorted competition; EU concern about barriers to an EU-wide market. **State aid guidelines since 2014** call for FITs to be replaced by subsidies exposing recipients to market competition (top-ups on market sales).
- Linked to **negative market prices** when supply exceeds demand - response: remove subsidy entitlement when prices go below zero.

### 6. Case study: Germany's EEG FIT
- **Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) 2000**: guaranteed purchase at set prices for **20 years**; transmission system operators obliged to buy from connected plant; costs recouped by market sales and system charges passed to consumers.
- Renewables grew from **6.2% (2000) to 25.8% (2014)** of electricity.
- But rising subsidy costs, public opposition, and European Commission opposition to schemes shielding generators from competition (2014-2020 state aid guidelines) led to replacement by premium schemes (see [premium-schemes-and-auctions.md](premium-schemes-and-auctions.md)).
- Illustrates FIT effectiveness, problems of political sustainability, and long-term incompatibility with liberalised markets.

## The law: obligation/certificate schemes

### 1. Common legal design features
- Legal **obligation** on sectoral actors (generators, suppliers or consumers) to include a proportion of renewables in their portfolios.
- Compliance proved by submitting **certificates** (issued to renewable producers) to a nominated body.
- Producers have **two revenue streams**: electricity sales and certificate sales; certificates can be traded separately from the electricity.
- Compliance motivated by a **penalty** and by recycling penalty money - higher payouts to fully compliant parties.

### 2. Claimed benefits
Two revenue streams together may reduce perceived price risk; the obligation incentivises suppliers to buy renewable electricity, reducing route-to-market risk; growth limited by the size of the obligation and effectiveness of sanctions.

### 3. Concerns for investors
- **High price risk** - double market exposure; both revenues uncertain.
- Some route-to-market risk remains.
- **Transaction costs** of operating in two markets.
- Deters new entrants, small and community generators, and investment in newer, less cost-competitive technologies; promotes established low-cost technologies; prevents learning-by-doing for newer ones.

### 4. Mechanisms to address weaknesses
- **Banding**: more certificates per MWh for less established technologies (e.g. **2 per MWh for tidal**, **0.8 per MWh for onshore wind**).
- **Headroom**: annual obligation set above expected generation, keeping certificate demand and prices stable.

### 5. Appeal for incumbents
Market price plus certificate revenue can yield high profits when prices are high, especially for cheapest established technologies (onshore wind, solar PV); incumbents have specialist trading staff.

### 6. Case study: the UK Renewables Obligation (RO) Order
- Obliges electricity suppliers to source a specified proportion from renewables; discharged by presenting certificates to avoid a penalty; certificates issued to generators for renewable output; features **headroom** and **banding**.
- Gradually replaced by CfDs; closed to new (large-scale) generators from **2017** (closed to new large-scale RE from April 2017 per the materials) - see [contracts-for-difference.md](contracts-for-difference.md).
- Strengths/weaknesses: route-to-market risk reduced but present (suppliers failing to meet their full obligation); price risk; transaction costs; favours cheaper established technologies and incumbents.
- **Wood and Dow, "What lessons have been learned in reforming the Renewables Obligation?" (Energy Policy)**: UK policy since 1990 (the Non-Fossil Fuel Obligation - a centralised bidding system - then the RO) failed to meet targets: only 30% of NFFO projects were commissioned over 14 years; RO deployment rose but annual targets were missed. Government did not learn from the NFFO/RO transition; the reformed RO (with banding, extension to 2037) addressed many failures but price and financial risk remained; "bolt-on" reforms creating a FIT-like system showed loss of direction.

## Key authorities
- German Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) 2000.
- UK Renewables Obligation Order(s); closure to new generation in 2017.
- UK small-scale FIT scheme (withdrawn 2019).
- European Commission State Aid Guidelines 2014-2020.
- Directive 2009/28/EC (2020 targets; UK 15% of energy) - context in Wood and Dow.

## Debate and critique
- **Market exposure v protection** (the materials): market exposure is said to stimulate innovation and give lowest costs to consumers; the counterargument is that it deters investment, loses learning-by-doing and raises borrowing costs, producing higher-cost outcomes.
- **Couture and Gagnon**: FITs are the most effective policy for rapid, sustained deployment; design choices (inflation indexing, front-end loading) matter for investor risk.
- **Distributional effects**: FITs open the sector to new entrants and community energy; obligation schemes favour incumbents and established technologies.
- **Political economy**: FIT success breeds cost growth and backlash; obligation schemes avoid guaranteed prices but leave risk with generators - why the UK and EU moved to premiums and auctions.

## Applying it to a problem/briefing question
1. Identify the scheme type from its legal features (purchase obligation and fixed price = FIT; quota plus certificates = obligation scheme).
2. Assess each risk: price, route to market, availability, transaction costs, regulatory change.
3. Who is attracted? New entrants/community (FIT) or incumbents (RO)? Which technologies (banding)?
4. Political sustainability: cost to consumers, degression, negative price rules, windfall profit perceptions.
5. EU/state aid compatibility (FITs disfavoured since 2014) and post-Brexit UK context.
6. Recommend design changes (e.g. inflation indexing, banding, headroom, caps/budgets to avoid retroactive cuts).
