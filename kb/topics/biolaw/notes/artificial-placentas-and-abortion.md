# Artificial Placentas and Abortion

## Overview
Ethicists often describe artificial placentas as "the end of abortion": if a fetus can be removed alive and gestated ex utero, is there still a right to end a pregnancy in a way that causes fetal death? the materials of Topic 3 examines the criminal law framework of abortion in England and Wales, asks whether the technology would change legal access, considers whether a genetic progenitor could obtain an injunction to force "extraction", and reviews the moral debate on a "right to the death of the fetus". Romanis's conclusion: as the law stands, early abortion remains lawful notwithstanding the technology, but criminalisation makes access vulnerable - a reason to decriminalise.

## The law (England and Wales)

### 1. Abortion is a crime
- **Offences Against the Person Act 1861 s 58**: unlawfully procuring the miscarriage of a pregnant woman with intent - maximum life imprisonment. **s 59**: supplying drugs or instruments knowing they are intended to procure miscarriage.
- **Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929 s 1**: "child destruction" - intentionally causing the death before birth of a "child capable of being born alive"; per the materials, a fetus is presumed capable of being born alive at 24 weeks (the materials Parliament moved the threshold from 28 to 24 weeks in 1990).
- The OAPA and ILPA offences differ in when they can be committed and in their outcome.
- Northern Ireland: abortion partially decriminalised in 2019; before then a crime unless necessary to save the pregnant person's life. Great Britain (England, Wales, Scotland): still a crime with defences.

### 2. The Abortion Act 1967 defence - s 1(1)
A person is not guilty of an offence under the law relating to abortion when a pregnancy is terminated by a registered medical practitioner if **two** registered medical practitioners form the opinion **in good faith**:
- **(a)** the pregnancy has not exceeded its **24th week** and continuance would involve risk, greater than termination, of injury to the physical or mental health of the pregnant woman or any existing children of her family (the "social"/"socio-medical" ground - 98% of abortions in England and Wales in 2022, 247,440);
- **(b)** termination is necessary to prevent grave permanent injury to physical or mental health;
- **(c)** continuance would involve risk to life greater than termination;
- **(d)** substantial risk that, if born, the child would suffer such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped.
- (b)-(d) have no time limit. s 1(2): the woman's actual or reasonably foreseeable environment may be considered. s 1(3): place requirements (NHS hospital or approved place; approval may cover a class of places for medication abortion).
- Context: 251,277 abortions in England and Wales in 2022, 86% medical (DHSC 2024); early medical abortion = mifepristone + misoprostol; WHO recommends up to 12 weeks. The Act aimed at uniformity, clarity for doctors and ending harm from illegal abortions (Brazier and Cave).
- Romanis (ch 7): ground (a) is interpreted so broadly that effectively every pregnancy is legally terminable before 20 weeks, but people must still have medical permission and take medication at their home address or a licensed clinic; non-compliance risks prosecution.

### 3. Does the artificial placenta change legality?
- **Child destruction**: legality turns on "capable of being born alive". If a gestateling counts as born alive, the threshold drops. The concept is movable: Parliament lowered it from 28 to 24 weeks in 1990; in *C v S* [1987] 1 QB 135 the court considered evidence about an 18-week fetus. But the 1967 Act still provides a defence (the materials).
- **No duty to abort in a particular way**: s 1(1)(a) keeps early abortion accessible even with AAPT; the Act says nothing about method; method is a matter of patient preference among clinically reasonable alternatives (*Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11). The biggest threats are **clinical gatekeeping and conscientious objection**.
- **Viability**: after *Dobbs v Jackson* there is no US constitutional right to abortion. When *Roe*-era law applied, the right existed before viability; US viability meant independence from the pregnant person, while English law looks to capacity to be born and maintain some independent life. Romanis ("Is 'Viability' Viable?" (2020) JLB): viability is incoherent, fluid and unsupported by medical reality - a conceptually illegitimate basis for abortion regulation; if it must be used, English law's account is the more coherent. See also Halliday et al (2023) Med L Rev on the Netherlands and England and Wales.

### 4. Could a genetic progenitor stop an abortion?
- *Paton v BPAS* [1979] QB 276: a husband had no right enforceable in law or equity to be consulted - no injunction; a fetus has no rights until born. *Paton v UK* (1981) 3 EHRR 408: confirmed at Strasbourg. Baker P: a judge interfering with doctors' discretion under the 1967 Act would be "bold and foolish" save possibly in clear bad faith.
- *C v S* [1987/1988]: Mr Carver sought to prevent his former partner's abortion at 18-21 weeks, claiming it would be child destruction under ILPA; failed.
- **Romanis, "Abortion & 'artificial wombs'" (2021) 8 JLB**: some argue (Brassington 2009; Räsänen 2017) that with ectogestation abortion should require both progenitors' agreement. But a non-gestating progenitor still lacks **locus standi** - not a sufficiently proximate party, and nothing to "stand against" unless a crime is proved; standing would need amendment of the 1967 Act. Even with standing, an injunction would fail because of the pregnant person's **bodily and reproductive autonomy** and right not to become a parent. The disputes are gendered - men seeking to control women's bodies (Fox).
- **Seminar scenario** (Abi and Brian): Abi learns at 18 weeks she is pregnant and wants an abortion; Brian wants the fetus extracted for partial ectogestation and to raise the child. His chances of preventing the abortion are very low on *Paton*, *C v S* and Romanis's analysis.

## Key authorities
- OAPA 1861 ss 58-59; ILPA 1929 s 1; Abortion Act 1967 s 1(1)(a)-(d), (2), (3).
- *Paton v BPAS* [1979] QB 276; *Paton v UK* (1981) 3 EHRR 408; *C v S* [1987] 1 QB 135.
- *Montgomery v Lanarkshire HB* [2015] UKSC 11 - patient choice among reasonable alternatives.
- *Re F (In Utero)* [1988] Fam 122 - no order in respect of a fetus.
- *Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) - no US constitutional right.

## Debate and critique

### Is there a right to the death of the fetus?
- **Thomson (1971)** "A Defense of Abortion" (violinist): no one is morally required to make large sacrifices to keep another alive. But this grounds a right to *detach*, not necessarily to kill.
- **Singer and Wells (1984)**: freedom to choose what happens to one's body differs from freedom "to insist on the death of a being that is capable of living outside one's body"; a healthy fetus need not die if someone will adopt it.
- **Hendricks (2018)**: a right to detach does not entail a right to the death of the detached organism. Question: does the right to be detached include how and when?
- **Mathison and Davis (2017) 31 Bioethics 313**: there is a right to abortion but reasons to doubt a right to fetal death; they reject arguments based on a right not to be a biological mother, genetic privacy, and the fetus as property.
- **Räsänen (2017)**: genetic parents may have a *collective* right to fetal death.
- **Stratman (2020/2021) Philosophy & Technology**: no right to the death of a fetus that could be safely removed and gestated when competent people will raise it; claims transfer could be as or less invasive than lethal termination - the materials asks whether that fits the science.
- **Blackshaw and Rodger (2019)**: one may be obliged to undergo a procedure "significantly worse than abortion, but not substantially worse" to remove a fetus alive.
- **Simkulet (2020) 46 JME 93**: ectogenesis offers moral compromise; abortion opponents have a prima facie duty to pursue it and provide it for disconnected fetuses.
- **Kendal (2020) Monash Bioethics Review**: ectogenesis exposes flawed conceptions of the fetus and pregnancy in some pro-choice arguments, but need not affect existing abortion rights, viability definitions or moral status.

### Feminist and reproductive autonomy responses (the materials; Romanis; Horn)
- The arguments misdescribe reality: NICU-based viability already exists without giving access to an alternative; fetal extraction is a different procedure with different timing and outcome and social consequences.
- Duties to detach in a fetus-saving way treat people who can become pregnant as **"fetal containers"**, reinforcing self-sacrificial expectations attached to female and pregnant bodies.
- **Horn (2020)** "Ectogenesis is for Feminists": even if extraction were as easy as a pill, feminists would not accept artificial wombs as a forced alternative to abortion.
- **Reproductive autonomy**: very few seek abortion solely to avoid pregnancy and labour (**Jackson 2008**); control over whether one reproduces is central to identity and dignity (**Robertson 1994**); termination is the only conclusive way to avoid the social and legal consequences of reproducing. Handing "responsibility" to another progenitor is socially unrealistic for a "mother", and the comparison with killing a newborn is flawed because the entity does not yet exist as such.
- **Romanis and Horn (2020)**: the ectogenesis debate overstates technology, neglects immediate problems, treats abortion as a "problem with a technological solution" (bolstering anti-abortion rhetoric), and presumes the stability of reproductive rights. "Abortion is not a 'problem'."
- **Decriminalisation**: Romanis (building on Horn) argues decriminalisation is a necessity for a future with gestative technologies and reproductive justice; England and Wales is among the strictest regimes on paper in Europe; only Canada has fully decriminalised. Mesure (2023 thesis) argues artificial wombs and uterine transplants de-gender gestation and strengthen the case for decriminalisation, noting the rise in prosecutions in 2023.
- These debates are always about **partial** ectogestation (the materials).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Which offence?** Before 24 weeks: OAPA s 58 (and s 59 for suppliers); fetus "capable of being born alive": ILPA s 1.
2. **Is there a 1967 Act defence?** Two doctors, good faith, which ground; place and method rules.
3. **Effect of AAPT**: no legal requirement to choose extraction; *Montgomery* choice; only if ILPA "capable of being born alive" shifts would late terminations be affected - and s 1(1)(b)-(d) still apply.
4. **Third parties**: progenitor injunction - no locus standi (*Paton*, *C v S*, *Paton v UK*); no fetal rights (*Re F*); autonomy.
5. **Moral/policy analysis**: right to detach v right to fetal death; feminist critique; decriminalisation.
Traps: assuming the technology automatically makes abortion unlawful; forgetting the 24-week limit applies only to ground (a); describing the fetus as having rights before birth.
