# Overriding mandatory rules, illegality at the place of performance and public policy

## Overview
Party autonomy and the ordinary choice-of-law rules identify the law that governs a contract, but that law can be displaced. In England the displacement tools are: non-derogable rules in domestic or protected-party situations, the overriding mandatory provisions of Article 9 of Rome I (forum and place of performance), the public policy exception in Article 21, and the older common law rule from *Ralli Brothers* that English courts will not enforce a contract requiring an act that is illegal where it must be performed. the materials asks how these overlap and what survives after Brexit.

## The law
**1. The menu of displacement (seminar slide 24).**
- (a) Rules that cannot be derogated from by agreement: Art 3(3) (all other elements in one country other than that of the chosen law) and Art 3(4) (all elements in Member States; of less relevance after Brexit).
- (b) Special rules for particular contracts (consumer, individual employment, immovable property).
- (c) Overriding mandatory rules, Art 9.
- (d) Public policy of the forum, Art 21 ("manifestly incompatible with the public policy (ordre public) of the forum").

**2. Definition (Art 9(1)).** Overriding mandatory provisions are those whose respect is regarded as crucial by a country for safeguarding public interests such as its political, social or economic organisation, so that they apply to any situation within their scope irrespective of the law otherwise applicable. Briggs stresses that the definition is deliberately restrictive and narrower than rules that merely cannot be contracted out of.

**3. Mandatory rules of the forum (Art 9(2)).** Nothing in the Regulation restricts the forum's overriding mandatory provisions. They apply however the governing law was identified (party choice, closest connection, characteristic performance). Whether a statute is overriding, and what it does to the parties' choice, depends on its terms. In *The Hollandia* [1983] 1 AC 565 the Hague-Visby Rules, scheduled to the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1971 and given "the force of law", made the choice of Dutch law and jurisdiction void because it would reduce the carrier's liability below the Rules. Briggs gives as examples of English forum rules: legislation controlling exclusion of liability (Consumer Rights Act 2015, s 74), the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 provisions (ss 26-28) making agreements made through unauthorised persons unenforceable, and sanctions legislation.

**4. Mandatory rules of the place of performance (Art 9(3)).** Effect "may" be given to overriding mandatory provisions of the country where the obligations have to be or have been performed, in so far as they make performance unlawful. The court must consider their nature and purpose and the consequences of applying or not applying them. Points from the slides and Briggs:
- It is discretionary ("may"); the Regulation does not oblige a court to apply foreign overriding rules (Briggs cites *Hellenic Republic v Nikiforidis* on the point that a foreign state's rules are addressed to its own judges).
- "Have to be performed" means the place where performance was contractually required, not where it happened to occur; Briggs, citing the common law, says "required" rather than intended or expected.
- The foreign rule must make performance unlawful, not merely unenforceable or invalid.
- The slides suggest a court may be more ready to apply the rule where the parties meant to evade it and the case has a strong connection with the place of performance.

**5. Public policy (Art 21).** Unlike Art 9, Art 21 does not superimpose another system's rule; it blanks out the offending provision of the governing law, which then applies as if that rule did not exist (Briggs). Examples there: a rule validating a contract obtained by horrific duress (*Royal Boskalis Westminster NV v Mountain* [1999] QB 674, hostages used as human shields), or an unreasonable restraint of trade.

**6. The common law rule in *Ralli Brothers*.** As stated in *Ralli Bros v Compania Naviera Sota y Aznar* [1920] 2 KB 287 (CA), adopting Dicey: a contract, whether lawful by its proper law or not, is in general invalid in so far as performance is unlawful by the law of the country where the contract is to be performed. The rule has been extended:
- *Foster v Driscoll* [1929] 1 KB 470: the rule covers illegality existing when the contract was made, not only supervening illegality. A partnership to smuggle whisky into the US during Prohibition was unenforceable even though never carried out, because a court enforcing it would offend international comity.
- *Regazzoni v KC Sethia (1944) Ltd* [1958] AC 301 (HL): approved *Foster v Driscoll*; contract unenforceable where, to the parties' knowledge, performance required an act illegal in a friendly foreign state.

## Key authorities
- *Ralli Brothers v Compania Naviera Sota y Aznar* [1920] 2 KB 287 (CA): English charterparty (implied English law from London arbitration clause) for jute from Calcutta to Barcelona; freight (£50 per ton) was payable in Spain, where a Spanish decree later capped freight at 875 pesetas per ton. Charterers were excused from paying the excess. Scrutton LJ rested the rule on international comity. Passages also suggest an alternative explanation as an implied term of English contract law, close to frustration. Slides ask whether it is a true conflicts rule or domestic contract law.
- *Foster v Driscoll* [1929] 1 KB 470 (CA): English proper law; plan to ship whisky to the US in the Prohibition era; held unenforceable (Scrutton LJ dissented on the facts, not the law). Lawrence LJ: recognition would give the US just cause for complaint and offend international comity.
- *Regazzoni v KC Sethia (1944) Ltd* [1958] AC 301: English sale of jute bags, destined (known to both parties) for South Africa, which was illegal under Indian law as the place from which the jute had to be shipped. Unenforceable; contract legal on its face but its performance necessarily involved breach of Indian law. Viscount Simonds: public policy demands deference to international comity. The summary adds that the rule against enforcing foreign penal or revenue laws concerns suits by the foreign state, not private contracts involving them.
- *Isaphani v Bank Melli Iran* [1998] (as quoted in the slides): intended commission of prohibited acts in a friendly foreign state is an essential ingredient of the *Foster* principle.
- *The Hollandia* [1983] 1 AC 565: forum overriding rule.
- *Royal Boskalis v Mountain* [1999] QB 674: public policy and duress (Briggs).
- Related contract-validity cases covered in the same seminar: *Egon Oldendorff v Libera Corporation* (Mance J [1995]; Clarke J [1996]) on implied choice and Art 8(2) of the Rome Convention (see party-autonomy-express-implied-choice.md).

## Debate and critique
- **Source of the Ralli rule.** Is it a conflicts rule applying whatever the proper law, or a rule of English domestic contract law? Briggs says a view limiting non-enforcement to contracts governed by English law is "indefensible": judges should not order acts that are crimes in friendly foreign states.
- **Ralli versus Art 9(3).** The slides say Art 9(3) is narrower: the foreign rule must itself be an overriding mandatory provision, whereas *Ralli* is triggered by any prohibition at the place of performance. Art 9(3) is discretionary, but the *Ralli* rule must be applied if its requirements are met. Art 21 can produce a *Ralli*-like result by refusing a foreign governing law that would enforce the contract. Briggs thinks the retained Regulation could not direct a different conclusion in smuggling cases.
- **Remoteness of illegality.** *Foster* was a scheme whose entire purpose was breach; in *Regazzoni* the contract was lawful on its face and India was involved only because the jute came from there. The slides query whether the HL's attitude was convincing, given the contract might not have required any offence in India and the Indian actors might have lacked mens rea, but note that English courts take a "purposive" view.
- **Third-country laws.** Briggs notes that where the governing law itself chooses to treat a third state's prohibition as affecting performance, that is a matter for the governing law (Art 12); English domestic law has so far been unsympathetic (*Banco San Juan Internacional v PDVSA* [2020]; *Celestial Aviation v UniCredit* [2023]).
- **Overreach of Art 9(1).** Briggs: where Parliament has told judges to apply a statute regardless of conflicts rules, the strict wording of Art 9(1) hardly matters; it is more an exhortation than a hard test.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the governing law first (Art 3 choice or Art 4). Then ask whether anything displaces it.
2. Is there an English statute that is meant to apply regardless of the governing law (consumer protection, financial services authorisation, sanctions, carriage of goods rules)? If so, Art 9(2) and the statute's own terms control.
3. Is performance due in a foreign state whose law makes it illegal? Apply *Ralli*/*Foster*/*Regazzoni* (performance must be unlawful there, the parties' knowledge or intention matters, the state must be friendly), and consider Art 9(3) as an alternative: is the foreign rule overriding, does it make performance unlawful, and does the court wish to give effect?
4. If a foreign governing law produces an intolerable result, consider Art 21.
5. Check Art 3(3) if every other element lies in one country.
6. Typical traps: confusing "unlawful" with "unenforceable"; forgetting "have to be performed" is about contractual place of performance; treating Art 9(3) as mandatory; overlooking that mere knowledge of a buyer's illegal plan is not enough without active participation in the adventure (per the materials summary).
