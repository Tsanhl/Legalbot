# Jurisdiction agreements at common law

## Overview
A jurisdiction (choice of court) agreement is a contractual promise about where disputes will be litigated. In an English court the first question is always whether the **2005 Hague Convention** applies: it covers exclusive agreements for Contracting States' courts in civil or commercial matters not excluded by Art 2. If it does not apply, for example because the clause is non-exclusive or asymmetric, or chooses a non-Contracting State's court, or concerns an excluded matter, the **common law** governs. At common law a jurisdiction agreement does not create or remove jurisdiction. It gives the court a reason to exercise its discretion:
- to stay English proceedings brought in breach of a foreign clause ("strong cause" needed to resist);
- to let a claimant serve out without permission for an English clause;
- to restrain foreign proceedings by anti-suit injunction;
- possibly to award damages for breach.

Everything turns on interpretation, especially whether the clause is exclusive. *Hin-Pro v CSAV* is the module's main case study.

## The law

### 1. Effects at common law (outside Hague)
**Agreement for the English court**:
- Service in: English proceedings are most unlikely to be stayed.
- Service out: permission not required (CPR 6.33(2B)(b)–(c)).
- It may found an **anti-suit injunction** against a party suing abroad in breach ("a legal right not to be sued" elsewhere; see anti-suit-injunctions.md).
- It may found a claim for **damages** for breach of contract if loss is proved.

**Agreement for a foreign court**:
- It does **not** oust or deny the English court's jurisdiction, which still rests on service. It gives the defendant an enhanced expectation of a **stay**.
- Briggs: the application is for a stay, not to set service aside. It differs from forum non conveniens; "forum non consentiens" will do as a label. If the claimant is shown to be breaking her contract, a stay will be ordered unless she shows "jolly good reasons", citing *Donohue v Armco Inc* [2001] UKHL 64. That is an uphill task (Briggs Ch 2 pp 71–75).
- the materials slide 23: the claimant bears a heavy burden. "**Strong cause**" is needed to overcome the presumption that the clause will be enforced by a stay.
- **Possible strong causes** (Briggs): England is the natural forum *and* there are additional powerful reasons. In particular, non-parties to the agreement may be genuinely involved, so that enforcing it would fragment litigation (*Bouygues Offshore v Caspian Shipping* [1998] 2 Lloyd's Rep 461 (CA); *Donohue v Armco*). A choice of court agreement does not "give instructions" to the court. It provides data for the court's duty to administer justice properly, so it may yield to the greater good (*AIG Europe SA v John Wood Group plc* [2022] EWCA Civ 781, [63]). See also *The El Amria* [1981] 2 Lloyd's Rep 119 (CA).
- **Damages despite refusal of a stay**: Briggs argues that if the action proceeds in breach, nothing obviously prevents a counterclaim for damages for any demonstrable loss. Refusing a stay only refuses specific enforcement.
- **Arbitration agreements** differ. Under Arbitration Act 1996 s 9 a stay is mandatory, with no discretion (Briggs n 92).

### 2. Nature and terminology
- A jurisdiction agreement is a contractual promise between the parties. Is it different in kind from, say, a sale contract? Can damages be recovered for its breach? At common law it cannot by itself finally determine the chosen court's jurisdiction.
- Ultimate question: **what was the substance of the agreement the parties made?** (exclusive or non-exclusive; one-sided; scope).

### 3. Substantive validity and separability
1. The agreement is treated as valid even if a party has grounds to challenge the main contract. It is **severable** from the contract.
2. The agreement can be attacked on the ground that it was never made or agreed to, for example in a battle of forms. But rescission of the main contract for fraud, misrepresentation, non-disclosure, duress or undue influence does not affect the dispute-resolution agreement.
3. *Fiona Trust & Holding Corp v Privalov* [2007] UKHL 40, [2007] Bus LR 1719 (slides 17–19). Russian-group owners alleged that eight charterparties, each with an English arbitration clause, were induced by bribery and purported to rescind them. The HL held:
   - (a) clauses are construed on the assumption that rational businessmen intend all disputes arising from their relationship to go to the same tribunal, unless the language clearly excludes certain questions (the "one-stop" presumption);
   - (b) under separability (s 7 Arbitration Act 1996), the arbitration agreement can be challenged only on grounds relating directly to it;
   - (c) the bribery allegations went to the charters, not specifically to the arbitration clause, so they were for the arbitrators.

   Principle carried over to jurisdiction agreements: only allegations **specific to the jurisdiction agreement itself** can undermine it.

### 4. Interpretation
1. **Governing law**: interpretation follows the law that English private international law treats as applicable to the agreement. For a term in a larger contract, that is very likely the law of the whole contract. A freestanding agreement has its own governing law. For contracts made after 17 December 2009, the governing law usually comes from Rome I. Rome I does not claim to govern choice of court agreements (they are excluded), but common law refers the question to the law of the contract containing the clause. The common law principle is that the governing law applies to the whole contract unless there are very clear reasons otherwise.
2. No distinction is drawn between an expressly chosen and an objectively identified governing law. There was never any suggestion that the law of the chosen court governs the clause's meaning (slide 23).
3. **Contrast arbitration**: *Enka Insaat Ve Sanayi AS v OOO Insurance Company Chubb* [2020] UKSC 38. Absent a choice for the arbitration agreement, it was governed by the law of closest connection, generally the law of the chosen seat. Now see **s 6A Arbitration Act 1996** (2025): the law of the seat unless the parties expressly agree otherwise (slide 23).
4. **Foreign-law clauses**: interpretation of a term governed by foreign law is in principle a matter for expert evidence (*Bank Paribas SA v Trattamento Rifiuti Metropolitani SpA* [2018] EWCA Civ 768). In *Deutsche Bank AG v Comune di Savona* [2018] EWCA Civ 1740, the issue was whether advice-reliance claims fell under an English clause in swap agreements or an Italian clause in a separate advisory agreement. Foreign law evidence was relevant only to show any **different principles of construction**. It was not a contest over how the foreign supreme court would decide. The English court then construes both clauses itself. Default rule and presumption of similarity apply (*Brownlie*) (slides 21–22).
5. **Ample and broad construction under English law**: a claim framed in tort or equity is not outside "all disputes arising under this contract". But clauses are aimed at enforcing the contract's mutual obligations, and absent express words do not cover, for example, disputes from a party's insolvency or the enforcement of judgments (slide 24).
6. **Incorporation** of a clause from contract 1 into contract 2 is in principle for the law governing contract 2. **Assignees and subrogated insurers** can exercise rights only within the original jurisdiction agreement, because it is inequitable to take the benefit without the agreed enforcement framework (slide 25). Briggs: the assignee cannot "pick out the plums and leave the duff".

### 5. Exclusive or non-exclusive?
- Decided by the law governing the agreement, not necessarily the lex fori. If it is foreign and not proved, English rules apply by default.
- A simple exclusive / non-exclusive dichotomy is sufficient. The material question: **is bringing the proceedings complained of contrary to what was promised?**
- There is **no presumption** of exclusivity. Commercial common sense matters. The word "exclusive" is not decisive: substance over form.
- Briggs: wording like "the parties submit to the jurisdiction of the courts of X" is harder to construe, which defeats the aim of certainty. It may still be a breach of a *non-exclusive* clause to sue or continue outside the nominated court (*Sabah Shipyard (Pakistan) Ltd v Pakistan* [2002] EWCA Civ 1643).
- **Asymmetric clauses** (one party confined, the other free) fall outside Hague 2005 and are left to the common law.

## Key authorities
- *Hin-Pro International Logistics Ltd v Compania Sud Americana de Vapores SA* [2015] EWCA Civ 401.
  - Clause 23 of CSAV's bills of lading: "This Bill of Lading and any claim or dispute arising hereunder shall be subject to English law and the jurisdiction of the English High Court of Justice in London. If, notwithstanding the foregoing, any proceedings are commenced in another jurisdiction, such proceeding shall be referred to ordinary courts of law..." (plus a Chile sentence agreed to be void under Chilean law).
  - The CA held it **exclusive**:
    - (1) "shall be subject to" is imperative and directory, and makes English law mandatory, so presumptively English jurisdiction too. It is stronger than *Svendborg*'s "subject to English law and jurisdiction", and is "transitive": the parties submit their disputes to the English court;
    - (2) the natural commercial purpose: an optional English forum with no obligation gives little benefit;
    - (3) it makes sense for English law and English courts to go together;
    - (4) "notwithstanding the foregoing" in sentence two recognises that sentence one requires English litigation as a matter of contract;
    - (5) sentences two and three deal with cases where sentence one is ineffective, e.g. Hamburg Rules countries (such as Chile) or states, such as China, that do not recognise exclusive clauses;
    - (6) contra proferentem is of limited help in commercial contracts, and it is unclear which party exclusivity favours;
    - (7) English authority tends to read such "law and jurisdiction" clauses as exclusive.
  - Interpretation draws on facts known or reasonably available to the original parties. The parties are taken to have agreed that the English-language, English-law contract will be read as a native English speaker would read it. It was a standard term in a carrier's form between trading companies, not consumers.
- *Import Export Metro Ltd v CSAV* [2003] EWHC 11 (Comm), [2003] 1 Lloyd's Rep 11. An identical clause. *CSAV* argued it was non-exclusive in order to stay English proceedings in favour of Chile. The Commercial Court held it **non-exclusive**, reading sentence two as recognising that some jurisdictions (e.g. Hamburg Rules states) might disregard the English clause. But it refused a stay because CSAV failed to show England was forum non conveniens. The contrast with *Hin-Pro* (where CSAV argued exclusivity) is striking.
- *Fiona Trust v Privalov* [2007] UKHL 40: one-stop construction and separability.
- *Donohue v Armco* [2001] UKHL 64: strong reasons needed to escape a clause; multi-party fragmentation as a possible reason.
- *Deutsche Bank v Comune di Savona* [2018] EWCA Civ 1740: competing clauses and the role of foreign law evidence.
- *Enka v Chubb* [2020] UKSC 38; Arbitration Act 1996 s 6A (2025): law of the arbitration agreement.
- CPR 6.33(2B): service out without permission.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the *Hin-Pro* interpretation convincing?**. The clause's second sentence ("If … any proceedings are commenced in another jurisdiction, such proceeding shall be referred to ordinary courts of law") arguably *contemplates* proceedings elsewhere. That supports the Chinese courts' and *Import Export Metro*'s non-exclusive reading. The CA treated it as a fallback for jurisdictions that disregard exclusivity. Two English courts read the same words differently. Is that certainty?
- **Party autonomy versus the court's control**: at common law the clause is "data" for the court rather than an instruction (Briggs). It is therefore weaker than under Hague 2005, where the chosen court must hear the case and others must decline. Is the "strong cause" discretion a defensible safety valve (multi-party litigation, non-parties) or an erosion of party autonomy?
- **Damages for breach**: should a claimant who sued abroad in breach pay the other side's costs and losses? The *Hin-Pro* litigation included damages awards to CSAV. Critics see this as indirectly penalising the use of foreign courts that took jurisdiction lawfully under their own law (see jurisdiction-conflicts-and-comity.md).
- **Governing law of the clause**: English law refers it to the main contract's law. Arbitration now refers to the seat's law (s 6A). Is the divergence justified?
- **Foreign-law evidence at the jurisdiction stage** (*Savona*): limiting it to principles of construction keeps hearings proportionate, but risks an English-flavoured reading of a foreign clause.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Hague 2005 first**: international case, exclusive clause for a Contracting State, concluded after the Convention's entry into force for that state, civil or commercial matter, not excluded by Art 2 (see hague-choice-of-court-convention-2005.md). If it applies: the chosen English court must hear (Art 5), or a non-chosen English court must suspend or dismiss (Art 6, subject to exceptions).
2. **Otherwise common law**. Identify the governing law of the clause (usually the main contract's law under Rome I or the common law). Consider proof of foreign law or the default rule.
3. **Validity**: is there an agreement at all (battle of forms, authority)? Separability: only attacks specific to the clause count (*Fiona Trust*).
4. **Scope**: construe widely (*Fiona Trust*). Does it cover tort or equity claims, or competing clauses (*Savona*)? Insolvency or enforcement disputes? Is the litigant bound (assignee, insurer)?
5. **Exclusivity**: does suing here or abroad breach a promise? Use the *Hin-Pro* factors. Contrast *Import Export Metro*.
6. **Remedy**:
   - English clause, foreign suit: anti-suit injunction (*Angelic Grace* principles) and damages.
   - Foreign clause, English suit: stay unless strong cause (*Donohue*; non-parties; natural forum plus powerful reasons).
   - Non-exclusive foreign clause: ordinary *Spiliada*, with the clause as a factor.
7. **Trap**: treating a foreign clause as depriving the English court of jurisdiction. At common law it only grounds a stay.
