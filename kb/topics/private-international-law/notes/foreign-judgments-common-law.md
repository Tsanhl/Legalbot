# Recognition and enforcement of foreign judgments at common law

## Overview
A judgment is an exercise of sovereign power and normally has no effect outside the territory of the court that gave it. It takes effect in England only if English law agrees to recognise it, and if it orders payment, to enforce it. After Brexit the Brussels/Lugano regime covers only judgments in proceedings begun before Completion Day. Most foreign judgments, including those from EU states, now fall under:
- the **common law** (available for judgments from any country);
- the statutory registration schemes (AJA 1920, FJ(RE)A 1933, CJJA 1982);
- the Hague 2005 and 2019 Conventions.

At common law, a foreign judgment is not executed directly. It creates an obligation that founds a fresh English action. It is recognised only if the foreign court had "international jurisdiction" in the English sense, through the defendant's presence or submission, and if no defence applies. The merits cannot be reopened.

## The law

### 1. Why recognise foreign judgments?
1. **Comity**: vague. Is it courtesy or obligation? It depends on cooperation and goodwill between countries.
2. **Reciprocity**: give a foreign judgment only the effect that the foreign country gives English judgments. It encourages mutual recognition, but its effectiveness is criticised. It punishes individuals where no reciprocal relationship exists, and creates disputes about what the foreign attitude is.
3. **Vested rights and obligations**: the judgment creates rights under foreign law that should be enforced because rights duly created deserve enforcement, not out of respect for foreign power. Enforcement and its consequences remain governed by the enforcing court's law. This shifts the focus from sovereignty to individual rights.
4. **Briggs: the doctrine of obligation** (*Godard v Gray* (1870–71) LR 6 QB 139; *Schibsby v Westenholz* (1870) LR 6 QB 155). A recognisable foreign judgment creates "a tie of law" binding the parties. It arises from the bilateral relationship the litigants form before or when the foreign proceedings begin, through presence or submission. That explains why the common law looks at the parties' relationship with the court, not at the cause of action's connection with it. Briggs firmly rejects recent criticism that the doctrine "adds nothing" (*Rubin v Eurofinance SA* [2012] UKSC 46) or fails to explain when the obligation arises (*Merck Sharp & Dohme v Merck KGaA* [2021] SGCA 14). Statutory schemes rest on parliamentary sovereignty instead.

### 2. The schemes (slides 23–24)
These schemes are cumulative, not mutually exclusive:
- (a) **Brussels I / Lugano**: for judgments of Member State or Lugano courts in proceedings begun before Completion Day. Recognition is "straightforward and almost automatic" and hard to derail. Under SI 2019/479 the key question is whether the foreign court was seised before Completion Day.
- (b) **Common law**: recognition as res judicata and enforcement by action on the judgment debt, for judgments from any country.
- (c) **Registration** under the Administration of Justice Act 1920 (many Commonwealth territories) and the Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933 (bilateral treaty-based). Both are derived from the common law. A registered judgment is executed as if it were English.
- (d) **Registration under CJJA 1982** for judgments from the rest of the UK and Gibraltar, and for Hague 2005 judgments.
- (e) **Hague 2019 Judgments Convention**: in force for the UK from 1 July 2025, for proceedings begun on or after that date (see hague-judgments-convention-2019-and-international-commercial-courts.md).

Which scheme applies depends on the identity of the court, the nature of the claim and the date proceedings began. The common law (and statutes derived from it) gives the judgment debtor a wider range of objections than the European schemes, especially lack of international jurisdiction (slide 24).

### 3. Requirements at common law (slides 25–27)
1. A foreign judgment cannot itself be executed in England. Its principal function is to **furnish a cause of action** in English proceedings, leading to an English judgment that is then enforced.
2. The rules apply to final judgments from all courts anywhere, without limits of geography or subject matter, unless legislation has removed the common law route.
3. **Final and conclusive**:
   - *final*: it cannot be reopened by the court that gave it, even if appealable to a higher court;
   - *conclusive*: it is the court's settled answer on the point.
4. For **enforcement** as a debt: a judgment for a **fixed sum of money**. The creditor sues on the debt created by the judgment.
5. **The foreign body must be a court**. Judgments of a foreign sovereign's court where the defendant was present are recognised because of comity and territoriality. English law decides for itself whether the body is a court, which separates judicial judgments from other ways of ending disputes (slide 28).
6. **International jurisdiction**, as English private international law defines it (slide 27).

### 4. International jurisdiction in personam (slides 29–32)
- **Presence**: the defendant was physically present in the foreign territory when proceedings were instituted. Briggs: probably when process was served (*Adams v Cape Industries plc* [1990] Ch 433 (CA)). It means actual presence, not constructive or deemed presence.
  - The rule's purpose is to let a reasonably informed defendant decide quickly whether to appear and defend abroad.
  - Briggs explains it through comity and territorial sovereignty, or more mundanely as an "open offer" by being present.
  - The courts have refused to recast it as residence (*Rubin*; *Vizcaya Partners Ltd v Picard* [2016] UKPC 5). Briggs says a residence test would be "a calamity" because residence lacks sharp edges.
  - **Companies**: present only if they have a reasonably fixed and definite place of business maintained by the company, from which its business is done, or a representative carrying on the company's business from a fixed place for a sufficient period.
    - A CEO on the golf course, a travelling salesman in a motel or a trade-fair stand do not count.
    - A mere conduit for customers is not enough (cf *Littauer Glove*).
    - A local representative empowered to make binding contracts from a fixed place probably is enough (*Adams v Cape* at 531).
    - There is no group-presence or alter ego doctrine without lifting the veil (*Adams v Cape* at 532–39; *Prest v Petrodel* [2013] UKSC 34).
    - A website has not yet been held enough.
    - Briggs: a looser test would make London risky for parent companies.
- **Submission**:
  - *By agreement*: did the parties agree that the court should have jurisdiction to decide the question it decided? If a clause chooses a country's courts, whether it covers all or only some of them is construed under the contract's law, as at the jurisdiction stage (slide 30).
  - *By voluntary appearance*: did the defendant take a step showing an intention to contest the merits, read against the foreign procedural law (slide 31)? **CJJA 1982 s 33(1)**: appearing only to contest jurisdiction, to seek a stay in favour of another court or arbitration, or to protect property threatened with seizure, is **not** submission. Briggs: if s 33(1) excludes it, "that is the end of the argument".
  - A claimant who sued abroad necessarily submitted, for example to a counterclaim judgment.
- **Nothing else counts** (slide 32): nationality or domicile; the cause of action arising there; that England would have taken jurisdiction on similar facts; that the foreign court was the natural forum. The rationale is a clear rule, so foreign defendants can predict whether a judgment will be recognised.

### 5. Judgments in rem (slide 33)
A foreign court has jurisdiction in rem if the res was within its territory when proceedings began. A judgment in rem determines title to a thing, the status of a person, or a company's legal existence. It is the **order** that must be examined to see whether it takes effect in personam or in rem.

### 6. Defences (slides 34–39)
If the foreign court was competent, the losing party is in principle bound. The argument that the foreign court got the facts or the law wrong is **inadmissible** (Briggs: otherwise there would be relitigation, not recognition). The seven defences:
1. **Not final and conclusive** on the issue.
2. **Judgment contrary to a valid dispute-resolution agreement**: CJJA 1982 s 32. A judgment that, in English law's view, violated an applicable choice of court or arbitration agreement will not be recognised, even if the foreign court considered the point and found no breach under its own law. Only a party who did not agree to the foreign proceedings can rely on it, which excludes the claimant.
3. **Fraud** by the successful party.
4. **Breach of natural (or perhaps substantial) justice**.
5. **Public policy**:
   - Recognition reflects a policy in favour of finality: a debtor who had a fair chance to contest liability should be bound. The defence applies only where public policy against the particular judgment outweighs that.
   - Examples: conflict with the **"one voice"** principle (conclusive executive statements, such as who is recognised as a foreign head of state); judgments or causes of action that appear to be products of bribery or corruption; judgments based on an agreement to break a friendly state's laws in a fundamental way.
6. **Human Rights Act 1998**: if recognition would infringe Art 6 ECHR (fair trial), the judgment is not recognised. This needs careful reflection, since it risks imposing ECHR standards on non-Convention states.
7. **Inconsistency with a prior judgment**: an inconsistent English judgment, or an earlier inconsistent recognisable foreign judgment between the same parties. The critical timing appears to be the date of judgment, not of the proceedings.

### 7. Effects of recognition and non-recognition (slides 40–41)
- The **creditor** may sue in England on the obligation or debt, serving in the usual way, or out of the jurisdiction with permission (CPR r 6.36) for common law enforcement. Registration under the 1920 and 1933 Acts follows separate arrangements.
- The creditor **cannot** sue instead on the original cause of action, which is superseded.
- A **successful defendant** may rely on the judgment as an **estoppel** against fresh proceedings or relitigation of decided issues.
- If recognition is refused, for example because the defendant was not present and did not submit, the judgment has **no effect** in the English legal order.

### 8. Judgments against states (slide 42)
CJJA 1982 s 31: a foreign judgment against a foreign state is enforced only if:
- (a) it would be recognised if not against a state; and
- (b) the foreign court would have had jurisdiction under rules corresponding to ss 2–11 State Immunity Act 1978.

Issues include whether the state submitted, was "present", whether an entity or building is the state or an organ of it, and whether the proceedings related to a commercial transaction.

## Key authorities
- *Godard v Gray* (1870–71) LR 6 QB 139; *Schibsby v Westenholz* (1870) LR 6 QB 155: the doctrine of obligation.
- *Adams v Cape Industries plc* [1990] Ch 433 (CA): presence of individuals (at service) and companies (fixed place of business; no group presence).
- *Rubin v Eurofinance SA* [2012] UKSC 46: cited by Briggs as making plain that the courts may not reformulate the presence rule in terms of residence. It is also the source of criticism of the doctrine of obligation.
- *Vizcaya Partners v Picard* [2016] UKPC 5: same point.
- *Prest v Petrodel* [2013] UKSC 34: veil-lifting is rare.
- CJJA 1982 ss 31, 32, 33(1); AJA 1920; FJ(RE)A 1933; State Immunity Act 1978 ss 2–11; HRA 1998.

## Debate and critique
- **Presence versus connection**: the common law ignores the dispute's connection with the foreign court, so a judgment from the natural forum may be refused if the defendant was neither present nor submitted. Fleeting presence, on the other hand, suffices. Briggs defends this through obligation and predictability. Critics see it as arbitrary and out of line with the connection-based Hague 2019 Art 5 filters.
- **Rubin and judicial conservatism**: the Supreme Court and Privy Council have said it is not open to the courts to reformulate the bases, for example in terms of residence. Change is for Parliament, for example by joining treaties such as Hague 2019.
- **Section 32 and comity**: England refuses a foreign judgment obtained in breach of a dispute-resolution clause *as English law construes it*, even where the foreign court honestly reached a different view. Compare Hong Kong's Cap 46 and Fok J's hypothetical in jurisdiction-conflicts-and-comity.md. Is that respect for party autonomy or unilateralism?
- **HRA and foreign justice**: measuring non-ECHR states against Art 6 may look parochial. But recognition by an English court is itself an act engaging the HRA.
- **Brexit**: the move from almost automatic Brussels recognition to the common law plus Hague instruments increases the debtor's defences and uncertainty for EU–UK judgments. The 2019 Convention (from 1 July 2025) partly fills the gap.
- **Reciprocity as policy**: the 1920 and 1933 Acts depend on reciprocal extension. Critics say reciprocity punishes private litigants for their states' choices (slide 21).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Which scheme?** Identify the court of origin, the subject matter, and the date proceedings began:
   - EU or Lugano court seised before Completion Day: Brussels or Lugano;
   - Hague 2005 exclusive clause, or Hague 2019 (proceedings from 1 July 2025, Contracting State);
   - AJA 1920 or FJ(RE)A 1933 country: registration;
   - otherwise common law.

   Remember the schemes are cumulative.
2. **Common law checklist**:
   - (a) Is it a court's judgment?
   - (b) Final and conclusive?
   - (c) For a fixed sum, if enforcement is sought?
   - (d) International jurisdiction: was the defendant present at service (and for companies, a fixed place of business, *Adams v Cape*)? Or did it submit, by clause (construe it) or by appearance (CJJA s 33(1) exceptions)?
3. **Defences**: breach of a jurisdiction or arbitration clause (s 32), fraud, natural justice, public policy, HRA Art 6, prior inconsistent judgment. Reject "wrong on the merits" arguments.
4. **Remedy**: action on the judgment debt (service out under CPR 6.36 if needed), or registration. The original cause of action is merged. Consider estoppel uses.
5. **State defendant**: s 31 CJJA and the State Immunity Act.
6. **Traps**: treating domicile or the natural forum as enough; treating appearance to contest jurisdiction as submission; forgetting that a non-recognised judgment has no effect at all.
