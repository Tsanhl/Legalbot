# Freezing (Mareva) injunctions in cross-border litigation

## Overview
Money can now move across borders almost instantly. A judgment on the merits is worth little if the defendant has moved its assets beyond reach. The English freezing injunction, formerly the Mareva injunction, is an interim order made *in personam* that restrains the respondent from disposing of or dealing with its assets. It can be:
- **domestic** or **worldwide** (covering assets abroad), where England hears the merits;
- granted **in support of foreign proceedings** (CJJA 1982 s 25), where England does not hear the merits.

It is a powerful, sometimes "nuclear", weapon. In the module it appears in the *CSAV v Hin-Pro* saga, where a worldwide freezing order supported an anti-suit injunction. Šaranović argues the current tests are too claimant-friendly.

## The law

### 1. Origins and power
1. Before 1975 there was no effective English procedure for seizing a defendant's assets before judgment. The general rule was that a creditor cannot restrain a debtor dealing with its property before judgment (*Lister & Co v Stubbs* (1890) 45 Ch D 1, cited by Šaranović) (Dicey 10-009).
2. In 1975 the Court of Appeal, each time with Lord Denning MR presiding, granted such injunctions against foreign defendants: *Nippon Yusen Kaisha v Karageorgis* [1975] 1 WLR 1093 and *Mareva Compania Naviera SA v International Bulkcarriers SA* [1975] 2 Lloyd's Rep 509 (Dicey 10-010). Canada, Australia, New Zealand and other Commonwealth jurisdictions followed.
3. **Senior Courts Act 1981 s 37(3)**: the power to grant an interlocutory injunction restraining a party from removing assets from the jurisdiction, or otherwise dealing with assets located within it, is exercisable whether or not that party is domiciled, resident or present within the jurisdiction. Section 37(3) did not make the remedy statutory. It assumed and tacitly endorsed it (*Mercedes Benz AG v Leiduck* [1996] AC 284, 299 (PC)), and does not inhibit judicial extension (*Babanaft International Co SA v Bassatne* [1990] Ch 13, 26 (CA)) (Dicey 10-011; slide 30).

### 2. Nature (Dicey 10-012 to 10-014; slide 29)
- An interlocutory injunction restraining the defendant from disposing of or dealing with its assets. Strictly, it concerns assets in which the claimant claims **no proprietary interest**. Proprietary or tracing claims are different. There the injunction may attach to a specific fund, and the usual business or legal-fees proviso does not apply in principle, though the court has discretion.
- It operates **in personam**, not as an attachment. Lord Denning had suggested it operated in rem, but that view did not prevail. It gives **no priority** over third parties' security interests and no security against other creditors.
- The defendant may normally pay creditors in the ordinary and proper course of business. The order must not become an instrument of oppression that stops ordinary trading.
- The claimant almost always gives **undertakings** (a cross-undertaking in damages) protecting the defendant if the order should not have been made.

### 3. Requirements (Dicey 10-013, 10-015; slide 31)
1. At least a **good arguable case** on the merits.
2. A **real risk that a judgment would be unsatisfied** without the order, because of **unjustified dissipation** of assets.
3. Underlying principle: within its powers, no court should let a defendant act so as to make its later orders less effective.
4. The cause of action need not yet have accrued, and the claimant need not have a substantive cause of action against the respondent. *Broad Idea International Ltd v Convoy Collateral Ltd* (PC) endorsed Lord Nicholls's dissent in *Mercedes Benz v Leiduck* and disapproved contrary CA decisions. What matters is that a right to sue will arise, and that proceedings, domestic or foreign, will be brought in which the applicant has a good arguable case for a money judgment enforceable against the frozen assets.

### 4. Making it effective (Dicey 10-016 to 10-017)
- **Ancillary orders**: disclosure of assets; even an order restraining the defendant from leaving the jurisdiction and requiring delivery up of a passport.
- **Third parties**: a third party, such as a bank, that knowingly assists a breach commits contempt, whether or not the defendant has been served, because it interferes with the administration of justice.

### 5. International dimensions (Dicey 10R-007, 10-018)
**Rule 27(1): worldwide freezing injunctions (WFOs)**. Where the court has jurisdiction over the substance, it may **exceptionally** freeze assets outside England, before judgment or in aid of execution, with ancillary relief. Provisos:
- the order should not normally affect anyone outside England other than the respondent;
- it should not normally stop a third party complying with its obligations about the property under the applicable foreign law.

The WFO is "entirely a creature of English judicial development".

**Rule 27(2): interim relief in support of foreign proceedings**. Where England does not hear the substance, it may grant a freezing injunction or other interim relief in support of proceedings pending or contemplated abroad, especially where the respondent or its assets are in England. It may refuse where that is **inexpedient**, considering possible conflict with the foreign court hearing the merits.
- This power had to be conferred by Parliament. It was initially done to meet European obligations and later extended.
- **CJJA 1982 s 25**: s 25(1) allows interim relief where (a) proceedings have been or are to be commenced in a state party to the Hague Choice of Court Convention and (b) they are within its scope. Under s 25(3) the power has been extended and is now exercisable in relation to **any country** (slide 32).
- **Defendant abroad**: gateway CPR PD 6B para 3.1(5) (a claim for an interim remedy under s 25). CPR 6.37(3) still requires England to be the proper place (slide 32).
- Hague 2005 Art 7: interim measures are outside that Convention, which neither requires nor precludes them.

### 6. In the *CSAV v Hin-Pro* litigation
After Hin-Pro sued in Chinese maritime courts in breach of what the English court held to be an exclusive English jurisdiction clause:
- the English court granted an anti-suit injunction;
- it granted a **Mareva injunction freezing Hin-Pro's worldwide assets in aid of enforcing the ASI**;
- it ordered three months' imprisonment for contempt against Hin-Pro's director;
- it awarded CSAV damages for the costs of the foreign proceedings.

In Hong Kong, CSAV sought a Mareva over Hin-Pro's Hong Kong assets and a receiver. The lower courts refused, seeing it as intervening in an Anglo-PRC conflict. The **HKCFA** ([2016] HKCFA 79) granted it. It held that assisting enforcement of an English damages award for breach of an exclusive clause involved no breach of comity or public policy (see jurisdiction-conflicts-and-comity.md).

## Key authorities
- *Nippon Yusen Kaisha v Karageorgis* [1975] 1 WLR 1093 (CA) and *Mareva v International Bulkcarriers* [1975] 2 Lloyd's Rep 509 (CA): origins.
- *Lister & Co v Stubbs* (1890) 45 Ch D 1: the general rule against pre-judgment restraint that Mareva departed from.
- Senior Courts Act 1981 s 37(1), (3).
- *Mercedes Benz AG v Leiduck* [1996] AC 284 (PC): s 37(3) endorses but does not create the power.
- *Babanaft v Bassatne* [1990] Ch 13 (CA): judicial extension, including worldwide orders.
- *Broad Idea International v Convoy Collateral* (PC): no need for an accrued cause of action against the respondent.
- *Fourie v Le Roux* [2007] 1 WLR 320 (HL), [2]–[3]: purpose is to preserve assets for enforcement (cited by Šaranović).
- *Cheltenham & Gloucester BS v Ricketts* (CA): the freezing order as a "nuclear weapon" (Šaranović).
- *Energy Venture Partners v Malabu* (CA): in practice a freezing order often gives the claimant security (Šaranović).
- *Candy v Holyoake* (CA): the reputational stigma of a freezing order (Šaranović).
- Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 s 25; CPR PD 6B para 3.1(5); CPR 6.37(3).
- *CSAV v Hin-Pro* (English and Hong Kong proceedings).

## Debate and critique
- **Weak doctrinal foundations (Šaranović)**: the 1975 decisions took practitioners by surprise. The reasoning in *Rasu Maritima* and earlier cases did not convincingly justify departing from the rule confining pre-judgment injunctions to proprietary claims. It exploited the fact that the *Lister v Stubbs* line did not directly address non-proprietary claims.
- **"Equipage equality"**: Šaranović's main justification is a level playing field in litigation, with a fair distribution of freedom between the parties. So any injunction needs workable exceptions and safeguards for the defendant.
- **Too claimant-friendly**:
  - The "real risk of dissipation" test has two readings: a low one (conduct making enforcement harder than usual) and a higher one (unjustified dealings). Since equity's touchstone is injustice, it is not unjust that enforcement is simply difficult, for example because of where the assets are.
  - He proposes requiring **objective evidence of conduct consistent with an intention to evade judgment**, closely linked to the assets, from which intention can be inferred.
- **Chabra injunctions** (against third parties' assets where there is no cause of action against them) have become a "dangerous weapon". The courts should protect third parties by focusing on deliberate evasion, and restrict cross-border use to avoid illegitimate interference with foreign courts.
- **Safeguards**: cross-undertakings should normally be **fortified by security** in commercial cases, with limited discretion to dispense for vulnerable claimants. The ordinary course of business exception should be easier to use. Full and frank disclosure on ex parte applications partly counterbalances the pro-claimant approach.
- **Good arguable case**: problematic with dishonesty, foreign law or complex facts. He welcomes dropping the *Canada Trust* gloss, and warns against conflating merits strength with risk of dissipation.
- **Tactical use**: claimants may seek freezing orders mainly to pressure settlement (cash flow, legal costs, reputation), not only to protect enforcement (Šaranović introduction).
- **Comity and WFOs**: worldwide orders and orders supporting ASIs (as in *Hin-Pro*) project English coercive power over assets and people abroad. The Dicey provisos (third parties abroad; "inexpedient" orders in support of foreign proceedings) are the comity safety valves. The Hong Kong courts' split in *Hin-Pro* shows the tension.
- **Essay angle**: interim measures, including freezing injunctions in support of arbitration, are an acceptable essay topic if focused on the English court's role.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Jurisdiction over the respondent**: present in England, or served out (substantive gateway, or PD 6B para 3.1(5) for s 25 relief, plus proper place under CPR 6.37(3))?
2. **Which kind of order**:
   - England has the merits: domestic order or **WFO** (exceptional; Dicey Rule 27(1) provisos protecting third parties abroad).
   - Merits abroad: **s 25** relief. Is it inexpedient given the foreign court's role?
3. **Requirements**:
   - good arguable case (if foreign law governs, consider proof);
   - real risk of unjustified dissipation: look for evidence of evasive dealings, not just foreign assets;
   - just and convenient overall.
4. **Terms**: ordinary business and legal costs exceptions; cross-undertaking (fortification?); ancillary disclosure; third-party banks (contempt if they knowingly assist breach).
5. **Proprietary claim?** If tracing a specific fund, a different analysis applies and the usual provisos may not.
6. **Enforcement abroad**: will the foreign court assist? Consider the comity concerns in *Hin-Pro* (Hong Kong).
7. **Traps**: treating the order as security or priority (it is in personam only); forgetting the s 37(3) point that the respondent need not be present; ignoring that Hague 2005 does not regulate interim relief.
