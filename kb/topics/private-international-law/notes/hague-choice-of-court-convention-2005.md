# The 2005 Hague Convention on Choice of Court Agreements

## Overview
The 2005 Hague Convention is now the main treaty regime binding English courts on jurisdiction agreements. It requires the courts of Contracting States:
- to give effect to **exclusive** choice of court agreements for Contracting States' courts: the chosen court must hear the case, and others must suspend or dismiss;
- to recognise and enforce the resulting judgments.

The EU joined, and the Convention came into force for EU states, including the UK, on 1 October 2015. Post-Brexit the UK acceded in its own right. Under the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020 it treats the Convention as in force without interruption since 1 October 2015. Its scope is narrow (exclusive clauses only, with many excluded matters), so the common law still governs much commercial practice, including non-exclusive and asymmetric clauses.

## The law

### 1. Status in the UK
- In force for the UK, as an EU state, from 1 October 2015. The UK made a new act of accession for continuity. The 2020 Act treats the Convention as in force "without hiatus" since 1 October 2015 (Sch 5 para 7, per Briggs). It has domestic force from 1 January 2021 through the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 as amended.
- Consequence (Briggs): an agreement made for English courts in, say, 2018 is treated as within the Convention in proceedings brought on or after 1 January 2021, without being restated. The UK regards itself as a country before, during and after membership of a "Regional Economic Integration Organisation" (Art 29). Whether the EU agrees "remains to be seen".
- CPR 6.33(2B)(a): a claim form may be served out without permission where the English court has power under the Convention and the defendant is party to an Art 3 exclusive agreement.

### 2. Conditions for application (Briggs's eight)
1. **International case** (Art 1). A case is international *unless* the parties are resident in the same Contracting State and all other relevant elements, whatever the location of the chosen court, are connected only with that state. Two US-resident parties choosing London make an international case. Briggs suggests residence is probably assessed at the date of the agreement. Residence of a company (Art 4(2)): statutory seat, place of incorporation, central administration or principal place of business.
2. **Date** (Art 16): concluded after entry into force for the chosen state. The slides and Briggs give 1 July 2015 for the UK. Restating an earlier agreement after that date is possible.
3. **Form** (Art 3(c)): in writing, or by other means that make the information "accessible so as to be usable for subsequent reference". No signature is required.
4. **Civil or commercial matter** (Art 1(1)), interpreted autonomously (Art 23). It includes public authorities acting in ways anyone could (e.g. buying computer equipment), but not unilateral regulatory powers.
5. **Not excluded by Art 2** as the *object* of proceedings:
   - consumer and employment contracts;
   - family law;
   - insolvency;
   - carriage of passengers and goods;
   - personal injury claims by or for natural persons;
   - infringement of IP rights other than copyright and related rights (unless the claim is or could be brought for breach of a contract between the parties).

   An excluded matter arising merely as a **preliminary question**, including by way of defence, does not exclude the proceedings. But rulings on such questions are not recognised under the Convention (Art 10). Insurance and reinsurance proceedings are not excluded just because the insured risk relates to an excluded matter (Art 17).
6. **Exclusive** (Art 3(a)–(b)): it designates the courts of one Contracting State, or specific courts there, to the exclusion of any other. A designation is **deemed exclusive unless the parties expressly provide otherwise**. Agreements stated to be non-exclusive are outside the Convention, and so are **asymmetric** agreements, even if written as two clauses (Briggs). Briggs flags hard cases, e.g. "claims by A against B must be brought in court X" with silence on B's claims (cf *Etihad Airways PJSC v Flöther* [2020] EWCA Civ 1707).
7. **Intrinsically valid** when made and still when proceedings begin. Separable from the main contract (Art 3(d)): the clause's validity "cannot be contested solely on the ground that the contract is not valid". The Convention gives no clear answer on fraud, mistake, lack of authority, non-acceptance or oral variation. The Explanatory Report (Hartley and Dogauchi, paras 93–95) says only that there must be consent.
8. **Within the scope** of the agreement and binding on the actual litigants. The Convention gives no governing law for these issues. Art 5(1) only makes the agreement ineffective if "null and void under the law of that State" (the chosen court's). An English court would instinctively refer scope, and probably validity, to the proper law of the agreement, usually the main contract's law (Briggs).

### 3. Effects on jurisdiction
- **Agreement for the English court (Art 5)**: the English court "shall have jurisdiction" unless the agreement is null and void under English law. It "shall not decline to exercise jurisdiction on the ground that the dispute should be decided in a court of another State" (Art 5(2)). So there is **no forum non conveniens stay** and no power to dismiss in favour of another state. Rules on subject matter, value and internal allocation are unaffected (Art 5(3)).
- **Agreement for another Contracting State's court (Art 6)**: the English court **must suspend or dismiss** unless:
  - (a) the agreement is null and void under the law of the chosen court's state;
  - (b) a party lacked capacity under the law of the court seised;
  - (c) giving effect would lead to manifest injustice or be manifestly contrary to the forum's public policy;
  - (d) for exceptional reasons beyond the parties' control, the agreement cannot reasonably be performed;
  - (e) the chosen court has decided not to hear the case.

  Briggs: where Art 6 applies, "that is that". This is a statutory duty, quite different from the common law stay discretion.
- **Interim measures** are outside the Convention (Art 7). It neither requires nor prevents them (e.g. freezing orders; see freezing-injunctions.md).

### 4. Recognition and enforcement (Arts 8–9)
- A judgment of the chosen court of a Contracting State "shall be recognised and enforced" in other Contracting States. Refusal is allowed only on Convention grounds. There is no review of the merits, and the court addressed is bound by the original court's jurisdictional findings of fact, unless the judgment was by default (Art 8(1)–(2)). The judgment must be effective or enforceable in the state of origin (Art 8(3)). Recognition may be postponed pending review (Art 8(4)).
- **Grounds for refusal (Art 9)**:
  - (a) the agreement was null and void under the law of the chosen court's state, unless that court held it valid;
  - (b) a party lacked capacity under the requested state's law;
  - (c) defective notification;
  - (d) fraud in connection with procedure;
  - (e) manifest incompatibility with public policy, including fundamental procedural fairness;
  - (f) inconsistency with a judgment of the requested state between the same parties;
  - (g) inconsistency with an earlier recognisable foreign judgment between the same parties on the same cause of action.
- **Art 22 reciprocal declarations** can extend recognition to judgments under non-exclusive agreements between declaring states, if:
  - the court of origin was designated;
  - no other designated court has given judgment or has the same proceedings pending;
  - the court of origin was first seised.

### 5. Relationship with the common law
If the Convention does not apply (non-exclusive, asymmetric, non-Contracting State court, excluded matter, pre-entry-into-force agreement), the common law applies: stay unless strong cause, anti-suit relief, damages, and CPR 6.33(2B)(b) service out.

## Debate and critique
- **Narrow scope**: commercial parties often use asymmetric clauses in finance (lender may sue anywhere, borrower only in London) and non-exclusive clauses. Both fall outside the Convention. Its practical reach in banking may therefore be limited, leaving the common law and its discretion (see commercial-transactions-financing.md).
- **Gaps on validity and scope**: the Convention says nothing about which law governs consent defects, scope and binding effect on third parties, apart from the chosen court's law for "null and void". English courts fill the gap with the proper law of the agreement. That may diverge from other Contracting States, undermining uniformity (Briggs).
- **The continuity argument**: the UK says the Convention applied continuously from 2015. EU courts might treat UK agreements made before 1 January 2021 as outside the Convention. That creates uncertainty about recognition of English judgments in the EU.
- **Post-Brexit significance**: with no Lugano, the 2005 Convention (with the 2019 Judgments Convention; see hague-judgments-convention-2019-and-international-commercial-courts.md) is the main multilateral route for recognition of English judgments in the EU. That raises the stakes of drafting genuinely exclusive clauses.
- **Mandatory rules versus discretion**: Art 5(2) removes forum non conveniens for the chosen court. Art 6 gives limited exceptions. This is closer to the Brussels philosophy than to the common law's "strong cause" flexibility. Is that better for certainty, and fair to third parties and multi-party disputes?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there a choice of court agreement in writing or an accessible equivalent?
2. Is the case **international**? Is the chosen court in a **Contracting State**? Was the agreement concluded after entry into force for that state (UK: as sources state, 1 July / 1 October 2015 continuity)?
3. **Civil or commercial**? Is an **Art 2 exclusion** the object of the proceedings (consumer, employment, carriage of goods, personal injury, insolvency, IP infringement)? Or only a preliminary question?
4. **Exclusive**? Silence means deemed exclusive. Expressly non-exclusive or asymmetric clauses are outside.
5. **Valid and within scope**? Use the chosen court's law for "null and void". Otherwise English courts look to the agreement's proper law. Separability applies.
6. **Effect**:
   - English court chosen: it must hear the case; no FNC stay.
   - Foreign Contracting State chosen: suspend or dismiss unless an Art 6 exception applies.
   - Remember that interim relief remains available (Art 7; CJJA 1982 s 25).
7. **Judgment stage**: recognition under Arts 8–9; refusal only on listed grounds.
8. If the Convention does not apply, go to jurisdiction-agreements-common-law.md.
