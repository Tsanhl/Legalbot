# Jurisdiction conflicts, parallel proceedings and comity (CSAV v Hin-Pro)

## Overview
When courts of several countries each think they have jurisdiction over the same dispute, the parties can end up with parallel proceedings, clashing interim orders, inconsistent judgments, and disputes about recognition. The EU dealt with this by a rigid first-seised (lis pendens) rule. England, outside that regime since Brexit, relies on discretionary tools: forum non conveniens stays, enforcement of jurisdiction agreements, anti-suit injunctions, freezing orders, damages for breach and refusal to recognise judgments. Each tool is defended or criticised by reference to **comity**, which means different things in different contexts. The *CSAV v Hin-Pro* litigation is the case study. Courts in England, mainland China and Hong Kong reached opposite views on one bill of lading clause, and almost every intrusive English remedy was used together.

## The law

### 1. The post-Brexit framework for parallel proceedings
- Under Brussels Ia, a court second seised of the same cause of action between the same parties had to stay in favour of the court first seised. That rule no longer applies in England to proceedings begun after 31 December 2020. It survives only for transitional cases (SI 2019/479 reg 92) and for related proceedings covered by the EU's transitional approach.
- For new proceedings, a foreign court being seised first is only **one factor** in the *Spiliada* analysis (lis alibi pendens, the "Cambridgeshire factor"). It is not an automatic bar (see forum-non-conveniens-spiliada.md). The student's own "basics" notes record the same distinction. They also note that an EU judgment already given is a matter for recognition rules, not lis pendens (treat as a pointer only).
- England's remedial tools against foreign proceedings:
  - a stay of English proceedings;
  - an anti-suit injunction;
  - damages for breach of a jurisdiction clause;
  - a freezing injunction in aid of the ASI;
  - non-recognition of a foreign judgment obtained in breach of a dispute-resolution agreement (CJJA 1982 s 32; see foreign-judgments-common-law.md).

### 2. The CSAV v Hin-Pro saga
**Facts**:
- CSAV is a major Chilean carrier. Hin-Pro is a Hong Kong company with operations in mainland China.
- CSAV issued about 70 straight bills of lading for carriage from Chinese ports (e.g. Nanjing) to Venezuela. Hin-Pro claimed that CSAV delivered cargo without production of the original bills.
- Clause 23 provided that the bill and disputes "shall be subject to English law and the jurisdiction of the English High Court of Justice in London". If proceedings were nonetheless begun elsewhere, they were to go to "ordinary courts of law". For Chile, arbitrators were not competent, a sentence agreed to be void under Chilean law.

**Chinese proceedings** (Bath):
- Hin-Pro sued in several maritime courts (Wuhan, Guangzhou, Qingdao, Tianjin, Ningbo, Shanghai).
- Art 34 PRC Civil Procedure Law allows written choice of a court in a place with an **"actual connection"** with the dispute. The Chinese courts read this **objectively**. A choice of English law alone is not an actual connection, so the English clause was ineffective. The Qingdao Maritime Court, upheld by Shandong High Court, did not decide whether the clause was exclusive.
- The Ningbo Maritime Court, upheld by Zhejiang High Court, held the clause **non-exclusive** in a one-sentence conclusion.
- Chinese courts treat exclusivity as a prerequisite for excluding their jurisdiction.

**English proceedings**:
- The Commercial Court ([2014] EWHC 3632 (Comm)) and the CA ([2015] EWCA Civ 401, Christopher Clarke LJ) held clause 23 **exclusive** (reasons in jurisdiction-agreements-common-law.md).
- Remedies granted:
  - an **anti-suit injunction** restraining the Chinese proceedings;
  - a **Mareva injunction** freezing Hin-Pro's worldwide assets in aid of the ASI (granted ex parte by Walker J in June 2014);
  - **committal for three months** of Hin-Pro's director for contempt, after she took part in a mediation and a preliminary hearing on jurisdiction in the Wuhan Maritime Court despite the order;
  - **damages** for CSAV's costs in the foreign proceedings.

  One commentator calls this a rare case in which the most intrusive orders were all issued together.
- **Contractual background point**. The CA treated the Hamburg Rules, referred to in clause 2 of the bills, as part of the background explaining sentences two and three ([54]). But commentators note that the Rules would not have applied mandatorily:
  - the loading port (Nanjing, China) was not in a Contracting State;
  - the discharge port (Puerto Cabello, Venezuela) was not in a Contracting State;
  - the bills were probably issued in Hong Kong or mainland China, neither of which is party to the Rules.

  That weakens the CA's reading. *Arnold v Britton* [2015] UKSC 36 at [17], [20] (natural meaning should not be undervalued by commercial common sense) supplies a counterpoint.

**Merits in China**:
- CSAV defended some Chinese cases on the merits, alleging fraud and forged documents. In late 2015 it had the Ningbo judgments overturned by the Zhejiang High Court.
- The parties pleaded Chinese law and gave no evidence of English law, so Chinese law applied. Whether CSAV's responsibility could be waived at the Venezuelan port was for Venezuelan law, but the expert's qualifications and the content of that law were unclear.
- CSAV was found responsible for releasing cargo without original bills, but Hin-Pro failed to prove its loss, and its appeal was dismissed.

**Hong Kong** (*CSAV v Hin-Pro* [2016] HKCFA 79):
- CSAV sought a Mareva over Hin-Pro's Hong Kong assets and a receiver (s 21M application). The lower courts refused, not wanting to intervene in an English–PRC conflict.
- The **HKCFA granted relief**. It was being asked to help enforce an English damages award for breach of an exclusive clause. The English court's action involved **no breach of comity**, and nothing in public policy barred assisting enforcement or granting a Mareva in support. It relied on Millett LJ in *The Angelic Grace*: no court would be offended by an injunction restraining a party from invoking a jurisdiction it had promised not to invoke and which it was its own duty to decline.
- CSAV had a good arguable case. Hin-Pro had sued in breach of contract, continued in contempt of the English order, and relied on fraudulent and forged documents. CSAV had incurred large costs. The Court of Appeal erred in refusing all relief as a matter of discretion.
- Hong Kong's Foreign Judgments (Restriction on Recognition and Enforcement) Ordinance (Cap 46) bars recognition of overseas judgments given in breach of a dispute-settlement agreement.

**Contrast**: *Import Export Metro Ltd v CSAV* [2003] EWHC 11 (Comm). On an identical clause, *CSAV* argued it was non-exclusive in order to obtain a stay in favour of Chile, whose law on discharge of performance it apparently found more favourable. The court held the clause non-exclusive but refused a stay (Gross J: inconvenience was the price of a neutral forum). Christopher Clarke LJ in *Hin-Pro* at [52] noted that CSAV then had no interest in arguing exclusivity.

### 3. Fok J's comity hypotheticals (paras 73–76)
Mr Justice Joseph Fok (HKCFA PJ), "Comity in multi-jurisdictional disputes" (Asia-Pacific Judicial Colloquium 2017):
1. **(73)** If the PRC courts had conclusively held the clause *positively non-exclusive* (rather than void), Hin-Pro would have been justified in suing in China. The English damages claim would be cast into doubt, at least as regards the PRC proceedings. A Hong Kong injunction in support of England would directly contradict a final PRC ruling between the parties, which Hong Kong ought, as a matter of comity, to respect.
2. **(74)** If Hong Kong were asked to enforce a PRC judgment against CSAV but itself concluded the clause was exclusive, should it refuse enforcement as a judgment obtained contrary to a dispute-resolution agreement?
3. **(75)** If both parties made competing s 21M applications in aid of their respective proceedings, Hong Kong would be squarely caught between the two foreign jurisdictions.
4. **(76)** Even on the actual facts, is it consistent with comity for England to award, as damages for breach of the clause, the sums a PRC court ordered to be paid in the very actions alleged to be the breach?

**Fok J's conclusions** (paras 77–82):
- Comity does not mean the same thing in every context, so the correct proposition must be identified.
- Briggs's caveat that comity can be overridden raises the spectre of "judicial chauvinism". Should comity always proceed on Lord Diplock's "gentlemanly assumption"?
- Judicial cooperation is valuable. Examples:
  - *Chan Chin Cheung v Chan Fatt Cheung* [2010] 1 SLR 1192 (Singapore CA): a limited temporary stay pending Malaysian proceedings avoided inconsistent findings and a "zero-sum game";
  - *Joujou v Masri* [2011] EWCA Civ 746 at [55] (Toulson LJ): comity involves self-restraint, but courts may state their view to help a foreign court, and should lend each other assistance.
- Comity and common sense should produce the same answers.

## Debate and critique
- **What is comity?**
  - **Joel R Paul**: in the US, comity is the foundation of private international law. Courts apply foreign law or limit domestic jurisdiction out of respect for foreign sovereignty, balancing public and private interests. But scholars and courts have described it inconsistently: as a choice-of-law principle, a synonym for private international law, a rule of public international law, a moral obligation, expediency, courtesy. Paul treats private international law as working "much like a constitution" in allocating authority.
  - **Childress, "Comity as Conflict"**: comity should be re-situated as a conflict of laws doctrine. He evaluates its use in *In re South African Apartheid Litigation* (Alien Tort Statute).
  - **Worster, "Competition and Comity in the Fragmentation of International Law"**: competing tribunals and regimes and the role of comity in managing them.
  - : comity is "a vague concept". Is it courtesy or obligation? It depends on cooperation and goodwill.
- **Is the English approach chauvinistic?** Combining an ASI, a worldwide freezing order, committal and damages against a party who sued in courts that accepted jurisdiction under their own law looks like England imposing its reading of the clause on China. Defenders cite *pacta sunt servanda*: the English court only holds the party to its bargain. Fok J's para 76 asks whether recovering, as damages, sums awarded by the PRC court effectively overrides the foreign judgment.
- **The "conflict of conflicts"**: the clash was not only about forum but about interpretation and validity of the clause under different systems. English law reads such clauses as exclusive. Chinese law requires an actual connection and treats an ambiguous clause as non-exclusive. Neither is "wrong" by its own lights. Hence the case for coordination mechanisms.
- **Bath**: the Chinese approach does not prevent parallel proceedings. A Chinese court must reject a suit on the same dispute only if a foreign judgment has been recognised in China. Enforcing foreign judgments in China is very difficult without a bilateral judicial assistance agreement. So foreign clauses and foreign judgments offer limited protection there.
- **Reform: a Hague parallel proceedings convention** (Herrup and Brand). Neither civil law lis pendens nor common law forum non conveniens works well. A convention should channel litigation to the "better forum", with:
  - criteria for identifying it;
  - mechanisms to move cases there;
  - notification of parallel proceedings to the courts;
  - judicial communication;
  - a fallback if the better forum declines;
  - procedural provisions, for example on evidence.
- **Forum selection for parties**: enforceability of judgments, recognition of the clause in the other party's home courts (e.g. China's actual-connection test), interim relief, and neutral international commercial courts all matter (see hague-judgments-convention-2019-and-international-commercial-courts.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Map the proceedings**: where, when (first seised?), parties, cause of action. Is any transitional Brussels or Lugano rule relevant?
2. **Interpret the clause** under its governing law (English law: *Hin-Pro* factors, *Arnold v Britton*). Exclusive?
3. **How will the foreign court view it?** (e.g. PRC "actual connection"; exclusivity required). Has the foreign court already decided the point? If so, consider Fok J's para 73 point about respecting a final foreign ruling.
4. **Choose English remedies and test each against comity**:
   - stay of English proceedings (*Spiliada* or strong cause);
   - ASI (contractual: little role for comity; non-contractual: caution);
   - WFO in aid; contempt;
   - damages for breach (para 76 concern);
   - refusal to recognise the foreign judgment (CJJA s 32).
5. **Third-country enforcement** (e.g. Hong Kong): will it assist the English judgment (*HKCFA* in *Hin-Pro*), or face competing applications (para 75)?
6. **Practical advice**: think about where the defendant's assets are, which judgment will be enforceable where, and drafting (a clearly exclusive clause; a forum with a real connection).
