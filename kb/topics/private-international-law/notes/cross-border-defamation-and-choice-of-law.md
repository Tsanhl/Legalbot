# Cross-border defamation, libel tourism and choice of law

## Overview
Defamation is the awkward case in private international law. It engages free speech, is excluded from the modern choice-of-law
reforms (the 1995 Act's Part III and Rome II), and the English approach to "publication" made England a magnet for claimants
("libel tourism"). The Defamation Act 2013 s 9 tightened jurisdiction over foreign defendants, but choice of law still rests on
the old double actionability rule. Online and social media publication exposes the weakness of any rule tied to a place.

## The law

### 1. Libel tourism before the Defamation Act 2013
1. **Meaning.** Forum shopping by choosing the court thought most likely to give a favourable result. English law was seen as
   the most claimant-friendly: the claimant needs only a defamatory publication about him, need not prove falsity (truth is a
   defence) or malice, and damages could be high.
2. **Publication.** Each communication to another person is a separate publication (*Duke of Brunswick v Harmer* (1849) 14 QB
   185). Each newspaper sale or broadcast reception is a separate tort in the place where it occurs, so one copy sold in
   England suffices. There is no single-publication rule. This applies to jurisdiction, forum non conveniens and choice of law.
   English courts could take jurisdiction only for publication in England and the remedy was limited to harm from that
   publication.
3. **Jurisdiction.** For defendants not domiciled in an EU Member State (before Brexit) the claimant served out under CPR
   Part 6 and Practice Direction 6B (the slides cite "rule 3(1)(9)"): damage sustained in the jurisdiction or resulting from an act in it. Forum non conveniens was
   applied by looking only at the English publication, so England was almost always the appropriate forum, save where the
   claimant had no substantial reputation in England.
4. **Internet.** Material on the Internet is treated as published in England whenever it can be downloaded there (no targeting
   requirement), so the publication requirement offered little protection against exorbitant jurisdiction.
5. ***Bin Mahfouz v Ehrenfeld* [2005] EWHC 1156 (QB).** A US-published book alleging a Saudi businessman funded terrorism, not
   marketed in the UK, but with 23 copies sold online in England and a chapter accessible on a US website. Default judgment:
   a declaration of falsity, £10,000 damages to each of three claimants, costs (nearly £115,000 in total), and an injunction
   against publishing in England. The slides use it to show that a remedy for English publication in practice affects
   publication elsewhere and may deter publication entirely.
6. **US reaction.** Ehrenfeld sued in New York for a declaration of non-liability and unenforceability; the New York Court of
   Appeals found no jurisdiction over Mahfouz. Several US states then barred enforcement of foreign libel judgments, and the
   federal SPEECH Act 2010 bars enforcement unless the defendant would be liable under the First Amendment (passed unanimously).

### 2. The Defamation Act 2013, s 9
1. Applies to defamation actions against a person **not domiciled in the UK** (the slides give the rest of s 9(1) as elided
   text, so also check the statute for the EU/Lugano carve-out). The court has no jurisdiction unless satisfied that, of all
   the places in which the statement has been published, England and Wales is **clearly the most appropriate place** to sue
   (s 9(2)). A statement conveying the same or substantially the same imputation counts (s 9(3)).
2. The slides present it as a jurisdictional rule admitting no exception: even submission or appearance cannot confer
   jurisdiction. A dismissed claimant may need to start again and risk limitation.
3. It requires the court to look at all places of publication, even those not sued on. Open questions: does "appropriateness"
   include the "access to justice" strand of forum non conveniens? What if publication is equally spread between England and
   the USA: it seems England can no longer be used, which may deny effective protection to deserving claimants.
4. **Residual routes.** Defendants served in England (presence, or a fixed place of business) can still be sued. For service out,
   the tort must have occurred in England (publication or damage there) and England must be the most appropriate place.
5. ***Soriano v Forensic News LLC* [2021] EWCA Civ 1952.** A UK resident with dual British-Israeli citizenship sued a
   Californian outlet, its founder and three US journalists in libel, misuse of private information, malicious falsehood,
   harassment and data protection over a podcast and seven articles. Jay J gave permission to serve out for the libel claims and
   some misuse of private information claims ([2021] EWHC 56 (QB)). The Court of Appeal (Warby LJ) held:
   - s 9 does not remove subject-matter jurisdiction; it modifies the *Spiliada* forum conveniens test, in two ways: the court
     considers all places of publication, and substantially identical imputations are treated as the statement complained of
     (*Spiliada*: the claimant must show England is *clearly* the appropriate forum).
   - The claimant bears the legal burden, but a defendant challenging jurisdiction bears an evidential burden to identify an
     available alternative forum and prove facts showing it is at least as suitable.
   - The standard is the "good arguable case" standard in forum cases: the better of the argument.
   - No rigid rule that foreign law on the suitability of a forum must be proved by experts.
   - Result: no reason to disturb the finding that s 9 was satisfied: the claimant's interests and reputation were centred
     in England, the harm claimed was in England only ("not a libel tourist"), and the defendants had not discharged the
     evidential burden about whether a Californian court would entertain the claim or whether a US remedy would suffice.
   Dickinson, "A boost for libel tourism" (2022) 138 LQR 357 is the commentary the slides cite.

### 3. Choice of law in defamation
1. **Excluded from reform.** Defamation falls outside Part III of the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act
   1995 and outside Rome II (violations of privacy and personality rights, including defamation, excluded). The common
   law rule continues.
2. **Double actionability.** *Phillips v Eyre* (1870) LR 6 QB 1: for a suit in England on a wrong done abroad, (1) the wrong
   must be actionable if committed in England and (2) not justifiable by the law of the place where done. Facts: the Governor of
   Jamaica was sued in England for assault and false imprisonment arising from suppression of an uprising, after he had had
   the colonial legislature indemnify him. The slides explain the rule as treating tort like public law (forum law) combined
   with territorial regulation of conduct.
3. **Exceptions.** *Boys v Chaplin* [1969] 2 All ER 1085 (HL): a Malta car accident between two English parties; double
   actionability could be disapplied exceptionally in favour of English law alone, reflecting a loss-distribution view of tort.
   *Red Sea Insurance v Bouygues* [1995] 1 AC 190 (PC): a Hong Kong claim over construction work in Saudi Arabia governed
   exclusively by Saudi law, applying a "most significant relationship" exception; suggests a move to a conduct-regulation
   view (lex loci delicti).
4. **Today.** Double actionability is the starting point for defamation, with a flexible exception for the law common to the
   parties or the law of the place of the tort, applied case by case. Critics call the rule "chauvinist" and "parochial".
5. **Location of publication.** The tort occurs where the material reaches the reader or hearer. Examples: *Church of
   Scientology v Commissioner of Metropolitan Police* (1976) 120 SJ 690 (report sent from England to Germany published in
   Germany); *Chadha v Dow Jones* [1999] ILPr 829 and *Berezovsky v Forbes* [2000] UKHL 25 (even small English circulation of a
   US magazine is an English publication, so only English law applies to it). Online: where it is downloaded (*Metall und
   Rohstoff AG v Donaldson Lufkin & Jenrette* [1990] QB 391 (CA)). Material published in England is judged by English law
   alone; for foreign publications, double actionability country by country, although it is unclear whether the Red Sea
   exception was ever applied in defamation.
6. **Why defamation is left out of reform (slides' reasons).** The double actionability rule favours English media (defendant
   has two sets of defences: English law and the law of the place of publication), and there is little political will to take on
   media interests. Defamation also engages distinct public interests: its scope defines the contours of free speech in a legal
   system, and the rule preserves a role for the forum's own free speech balance.

### 4. Online and social media difficulties
- Over-application of English standards: English free-speech standards always apply, and the "received and read" test plus
  worldwide Internet access projects English defamation law abroad. Any damages award or injunction usually leads to global
  withdrawal of material.
- Locating the tort: place of download pushes towards the lowest level of speech protection (chilling effect); place of upload
  towards the highest (under-projection of other states' balance). Claimants face proving many laws; defendants risk the lowest
  common denominator.
- Social media: informal speech but legally a publication; defendants are often individuals who cannot anticipate foreign law;
  causation and liability for reposts or retweets (the original poster may be liable for damage that was reasonably foreseeable
  from re-communication); anonymity makes localisation and enforcement harder.
- Defamation Act 2013 s 5: website operators are generally not liable for user-posted material, but the defence is lost where
  the operator does not take the material down or give the claimant enough information to identify the poster.
- The slides ask whether the dispute needs localising at all, or whether a non-territorial approach (including private
  regulation of social media) is better.

## Key authorities
- Defamation Act 2013 ss 5, 9; PIL (Misc Provisions) Act 1995 Part III; Rome II (exclusion); SPEECH Act 2010 (US).
- *Duke of Brunswick v Harmer*; *Bin Mahfouz v Ehrenfeld*; *Soriano v Forensic News*; *Phillips v Eyre*; *Boys v Chaplin*;
  *Red Sea v Bouygues*; *Berezovsky v Forbes*; *Chadha v Dow Jones*; *Metall und Rohstoff v Donaldson*.

## Debate and critique
- Is libel tourism objectionable? Free-speech states may complain that English courts undermine their citizens' freedoms, and
  threats of English proceedings have been used to remove material about corruption from foreign websites; defendants may
  settle because they cannot fund London litigation.
- Does s 9 over-correct? It may deny access to justice for deserving claimants with substantial reputations in several countries
  and reduces the effect of English reputation law, yet incentives to forum-shop in favour of English law remain (so choice of
  law reform matters as much as jurisdiction).
- After *Soriano*: Dickinson's commentary is titled "A boost for libel tourism", pointing to the flexible forum conveniens
  test and the weight given to the claimant's advantages in suing in England.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the defendant domiciled in the UK? If not, s 9: of all places of publication, is England clearly the most appropriate?
   Consider the claimant's reputation centre, scope of publication abroad, and the defendant's evidence on an alternative forum.
2. If the claim is within the gateway, check service out and forum conveniens (*Spiliada*).
3. Where was each publication received? English publication means English law; foreign publication means double actionability
   (plus the Red Sea or common-law exceptions).
4. Remember that defamation is outside Rome II and the 1995 Act; but any associated claim such as data protection or other
   torts may be within Rome II, and misuse of private information needs separate analysis.
5. For online posts: identify the poster, platform operator (s 5), reposts, and the effect of any injunction abroad.
