# Anti-suit injunctions

## Overview
An anti-suit injunction (ASI) is an English court order, made *in personam*, telling a party not to start or continue proceedings in a foreign court. It is the main tool by which English courts protect their own jurisdiction and enforce exclusive jurisdiction and arbitration agreements, and it is a frequent source of friction with foreign courts. There are two broad grounds:
- **contractual**: the foreign proceedings breach a promise not to sue there. The injunction is granted almost as of course unless there is strong reason against it, and comity plays little part;
- **non-contractual**: the foreign proceedings are vexatious or oppressive. Here comity requires caution, and England must usually be the natural forum.

Within the EU regime ASIs were barred between Member States (*Turner v Grovit*, *West Tankers*). Since Brexit they are again available against proceedings in EU courts. *UniCredit v RusChemAlliance* [2024] UKSC 30 extended the contractual ASI to arbitration agreements with a **foreign** seat. The sequel, [2025] EWCA Civ 99, shows the practical limits of the remedy.

## The law

### 1. Nature and jurisdiction
1. An ASI is an **equitable and discretionary** remedy restraining a party from instituting, prosecuting or continuing foreign proceedings.
2. It operates **in personam**. It rests not on any claim to exercise judicial rights abroad, but on the respondent being subject to the English court's personal jurisdiction. So the court must first have **personal jurisdiction** over the respondent:
   - English jurisdiction agreement: service out without permission (CPR 6.33(2B));
   - arbitration agreement with an English seat, or no designated seat: permission under CPR 62.5(1)(c);
   - foreign-seated arbitration agreement: permission under CPR 6.37, e.g. PD 6B para 3.1(6)(c) if the agreement is governed by English law (as in *UniCredit*).
3. **Source of power**: s 37 Senior Courts Act 1981 lets the High Court grant an injunction "in all cases in which it appears to the court to be just and convenient to do so". That power is confirmed by, but does not derive from, s 37. It comes from the pre-1873 powers of the Chancery and other courts (*Fourie v Le Roux* [2007] 1 WLR 320 (HL), Lord Scott at 329–330).
4. **Brussels/Lugano era**: before the end of the transition period, ASIs restraining proceedings in another Member State or Lugano State in civil and commercial matters were held inconsistent with the scheme. That applied both on vexation or oppression grounds (*Turner v Grovit* [2005] 1 AC 101) and for breach of an arbitration agreement (*West Tankers Inc v Allianz SpA* [2009] 1 AC 1138). This restriction no longer applies to new proceedings.

### 2. When an ASI may be granted
- **Underlying principle**: the court intervenes where appropriate to avoid injustice, or where the foreign proceedings are "contrary to equity and good conscience". The "width and flexibility of equity are not to be undermined by categorisation" (*Castanho v Brown & Root (UK) Ltd* [1981] AC 557 at 573, Lord Scarman).
- **The most significant category** is enforcing arbitration agreements and (exclusive) jurisdiction agreements. The court may also restrain foreign proceedings that are vexatious or oppressive.

### 3. Non-contractual ASIs: vexatious or oppressive proceedings
1. The test is **not** a mirror of forum non conveniens. In *Société Nationale Industrielle Aérospatiale v Lee Kui Jak* [1987] AC 871 (PC, Lord Goff), it would be wrong to let the English court, through an injunction, resolve a mere difference of view with the foreign court about which forum is natural. That would "be inconsistent with comity" and ignore the requirement that an injunction is granted only where the ends of justice require (at 895).
2. **Requirements**:
   - England should be the natural forum;
   - the court weighs the injustice to the applicant if the foreign proceedings continue against the injustice to the respondent if they are stopped.
3. English courts have refused to give a comprehensive or limiting definition of "vexatious or oppressive".
4. **Comity requires caution**, especially where the respondent is suing in its own courts. The stronger the foreign court's connection with the parties and the dispute, the stronger the case against intervention.
5. Lord Scarman in *British Airways Board v Laker Airways Ltd* [1985] AC 58 at 95: restraining someone from pursuing a remedy abroad, where they have a cause of action, is "however disguised and indirect" an interference with the foreign court's process.
6. **Exorbitant foreign jurisdiction**: Hoffmann J in *Barclays Bank plc v Homan* [1992] BCC 757 at 762, approved by Glidewell LJ at 777 and regarded as authoritative since. A recurring theme is a foreign court asserting, by its own law, a jurisdiction so wide in persons or subject matter that it seems contrary to accepted principles of international law. English courts have sometimes then intervened to protect a party from having to litigate somewhere with which it has little connection.

### 4. Contractual ASIs: *The Angelic Grace* principle
1. *Aggeliki Charis Cia Maritima SA v Pagnan SpA (The Angelic Grace)* [1995] 1 Lloyd's Rep 87 (CA), Millett LJ at 96. It is time "to lay aside the ritual incantation" that the jurisdiction be exercised sparingly and with great caution. That caution makes sense in forum non conveniens cases, but there is no reason for diffidence where foreign proceedings breach an arbitration agreement, "on the clear and simple ground that the defendant has promised not to bring them". Without the injunction the applicant loses its contractual rights where damages are manifestly inadequate. The remedy is discretionary, but good reason must be shown why it should *not* be granted.
2. On the facts, the Italian claimant gave no reason, beyond Italian tort jurisdiction over a port collision, why its rights should differ. There was no evidence that the Italian court would do anything other than stay under the New York Convention. The question is how far such simple facts can guide harder cases.
3. **Settled features of the contractual basis**:
   - strong reasons are needed to outweigh the prima facie entitlement to enforce an English exclusive jurisdiction clause or English-seated arbitration;
   - no need to show oppression or vexation, or that England is the natural forum;
   - comity plays little or no part, since the order only makes the respondent keep its bargain (*pacta sunt servanda*);
   - the injunction goes unless there is a strong reason to the contrary;
   - the applicant need not show that proceedings in the chosen forum are pending, imminent or even desired. It is enough that the respondent has gone to a non-contractual forum.
4. **Puzzle**: why is a contractual ASI not an interference with the foreign court? What if the foreign court takes a different view of the agreement's validity or effect (a "conflict of conflicts")? *CSAV v Hin-Pro*, where Chinese courts treated the clause differently, illustrates the problem (see jurisdiction-conflicts-and-comity.md).

### 5. *UniCredit Bank GmbH v RusChemAlliance LLC*
**Facts**:
- RusChem, a Russian company, contracted with two German companies to build energy facilities in Russia and paid an advance of about €2bn.
- UniCredit, a German bank, issued seven on-demand bonds. Each was expressly governed by English law and provided for ICC arbitration in Paris.
- After the invasion of Ukraine, EU sanctions led the German contractors to stop work. RusChem terminated and demanded payment under the bonds. UniCredit refused, citing sanctions.
- RusChem sued in the St Petersburg Arbitrazh Court for about €450m, relying on **Art 248.1 Arbitrazh Procedural Code** (2020). It gives Russian courts exclusive jurisdiction over sanctions-related disputes and treats foreign arbitration agreements as incapable of performance. The Russian court held the arbitration agreements inoperative but stayed pending the English ASI application.

**English proceedings**: RusChem had no English presence, so UniCredit needed permission to serve out. It had to show a serious issue, a gateway and that England was the proper place. The gateway depended on English law governing the arbitration agreement, which the UKSC held it did. The rest concerned proper place.
- First instance: no jurisdiction.
- CA ([2024] EWCA Civ 64, Males LJ): England was the proper place because the relief could not be obtained in France. Courts should ordinarily grant ASIs supporting foreign-seated arbitrations, provided the seat court would not see this as an "unwarranted interference" with its jurisdiction. Injunction granted.

**UKSC** (Lord Leggatt; Lords Reed, Sales, Burrows and Lady Rose agreeing):
1. Both parties were wrong to frame the question through **forum non conveniens**. It is wrong to assume that at most one court can properly restrain a breach of an arbitration agreement, so that England must decline unless clearly the most suitable ([73]). The question is whether to **enforce the parties' agreement** ([74]).
2. Following *Airbus Industrie GIE v Patel* (HL) and *IPOC v CT-Mobile* (CA Bermuda): where an ASI is sought because a *third* forum is appropriate for the substance, the test is not whether England is the most suitable forum for anti-suit relief, but **whether English intervention is consistent with comity** ([77]).
3. **Comity**:
   - No breach as regards Russia. Its courts, through Art 248.1, no longer honour their New York Convention obligations.
   - No breach as regards France. The French law evidence confirmed that French courts would not object.
   - A **substantial connection** with England existed because the contractual rights were governed by English law ([83]).
4. **Foreign seat**: that alone does not bar an English ASI. Lord Leggatt stepped back from statements, including in *Enka v Chubb*, that restraining breach of an English arbitration agreement is part of the supervisory jurisdiction. The power to grant an ASI is part of neither the supervisory nor the supporting jurisdiction ([95]–[96]). Seat courts supervise the arbitration but are not the only courts that can stop a breach (*IPOC* at [99]). The choice of Paris was no reason not to uphold the bargain ([100]).
5. **No effective remedy elsewhere**: French courts would have no jurisdiction over any claim by UniCredit for the breach ([101]). An arbitrator's order has no coercive force, since it creates only a contractual obligation, which RusChem had already ignored. Proper place: England. Injunction upheld.

**Aftermath ([2025] EWCA Civ 99, Vos MR, Asplin and Phillips LJJ)**:
- RusChem obtained a Russian ruling requiring UniCredit to take all measures to cancel the English order within two weeks, on pain of a €250m penalty. UniCredit had substantial Russian assets and applied under CPR 3.1(7), with RusChem's agreement, to revoke or vary the ASI.
- The CA held that it had power to vary a final continuing injunction where circumstances had materially changed. It discharged the **injunctive** parts ([8]–[11] of the 2024 order) but kept the parts reflecting the jurisdictional decisions of the CA and UKSC (governing law; English jurisdiction).
- The Attorney General appointed an Advocate to the Court to address the public interest.

### 6. The Arbitration Act 2025 amendment
New **s 6A Arbitration Act 1996**:
- (1) the law applicable to an arbitration agreement is (a) the law the parties **expressly** agree applies to it, or (b) failing that, the law of the **seat**;
- (2) agreement that a law governs the main contract does **not** count as express agreement that it governs the arbitration agreement.

Consequence: on facts like *UniCredit*, a Paris seat with only an English-law main contract and no express choice for the arbitration clause would now point to French law. That would undermine the English-law gateway and the "substantial connection" relied on.

## Key authorities
- *Fourie v Le Roux* [2007] 1 WLR 320 (HL): s 37 SCA 1981 confirms, but does not create, the injunction power.
- *Castanho v Brown & Root* [1981] AC 557: equity's flexibility; avoid rigid categories.
- *Aérospatiale v Lee Kui Jak* [1987] AC 871 (PC): vexation or oppression; England as natural forum; comity; balancing injustice.
- *British Airways v Laker Airways* [1985] AC 58: an ASI indirectly interferes with the foreign court.
- *Barclays Bank v Homan* [1992] BCC 757: intervention against exorbitant foreign jurisdiction.
- *The Angelic Grace* [1995] 1 Lloyd's Rep 87 (CA): contractual ASI without diffidence.
- *Turner v Grovit* [2005] 1 AC 101; *West Tankers v Allianz* [2009] 1 AC 1138: no intra-EU ASIs (pre-Brexit).
- *UniCredit v RusChemAlliance* [2024] EWCA Civ 64; [2024] UKSC 30; [2025] EWCA Civ 99.
- *CSAV v Hin-Pro* ([2015] EWCA Civ 401 and related): ASI against Chinese maritime court proceedings, backed by a worldwide freezing order and committal for contempt (see freezing-injunctions.md).
- Senior Courts Act 1981 s 37; Arbitration Act 1996 s 6A (added 2025); New York Convention Art II(3).

## Debate and critique
- **Is comity irrelevant in contractual cases?** The orthodox view says yes, because the ASI simply holds the party to its promise. But the order still blocks a foreign court that may have taken a different, and under its own law legitimate, view of the clause. Laker's "however disguised and indirect" interference applies. *UniCredit* re-introduces comity as the test for proper place, but finds it satisfied easily where the foreign state breaches the New York Convention.
- **Pacta sunt servanda as justification**. Is the bargain enough to justify both (a) England as the proper place and (b) the grant itself? Critics argue that the seat court is the natural supervisor and that England is acting as a global enforcer of arbitration agreements. Supporters point to the absence of any effective remedy in France or in the arbitration.
- **Why not Spiliada?** Lord Leggatt rejected a single-proper-forum analysis for anti-suit relief. Is the distinction between stay or service-out cases and ASI cases convincing?
- **Effect of Russian Art 248.1**: should domestic legislation making the arbitration agreement inoperative be relevant to the discretion? The UKSC saw it as a breach of the New York Convention, which removed comity concerns. Others might see it as a sovereign policy choice that English courts should respect.
- **Limits of effectiveness**: the 2025 CA decision shows that an ASI against a respondent with no English assets, met by foreign counter-measures (an anti-anti-suit order with €250m penalties), can become futile and oppressive to the *applicant*. Declarations survive but practical enforcement fails. This is a strong essay point about the reach of in personam relief.
- **Reform**: s 6A of the 1996 Act changes the governing-law analysis that drove *UniCredit*. Future foreign-seated cases may lack an English-law link.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Personal jurisdiction** over the respondent: present in England? If not, service out. Use CPR 6.33(2B) for an English clause, CPR 62.5 for English-seated or unseated arbitration, or CPR 6.37 with a PD 6B gateway (e.g. arbitration agreement governed by English law; check s 6A for agreements now governed by the seat's law).
2. **Basis**:
   - **Contractual**: is there a valid exclusive jurisdiction or arbitration agreement covering the dispute? Construe it widely (*Fiona Trust*). Is the foreign suit a breach? If so, grant unless strong reason. No need to show England is the natural forum or vexation. For a foreign seat, ask whether intervention is consistent with comity and whether an effective remedy exists elsewhere (*UniCredit*).
   - **Non-contractual**: is England the natural forum? Are the foreign proceedings vexatious or oppressive? Balance injustice on both sides (*Aérospatiale*). Consider exorbitant foreign jurisdiction (*Homan*). Comity calls for caution.
3. **Discretion**: is there any "strong reason" or "good reason" against relief? Has the foreign court already ruled on the clause (comity; Fok J's analysis in jurisdiction-conflicts-and-comity.md)?
4. **Enforcement and practicality**: respondent's assets in England; contempt; freezing orders in aid (*Hin-Pro*); risk of foreign counter-injunctions (*UniCredit* 2025).
5. **Date**: no ASIs against EU or Lugano courts in proceedings governed by the Brussels regime (transitional cases).
