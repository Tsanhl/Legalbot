# Foreign law in English courts; substance and procedure

## Overview
A choice-of-law rule that points to a foreign law does nothing until the foreign law is actually brought before the English court. In English procedure, foreign law is treated as a fact. It has to be pleaded and proved, normally by expert evidence. If nobody pleads it, English law applies by default. If it is pleaded but not adequately proved, the court may presume it is the same as English law, but only where that is a fair and reasonable assumption. The Supreme Court clarified both rules in *FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Lady Brownlie* [2021] UKSC 45. Separately, even where foreign law governs the substance, procedure is always for the forum. Where that line falls (evidence, limitation, damages) has been contested and partly changed by statute and by Rome I/II.

## The law

### A. Foreign law as fact
1. **Judicial notice covers only the forum's own law**, its private international law and notorious facts. Everything else, including foreign law, must be pleaded and proved.
2. **The fact doctrine**: *Mostyn v Fabrigas* (1774) 1 Cowp 161, 174, Lord Mansfield. Foreign laws are known by "admitting them to be proved as facts".
3. It is "a question of fact of a peculiar kind": *Parkasho v Singh* [1968] P 233, 250 (Cairns J). At bottom it is a question of law. Appellate courts are slow to disturb findings on it, but will reverse a manifestly wrong decision.
4. **Mode of proof**: plead it, then rely on the other side's admission or agreement. Otherwise plead it and prove it by expert evidence.
5. Proof can be difficult. *Neilson v Overseas Projects Corpn of Victoria Ltd* [2005] HCA 54 (High Court of Australia) shows a case where the evidence was not enough to show how a Chinese court would interpret the relevant rule.

### B. Two separate rules after *Brownlie II*
Lord Leggatt held that the "presumption" and the "default rule" should not be used interchangeably. They are two distinct rules with different rationales ([112]).

**(1) The default rule (a rule of pleading)**
- If neither party pleads that foreign law applies, the court applies English law **in its own right**. It does not do so by presuming anything about foreign law ([112]–[113]).
- Justification: in an adversarial system the parties' statements of case define the issues. A court need not apply of its own motion a rule nobody relies on ([113]).
- The rule survives Rome I and II. Art 1(3) of each excludes "evidence and procedure", and the rule is one of English civil procedure. So the court is not obliged to apply the Rome rules if neither party pleads foreign law. The parties can be seen as tacitly agreeing to English law, which Art 3(2) Rome I and Art 14(1)(a) Rome II expressly allow after the event ([114]).
- Example: *Aluminium Industrie Vaassen BV v Romalpa Aluminium Ltd* [1976] 1 WLR 676. The contract was in Dutch and expressly governed by Dutch law, but neither side pleaded Dutch law, so English law was applied ([115]).
- Applying foreign law is rarely mandatory; status issues may be an exception.
- **The limit**: if a party pleads a well-founded case that foreign law applies, the court must apply it. Applying English law would then be unlawful ([116]). Lord Leggatt rejected Briggs's view that English law applies "faute de mieux" when the party with the burden fails to prove foreign content. If that party fails, its claim or defence fails ([117]).

**(2) The presumption of similarity (a rule of evidence)**
- Where foreign law is pleaded but its content is not proved, the court may presume it is materially similar to English law on the point. As a rule of evidence, it is unaffected by Rome I and II (*OPO v MLA* [2014] EWCA Civ 1277, cited at [119]).
- It is justified by four factors ([123]–[125]):
  - (a) legal systems, especially common law ones, often share similarities;
  - (b) there is no point making a party prove foreign law unless differences are really likely to change the outcome (materiality);
  - (c) the presumption decides no legal issue and operates only until evidence of foreign law is given;
  - (d) it does not shift the legal burden of proof. It only places on the party wishing to displace it the burden of adducing evidence.
- **The guiding limit**: it applies only where it is a fair and reasonable assumption on the facts. Is it reasonable to expect the foreign law to be materially similar, so that any differences are unlikely to change the result ([126])?
- Guidance at [143]–[147]:
  - it is more likely to be appropriate where the foreign system is a common law one rather than one based on Roman law, though "great and broad" principles may be presumed anywhere;
  - it is less likely to be appropriate where the English rule is statutory, especially a local regulatory scheme rather than a codification. For example, one might presume a bereavement claim exists, but not that it extends to a cohabitant as under s 1A Fatal Accidents Act 1976;
  - its application may be uncertain, but that is acceptable, because either party can always adduce actual evidence;
  - the procedural context matters (for example, an interim stage).
- Illustrations from the cases reviewed:
  - *Bentinck v Willink* (1842): equitable principle presumed in Dutch law;
  - *Guepratte v Young* (1851): no presumption about French rules on married women's capacity;
  - *Saxby v Fulton* [1909] 2 KB 208: no presumption that Monte Carlo gaming law matched English law, since roulette there was notoriously lawful;
  - *Cuba Railroad Co v Crosby* (1912) (US).

### C. No choice of law for procedure
1. Procedure is governed by the lex fori. Foreign law's role is to supply the parties' substantive rights and obligations. A wider role would be unnecessary and impractical. Procedure is the most technical part of a system and may be unworkable in a court designed on different lines. The line between substance and procedure is often clear but sometimes hard to draw.
2. **Existing categories**:
   - **Capacity or standing to be a party**: procedural. Courts sometimes use discretion to let a foreign party sue.
   - **Evidence**: generally for the forum, but distinguish:
     - evidence of the *meaning* of express terms is substantive (interpreted by the governing law);
     - evidence proving the *existence* of a document, supplementary provisions or a collateral contract is procedural (proved as the forum requires). So technical expressions are read under the governing law, but the contract's existence is proved the forum's way.
   - **Limitation**: now substantive under the Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984.
     - Before 1984, foreign rules that extinguished the right were substantive, while rules that merely barred the action were procedural. Since English Limitation Acts bar actions ("No action shall be brought..."), both time bars could apply and the shorter would decide, which Briggs calls the "desiccated product of legal logic".
     - Section 1 makes the lex causae's time bar apply.
     - English law still defines when proceedings begin (s 4).
     - English law's own period applies if applying the foreign one would offend public policy, including undue hardship to a party (s 2).
     - Section 1(5) excludes renvoi on limitation (Briggs Ch 4).
   - **Assessment of damages**: substantive within Rome I and Rome II. In areas they do not cover, such as defamation, it is procedural (*Harding v Wealands* [2006] UKHL 32). But the methods of proving recoverable loss, such as what expert evidence is needed, are evidence and procedure within Art 1(3) Rome II and are governed by English rules (*Wall v Mutuelle de Poitiers Assurances* [2014] EWCA Civ 138 at [12], [20], [41]–[45], [48]).
   - **Remedies at common law**: an English court grants only remedies its own law knows, and only where they fit the right under the lex causae. *Phrantzes v Argenti* [1960] 2 QB 19 (CA): a daughter's claim to have her father constitute her dowry under Greek law failed, as no English remedy corresponded (Briggs Ch 4).
   - **Currency of judgment**: no longer procedural. *Miliangos v George Frank (Textiles) Ltd* [1976] AC 443 and *The Despina R* [1979] AC 685 allow judgment in the foreign currency in which the loss was suffered (Briggs Ch 4).

## Key authorities
- *FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Lady Brownlie* [2021] UKSC 45, [2021] 3 WLR 1011. The claim was for injuries and death from an excursion in Egypt, and was pleaded only "pursuant to Egyptian law". Because Egyptian law was pleaded, the default rule could not apply. English law could matter only through the presumption of similarity ([118]). Leading modern statement of both rules.
- *Mostyn v Fabrigas* (1774): foreign law proved as fact.
- *Parkasho v Singh* [1968] P 233: "a question of fact of a peculiar kind".
- *Aluminium Industrie Vaassen v Romalpa* [1976] 1 WLR 676: default rule in action.
- *OPO v MLA* [2014] EWCA Civ 1277: the presumption is evidential, so Rome II leaves it untouched.
- *Neilson v Overseas Projects Corpn of Victoria* [2005] HCA 54: difficulty of proving how a foreign court would apply its rule.
- *Harding v Wealands* [2006] UKHL 32: quantification of damages was procedural at common law, under PIL(MP)A 1995. Briggs says the HL "missed an opportunity". Section 14(3) of the 1995 Act only preserved what was procedural before it. The Rome II position replaces "a bad rule with a better one" (citing *Cox v Ergo Versicherung AG* [2014] UKSC 22).
- *Wall v Mutuelle de Poitiers* [2014] EWCA Civ 138: under Rome II, how loss is proved is procedural.
- Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984 ss 1, 2, 4, 1(5).

## Debate and critique
- **Fiction or inference?** Critics such as Fentiman (*Foreign Law in English Courts*, 1998), Briggs and Gray attacked the presumption as a legal fiction with no place in modern law (summarised at [120]–[121]). Lord Leggatt answered that the criticism would have force if the presumption were applied mechanically. Properly limited to fair and reasonable cases, it is justified on pragmatic grounds.
- **Marcus Teo, "The Inference of Similarity"**: argues there is no true "presumption" at all. Courts should simply draw an *inference* that English and foreign courts would rule alike on the same facts, and only where that conclusion can reliably be drawn. Reframing it this way helps predict foreign decisions accurately and resolves disputes about its "use" in civil proceedings. Teo argues it does not make proof unpredictable or inconvenient in practice.
- **Party autonomy versus duty to apply law**: the default rule gives great weight to the parties' procedural choices. Some argue that undermines mandatory choice-of-law rules such as Rome I and II. *Brownlie* treats that as acceptable because the parties could agree on English law anyway.
- **Briggs on substance and procedure**: he criticises the over-procedural characterisation of remedies at common law and the missed opportunity in *Harding v Wealands*. The *Miliangos* currency reform shows courts can abolish obsolete procedural classifications.
- **Comparative angle**: the "Application of Foreign Law in the British..." material notes that courts also use foreign law short of applying it, for comparison. Koopmans identified two uses: to find an answer, and to justify one. Comparison is particularly important in interpreting incorporated treaties uniformly.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Has either party **pleaded** foreign law? If not, English law applies under the default rule, even if Rome I or II would point abroad.
2. If foreign law is pleaded, does the choice-of-law rule actually select it? If so, the court must apply it.
3. Has its **content been proved**, by agreement or by expert evidence? If not, ask whether the presumption of similarity is a fair and reasonable assumption:
   - Is the foreign system a common law one?
   - Is the English rule a broad principle or a local statutory scheme?
   - What stage are the proceedings at?
   If the presumption is unavailable, the party with the burden fails.
4. **Classify each issue** as substance or procedure:
   - limitation: lex causae (FLPA 1984), subject to the public policy and undue hardship exception;
   - damages: heads and quantification are substantive under Rome I and II, but the proof of loss is procedural. In defamation (outside Rome II), quantification is procedural (*Harding*);
   - evidence: meaning of terms is substantive, proof of existence procedural;
   - remedies: only English-type remedies are available.
5. **Traps**: treating the presumption as shifting the legal burden; applying English law where a party has properly pleaded and relied on foreign law; forgetting that English law fixes when time stops running.
