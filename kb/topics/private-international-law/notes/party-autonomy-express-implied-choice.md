# Party autonomy: express and implied choice of law, and what "law" can be chosen

## Overview
Party autonomy is "one of the cornerstones" of contract choice of law (Rome I Recital 11). Under Art 3(1) Rome I, a contract is governed by the law the parties choose, either expressly or as "clearly demonstrated by the terms of the contract or the circumstances of the case". An implied choice must be **real**. A court may not invent a choice the parties never made; that situation goes to Art 4. The leading English illustrations are *Amin Rasheed* (common law; inference from an English-form marine policy) and *Oldendorff v Libera* (Rome Convention; inference from a London arbitration clause). The parties may choose only the law of a **country**. Non-state rules such as lex mercatoria or Sharia can only be **incorporated** as contract terms, which raises the distinction between choice of law and incorporation by reference (paramount clauses in bills of lading).

## The law

### 1. Theory and history
- Party autonomy is "almost as ancient as conflicts law itself". Example: an edict in Hellenistic Egypt (120–118 BC) sent contracts written in Egyptian to Egyptian courts applying Egyptian law, and Greek contracts to Greek courts applying Greek law.
- In the Middle Ages, the parties' intention was a fictional implied intention. In the 19th century, contract came to be seen as an obligation created by the parties, so the place of contracting lost its importance and intention became the governing principle.
- **Vita Food Products Inc v Unus Shipping Co Ltd** [1939] AC 277 (PC):
  - Facts: herring was shipped from Newfoundland to New York on a Nova Scotian ship. The bills of lading contained negligence exemptions and stated "This contract shall be governed by English law". They lacked the express Hague Rules paramount clause required by Newfoundland's Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1932.
  - Lord Wright: the proper law is the law the parties intended. Where there is an express choice, it is hard to see what qualifications are possible, "provided the intention expressed is bona fide and legal" and there is no public policy objection. "Connection with English law is not as a matter of principle essential."
  - The Newfoundland Act did not apply, and the contract was upheld.

### 2. Express choice: Art 3(1) Rome I
- "A contract shall be governed by the law chosen by the parties. The choice shall be made expressly or clearly demonstrated…". The parties may choose a law for the whole or part of the contract (Art 3(1)) and may change the choice later (Art 3(2)), without prejudicing formal validity or third parties' rights.
- **Existence** of a choice: Art 3(5) with Art 10(1). Determined by the law that would govern if the choice existed (the putative law).
- **Interpretation**: whether a clause amounts to a choice is a matter of interpretation, not existence. Art 12(1)(a) gives interpretation to the applicable law, but using it here would beg the question. So interpretation should follow a broad, Regulation-based approach not constrained by national rules of construction.
- **Limits** (Art 3(3)–(4)): in a purely domestic situation (all other elements in one country), the choice cannot prejudice that country's non-derogable rules. In an all-Member-State situation, it cannot prejudice non-derogable EU law (now less relevant). See overriding-mandatory-rules-and-illegality.md.

### 3. Implied choice
1. **Wording**: Rome Convention Art 3(1) said "demonstrated with reasonable certainty". Rome I says "clearly demonstrated". There is no significance in the change. It brought the English and German texts into line with the French.
2. **Giuliano–Lagarde Report** (p 17):
   - the court may, in the light of all the facts, find that the parties made a **real choice** not expressly stated;
   - but it may not "infer a choice of law that the parties might have made where they had no clear intention of making a choice". That case falls under Art 4;
   - an example is a standard form known to be governed by a particular law, "such as a Lloyd's policy of marine insurance".
3. **Recital 12 Rome I**: an agreement conferring exclusive jurisdiction on a Member State court "should be one of the factors" in deciding whether a choice is clearly demonstrated. Arbitration clauses are not mentioned.
4. **No rule of law dictates intention**: *The Komninos S* [1991] 1 Lloyd's Rep 370, 375 (Bingham LJ). When ascertaining intention, the court cannot be bound by any rule to reach a given conclusion. When considering closest connection, it must weigh all relevant features without giving some preponderant weight.

### 4. *Amin Rasheed Shipping Corp v Kuwait Insurance Co* [1984] AC 50 (HL) (common law)
- **Facts**: the assured was a Liberian company based in Dubai. Its vessel was insured by a Kuwaiti insurer, under a policy issued in Kuwait on the Lloyd's SG form with Institute Clauses. The vessel was seized by the Saudi authorities, and the insurer alleged smuggling. The assured sought leave to serve out in England. That required (1) that the policy was "by its terms, or by implication, governed by English law" (the jurisdiction point) and (2) that England was forum conveniens (the discretion point).
- **Jurisdiction point (Lord Diplock)**:
  - Examine the policy to see whether, by its express terms or necessary implication, the parties evinced a common intention as to the governing system. Its provisions as a whole "point ineluctably" to English marine insurance law (p 62).
  - **Crucial circumstance**: when the policy was made, Kuwait had **no indigenous law of marine insurance**. Kuwait had a 1961 Commercial Code but no marine insurance code until 15 August 1980, and that code had no retrospective effect.
  - Marine insurance is highly idiosyncratic (sue and labour, subrogation, abandonment, constructive total loss). The obsolete language of the Lloyd's SG policy, scheduled to the **Marine Insurance Act 1906**, cannot be understood without that Act and the case law interpreting it (p 64).
  - "Contracts are incapable of existing in a legal vacuum." They must be made by reference to some system of private law. Saying English law is merely "a useful source of persuasive authority" on an international form "begs the whole question". So the proper law was English law.
  - Robert Goff LJ and Bingham J below had treated the form as an international one with little connection to English law. That view was rejected.
- **Discretion point**: leave was still refused. The central issue was factual (smuggling, involving Saudi law). The witnesses were Indian, Bangladeshi and Saudi. Kuwait was accessible, and a Kuwaiti judge would be familiar with Gulf trading and could apply English law.
  - It would be "wholly wrong" for an English court to condemn as inferior another country's (civil law) fact-finding procedure. The exorbitance of service out weighs against leave unless justice could not be obtained abroad, or only at excessive cost, delay or inconvenience. Appeal dismissed.
- **Question**: is it right to infer a choice of English law just because English law is needed to *interpret* the contract? Can a contract be interpreted by one law but governed by another? The CA's view was that interpretation depends on the language and that the need for some governing law does not change that.

### 5. *Oldendorff v Libera Corp* (Rome Convention)
- **[1996] 1 Lloyd's Rep 380 (Clarke J)**: a German partnership and a Japanese corporation agreed a ten-year time charter of two ships to be built in Japan, with a purchase option. It used a well-known English-language form with standard clauses of known English meaning, had no express choice of law, and provided for **London arbitration**.
  - The test under Art 3: is the choice of English law demonstrated with reasonable certainty? It must be a real choice that the parties clearly intended (Giuliano–Lagarde). A purposive, Convention-based approach applies.
  - **At common law**, *Compagnie d'Armement Maritime SA v Compagnie Tunisienne de Navigation SA* [1971] AC 572 held that a London arbitration clause did not by itself make English law the proper law, but it gives guidance on inferences:
    - a clause showing arbitration in a particular country, by arbitrators of that country or carrying on business there (e.g. London maritime arbitrators, London brokers, a local association), **permits an inference** that its law applies;
    - the indication is **much weaker** where the clause specifies a place but leaves appointment to an international body such as the ICC.
  - **Held**: the arbitration clause, in the context of the English form and clauses, was a **strong indication** of a choice of English law as both applicable and curial law. Having agreed a "neutral" forum, the parties reasonably intended a "neutral" law, English, not German or Japanese. The related MOA expressly chose English law and arbitration, which reinforced the conclusion.
- **[1995] 2 Lloyd's Rep 64 (Mance J)**: Libera argued that no contract was concluded, or that the arbitration clause was not incorporated, and invoked Art 8(2) (Japanese law). Arbitration in a third country with an established maritime arbitration system, before arbitrators conversant with shipping, made the idea that the tribunal should apply a foreign law unconvincing. Art 8(2) failed (see scope-of-applicable-law-validity-capacity.md).
- **Question**: is it proper to treat an arbitration or choice of court clause as an indication of the governing law? Note Recital 12 (jurisdiction clauses as a factor) and Art 1(2)(e) (the clauses themselves are excluded).

### 6. What "law" can be chosen?
1. **Only the law of a country**:
   - Art 3 refers to "the law chosen"; Art 4 to "the law of the country where…".
   - **Recital 13**: the Regulation "does not preclude parties from incorporating by reference into their contract a non-State body of law or an international convention".
   - So a choice of the lex mercatoria or general principles is **not** an Art 3 choice of law.
2. *Shamil Bank of Bahrain EC v Beximco Pharmaceuticals Ltd* [2004] EWCA Civ 19, [2004] 1 WLR 1784: a choice of the principles of Sharia was not a choice of the law of a country under the Rome Convention.
3. **Briggs**: the Regulation excludes the lex mercatoria and religious teachings (Jewish law, *Halpern v Halpern* [2007] EWCA Civ 291; Sharia, *Shamil*). Parties who want such rules must use **arbitration**.
   - Non-state law would also be unworkable under Art 4. Which "law" governs a contract between co-religionists in England?
   - Excluding non-state law while allowing incorporation of sufficiently clear principles as terms "is not a bad solution; all others would be far worse".
   - The Art 23 "optional instrument" provision was excluded from retained Rome I.
   - It is "far from clear" that a choice of the laws of the Dubai International Financial Centre is permissible, despite *Dhir v Flutter Entertainment plc* [2021] EWHC 1510 (QB).
4. **Paramount clauses in bills of lading**:
   - Context: carriers used sweeping exemptions. The Hague (1924), Hague-Visby (1968), Hamburg (1978) and Rotterdam (2008) Rules were attempts at unification. The Rotterdam Rules need 20 ratifications and had 5.
   - A paramount clause typically makes the Hague Rules "as enacted in the country of shipment" apply, with fallbacks.
   - *Marifortuna Naviera SA v Government of Ceylon* [1970] 1 Lloyd's Rep 247 (Mocatta J): "paramount" sometimes just means a clause incorporating the Rules, and sometimes also making them prevail over conflicting terms.
   - A foreign statute referred to in a paramount clause operates **not as statute but as contractual terms**. The contract is read as if the Act's provisions were written out in it. It is neither a choice of that foreign law nor an indication that it governs, and it is construed by ordinary contract principles.
5. **Incorporation versus choice of law**:
   - **Time**: a governing law applies as at the date of the event, as a "living and changing body of law". Incorporated rules are frozen as terms.
   - **Construction**: a chosen law is applied by its own principles. Incorporated rules are construed by the governing law's principles.
   - **Restrictions**: there are greater restrictions on choosing a governing law.
   - **Gaps**: if incorporated rules are silent, the governing law fills the gap. If a chosen foreign law is silent, the forum's law may apply.
   - **International conventions** can be incorporated but not chosen as governing law. A convention the forum state has ratified applies by its own terms regardless of the parties. For example, an old clause referring to the Warsaw Convention 1933 yields to the Montreal Convention 1999 if the forum state is party.
   - Incorporation only works sensibly if the rules are clearly identified. That is problematic for "lex mercatoria" or "general principles".

## Debate and critique
- **How real is an "implied" choice?** *Amin Rasheed* finds intention largely in the impossibility of operating the policy without English law. Critics say that confuses the law needed to *interpret* the words with the law that *governs*. The Giuliano–Lagarde warning against invented choices pulls the other way. Rome I's "clearly demonstrated" arguably invites stricter scrutiny, though commentators say the change is linguistic only.
- **Forum clause as choice of law**: *Oldendorff*'s "neutral forum, neutral law" reasoning is commercially attractive but risks collapsing Art 3 into Art 4-style connection analysis. Recital 12 refers only to jurisdiction clauses for Member State courts. Should arbitration clauses carry the same weight? The ICC point (weaker where no local arbitrators are designated) shows the inference is fact-sensitive.
- **Non-state law**: should parties be free to choose the UNIDROIT Principles, lex mercatoria or Sharia in litigation as they can in arbitration?
  - For: autonomy, neutrality, commercial suitability.
  - Against: uncertainty, gaps, proof, Art 4 incoherence (Briggs).
  - Arbitration's flexibility is an advantage; lack of coercive power and appeal are trade-offs.
- **Vita Food's breadth**: no connection is needed. But was the choice in *Vita Food* a device to evade Newfoundland's mandatory rules? Art 3(3) and Art 9 now limit evasion in purely domestic situations and for overriding rules.
- **Paramount clauses**: treating foreign statutes as terms protects party autonomy, but can produce complex hybrids: a governing law, plus incorporated foreign rules, plus overriding conventions such as Hague-Visby under COGSA 1971 (*The Hollandia*).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an **express choice**? Interpret the clause autonomously. Is the chosen "law" a country's law (not lex mercatoria or Sharia)? Is it a split choice, or a later change (Art 3(2))?
2. If not express, is a choice **clearly demonstrated**? Consider:
   - a standard form tied to a legal system (Lloyd's policy; *Amin Rasheed*);
   - technical terms meaningful only under one law;
   - a jurisdiction clause (Recital 12) or arbitration clause (*Oldendorff*: local arbitrators strong, ICC weak);
   - related contracts with express choices (the MOA in *Oldendorff*);
   - course of dealing.

   Do not invent a choice. If unclear, go to Art 4.
3. **Existence or validity of the choice** (Art 3(5), Art 10): apply the putative chosen law, subject to Art 10(2).
4. **Incorporation**: distinguish incorporated foreign statutes or conventions (terms, construed by the governing law) from the governing law itself. Check for overriding conventions the forum applies anyway.
5. **Limits**: Art 3(3) (purely domestic), Arts 5–8 (protected parties), Art 9 (overriding mandatory rules), Art 21 (public policy).
6. **Common law** (pre-1991, or excluded issues): express choice (*Vita Food*: bona fide, legal, not against public policy), then inferred intention (*Amin Rasheed*), then closest connection.
