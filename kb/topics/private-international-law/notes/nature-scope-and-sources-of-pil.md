# Nature, scope and sources of private international law (incl. Brexit)

## Overview
Private international law ("conflict of laws") is the set of rules an English court uses when a case contains a foreign element. That means contact with some legal system other than the forum's. It is national law, sometimes supplemented by treaties the forum has joined. It is not "international" in where it comes from or how it applies, so the rules applied depend on where the claim is brought. The module looks at English rules for cross-border commercial disputes and at how English courts deal with foreign courts. Since Brexit the sources have changed a great deal: the EU Brussels regime on jurisdiction and judgments has gone (apart from transitional cases), while the Rome I and Rome II choice-of-law Regulations stay in force as assimilated domestic law.

## The law

### 1. The three core questions
Every foreign-element case can raise three linked questions:
1. **Jurisdiction**: is it right for the English court to hear the dispute, even though it is connected with a foreign country?
2. **Choice of law**: should the court apply only English law, or does the foreign element mean it should apply another system's law (the *lex causae*)?
3. **Foreign judgments**: has a foreign court already decided the dispute, and should England give that decision effect?

The handbook compares these rules to road signs. They point to the answer and do not decide the merits.

### 2. Terminology
- **"Jurisdiction"** has several meanings. Sometimes it just means a country or legal system. In public international law it covers a state's legislative and adjudicative power. In private international law it means the legal power of a court to rule on a matter put before it. That raises (a) whether the court is **competent** to decide and (b) whether it is **appropriate** for it to decide. A third dimension is whether a court that is competent and appropriate can resist foreign proceedings, and how it should handle parallel proceedings.
- **"Foreign element"**: contact with some system of law other than that of the forum.
- **"Country"**: the whole of a territory under one sovereign and subject to one body of law (a "law district"). Sweden, Italy and New Zealand are countries in this sense. So are England, Scotland, New York and California, because each has its own legal system [1-020]–[1-026]).

### 3. English courts and English law: strengths
- London and English law lead the world in international dispute resolution. The Commercial Court Report 2023–2024 says international cases make up about 75% of the Commercial Court's business. (The induction slides cite the 2022–2023 report figure of about 63%.)
- English common law is by far the most popular governing law for cross-border contracts. The legal services sector supports the UK's position as a financial, insurance and commercial centre.

### 4. The European influence (pre-Brexit)
- Common law private international law rules were traditionally built up through cases. An English statute applied only where English law was the *lex causae*, unless its instruction to the judge was so peremptory that it overrode the ordinary choice-of-law result.
- EU private international law took effect in England under the European Communities Act 1972. Before Brexit, jurisdiction and judgments were mostly governed by EU legislation (the Brussels regime). Choice of law for contractual and non-contractual obligations, maintenance, some family law and insolvency was largely European too. Other EU law, such as freedom of establishment, affected the conflicts treatment of companies.
- The ECHR also matters, through the Human Rights Act 1998.
- Before Brexit there were two regimes. The traditional one depends on serving the defendant under the CPR. The Brussels I regime allocated jurisdiction among Member State courts, mainly triggered by the defendant's domicile in a Member State.

### 5. The position after Brexit
1. **End of the implementation period**: 11pm on 31 December 2020 ("Completion Day"). The UK left without a post-exit agreement on judicial cooperation in civil and commercial matters.
2. **Lugano II**: the UK took part in the Lugano Convention as an EU Member State and during the implementation period, but that ended at Completion Day. The UK applied to accede as an independent state on 8 April 2020. Accession would need the consent of the EU, Denmark, Iceland, Norway and Switzerland. On 22 June 2021 the EU said it was "not in a position to give its consent" (slide 12).
3. **SI 2019/479** (Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments (Amendment) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019, amended by SI 2020/1493 and SI 2020/1574) came into force at the end of the transition period. It revoked Brussels Ia and its predecessors as they applied in the UK, and ended the effect of Lugano 2007 and the EU–Denmark Agreement. Savings provisions keep Lugano 2007 and the 1968 Brussels Convention for cases still pending (slide 14).
4. **Transitional rule (reg 92)**: if a UK court was seised before Completion Day and the proceedings had not ended, the Brussels I Regulation or the Brussels/Lugano Convention still applies, even to later hearings and appeals. Reg 95(2) uses the Art 32 Brussels Ia definition of when a court is seised. In effect, proceedings have been issued and service has not failed. No reference to the CJEU is possible after Completion Day, even in a case still under the European instruments.
5. **Judgments under the transitional rule**: what matters is whether the *foreign* court was seised before Completion Day. If it was, recognition is decided as if the European instrument were still in force and the UK were still a Member State, even if the judgment comes much later (review slide 10).
6. **EU's view** (Notice to Stakeholders, 27 August 2020): EU rules keep applying to proceedings begun before the end of transition and to related proceedings (lis pendens) begun afterwards. For later proceedings, Member State courts use EU instruments or their national rules. Recognition of UK judgments in later proceedings depends on any applicable convention, or otherwise the national law of the state addressed (slides 15–16).
7. **Hague Conventions**: the UK remains party to several Hague Conventions that now apply between the UK and EU states: Service 1965, Divorce 1970, Evidence 1970, Child Abduction 1980, Child Protection 1996, Choice of Court 2005 and Maintenance 2007. The last three are part of UK law independently of EU law, through the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 as amended by the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020 (slide 13). The 2005 Choice of Court Convention has applied to the UK without interruption since 1 October 2015, and was given domestic force on 1 January 2021 (slide 14).
8. **Choice of law**: Rome I and Rome II are retained in all parts of the UK, because they do not depend on reciprocity. They are now "assimilated law" (s 5 Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023) (slide 17). The 2023 Act lets secondary retained EU law, including Rome I and II, be restated or replaced by regulations before 23 June 2026. Whether that will happen is uncertain.

### 6. Why the common law rules matter
- **Choice of law**: common law rules still govern issues outside Rome I and Rome II, such as property and corporate matters, which are central to commercial practice (slide 17).
- **Jurisdiction and judgments**: before Brexit, common law rules applied outside the Brussels regime. They now apply generally, as the rules already used for non-EU, non-EFTA cases. The limits the Brussels regime put on forum non conveniens no longer apply. The review session warns students to explain why the Brussels regime is relevant whenever they mention it.

## Key authorities
- *The Atlantic Star* (Case Study 1). A Dutch owner's vessel (A) collided with two others near Antwerp. Proceedings were already under way against A in the Antwerp Commercial Court, and a court-appointed surveyor's report there seemed to favour A. R, another Dutch owner, then brought an action in rem in the English Admiralty Court when A's ship called at an English port. A sought a stay.
  - Brandon J [1972] 1 Lloyd's Rep 534 held that a stay required the action to be oppressive, vexatious or an abuse of process. Convenience or multiple proceedings alone were not enough. Stay refused.
  - CA [1972] 2 Lloyd's Rep 446, 451: Lord Denning MR said any "friendly foreigner" may come to England. If that is forum shopping, England is "a good place to shop in, both for the quality of the goods and the speed of the service". Appeal dismissed.
  - HL [1973] 2 Lloyd's Rep 197, 200: Lord Reid said this recalled the days when people in this island felt "an innate superiority" over other races, and that the time was ripe to re-examine the "rather insular doctrine". The words "oppressive or vexatious" were to be read liberally. A distinction was drawn between a case where England is the claimant's natural forum and one where the claimant merely comes to serve his own ends. By 3–2 (Lords Reid, Wilberforce and Kilbrandon; Lords Morris and Simon dissenting) a stay was granted. R's advantage from arresting the ship was outweighed by A's disadvantages: delay, difficulty getting witnesses, extra expense, and A having already faced a full inquiry by the Antwerp surveyor at R's instigation.
  - Significance: the start of the move towards forum non conveniens, later recognised in *Spiliada* [1987] AC 460.
- *Song Mao v Tate & Lyle Industries Ltd* (Case Study 2; EWHC (QB), Claim No 2013 Folio 451). Economic land concessions were granted to KKS and KKP in Cambodia in 2006. In 2009 they agreed a five-year contract to sell raw sugar to Tate & Lyle. In 2013 about two hundred Cambodian villagers sued the English-domiciled T&L companies in conversion, claiming they owned the cane from which sugar delivered in London was made. Issues raised:
  - Jurisdiction: under Brussels I (2001) the English domiciled defendants could be sued in England as of right. Even at common law, service within England would found jurisdiction. But would English courts be the best forum, given they would have to rule on Cambodian land title?
  - Characterisation: is conversion a "tort" under Rome II or the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1995, or a property issue under the common law?
  - Applicable law: probably English law for the tort, because the alleged interference happened in England. But the claimants' property interest in the sugar is an incidental question for the *lex situs*, Cambodian law, which would have to be proved.
  - Privacy: the case later disappeared from the court listings, probably because of a confidential settlement.
- European Communities Act 1972: the vehicle for EU private international law before Brexit.
- Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020: gives the Hague 2005, 1996 and 2007 Conventions domestic force through the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982.
- SI 2019/479 (as amended): revocation of the Brussels and Lugano instruments, with transitional savings (regs 92, 95(2)).
- Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 s 5: the "assimilated law" label for Rome I and II.

## Debate and critique
- **"Innate superiority" and forum shopping**: Lord Denning's "good place to shop in" against Lord Reid's rebuke frames a lasting essay theme. Is England a neutral, high-quality forum attracting business on merit, or a forum too willing to keep cases with little connection to England? The formative assessment question builds on this passage. The slides ask whether London is still a good forum to shop in, given judicial specialisation and competition. Examples are the Singapore International Commercial Court (5 January 2015), modelled on the English Commercial Court, and the English Financial List.
- **Brexit uncertainty**: without Lugano or a successor, the predictable Brussels rules (domicile-based jurisdiction, the lis pendens first-seised rule, near-automatic recognition) have been replaced by discretionary common law rules and a limited set of Hague instruments. Whether that is a loss of certainty or a gain in flexibility is a good essay question. Link it to the return of forum non conveniens.
- **Mills on Song Mao**: Alex Mills says the case looks "ordinary", since English defendants were sued in England, which is common in a globalised "law market". He finds it notable for three reasons:
  - the private international law questions it raises, including the peculiar features of domicile jurisdiction under Brussels I;
  - the fact that it is at heart about Cambodian land rights, a field traditionally kept out of global dispute resolution as part of territorial sovereignty;
  - its link to the supply chain connecting the Cambodian claimants with the English consumer-facing defendants.

  This leads into the wider question of using home-state courts to hold corporate actors to account for overseas harms (see multinational-companies-human-rights.md).
- **"Private" litigation**: confidential settlement and the removal of filings limit what public law-making such litigation produces.
- **Essay guidance**: focus on English private international law as applied after Brexit. Keep jurisdiction and choice-of-law issues distinct. Start any discussion of a convention's scope from its opening articles. Back every point with case law. Arbitration-related topics are acceptable only as to the English court's role (validity of arbitration agreements, stays, anti-suit injunctions, interim measures), not enforcement of awards.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Spot the foreign elements**: parties' domicile or residence, place of contracting and performance, place of the tort, situs of property, foreign proceedings or judgments.
2. **Sort the issues into the three questions**: jurisdiction, choice of law, judgments. Deal with each in turn, and do not mix jurisdiction and choice-of-law reasoning.
3. **Date check**: were English proceedings issued (and served) before 11pm on 31 December 2020? If so, the Brussels/Lugano regime may still apply (SI 2019/479 reg 92). For a foreign EU judgment, ask when the foreign court was seised.
4. **Jurisdiction after Brexit**: use the common law rules (service in or service out, forum non conveniens, jurisdiction agreements), unless the Hague 2005 Convention applies to an exclusive choice of court agreement.
5. **Choice of law**: ask whether the issue falls within retained Rome I (contract) or Rome II (non-contractual obligations). If not (property, corporations, defamation), use the common law rules.
6. **Foreign law**: remember it must be pleaded and proved. If it is not, English law applies (see foreign-law-and-substance-procedure.md).
7. **Trap**: citing Owusu or Brussels Ia Art 4 as current law for a post-2020 claim. They apply only under the transitional rules.
