# The 2019 Hague Judgments Convention and international commercial courts

## Overview
After Brexit, the near-automatic movement of judgments between the UK and the EU ended. Apart from the narrow 2005 Choice of Court Convention, the main replacement is the **Hague Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Judgments in Civil and Commercial Matters 2019**. It came into force for the UK on 1 July 2025 and covers proceedings begun on or after that date. It works through "filters": jurisdictional bases in Art 5 and an exhaustive list of defences in Art 7. Briggs calls it "a wan shadow" of Brussels/Lugano. At the same time, states are competing for litigation by creating **international commercial courts** (Singapore, China, the Gulf, European states). This raises questions about forum choice, the "adjudication business", and whether London's attraction depends on a special court or on the quality of its justice.

## The law

### A. The 2019 Hague Judgments Convention

**1. Status and timing**
- Inserted into the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 by s 3F, with the text in Sch 3H (Briggs).
- The UK signed on 12 January 2024 and ratified on 27 June 2024. It entered into force for the UK on **1 July 2025**, 12 months after ratification under Arts 28(2)(a) and 29(2) (Briggs).
- Contracting States (as sources state): EU Member States except Denmark (from 1 November 2023), Ukraine (1 November 2023), Uruguay (1 October 2024) and the UK. Signed but not ratified by the US (2022), Costa Rica, Israel, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Russia (Briggs Part II).
- **Temporal scope**: for incoming EU judgments and outgoing English judgments, the proceedings must have been **commenced on or after 1 July 2025** (Briggs Part III). So the Convention "will get off to a slow start".

**2. Material scope** (Art 1; Briggs Part IV)
- It covers recognition and enforcement in one Contracting State of a **judgment** of a court of another, in **civil or commercial matters**. Revenue, customs and administrative matters are excluded (Art 1(1)).
- **"Judgment"** (Art 3): any decision on the merits by a court, whatever it is called, including costs orders.
  - Interim measures of protection are **not** judgments.
  - Default judgments are included if on the merits.
  - It is not limited to money judgments.
  - Briggs: dismissal under the Limitation Act 1980, or striking out a defence for non-compliance, should still count as on the merits.
  - Exequatur decisions (local enforcement of a third state's judgment) are not judgments ("exequatur sur exequatur ne vaut").
- **Excluded matters** (Art 2(1)) include:
  - (e) insolvency and analogous matters;
  - (f) carriage of passengers and goods;
  - (i) validity, nullity or dissolution of legal persons and the validity of decisions of their organs;
  - (k) defamation;
  - (l) privacy;
  - (m) intellectual property.
- **Arbitration** and related proceedings are excluded (Art 2(3)). So Contracting States have no obligation to give effect to an English anti-suit injunction protecting an arbitration agreement.

**3. Bases for recognition: Art 5 "filters"**
- The recognising court alone decides whether a basis is satisfied. Art 5 supplies the connection between the losing party, or the cause of action, and the foreign court, which justifies holding the loser to the decision.
- Grounds (a), (b), (d): a **residential** connection of the person against whom recognition is sought with the state of origin.
- Grounds (c), (e), (f): **consent or submission** to the original court.
- Grounds (g)–(l): framed by **cause of action** (particular kinds of claim).
- Ground (m): **non-exclusive choice of court agreements**.

**4. Defences: Art 7, exhaustive (Art 4(1))**
- (a) defective service or notification, or service in the requested state offending its public policy;
- (b) fraud;
- (c) recognition "manifestly" incompatible with public policy;
- (d) proceedings contrary to a choice of court agreement;
- (e) conflict with a local judgment between the same parties;
- (f) conflict with an earlier recognisable foreign judgment.
- Art 7(2): recognition may be postponed or refused if the recognising court was seised first of proceedings between the same parties on the same subject matter, and there is a close connection between the dispute and the recognising state.
- The judgment must be effective, and for enforcement enforceable, in the state of origin. Briggs notes that Art 4(3) is obscure on what "recognition" entails, for example effects on non-parties.

**5. Relationship with other routes** (Briggs Part X)
- The Convention "giveth but taketh nothing away".
  - Art 15 preserves national law routes, including the common law.
  - Art 23 preserves other treaties, including any revived pre-EU bilateral treaties.
- **Enforcement**: the creditor may choose the Convention (registration under the 1982 Act, perhaps quicker) or a common law action on the judgment.
- **Recognition**: at common law it is enough to plead the judgment as a fact. Under the Convention, registration and a possible set-aside application are needed, which may be slower.

**6. Comparison with the 2005 Convention**
The 2005 Convention covers only judgments of courts chosen in **exclusive** choice of court agreements. Hague States are listed as the EU states, the UK, Singapore, Mexico, Albania, Moldova, Ukraine and Montenegro. Under Arts 8–9 the chosen court's judgment must be recognised, subject to limited grounds (see hague-choice-of-court-convention-2005.md).

### B. International commercial courts and the adjudication business
1. **The trend**: judicial specialisation and competition for judicial business.
   - **Singapore International Commercial Court (SICC)**, January 2015: inspired by the English Commercial Court, to provide "a neutral and well-regarded dispute resolution hub" in Asia. Its jurisdiction is founded on party **consent**, which gives party autonomy an enhanced role.
   - **China International Commercial Court (CICC)**, June 2018: party autonomy is one basis of its jurisdiction. China is also promoting its maritime courts as an international centre, part of its policy to strengthen its voice in international legal affairs.
   - **England**: the Commercial Court was not set up specifically for cross-border disputes but attracts foreign parties. That suggests it is not a discrete "international commercial court" that draws parties, but the **quality of justice and the manner of its administration**. England has also rebranded and created the Financial List.
2. **Bookman, "The Adjudication Business"** (2020) 45 Yale J Int'l L 227:
   - Providing adjudication services is a "growth industry". London and New York are traditional litigation forums; London, Paris, New York and Geneva traditional arbitral seats.
   - New international commercial courts opened in Dubai (2004), Qatar (2009), Singapore (2015), Abu Dhabi (2015), Kazakhstan (2018) and China (2018). After the Brexit vote, Germany, France, the Netherlands, Belgium and Switzerland opened or considered such courts.
   - They tend to be English-language friendly, receptive to common law procedure and substantive law, technologically advanced, and borrow features of arbitration (confidentiality, customised procedure). They compete on the quality of legal services and procedure rather than supplying substantive law.
   - **Three categories**:
     - (1) **investment-seeking courts** (Qatar, Dubai; also Abu Dhabi and the Astana International Financial Centre Court, whose first chief justice was Lord Woolf), built to attract investment;
     - (2) **"litigation destinations"** (Singapore and the European courts), aiming to be regional gold standards;
     - (3) **China's CICC**, a one-stop shop focused on Belt and Road disputes, with unique potential for global influence.

## Debate and critique
- **Briggs: "no match" for Brussels/Lugano**:
  - The most dramatic difference is that a judgment debtor may contest the original court's "jurisdiction" in the Art 5 sense, unlike under Brussels.
  - Cross-Channel movement of judgments is now "more troublesome, expensive and painful", and the Convention offers only "a modest amelioration".
  - In EU eyes, English judgments are now equated with those from Ukraine and Uruguay.
  - Practical lesson: a properly drafted **choice of court or arbitration agreement** matters more than ever, since Hague 2005 or the New York Convention 1958 give more effective enforcement.
- **Exclusions**: defamation, privacy, IP, insolvency, carriage of goods and arbitration-related matters are outside the Convention. In shipping, for example, bill of lading claims (carriage of goods) fall outside it, so the common law and local rules still matter. Compare the *Hin-Pro* saga.
- **ASIs not exportable**: the arbitration exclusion means the Convention cannot spread English anti-suit relief. That may increase reliance on in personam coercion and its limits (*UniCredit* 2025).
- **Competition for adjudication**: is the rise of international commercial courts healthy competition that improves dispute resolution, or does it fragment the field and threaten London's position? Commentators suggest London's draw lies in quality, not labels. Bookman stresses state motives (investment, influence) and borrowing from arbitration. Mills notes Singapore's court as a "market disruptor" in a globalised "law market".
- **Forum choice for parties**: enforceability of the eventual judgment (Hague 2005 or 2019, bilateral treaties, the common law), reliability of jurisdiction clauses in the counterparty's state, availability of interim relief and ASIs, neutrality, cost and speed, quality of judges, and confidentiality.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Incoming judgment from a Contracting State**:
   - Were the proceedings begun on or after 1 July 2025?
   - Civil or commercial matter, not excluded by Art 2? Is it a judgment on the merits (not interim)?
   - Is an Art 5 basis satisfied (residence, consent or submission, cause-of-action link, non-exclusive clause)?
   - Is any Art 7 defence available (exhaustive)?
   - Is it effective or enforceable in the state of origin?

   If it fails, try the common law (Art 15 keeps that route open).
2. **Exclusive choice of court agreement**: use Hague 2005 instead (stronger).
3. **Outgoing English judgment to the EU**: same mirror-image analysis. Advise on drafting an exclusive clause or arbitration for future contracts.
4. **Choice of procedure**: registration under the 1982 Act or an action on the judgment at common law. For mere res judicata, the common law may be simpler.
5. **Forum-selection advice**: weigh enforceability and international commercial court options against London.
