# Common law jurisdiction: concept, service within the jurisdiction and submission

## Overview
At common law, an English court's jurisdiction *in personam* rests on one procedural event: valid service of the claim form on the defendant. If the defendant is in England, service is available as of right ("service in"). If the defendant is abroad, the court's permission is normally needed ("service out"). Competence obtained by service is then qualified by the court's discretion to stay, mainly under forum non conveniens and foreign jurisdiction clauses. Some subject matters, such as title to foreign land, are outside jurisdiction altogether. After Brexit these common law rules apply to almost all defendants, including those domiciled in EU states, so they are now the core of English jurisdiction law.

## The law

### 1. The concept of jurisdiction
- **Jurisdiction to adjudicate** is the court's authority to decide proceedings. It raises two questions: is the court **competent**, and is it **appropriate** for it to decide? A third dimension is whether a competent and appropriate court should resist foreign proceedings, and how it should deal with parallel litigation.
- **Briggs**: a court must have jurisdiction over the *subject matter* of the claim and *personal* jurisdiction over the defendant. A court has inherent power to decide on its own jurisdiction. "Jurisdiction" is a slippery term of art (Briggs Ch 2).
- **Justifying principles in personam** (the European conception):
  - **general jurisdiction**: an appropriate link between defendant and forum, so the claimant sues in the defendant's court (domicile, residence or presence);
  - **specific jurisdiction**: the main facts of the claim happened in the forum, such as the place of performance of a contract, and jurisdiction is limited to claims arising from those facts;
  - **consent**: a jurisdiction agreement (limited to claims within its scope), or defending on the merits without objecting;
  - **in rem**: only the presence of the res (such as a ship) in the territory.

### 2. The common law conception
1. An English court has **inherent jurisdiction** over a defendant once process has been, or is deemed to have been, served, together with the **power to decline** to exercise it on the defendant's application. It also has inherent power to regulate its own procedure.
2. Jurisdiction is treated as **entirely a matter of procedure** and depends wholly on serving the claim form, originally a writ of summons issued in the monarch's name.
3. **Two types of service**:
   - **service in**: the defendant is within the jurisdiction;
   - **service out**: the defendant is outside, and permission is needed unless legislation or the CPR say otherwise (for example CPR 6.33(2B) for English jurisdiction agreements).

   Appropriateness is tested differently in each (see forum-non-conveniens-spiliada.md and service-out-of-the-jurisdiction.md).
4. **Originating process** triggers jurisdiction. It must be issued by the court and served on the respondent.

### 3. Service within the jurisdiction (as of right)
- **Individuals**: any claimant may serve anyone *present* in England. The common law treats a person present in England as having put themselves in the position of being liable to be summoned. Presence means physical presence, however brief. The US equivalent is "tag" jurisdiction. Service on a person appointed in England to accept service counts as service on a present defendant (Briggs Ch 2). For the marginal case of good service "within" the jurisdiction on someone temporarily abroad, Briggs cites *SSL International plc v TTK LIG Ltd* [2011] EWCA Civ 1170, [54].
- **Mechanics**: time, manner and place are set by procedural rules. Service may be personal, by post, by certain electronic means, or by other court-approved means (CPR r 6.3) (Briggs Ch 2).
- **Companies**:
  - An English company may be served at its registered office (Companies Act 2006 s 1139(1)). If it is in liquidation, service is on the liquidator with permission (Insolvency Act 1986 s 130(2)).
  - An overseas company may be served on the person authorised to accept service. Failing that, process may be left at or posted to any place of business within the jurisdiction (CA 2006 s 1139(2)).
  - A "place of business" is a fixed and definite place from which the company's business is carried on: *South India Shipping Corp v Export-Import Bank of Korea* [1985] 1 WLR 585 (CA). A place where binding contracts are made will probably qualify (cf *Adams v Cape Industries plc* [1990] Ch 433).
  - CPR Part 6 methods add to the statutory methods: *Saab v Saudi American Bank* [1999] 1 WLR 1861 (CA).
- **In rem** (admiralty): jurisdiction is founded by serving the vessel while it is in territorial waters. This is how R sued in *The Atlantic Star*.

### 4. Upholding jurisdiction: submission
- A defendant served within the jurisdiction may also have **submitted**. The categories of submission are not closed. The critical question is whether what was done looked like acceptance of the jurisdiction and a waiver of any objection that might otherwise have been raised.
- **Procedure (Briggs)**: a defendant who wishes to challenge jurisdiction must apply under **CPR Part 11**. If the defendant acknowledges service but makes no Part 11 application, or takes a sufficiently unequivocal step in the action unrelated to a jurisdiction challenge, they are treated as having submitted (*Deutsche Bank AG London Branch v Petromena ASA* [2015] EWCA Civ 226, [32]).
- A Part 11 challenge puts everything except provisional and protective measures on hold.

### 5. Disputing jurisdiction after service in England
Briggs Ch 2 lists the objections:
1. **No legal basis**: an applicable convention requires suit elsewhere, for example Hague 2005 Art 6 where an exclusive choice of court agreement designates another Contracting State's court. Pre-Brexit, the Brussels I Regulation played this role. The defendant may also plead personal immunity, lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, or defective service. If upheld, service is set aside and the action dismissed.
2. **Stay** in favour of another court: forum non conveniens (*Spiliada*), or a foreign jurisdiction or arbitration clause, where a "strong cause" is needed to resist a stay.
3. **Foreign jurisdiction clauses**. The application is for a stay, which is discretionary.
   - The claimant suing in breach carries a heavy burden to show why the breach should not lead to a stay.
   - "Strong cause" is needed to displace the presumption that the agreement will be enforced.
   - Validity and construction of the clause are governed by the law of the contract containing it. English law reads such clauses widely and inclusively.
   - Damages for breach may be available.
   - Details are in jurisdiction-agreements-common-law.md.

### 6. Matters over which English courts lack jurisdiction
Parties' submission cannot cure an absence of subject-matter jurisdiction (Briggs Ch 2):
- **Title to foreign land**: the *Moçambique* rule [1893] AC 602. There are two exceptions: personal obligations relating to foreign land, and claims in tort or trespass to foreign land. Statute now allows claims in tort relating to foreign land unless principally concerned with title (Briggs).
- **Defamation**: no jurisdiction unless England and Wales is clearly the most appropriate place to bring the action (Defamation Act 2013; see cross-border-defamation.md).
- **Validity of foreign patents**: no jurisdiction at common law.
- Matters where **public international law** denies jurisdiction, such as immunities.

### 7. Brexit and the renewed role of the common law
- Before Brexit the common law rules applied only to cases wholly outside the Brussels/Lugano regime, or where Art 6 of the Regulation preserved national ("residual") rules.
- Since the end of the transition period (subject to transitional cases) they apply to **all** defendants, except where a convention or statute makes special provision. The Brussels limits on forum non conveniens no longer apply.
- Within the UK, allocation between parts of the UK follows the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 s 16 and Sch 4. The court keeps its power to stay on forum non conveniens grounds (*Kennedy v National Trust for Scotland* [2019] EWCA Civ 648, cited in Briggs).

## Key authorities
- *British South Africa Co v Companhia de Moçambique* [1893] AC 602: no jurisdiction over title to foreign land.
- *The Atlantic Star* [1973] (HL): an action in rem founded on arresting a ship in an English port. It shows how easily jurisdiction "as of right" arises, and why a discretion to stay became necessary.
- *South India Shipping Corp v Export-Import Bank of Korea* [1985] 1 WLR 585 (CA): meaning of "place of business".
- *Saab v Saudi American Bank* [1999] 1 WLR 1861 (CA): CPR methods supplement statutory service on companies.
- *Deutsche Bank AG London Branch v Petromena ASA* [2015] EWCA Civ 226: what counts as an unequivocal step amounting to submission.
- Companies Act 2006 s 1139: service on companies.
- CPR Part 11: the procedure for disputing jurisdiction. CPR r 6.3: methods of service. CPR 6.33(2B): service out without permission for English jurisdiction agreements.

## Debate and critique
- **Presence-based jurisdiction is exorbitant**: jurisdiction founded on fleeting presence ("tag") has no necessary link with the dispute. The common law answers that the discretion to stay (forum non conveniens) controls it. That makes jurisdiction a two-step process: easy competence, then discretionary appropriateness. Contrast the European model, which prefers fixed connecting factors such as domicile and gives little discretion.
- **Procedure or principle?** Treating jurisdiction as "entirely a matter of procedure" (service) hides the underlying justifications (connection, consent, presence of the res). Those justifications come back in through the discretion and the service-out gateways.
- **After Brexit**: should England have joined Lugano or a similar scheme? The return of discretion gives flexibility but removes certainty for EU-domiciled defendants and claimants, especially in group actions against UK parent companies (see multinational-companies-human-rights.md).
- **Subject-matter exclusions**: the *Moçambique* rule rests on territorial sovereignty over land. Mills's discussion of *Song Mao* (land rights in Cambodia) shows the tension when title to foreign land arises indirectly in a tort claim.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Where is the defendant?** In England at the time of service (individual), or with a registered office or place of business here (company)? Then service in is as of right. If abroad, go to service out (gateway, merits, forum conveniens), unless an English jurisdiction clause allows service without permission (CPR 6.33(2B)).
2. **Is there a subject-matter bar?** Title to foreign land, validity of foreign patents, defamation under the 2013 Act, immunity.
3. **Has the defendant submitted?** Look for failure to apply under CPR Part 11, or steps on the merits.
4. **Is a stay available?**
   - Hague 2005 exclusive clause for another Contracting State: the court must decline (Art 6).
   - Foreign jurisdiction clause outside Hague: stay unless strong cause.
   - Otherwise: forum non conveniens under *Spiliada*.
5. **Date**: proceedings before 31 December 2020 may still be under Brussels/Lugano.
6. **Traps**: treating presence as the end of the analysis (always consider a stay); forgetting that a company can be "present" through a place of business; assuming submission can cure lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
