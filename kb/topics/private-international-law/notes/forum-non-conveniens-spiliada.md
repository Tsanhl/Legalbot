# Forum non conveniens and the Spiliada test (service-in cases)

## Overview
Where a defendant has been validly served in England, the court has jurisdiction as of right. It may still stay the action if another available forum is clearly more appropriate. The modern test comes from Lord Goff in *Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd* [1987] AC 460. It grew out of *The Atlantic Star* and *MacShannon v Rockware Glass*. The test has two limbs: first, the defendant must show a clearly more appropriate available forum; second, the claimant may show that justice nonetheless requires a trial in England. The doctrine is again central after Brexit. While Brussels Ia applied, *Owusu v Jackson* stopped English courts staying claims against UK-domiciled defendants. That restriction has gone for proceedings started after 31 December 2020, which matters greatly for group actions against UK parent companies.

## The law

### 1. Basis and procedure
- If service is validly made in England, the court can decline to exercise jurisdiction on forum non conveniens grounds. The exception is where Parliament has curtailed or removed the inherent power to stay, as in *The Hollandia* [1983] 1 AC 565.
- The defendant applies for a stay under CPR Part 11. A stay leaves the action pending but in abeyance, and it can be lifted if the defendant breaks an undertaking given to the court.
- **Rationale**: if the parties are content with an English trial, nobody stands in their way. If they disagree, there is no compelling reason why the claimant should automatically get an English trial.

### 2. History
1. Before *The Atlantic Star*, a stay required the English action to be vexatious, oppressive or an abuse of process (Brandon J [1972] 1 Lloyd's Rep 534). Lord Denning MR called England "a good place to shop in".
2. The HL in *The Atlantic Star* [1973] (Lord Reid) read "oppressive or vexatious" liberally and contrasted a claimant for whom England is the natural forum with one who "merely comes here to serve his own ends". By 3–2 it granted a stay in favour of Antwerp.
3. *MacShannon v Rockware Glass Ltd* (1978), Lord Diplock, set out two conditions (as quoted by Helston):
   - (a) another forum to which the defendant is amenable where justice can be done at substantially less inconvenience and expense; and
   - (b) the stay must not deprive the claimant of a legitimate personal or juridical advantage.
4. *Spiliada* (HL 1986) restated this as the "appropriate forum" principle and applied common principles to stay and service-out cases.

### 3. The Spiliada test (Lord Goff at 476–478)
**Facts**: Cansulex shipped sulphur from Vancouver to India on the *Spiliada*, a Liberian vessel. The owners said the sulphur was wet and corroded the holds. They obtained leave to serve out in Canada because the contract was governed by English law. Staughton J refused to set aside leave. He was already trying a similar action (*The Cambridgeshire*) involving Cansulex. The CA reversed; the HL restored Staughton J. The case concerned service out, but the HL laid down principles common to both service-out and stay cases.

**First limb (burden on the defendant)**
1. The defendant must show some other **available** forum with competent jurisdiction that is **the appropriate forum**: one with which the action has "a closer and more real connection than it has with England" (*VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp* [2013] UKSC 5; *Deutsche Bank AG* [2009] EWCA Civ 725 at [55]).
2. **Availability**: the foreign court must have personal jurisdiction over the defendant, through presence or residence, or advance contractual submission. If the claimant needs the foreign court's permission, it is enough that the defendant has undertaken to submit by the time of the stay hearing. Whether the foreign court would decide the claim in the claimant's favour, and whether the trial there would be fair, belong to the second limb.
3. **"Clearly or distinctly more appropriate"**: *Unwired Planet International Ltd v Huawei Technologies (UK) Co Ltd* [2020] UKSC 37 at [94]. The test is comparative, not absolute. What matters is the strength of connection between the facts and the forum.
4. **Rules of thumb** for the natural forum:
   - tort: the place of the tort;
   - corporate governance: the place of incorporation;
   - insurance or reinsurance governed by English law: England;
   - the court where all issues between all parties can be resolved in one proceeding;
   - validity of IP rights: the place of creation or protection.
5. The defendant must identify the **particular issues** to be decided and why they are better tried abroad.
6. **Factors**:
   - **Personal connections**: if both parties reside in one country, it is likely the natural forum. That is partly why claimants in environmental torts sue the foreign parent company (e.g. *Lungowe v Vedanta* [2019] UKSC 20). Residence acquired for artificial reasons, or not that of the real party, counts for less (*Spiliada* at 481–482).
   - **Events and evidence**: location of witnesses; translation, which affects assessment of credibility; witnesses who cannot be compelled abroad. The place of a tort weighs heavily, especially if its law will apply, but less so if the elements are scattered. Remote evidence has reduced, but not removed, this factor.
   - **Applicable law**: difficult foreign law points abroad, but not all foreign laws are equally hard for an English court, the foreign court may apply another law, and there may be several issues. An express English choice of law weighs towards England.
   - **Lis alibi pendens and "the Cambridgeshire factor"**: in *Spiliada*, the experience gained by lawyers, experts and judge in the parallel *Cambridgeshire* action tipped the balance to England. Later attempts to rely on this have failed. Parallel litigation in complex multi-party claims may even justify not enforcing an English jurisdiction clause (*Donohue v Armco Inc* [2001] UKHL 64).
   - **The overall shape of the litigation**: the analysis is evaluative, not a mechanical weighing. It considers the expected defence as well as the claim. Beware selective pleading: in *Lubbe v Cape plc* [2000] 1 WLR 1545 (HL), asbestos victims sued only the English parent, presumably to give the case an English flavour and guard against a stay.
7. If the defendant shows a clearly more appropriate available forum, a stay will **normally** be granted.

**Second limb (burden on the claimant)**
1. A stay will be refused if justice requires a trial in England. The claimant must show by **clear and cogent evidence** that it would be unjust to send them abroad (*The Abidin Daver* [1984] AC 398).
2. **Juridical advantages**: some usually carry little weight (higher damages, fuller disclosure, interest). Others are clearly significant (legal aid or funding, experience of the courts, fair trial, enforceability).
3. **Differences in law and procedure**: an isolated difference from English rules is not enough, because every decent system strikes its own balance. Examples that may justify refusing a stay:
   - extreme delay, especially in personal injury rather than commercial cases;
   - disproportionate foreign costs rules;
   - funding available only in England;
   - the foreign court's inability to join all parties, with a risk of irreconcilable judgments.
4. **Time bars**: if the claimant would be time-barred abroad, a stay may be unjust (*Spiliada* at 483). The key question is whether the claimant has only himself to blame. Normally no real injustice is done to the defendant by keeping the case in England. Under the Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984 s 1 the lex causae's time bar applies anyway. Where the stay is sought under an exclusive foreign jurisdiction clause, a claimant who failed to protect its position in the agreed forum will rarely be excused.
5. **Differences in outcome** are normally not weighty. A stay may be refused if the foreign court will not respect the parties' express choice of law. The plea "I would win in England but lose abroad" is inadmissible except in that exceptional case. For proceedings started before Completion Day, unenforceability of the foreign judgment under the Brussels regime could weigh against a stay.
6. **Attacks on the foreign court**:
   - Lord Diplock in *The Abidin Daver* accepted that the "possibility cannot be excluded" that some countries' courts will not provide justice. The Human Rights Act 1998 may require the court to rule on such submissions.
   - *AK Investment CJSC v Kyrgyz Mobil Tel Ltd* [2011] UKPC 7, [2012] 1 WLR 1804. It is enough to show a **real risk** that justice will not be obtained abroad because of incompetence, lack of independence or corruption ([95]). There is no rule against examining whether a foreign court system is corrupt. Comity only demands **cogent evidence** before such a finding ([101]). Otherwise the paradox would follow that the worse the foreign system, the less the court could say so. On the facts, without a trial in the Isle of Man there would be no trial anywhere ([151]), so "practical justice" favoured the local forum.
   - In a service-in case, a real risk of injustice abroad can justify refusing a stay.
7. **Funding**: *Connelly v RTZ Corp plc* [1997] (HL). Connelly, a Scottish resident, developed throat cancer allegedly from work in a uranium mine in Namibia, and sued the English parent companies. The general rule is that lack of financial assistance abroad will not by itself defeat a stay. Exceptionally, it can matter where substantial justice cannot be done without it. Because the case was complex and needed expert scientific evidence, it could not be presented at all in Namibia without funding, so no stay was granted. The HL also held that s 31(1)(b) Legal Aid Act 1988 did not stop the court considering the availability of legal aid.

### 4. Overarching principle
The question is whether the interests of all the parties and the ends of justice require a stay. The two limbs are "pointers rather than boundary marks". The decision is largely an evaluation for the first-instance judge, and appeals, especially from the Commercial Court, should be rare.

### 5. Comparative: the Australian approach
Australia grants a stay only if the local court is a **"clearly inappropriate forum"**. In practice its analysis defers more to a local claimant's choice of forum than to a foreigner's. Many leading Australian cases involve a consumer-type local claimant suing a powerful foreign entity, for whom an Australian trial would hardly be a burden.

### 6. Brexit
- Under Brussels Ia, a defendant domiciled in a Member State had to be sued there. *Owusu v Jackson* (C-281/02) [2005] QB 801 made forum non conveniens arguments irrelevant for UK-domiciled defendants.
- Since the end of the transition period, forum non conveniens again applies to UK-domiciled companies, adding a hurdle for claimants in overseas-harm litigation.

## Key authorities
- *Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd* [1987] AC 460 (HL): the two-limb test and the Cambridgeshire factor. England was the appropriate forum.
- *The Atlantic Star* [1973] (HL): the shift away from an "insular" approach.
- *MacShannon v Rockware Glass* (1978): Lord Diplock's interim two-stage test.
- *The Abidin Daver* [1984] AC 398: clear and cogent evidence; the possibility of injustice abroad.
- *VTB Capital v Nutritek* [2013] UKSC 5; *Unwired Planet v Huawei* [2020] UKSC 37, [94]: "clearly or distinctly more appropriate".
- *Connelly v RTZ* [1997] (HL): lack of funding abroad can exceptionally defeat a stay.
- *Lubbe v Cape plc* [2000] 1 WLR 1545 (HL): selective pleading against the English parent.
- *AK Investment v Kyrgyz Mobil Tel* [2011] UKPC 7: real risk of injustice; cogent evidence; comity.
- *Donohue v Armco* [2001] UKHL 64: parallel litigation and the enforcement of jurisdiction clauses.
- *The Hollandia* [1983] 1 AC 565: statute may remove the power to stay.
- *Owusu v Jackson* (C-281/02): no stay for EU-domiciled defendants under Brussels. No longer binding after Brexit for new proceedings.

## Debate and critique
- **Convenience or propriety? (Helen Helston, Denning LJ)**: *Spiliada* did more than adopt forum non conveniens. By choosing what is "appropriate" rather than "convenient", Lord Goff turned it into an "appropriate forum" doctrine, so talk of "convenience" may mislead. *Spiliada* also unified the principles for service out (then Order 11) and stays, with the burden reversed. Helston criticises later first-instance reasoning that mixed the old "vexatious or oppressive" language with the appropriate forum test.
- **Was the Cambridgeshire factor right?** Making the outcome turn on the chance existence of another case involving Cansulex in England looks like a factor outside the dispute between the parties. Later courts have not extended it.
- **Forum shopping and the live question**: Lord Denning's view of the English forum as a superior product, set against Lord Reid's rebuke. Should English courts welcome litigants or defer to natural forums? Consider the business of litigation and international commercial courts.
- **Judging foreign courts**: is it right for English judges to hear arguments that foreign courts are corrupt? *AK Investment* answers yes, with comity requiring cogent evidence. Critics see a risk of chauvinism, and of satellite litigation that is costly for claimants.
- **Australia's "clearly inappropriate" test** gives more protection to local claimants. Would a similar approach better protect weaker parties in England (for example tort victims abroad), or encourage forum shopping?
- **Access to justice in MNC litigation**: *Connelly*, *Lubbe* and *Vedanta* show funding, group litigation capacity and risk of injustice weighing towards England. Post-Brexit, the revived doctrine may be used to send such claims back to host states (see *Limbu v Dyson* in multinational-companies-human-rights.md).
- **Uncertainty and cost**: the evaluative test produces costly interlocutory battles. Appellate deference limits review, but the question is whether the test is unpredictable. That is a typical essay theme.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm jurisdiction as of right (valid service in, see common-law-jurisdiction-service-within.md). Check for a statutory bar on the stay power.
2. Check whether a jurisdiction or arbitration clause applies. If so, the clause analysis replaces *Spiliada* (strong cause test; Hague 2005; stay of arbitration).
3. **First limb (defendant)**:
   - Name the alternative forum and show it is available: jurisdiction over the defendant, or an undertaking to submit.
   - Go through the connecting factors issue by issue: residence, witnesses and language, place of events, governing law, related proceedings.
   - Is it *clearly* more appropriate?
4. **Second limb (claimant)**:
   - funding or legal aid (*Connelly*);
   - delay;
   - time bar and whether the claimant is to blame;
   - inability to join all parties;
   - real risk of injustice, corruption or lack of independence (*AK Investment*), with cogent evidence;
   - the foreign court ignoring a choice of law.
5. Note factors that are *not* enough: higher damages, better disclosure, an isolated procedural difference, or the claim being weaker abroad.
6. **Post-Brexit** for UK-domiciled defendants: forum non conveniens is available. Do not cite *Owusu* as current law, unless transitional.
7. **Outcome**: stay (possibly conditional on undertakings) or refusal. Comment on appellate deference.
