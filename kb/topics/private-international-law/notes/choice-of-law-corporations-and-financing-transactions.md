# Choice of law for corporations, winding up and international financing transactions

## Overview
Companies and cross-border finance raise combined questions of choice of law, jurisdiction and enforcement. For a company as
such, the law of its place of incorporation (lex incorporationis) is the main choice-of-law rule. For a loan or security
package, the parties' chosen law governs the contract, but cannot decide capacity, title to assets, status or insolvency
questions, and may be overridden by mandatory rules or the borrower state's insolvency law. the materials uses a loan from a
bank to a company guaranteed by its parent to show how these issues interlock.

## The law

### 1. Solvent corporations: lex incorporationis
1. A corporation is a legal person created by law; the law under which it was created (lex incorporationis) governs a wide
   range of issues: whether it is incorporated or dissolved, whether it has separate legal existence, the nature of the entity
   (company or limited partnership), capacity, and internal management (composition and powers of organs, validity of
   appointment of directors, directors' duties to the company, who may act on its behalf).
2. It also applies to title to registered shares (*Macmillan Inc v Bishopsgate Investment Trust plc (No 3)* [1996] 1 WLR 387
   (CA), see the property note) .
3. Advantages: a single law, and an effective one because the creating law controls the entity.
4. **Mergers and amalgamations.** Whether companies A and B merged is for the lex incorporationis. If that law provides true
   universal succession (succession in universum jus), the new company is recognised as succeeding to all rights and
   liabilities.
5. ***National Bank of Greece and Athens SA v Metliss* [1958] AC 509 (HL).** Metliss held English-law bearer bonds issued by a
   Greek mortgage bank and guaranteed by the National Bank of Greece. In 1949 Greece imposed a moratorium on payment. Suing in
   Greece was useless, and neither bank had assets in England. In 1953 the guarantor merged by Royal decree with the National
   Bank of Athens (which did have English assets) into a new bank as universal successor. The new bank argued it was not party
   to the bonds, that no foreign decree can substitute a debtor under an English-law obligation, and that the moratorium
   protected it. Held: universal succession under the law of incorporation is recognised and enforced, so the new bank took on
   the bond obligations. But the moratorium did not protect it: it was characterised as relating to discharge of contract (for
   the proper law, here English law), not to the status of a Greek corporation (for the law of incorporation).
6. **Contracts made by corporations.** Contractual choice-of-law rules apply, with differences on capacity and on whether the
   corporation is bound by contracts made for it. Capacity is tested under the lex incorporationis **and** the law governing the
   contract ("and", not "or"), since a corporation cannot exercise more power than the creating law gives it (for example a
   company forbidden to hold land cannot own land even if the lex situs allows corporate ownership). Ultra vires has been
   removed from many national company laws; acts beyond capacity are void or voidable.

### 2. Winding up and insolvency
1. **Winding up** ends corporate life: a liquidator is appointed, assets sold, creditors paid and any balance distributed to
   shareholders. A court order may vest company property in the liquidator (Insolvency Act 1986 s 145). The lex incorporationis
   governs: "the law that creates can destroy" (*Lazard Bros v Midland Bank* [1933] AC 289, 297 (HL)).
2. **English jurisdiction to wind up** is statutory (Insolvency Act 1986 s 117), covering companies incorporated under the UK
   Companies Act and, under s 221(5), unregistered companies, including foreign ones, where the company is dissolved or has
   ceased business, is unable to pay its debts, or the court thinks winding up just and equitable. Making an order is
   discretionary.
3. **Core principles for foreign unregistered companies** (approved in *Stocznia Gdanska SA v Latreefers Inc (No 2)* [2001]
   BCLC 116 (CA)): (a) sufficient connection with England and Wales (assets are not essential); (b) reasonable possibility of
   benefit to the petitioners; (c) at least one person interested in the distribution must be someone over whom the court has
   jurisdiction. "Sufficient connection" is not clearly defined; examples include persons in England who could benefit, such as
   a liquidator pursuing claims for fraudulent or wrongful trading or misfeasance.
4. ***Re A Company (No 003102 of 1991)* [1991] BCLC 539** (Harman J): petition to wind up a Guernsey company said to owe
   £16.6 million; no assets in England (largely in Portugal) but loans were negotiated and drawn in England, business was
   carried on there and there was a reasonable likelihood of benefit to creditors. Sufficient connection found.
5. **Cross-border insolvency** deals with recognition of foreign proceedings, access of foreign representatives to courts,
   cooperation and coordination of concurrent proceedings. English courts recognise insolvency under the borrower's law of
   incorporation; a bankruptcy court commonly applies its own rules on treatment of contracts and title.

### 3. Financing transactions (loan, security and guarantee)
1. **Structure.** Separate contracts (lender-borrower; lender-guarantor), property security, and corporate questions about
   each company. Each strand attracts its own choice-of-law rule.
2. **Chosen law.** Party autonomy is widely recognised, and sophisticated finance documents expressly choose the law. Limits:
   capacity; title or entitlement to assets given as security; insolvency of a party; matters seen as procedure; matters seen
   as status of the borrower. Mandatory rules and public policy, conventions and insolvency law may override.
3. **Common choices:** creditor's law, borrower's law, law of the market (where securities are issued or listed), or a neutral
   law. Factors: familiarity and convenience, commercial orientation, stability and predictability, use of the preferred forum,
   specialist lawyers, insulation from the borrower's state interference, language. English and New York law dominate major
   financial contracts because of financial centres, a commercial approach, a strong policy of upholding the bargain, more
   developed financial law and stability.
4. **Insulation.** Lenders try to insulate against borrower-state measures (cut interest by legislation, exchange control,
   repayment in local currency to a local agency, moratoria), particularly where the borrower is the state or state-related or
   in upheaval. Insulation is incomplete: assets may be mainly in the borrower's home state (foreign judgments may not be
   enforced there, and the lex situs governs property); borrower insolvency law is likely to override; universal succession
   under the lex incorporationis will be recognised (as in *Metliss*); and if the issue is characterised as one of status the
   lex incorporationis can displace the proper law of the contract.
5. **Choice of jurisdiction.** Major loan agreements nearly always select a forum. An exclusive clause gives one court
   exclusive jurisdiction. Loan agreements usually use **non-exclusive** clauses (often with the borrower appointing an agent
   for service in the chosen state), which add a forum without excluding others, since the borrower may have assets elsewhere
   and the lender may need that court's help for freezing assets or enforcing a judgment. An asymmetric clause (borrower must
   sue only in the chosen court; lender may sue elsewhere) is also used. Reasons for selecting a forum: guaranteeing at least
   one forum that accepts jurisdiction, usually outside the borrower's state; insulating against borrower-state interfering
   law (its own courts are more likely to apply it); and, where the borrower is a state or related entity, courts elsewhere are
   more likely to give effect to waivers of immunity from enforcement.

## Key authorities
- *Metliss* (universal succession; moratorium as discharge not status).
- *Lazard Bros v Midland Bank* (law that creates can destroy).
- *Stocznia Gdanska v Latreefers (No 2)* and *Re A Company (No 003102 of 1991)* (winding up foreign companies).
- *Macmillan v Bishopsgate (No 3)* (title to registered shares).
- Insolvency Act 1986 ss 117, 145, 221(5).

## Debate and critique
The slides pose rather than answer: can the insulation of a loan ever be complete, and which laws should courts give effect to
when a state in difficulty interferes with foreign loans? The tension is between party autonomy (the strong English and New
York policy of upholding the bargain without imposing a judicial view of fairness) and the practical control that the
borrower's state, courts and insolvency regime keep over the borrower's assets and its corporate status. Characterisation
(contractual discharge versus status) is decisive, as *Metliss* shows.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Separate the questions: jurisdiction (which court), choice of law (which law), and recognition and enforcement.
2. Identify the issue: contract performance (chosen law), capacity or internal management (lex incorporationis, and for
   capacity also the law governing the contract), title to security (lex situs or lex incorporationis for registered shares),
   or insolvency (law of the insolvency forum and incorporation).
3. Characterise a foreign decree or legislation carefully: discharge of the contract or status of the company?
4. For a winding-up petition against a foreign company use the three *Stocznia* principles and identify the connection and
   likely benefit.
5. Examine the clause: exclusive or non-exclusive; why choose that forum; where are the borrower's assets?
