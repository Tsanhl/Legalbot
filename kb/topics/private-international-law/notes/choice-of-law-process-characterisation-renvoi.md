# The choice of law process: characterisation, connecting factors and renvoi

## Overview
When English private international law has to choose a governing law (the *lex causae*), the common law works in three stages. First characterise the issue. Then find the conflict rule and its connecting factor. Then identify the legal system that factor points to. The English court controls the method at every stage, but has to apply it in an internationalist spirit. Renvoi asks a further question: once a foreign law is chosen, does that mean its domestic rules only, or its conflict rules too? These tools matter most where the statutory regimes (Rome I and Rome II) do not apply, such as property and corporations. The theory behind choice of law (vested rights, local law, US interest analysis, the English pragmatic approach) is good essay material.

## The law

### 1. The three-stage process: *Macmillan v Bishopsgate (No 3)*
Staughton LJ in *Macmillan Inc v Bishopsgate Investment Trust plc (No 3)* [1996] 1 WLR 387, 391–392 (CA) said finding the *lex causae* takes three steps:
1. **Characterise the issue** before the court. For example: is it about the formal validity of a marriage, intestate succession to movables, or interpretation of a contract?
2. **Select the conflict rule** that lays down a connecting factor for that kind of issue. Formal validity of marriage goes to the law of the place of celebration. Intestate succession to movables goes to the deceased's domicile at death. Interpretation of a contract goes to its proper law.
3. **Identify the system of law** tied by that connecting factor to the issue. This can be easy, or controversial, as with the proper law of a contract.

Stages two and three are generally for the *lex fori*. Stage one, characterisation, "presents more of a problem". Staughton LJ added that conflict rules should if possible be simple and easy to apply. He noted that litigants facing costly expert evidence that may prove unnecessary deserve sympathy.

### 2. Characterisation
- **Statutory rules come first.** The rules in Rome I, Rome II or domestic statutes apply *without* regard to common law characterisation. They apply to whatever the Regulation itself defines as, say, a contractual matter. Common law choice-of-law principles therefore apply only to issues that Rome I, Rome II or statute do not cover.
- **Definition**: characterisation places the facts within one legal category for which a choice-of-law rule exists. English law defines both the categories and how facts are fitted into them.
- The categories are set partly by authority and partly by principle: *Raiffeisen Zentralbank Österreich AG v Five Star Trading LLC* [2001] EWCA Civ 68, [2001] QB 825. The exercise should be done "in a broad internationalist spirit in accordance with the principles of the conflict of laws of the forum" (*Raiffeisen* at [27]).
- **Issue, not claim** (*Macmillan*):
  - The court characterises the *issue* raised by the claim and the defence, not the label the claimant puts on its cause of action.
  - Macmillan framed its claim as restitutionary. The CA, agreeing with Millett J, held the true issue was **priority of title to shares**: whether the banks were bona fide purchasers without notice. That was a property issue (see choice-of-law-property.md).
  - Auld LJ said characterisation is for the lex fori. But it should not be tied to the domestic categories of the lex fori or of the competing system, and the issue should not be defined so narrowly that it attracts a domestic rule with no counterpart elsewhere. The underlying aim is comity between legal systems.
  - Aldous LJ said one claim may raise several issues, each governed by its own law.
- **Dicey on method** (quoted in *Macmillan*): theories of characterisation are so abstract they "can produce almost any result" and have had little effect on English practice. The court should consider the rationale of the English conflict rule and the purpose of the substantive rule to be characterised. If neither conflict rule fits, a new one may be created. Commonsense, practical solutions are encouraged.
- **Illustration (Song Mao)**: the tort of conversion is relatively unusual, since civil law systems handle such claims through property law. It is unclear whether it is a "tort" under Rome II, or under the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1995, or a property claim governed by common law. Even if English tort law applies, title to the sugar is an **incidental question** for the lex situs, Cambodian law.

### 3. Connecting factors
- These are the points of contact linking a person or issue to a legal system. Examples:
  - succession to immovables: the law of the situs;
  - formal validity of marriage: the place of celebration;
  - capacity to marry: the parties' domicile.
- They are almost all defined by English law.
- **Personal** connecting factors: domicile, residence, nationality. **Causal** ones: place of contracting, place of the tort, place where land or a thing is.
- Problems can arise, for example locating (the *situs* of) an intangible right. Debts, shares and intermediated securities are covered in choice-of-law-property.md.

### 4. Renvoi
- **The question**: when a foreign law is chosen, does the reference include its choice-of-law rules (renvoi) or only its domestic law?
- **Statutory rules answer it themselves.** Rome I Art 20 excludes renvoi in contract: the chosen law means the country's substantive rules, excluding its private international law. The Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984 s 1(5) also excludes renvoi on limitation.
- **At common law**:
  - no renvoi where the judgment affects only the litigants themselves (for example contractual or other obligations);
  - renvoi may be possible where the judgment concerns status, such as ownership of a thing or a person's capacity to marry;
  - so how the issue is characterised is decisive.
- **Property and *Macmillan***: Staughton LJ (at 405) said the reference to New York, as the situs of the shares, was to New York domestic law. An argument for renvoi had once been made but "mercifully (or sadly, as the case may be)" abandoned. No authority was cited. Supporters of renvoi in property cases argue that a judgment on title that conflicts with what the situs authorities would decide is likely to be ineffective.
- **Worked case study**. A painting is stolen from Adam in London, taken to Shanghai and sold to Cindy, who sells it to Charlie in Shanghai. Charlie brings it to London. Assume English law gives no title to Charlie, Chinese law protects a bona fide purchaser for value, and the English rule is the lex situs at the time of the last disposition (China).
  - *No renvoi*: Chinese domestic law applies, so Charlie wins.
  - *Renvoi*: the English court applies whatever a Chinese court would apply. If Chinese conflict rules applied the lex situs at the *first* disposition (England), Charlie would lose.
  - In fact, China's Law on the Application of Laws to Foreign-related Civil Relationships (2010) excludes renvoi (Art 9) and applies the law where the movables are when the legal facts occur (Art 37). See also Art 311 of the Civil Code (2020).

## Key authorities
- *Macmillan Inc v Bishopsgate Investment Trust plc (No 3)* [1996] 1 WLR 387 (CA). Maxwell-controlled companies pledged Berlitz shares, owned by Macmillan and registered in New York, to banks as security for loans. Macmillan claimed the shares back.
  - Held: characterise the issue (priority of title to shares), not the claim (restitution). Apply the lex situs of the shares, New York law, without renvoi.
  - Significance: the leading statement of the three-stage process.
- *Raiffeisen Zentralbank Österreich AG v Five Star Trading LLC* [2001] EWCA Civ 68, [2001] QB 825: categories come from authority and principle; characterisation should be done in an internationalist spirit ([27]).
- Rome I Art 20: no renvoi in contract.
- Foreign Limitation Periods Act 1984 s 1(5): no renvoi on limitation.

## Debate and critique
- **Why apply foreign law at all? (Cheshire, North & Fawcett ch 2)**
  - *Vested (acquired) rights*: originated with Huber and was developed by Dicey and Beale. The forum enforces rights acquired under foreign law. It now has scant support.
  - *Local law theory*: Cook. The forum enforces a local right created by its own law, modelled on the foreign rule. Critics call it little more than a restatement.
  - *The American "revolution"*:
    - Currie's governmental interest analysis, which separates true from false conflicts. It is criticised as forum-oriented and as failing in the "disinterested third state" case;
    - comparative impairment;
    - Cavers's "principles of preference" (rule-selection rather than jurisdiction-selection);
    - Leflar-style choice-influencing factors;
    - the Second Restatement's "most significant relationship" test (Reese).
  - *The English approach*: applying foreign law involves no surrender of sovereignty. The forum's own rules recognise that domestic rules suit purely domestic matters, not foreign-element cases. English lawyers are pragmatists and empiricists. They reject a single overriding doctrine and build clear rules for each category of case, aiming at justice, convenience and the parties' expectations.
- **The 1933 Harvard Law Review "Critique of the Choice-of-Law Problem"** questions the "article of faith" that proper study will reveal rules which, like a "hazel wand of the diviner", point to the right jurisdiction. Cheshire records that Cavers advocated in 1933 abandoning jurisdiction-selection for rule-selection.
- **Baxter, "Choice of Law"**: on the prevailing theory, choice of law "shunts" a problem from N possible systems down to one, with no special weight for the lex fori. That looks internationalist, but choice-of-law rules are part of the forum's own law. A useful lens for critiquing neutrality claims.
- **Roosevelt, "The Myth of Choice of Law"** (Michigan Law Review): reviews vested rights (Beale), legal realism (Cook) and interest analysis (Currie), and argues that the traditional framing of "choosing" a law should be rethought.
- **Childress on the Third Restatement**: discusses presumptions, scope and priority in the new US Conflicts Restatement and its treatment of international comity. Useful for comparing the US and English approaches.
- **Renvoi in property**: Staughton LJ dismissed renvoi without authority. Is a situs-sensitive renvoi needed so that English judgments on title are effective where the property is? Compare China's exclusion of renvoi.
- **Characterisation and comity**: Auld LJ treated characterisation as aiming at comity between systems. That fits the "broad internationalist spirit" in *Raiffeisen*, but sits uneasily with English law defining every category.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there a statutory rule?**
   - Contract: Rome I (no common law characterisation; Art 20 excludes renvoi).
   - Non-contractual obligation: Rome II.
   - Torts outside Rome II, such as defamation: PIL(MP)A 1995 or the common law.
   - Only otherwise use common law characterisation.
2. **Identify each issue separately**, as in *Macmillan*: validity, interpretation, title, priority, capacity. Do not stop at the claimant's label (for example "restitution" or "conversion").
3. **Characterise** each issue in an internationalist spirit. Watch for incidental questions, such as title as a precondition to conversion.
4. **Apply the connecting factor**: situs, domicile, place of the tort, governing law. Locate intangibles carefully.
5. **Renvoi**: excluded for contract and limitation. Normally excluded for obligations at common law. Possibly available for title or status. Apply *Macmillan*'s approach to shares (domestic lex situs).
6. **Prove the foreign law** (see foreign-law-and-substance-procedure.md).
7. **Traps**: characterising the claim rather than the issue; applying common law characterisation to a Rome I issue; assuming renvoi always or never applies.
