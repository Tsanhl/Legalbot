# Donohue v Armco Inc [2001] UKHL 64 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. The date is given only as December 2001 (one source
> says 13 December, another page header says 14 December). The details of Lord Bingham's reasoning come from one source
> and should be checked against the report. Do not quote paragraph numbers.

## Overview
House of Lords, December 2001 (Lord Bingham of Cornhill, Lord Mackay of Clashfern, Lord Nicholls of Birkenhead, Lord
Hobhouse of Woodborough and Lord Scott of Foscote). Reported at [2002] 1 Lloyd's Rep 425. The case is the leading
statement that an English court will normally restrain a party from suing abroad in breach of an exclusive
jurisdiction clause unless there are **strong reasons** for not doing so, and applies it to a messy multi-party dispute.

## Facts
Mr Donohue was party to sale and purchase agreements with companies in the Armco group, in connection with the
management buy-out of the British National Insurance Group. The agreements contained an exclusive English
jurisdiction clause. The Armco companies later sued Mr Donohue and others in New York, alleging fraud. Mr Donohue
asked the English court for an anti-suit injunction to stop the New York proceedings. Other parties (potential
co-claimants) who were not bound by the clauses were involved in the New York claims, and there were issues about
joinder and service out.

## Outcome
Both the first-instance judge and the Court of Appeal refused the injunction, reasoning that there was a strong
advantage in having all the issues decided in one forum, New York. The House of Lords also **refused the
injunction** and accepted an undertaking given by the Armco companies instead, noting that Mr Donohue could seek damages
for breach of the clause if he suffered loss.

## Principles
- Where parties have agreed on an exclusive forum, the court will ordinarily hold them to it by injunction, unless
  strong reasons are shown not to (the sources cite *The Fehmarn* and the *Aérospatiale* line of authority for the
  basic rule).
- The one-source account of Lord Bingham's reasoning is that some of the New York claims against Donohue fell within
  the clause but were fairly peripheral, other claims fell outside it, and splitting the dispute between England and
  New York risked inconsistent findings. These were treated as strong reasons for not giving effect to the clause in
  Mr Donohue's favour.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify an exclusive jurisdiction clause and a claimant who has sued elsewhere in breach of it.
2. State the default rule: an injunction to enforce the clause unless strong reasons exist; the burden is on the
   party in breach.
3. Consider the strong reasons argument: multiple parties, claims inside and outside the clause, risk of
   inconsistent decisions, and the practical advantages of one forum.
4. Mention the alternatives to an injunction (undertakings, damages for breach of the clause).
