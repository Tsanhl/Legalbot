# Choice of law for non-contractual obligations (Rome II) and for property

## Overview
Once an English court has jurisdiction, it must decide which law governs the substance. For wrongs (tort), unjust enrichment
and similar obligations, the retained Rome II Regulation now supplies the rules; for title to property the common law
distinguishes land from movables and tangibles from intangibles, with the lex situs as the traditional anchor but with
awkward questions for debts, shares, securities and IP.

## The law

### 1. The Rome II Regulation (non-contractual obligations)
1. **Status after Brexit.** Rome I and II remain applicable in the UK from 1 January 2021 as retained EU law. The Retained EU
   Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 gives power, before 23 June 2026, to restate or replace secondary retained law, so
   their future is uncertain (the materials' own caution). Rome II applies to events giving rise to damage occurring on
   or after 1 January 2021: Art 31 looks at the date of the damage-causing event, not the date of the damage.
2. **Characterisation.** Under EU instruments the court does not use common law characterisation; it interprets the
   instrument's own categories.
3. **Scope (Art 2).** Any consequence of tort/delict, unjust enrichment, negotiorum gestio (benevolent intervention) or culpa
   in contrahendo. **Excluded** include: family relationships, matrimonial property, wills and succession; negotiable
   instruments (to the extent obligations arise from their negotiable character); company law matters (creation, capacity,
   winding up); voluntary trust relations between settlors, trustees and beneficiaries; State liability for acts of authority;
   personal liability of auditors in a statutory audit; nuclear damage; and **violations of privacy and rights relating to
   personality, including defamation** (see the defamation note).
4. **General rule (Art 4).**
   - Art 4(2): if the defendant and the victim are habitually resident in the same country when damage occurs, that country's
     law applies.
   - Art 4(1): otherwise, the law of the country where the damage occurs, irrespective of where the event giving rise to it
     occurred and where indirect consequences arise. The slides stress the distinction between where damage *occurs* and where
     it is suffered, felt or recorded, and the challenge of locating it.
   - Art 4(3) escape clause: if it is clear from all the circumstances that the tort is manifestly more closely connected with
     another country, that law applies; a pre-existing relationship such as a contract can supply the connection.
5. **Choice by the parties (Art 14).** Allowed by agreement after the event; before the event only if all parties pursue a
   commercial activity and the agreement was freely negotiated. It must be express or demonstrated with reasonable certainty.
   It cannot prevent application of non-derogable rules of the country where all other elements of the situation are located
   (Art 14(3)).
6. **Scope of the applicable law (Arts 15, 22, 24).** Covers basis and extent of liability, grounds of exemption, existence,
   nature and assessment of damage or remedy, burden of proof and presumptions. No renvoi (Art 24). Because Art 15(c) includes
   the assessment of damage, the law of the forum has no role there; but methods of proving loss, such as what expert
   evidence is needed, are evidence and procedure under Art 1(3) and remain governed by English procedural rules: *Wall v
   Mutuelle de Poitiers Assurances* [2014] EWCA Civ 138.
7. **Special provisions mentioned.** Art 7 (the "principle of ubiquity": claimant may choose the law of the place of the event
   giving rise to damage, in the context where it applies), Art 8(1) and (3) (IP: lex loci protectionis, parties cannot choose
   another law) and Art 16 (overriding mandatory provisions).

### 2. English rules before Rome II (and where they still matter)
- **Tort.** Common law "double actionability" (*Philips v Eyre* -> *Chaplin v Boys* -> *Red Sea*): a foreign tort was actionable
  in England only if actionable as if done in England and civilly actionable under the law of the place where done, with a
  flexible exception. Part III of the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1995 replaced this with a
  general rule applying the law of the country where the events constituting the tort occur (s 11(1)), special rules where the
  events occur in several countries, and a displacement rule where another country's law is substantially more appropriate.
  Defamation was excluded from Part III, so common law rules continue for it.
- **Restitution.** Terminology and characterisation were difficult; before Rome II the obligation to make restitution was
  governed by the proper law of the obligation (with flexible approaches in some cases); claims may be unknown to English law
  (for example negotiorum gestio).
- **Equitable obligations.** Unsettled; one suggestion is the law governing the relationship through which the beneficiary's
  assets were accessed; academic opinion was divided.
- **Policy questions** raised in the slides: multiple connecting factors (place of act, place of injury, residence, domicile),
  one law for all issues (capacity, vicarious liability, defences, damages)? same rule for all torts? risk of liability for
  torts unknown to English law (privacy, unfair competition)? should parties choose the law?

### 3. Property
1. **Basic division.** Common law treats immovables and movables differently (*Re Hoyles* [1911] 1 Ch 179). Whether a thing is
   movable or immovable is decided by the lex situs; where a thing or right is deemed situated is a question for the lex fori
   (*Rossano v Manufacturers Life Insurance* [1963] 2 QB 352).
2. **Land.** Title is governed by the lex situs as applied by the court at the situs, so renvoi is acceptable (recall
   "Charlie's Case"). Obligations relating to land are governed by their own law (for example the law of the contract).
3. **Tangible movables.** Title or right to possession is governed by the lex situs at the date of the event alleged to affect
   title (*Cammell v Sewell* (1860) 5 H & N 728; *Winkworth v Christie, Manson & Woods* [1980] Ch 496). Staughton LJ in
   *Macmillan v Bishopsgate (No 3)* [1996] 1 WLR 387 said the reference is to the domestic law of the situs, the renvoi
   argument having been abandoned. For goods in transit the law governing the transaction may be better. Rationale for the
   situs: objective, ascertainable by third parties, and the situs state controls the thing, so a judgment contrary to it is
   often ineffective.
4. **Documents of title.** Bearer shares are treated as tangible movables for choice of law (transfer by delivery of the
   warrant; Companies Act 2006 s 779, and no new share warrants after the Small Business, Enterprise and Employment Act 2015 s
   84 came into force). Negotiable instruments (bills, cheques, notes) pass good title to a good-faith purchaser for value
   free of equities.
5. **Intangibles.** A situs must be ascribed (a legal conclusion, not a physical fact): generally where they are properly
   recoverable or enforceable (*New York Life Insurance v Public Trustee* [1924] 2 Ch 101; *Alloway v Philips* [1980] 1 WLR
   888). Candidate rules: lex situs of the intangible, of the debtor, of the assignor, or lex creationis.
   - **Simple debt:** Dicey, Morris and Collins at [23-026]: situate where the debtor resides. *Kwok Chi Leung Karl v
     Commissioner of Estate Duty* [1988] 1 WLR 1035 (PC): a non-negotiable promissory note payable in Monrovia was located by
     the place of performance designated by the contract, provided the company resided there (tax context: property outside Hong
     Kong).
   - **Assignment of a debt:** two contracts. The contractual issues (assignability, relationship between assignee and
     debtor, discharge of the debtor) are governed by the law of the assigned claim (Art 14 Rome I; Art 12 Rome Convention);
     the assignor-assignee relationship by the law of their own contract. Critics: proprietary questions such as priority
     between competing claims should be governed by the situs, not lex creationis.
   - **Bundles of receivables** (factoring, receivables finance) with debtors in many states make locating the debt
     impractical. The UN Convention on Assignment of Receivables in International Trade 2001, Art 30, applies the law of the
     assignor's location to priority (not yet in force), subject to forum insolvency preferences.
   - **Shares:** certificate is only prima facie evidence of title (Companies Act 2006 s 768), the share being a bundle of
     rights and obligations (*IRC v Crossman* [1937] AC 26). *Macmillan Inc v Bishopsgate Investment Trust (No 3)* [1996] 1
     WLR 387 (CA): shares in a New York company, held by a nominee that without the owner's consent used them as security for
     English-law loans; the question was who had priority between the owner's equitable interest and bona fide purchasers.
     The slides note the CA's decision can only be authority for the direct holding system; *MCC Proceeds v Bishopsgate (No
     4)* [1999] CLC 417 was decided on NY law without choice-of-law analysis. Candidate connecting factors: lex incorporationis
     or place of the register.
   - **Intermediated securities:** the direct-holding rules fit poorly. The PRIMA (place of the relevant intermediary)
     approach is in the Hague Securities Convention, in force since 1 April 2017 for Mauritius, Switzerland and the USA.
   - **IP:** lex loci protectionis for title and assignability; Rome II Art 8.
6. **Seizure of assets** by the lex situs (for example land in Mauritius under its acquisition rules; an iPad in Singapore under
   confiscation rules) is generally recognised as effective and is not enforcement of a foreign penal or public law.

## Debate and critique
- Art 4(1) and human rights litigation: the slides report that where subsidiaries or suppliers of multinationals cause harm in
  host states, the law of the host state usually applies, risking weak standards. Two proposed solutions: (1) a specific rule
  mirroring Art 7's ubiquity principle, letting the claimant choose the law of the place of the event; (2) treat the proposed
  EU corporate sustainability due diligence directive as overriding mandatory provisions under Art 16.
- Which connecting factor for intangibles? Each has trade-offs (location of debtor, assignor, or law of the debt); reform
  instruments (UN Convention, Hague Securities Convention) have limited uptake.
- Renvoi: accepted for title to land, rejected by Rome II (Art 24) and by Staughton LJ for shares.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the claim non-contractual and within Rome II Art 2? Check exclusions (defamation/privacy, company law, trusts).
2. Date: damage-causing event on or after 1 January 2021?
3. Art 14: any post-event or freely negotiated commercial choice? Art 4(2): common habitual residence? Otherwise Art 4(1)
   place of damage. Then test Art 4(3).
4. Use the applicable law for liability, damages assessment and defences, but English law for procedure and methods of proof.
5. For property: movable or immovable? Tangible or intangible? Identify the lex situs at the relevant date; for intangibles
   decide how the situs is ascribed and whether the question is contractual (assignability) or proprietary (priority).
6. Consider any overriding mandatory rule or public-policy limit.
