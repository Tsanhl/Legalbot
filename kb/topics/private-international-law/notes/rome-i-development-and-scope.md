# Choice of law in contract: development and scope of Rome I

## Overview
English choice of law for contracts developed in three stages:
- the **common law "proper law"** doctrine, for contracts before 1 April 1991;
- the **1980 Rome Convention** (Contracts (Applicable Law) Act 1990), for contracts from 1 April 1991 to 17 December 2009;
- the **Rome I Regulation** (EC) 593/2008, for contracts concluded after 17 December 2009. It is **retained** (now "assimilated") law for contracts concluded on or after 1 January 2021.

Rome I is the main legacy of EU private international law still in force after Brexit, because it does not depend on reciprocity. It defines its own scope (Art 1), applies universally (Art 2) and excludes renvoi (Art 20). Matters outside it (property, company law, arbitration and choice of court agreements, agency) are left to the common law, which can still borrow the Rome I governing law for its own purposes.

## The law

### 1. Two aspects of contract law in choice of law
- **Freedom of contract**: give effect to what the parties intended, or would have intended. In choice of law, that means identifying the system the parties might reasonably have expected to apply.
- **Limits on freedom for policy reasons**: is it reasonable for a system to impose itself on the contract against the parties' will? This is the role of mandatory rules and public policy (see overriding-mandatory-rules-and-illegality.md).

### 2. The common law proper law doctrine
- The historical development went from the law of the place of contracting, to the place of performance, to the 19th-century acceptance that the parties may choose the governing law (*Vita Food Products Inc v Unus Shipping Co Ltd* [1939] AC 277 (PC); see party-autonomy-express-implied-choice.md).
- The "proper law" governed almost all substantive contractual issues. It was, in order:
  1. the law **expressly chosen** (if the choice was "bona fide and legal" and not contrary to public policy);
  2. failing that, the parties' **inferred intention** from the terms, the nature of the contract and the circumstances;
  3. failing that, the law of **closest and most real connection**.
- Marginal roles were left to:
  - the law of the **place of performance** (illegality there might affect whether performance would be ordered);
  - a party's **personal law** (incapacity);
  - the law of the **place of contracting** (formal validity);
  - the **lex fori** (public policy and procedure).

### 3. The Rome Convention 1980
- It applies to contractual obligations in civil and commercial matters under contracts made after **1 April 1991**. It was enacted by the **Contracts (Applicable Law) Act 1990**.
- It was negotiated after UK accession, so it is a compromise between common law and civil law.
- The **Giuliano–Lagarde Report** is an official guide to interpreting the Convention, and to the Rome I provisions derived from it.

### 4. The Rome I Regulation
1. Finalised in 2008. It applies to contracts concluded **after 17 December 2009** (Art 28).
2. It defines its own domain (Art 1) and the conflict rules within it. The common law may still, on its own authority, refer issues the Regulation does not claim to the governing law the Regulation identifies. For example, arbitration agreements are excluded (Art 1(2)(e)), but an English court applying common law principles may hold that an arbitration clause in a larger contract is governed by the contract's Rome I law. Note s 6A Arbitration Act 1996 (2025) for arbitration agreements now.
3. **Retained Rome I** applies to contracts concluded **on or after 1 January 2021**: Law Applicable to Contractual Obligations and Non-Contractual Obligations (Amendment etc.) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019, SI 2019/834. It is "not so very different" from the Convention or the original Regulation.
4. **Interpretation after Brexit**:
   - Decisions on earlier texts help interpret the Regulation, since EU law favoured consistency between Rome I and the Convention.
   - Parliament has freed courts to depart from CJEU case law (REUL Act 2023 s 6, amending the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 s 6). The power is unlikely to be used freely. Pre-2021 and post-2020 case law will guide, not dictate, English courts, especially in commercial cases where stability and predictability are essential.
5. **REUL Act 2023**: Rome I and Rome II were not on the end-2023 revocation list. There is a power to restate or replace them by regulations before **23 June 2026**, and it is uncertain whether it will be used. Rome I and II are now "assimilated law" (s 5).
6. **The common law remains relevant** for issues outside the Regulations (validity of arbitration agreements, property, corporate transactions). It may also inform how Rome I is applied, for example on implied choice, illegality and public policy.
7. **No characterisation in the common law sense**: Rome I applies to whatever it defines as contractual. The court interprets the instrument rather than "characterising".

### 5. Subject-matter scope: Art 1(1)
- It applies, "in situations involving a conflict of laws, to contractual obligations in civil and commercial matters". Revenue, customs and administrative matters are excluded.
- **"Contractual obligations"** are interpreted **autonomously**, not by domestic categories. By analogy with CJEU case law on "matters relating to a contract" under Brussels I Art 5(1), the core is an obligation **freely entered into with regard to another (identifiable) person**.
- Torts, unjust enrichment and other non-contractual obligations are outside, and go to Rome II. But Art 12(1)(e) brings the **consequences of nullity**, including restitution under a void contract, within the applicable law. Obligations from **pre-contractual dealings** belong to Rome II (Art 1(2)(i)).

### 6. Exclusions: Art 1(2)–(3)
- (a) status or legal capacity of natural persons, without prejudice to Art 13;
- (b)–(c) family, matrimonial property, wills and succession;
- (d) bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, and other negotiable instruments so far as obligations arise from their negotiable character;
- (e) **arbitration agreements and choice of court agreements**;
- (f) **company law**: creation, legal capacity, internal organisation, winding up, and personal liability of officers and members for the company's obligations;
- (g) whether an **agent** can bind a principal, or an organ a company, in relation to a third party;
- (h) trusts;
- (i) **dealings prior to conclusion** of a contract;
- (j) certain insurance schemes;
- Art 1(3): **evidence and procedure** are excluded, but without prejudice to Art 18. The governing law applies so far as it contains rules raising presumptions of law or determining the burden of proof.

### 7. International scope
- **Universal application (Art 2)**: any law specified is applied whether or not it is the law of a Member State. After Brexit, the same applies whether or not it is the law of the UK or part of it.
- **Intra-UK conflicts**: EU law did not require Rome I to apply between units of a Member State (Art 22(2)), but the UK applies it between England, Scotland and Northern Ireland as a matter of domestic law (Law Applicable to Contractual Obligations (England and Wales and Northern Ireland) Regulations 2009, SI 2009/3064). Art 7 (insurance) is the exception.

### 8. No renvoi (Art 20)
"Law" means the country's rules of law other than its private international law. Briggs: it would be perverse for parties to choose anything but domestic rules, and the law need not "indulge them in their folly". Also, "law" means the **domestic law of a country**, not non-state law (see party-autonomy-express-implied-choice.md).

### 9. Structure of Rome I
- Art 3: freedom of choice.
- Art 4: absence of choice.
- Arts 5–8: carriage, consumer, insurance and individual employment contracts (protective rules).
- Art 9: overriding mandatory provisions.
- Arts 10, 11, 13: consent, material and formal validity, incapacity.
- Art 12: scope of the applicable law.
- Art 14: voluntary assignment.
- Art 21: public policy.

These are covered in the linked notes.

- *Vita Food Products v Unus Shipping* [1939] AC 277 (PC): the high-water mark of party autonomy at common law.

## Debate and critique
- **Retention without reciprocity**: Rome I survives because it does not need reciprocity. But UK and EU courts may now diverge in interpretation. The REUL Act lets English courts depart from CJEU case law. Commentators predict restraint, for commercial stability.
- **Possible restatement or replacement by June 2026**: uncertainty over whether Rome I and II will be restated or reformed domestically. That is an essay angle on legal certainty versus the chance to improve rules.
- **Certainty in contract choice of law (Manning)**: contracts are planned in reliance on precedent, so stability and certainty matter more for contract than for tort. Yet traditionally contract choice of law was less certain than tort choice of law. Rome I's fixed rules (Art 4(1)) and strong party autonomy respond to that demand.
- **Born and Kalelioglu, "Choice-of-Law Agreements in International Contracts"**: discuss the historical treatment, separability and presumptive validity of choice-of-law agreements under international instruments. Useful for comparing Rome I Art 3 with global practice.
- **Gaps left to the common law**: company law, agency (Art 1(2)(g)), arbitration and choice of court agreements, and property mean a commercial transaction will often involve several choice-of-law regimes at once (see commercial-transactions-financing.md). Is the split coherent?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Date of contract**: before 1 April 1991, common law proper law; 1 April 1991 to 17 December 2009, Rome Convention; after 17 December 2009, Rome I; from 1 January 2021, retained Rome I.
2. **Within Art 1?**
   - Is it a contractual obligation (freely assumed towards an identifiable person)?
   - Civil or commercial?
   - Is the issue excluded (capacity of individuals, negotiable instruments, arbitration or jurisdiction clause, company law, agency, pre-contractual dealings, evidence or procedure)?
   - If excluded, apply common law rules, which may borrow the Rome I law.
3. **Universal application**: the chosen or identified law applies even if it is a non-UK or non-EU law.
4. **No renvoi**: apply the domestic rules of the identified law.
5. Then go to Art 3 (choice), Art 4 (no choice), any protective regime (Arts 5–8), and the limits (Arts 3(3), 9, 21).
6. **Trap**: using common law "characterisation" language for a Rome I issue. Interpret the Regulation autonomously instead.
