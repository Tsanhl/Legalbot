# Service out of the jurisdiction (gateways, merits and proper place)

## Overview
If the defendant is not in England, the court has jurisdiction only if the claim form can lawfully be served abroad. Normally that needs the court's permission under CPR Part 6. The claimant must show:
- a good arguable case that the claim falls within one of the "gateways" in CPR PD 6B para 3.1;
- a serious issue on the merits;
- that England and Wales is "the proper place in which to bring the claim" (CPR r 6.37(3)), which is the *Spiliada* forum conveniens test with the burden on the claimant.

Permission is not needed where there is an English jurisdiction agreement (CPR 6.33(2B)). Since Brexit this regime applies to defendants in EU states too, so it is the main route to English jurisdiction over foreign defendants. It decides group actions against foreign subsidiaries (*Vedanta*, *Okpabi*), tort claims for injuries abroad (*Brownlie*), and anti-suit applications against foreign parties (*UniCredit*).

## The law

### 1. Procedure: two stages
1. **Without-notice application** by the claimant for permission. The claimant must show:
   - (a) a **good arguable case** that the claim falls within a **gateway** in para 3.1 of CPR PD 6B;
   - (b) that the claim has a **real prospect of success**, i.e. a serious issue on the merits to be tried, enough to survive strike-out or summary judgment;
   - (c) that **England is the proper place** to bring the claim (CPR r 6.37(3)), applying *Spiliada*.
2. **Inter partes application** by the defendant (under CPR Part 11) to set aside permission. The court revisits the natural forum, any foreign or English jurisdiction clause, and forum conveniens. The burden remains in the ordinary way on the claimant.

### 2. Gateways: examples from the sources
- **Tort gateway, para 3.1(9)**: claims in tort where "damage was sustained … within the jurisdiction", among others. Construed in *FS Cairo v Brownlie* (below).
- **Contract gateway**: for example a contract governed by English law, para 3.1(6)(c). Used in *Spiliada* and *Amin Rasheed*. In *UniCredit*, PD 6B para 3.1(6)(c) was relied on because the foreign-seated arbitration agreement was governed by English law.
- **Necessary or proper party gateway**: lets a foreign co-defendant be joined to a claim properly brought against an "anchor" defendant served in England. Used against KCM in *Vedanta* and against SPDC in *Okpabi* (22).
- **Arbitration claims**: where the seat is England, or no seat is designated, permission may be obtained under CPR 62.5(1)(c).

### 3. Service out without permission: English jurisdiction agreements
CPR 6.33(2B): the claimant may serve outside the UK without permission where, for each claim:
- (a) the court has power under the 2005 Hague Convention and the defendant is party to an exclusive choice of court agreement within Art 3 conferring jurisdiction on the court; or
- (b) a contract contains a term that the court shall have jurisdiction to determine the claim; or
- (c) the claim is in respect of such a contract.

This partly preserves the pre-Brexit position, where service under a Brussels choice-of-court agreement needed no permission.

### 4. The merits threshold
- The summary judgment standard applies. It is decided without cross-examination or disclosure, in the interests of proportionality (*Vedanta*, issue 2).
- *Okpabi v Shell* [2021] UKSC 3: the court must not hold a "mini-trial". It focuses on the pleaded case. Factual assertions are accepted unless, exceptionally, they are "demonstrably untrue or unsupportable".
- If the claim against the anchor defendant is not arguable, the claim against the foreign co-defendant falls with it.
- Where foreign law governs, the merits are judged under that law. The presumption of similarity may help at this stage if fair and reasonable (*Brownlie*, issue 2; see foreign-law-and-substance-procedure.md).

### 5. Proper place (forum conveniens)
1. Dicey 12-059 endorses Lord Wilberforce in *Amin Rasheed Shipping Corp v Kuwait Insurance Co* and Lord Goff in *Spiliada*. The claimant must show good reason why a foreign defendant should be served, considering:
   - the nature of the dispute;
   - the legal and practical issues;
   - local knowledge;
   - witnesses and evidence;
   - expense.
2. The fundamental question is the same as in stay cases: in which forum can the case be tried most suitably for the interests of all the parties and the ends of justice? But the claimant must show that England is **clearly** the appropriate forum. This is a single overarching test (Dicey 12-059).
3. **Why the burden is reversed** (*Spiliada* at 480–482). In service-out cases the claimant asks the court to exercise a discretionary statutory power over a foreigner. In stay cases the defendant was served as of right. The burden in service-out cases is "quite simply, the obverse" of that in stay cases.
4. **Qualifications** (Dicey 12-059):
   - England may be the proper place even if not most closely connected, where in practice it is the only realistic forum for a fair hearing (e.g. *AK Investment*);
   - neutrality of England, or a choice of English law as a neutral law, does not by itself make England the natural forum.
5. **Tort**: start by standing back and identifying where the tort was committed, which is likely the appropriate forum absent other compelling factors. It is not conclusive (Dicey 12-060).
6. **Second limb**: even if a foreign forum is apparently more appropriate, permission may be given if **cogent evidence** shows a **real risk that substantial justice will not be obtainable** there (*Vedanta*, issue 4; *AK Investment*).
7. *Limbu v Dyson* [2024] EWCA Civ 1564 confirms that the same "most suitable forum" principle applies in service-in and service-out cases, with the burden of proof set according to which kind of case it is.

## Key authorities
- *FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Lady Brownlie* [2021] UKSC 45.
  - Facts: Lady Brownlie was injured and her husband and stepdaughter were killed in a car crash on a hotel-booked excursion in Egypt in January 2010. She sued the Egyptian hotel operator in England and sought permission to serve it in Egypt.
  - Issue 1 (tort gateway): does "damage … sustained within the jurisdiction" cover only direct damage? The majority (Lord Lloyd-Jones, with Lords Reed, Briggs and Burrows; Lord Leggatt dissenting) held it is enough that **some significant damage** is sustained in England. That can include consequential suffering and loss experienced in England. The Brussels distinction between direct and indirect damage, and Rome II's choice-of-law concept of damage, do not control the domestic gateway.
  - Issue 2 (merits under foreign law): unanimous on the default rule and presumption of similarity (see foreign-law-and-substance-procedure.md). Appeal dismissed 4–1.
- *Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd* [1987] AC 460: proper place in service-out cases. Burden on the claimant to show England is clearly appropriate. Leave upheld (English law contract plus the Cambridgeshire factor).
- *Amin Rasheed Shipping Corp v Kuwait Insurance Co* [1984] AC 50: a contract gateway was available because the policy was governed by English law, but permission was refused because England was not the forum conveniens. The central factual issue (alleged smuggling in the Gulf) pointed to Kuwait (Dicey 12-061 illustration 1).
- *Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe* [2019] UKSC 20. Vedanta was the anchor, sued as of right under Art 4 Brussels Ia; KCM was served out as a necessary or proper party.
  - Held: no abuse of EU law; a real triable issue against Vedanta.
  - England was **not** the proper place on connecting factors, because Vedanta's offer to submit to Zambia removed the "irreconcilable judgments" argument.
  - But there was a real risk of no substantial justice in Zambia (no funding for group claims; no adequately resourced legal teams), so the claims stayed in England.
- *Okpabi v Shell* [2021] UKSC 3: the CA wrongly conducted a mini-trial. There was a real issue to be tried against the UK parent, so the Nigerian subsidiary could be joined.
- *AK Investment CJSC v Kyrgyz Mobil Tel Ltd* [2011] UKPC 7: service out upheld despite Kyrgyzstan being the natural forum, because of a real risk of injustice and because otherwise there would be no trial at all ([95], [101], [151]).
- *UniCredit Bank GmbH v RusChemAlliance LLC* [2024] UKSC 30: service out of an anti-suit claim. The gateway depended on English law governing the arbitration agreement. Proper place was decided by asking whether intervention was consistent with comity, **not** by *Spiliada* (see anti-suit-injunctions.md).
- CPR r 6.37(3); PD 6B para 3.1; CPR 6.33(2B); CPR 62.5(1)(c).

## Debate and critique
- **Exorbitance and comity**: service out asserts English adjudicative power over people abroad. Hence the historical caution and the claimant's burden. Dicey traces the forum conveniens element back to the 1875 Order 11, which required consideration of "comparative cost and convenience" (12-058).
- **Helston** on *Spiliada*: one "appropriate forum" principle now governs both service out and stays, with the burden reversed. Is reversing the burden enough to reflect the different jurisdictional bases?
- **Brownlie and the breadth of the tort gateway**: the majority's "significant damage in England" test allows English residents injured abroad to sue at home on the basis of continuing suffering in England. Lord Leggatt's dissent preferred a narrower reading. Critics fear exorbitant jurisdiction. Supporters say the forum conveniens filter is the right control, not a narrow gateway.
- **Anchor defendants and MNCs**: *Vedanta* made a parent company's arguable duty of care the key to jurisdiction over the foreign subsidiary. It also allowed parent companies to manipulate the proper place analysis by offering to submit abroad, which raises jurisdiction-shopping concerns. Post-Brexit, the parent can also seek a forum non conveniens stay.
- **Proportionality**: Lord Templeman in *Spiliada* and Lord Briggs in *Vedanta* (paras 6–14) warned against costly, lengthy jurisdiction battles. Yet the evaluative, fact-heavy test invites them.
- **UniCredit's departure from Spiliada**: for anti-suit relief supporting a foreign-seated arbitration, the "proper place" question was reframed as a question of comity and of enforcing the bargain. Is that principled, or an ad hoc exception?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the defendant outside England? If yes, can service be effected without permission (English jurisdiction clause, CPR 6.33(2B))? If not, permission is needed.
2. **Gateway**: identify one with a good arguable case.
   - Tort: was some significant damage sustained in England (*Brownlie*)? Or was the tort committed here?
   - Contract: governed by English law, made here, breached here.
   - Necessary or proper party: is there a real issue against the anchor defendant?
   - Arbitration or anti-suit claims.
3. **Merits**: serious issue to be tried. No mini-trial. Accept pleaded facts unless demonstrably untrue (*Okpabi*). If foreign law governs, consider proof and the presumption of similarity.
4. **Proper place**:
   - Has the claimant shown England is *clearly* the appropriate forum? Consider the place of the tort as a starting point, witnesses, governing law, related proceedings and the risk of irreconcilable judgments (and whether the anchor defendant has offered to submit abroad).
   - Neutrality alone is not enough.
5. **Substantial justice**: is there cogent evidence of a real risk of injustice abroad? Consider funding, legal capacity, corruption or independence, and time bars.
6. **Set-aside application**: anticipate the defendant's Part 11 challenge and any foreign jurisdiction clause.
7. **Traps**: applying the stay burden (on the defendant) in a service-out case; assuming a choice of English law makes England the natural forum; forgetting that after Brexit EU-domiciled defendants need a gateway too.
