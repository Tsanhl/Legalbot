# Pension issues in corporate transactions - share sales and asset sales

## Overview
The final part of the materials asks what a lawyer must watch when a company that sponsors or participates in a defined benefit (DB) occupational scheme is sold, or sells its business. The handbook calls two risks "unexploded nuclear bombs": the **section 75 debt on the employer** (Pensions Act 1995) and the **Pensions Regulator's moral hazard powers** (contribution notices and financial support directions, Pensions Act 2004). A third set of rules is the duty to give the Regulator notice of **notifiable events**. How the deal is structured (shares or assets) determines which risks arise. This is described as a good seminar for commercial awareness. Jurisdiction: England and Wales / UK, DB schemes under irrevocable trust. The funding and accounting parts of the same seminar are in db-scheme-funding-and-valuation.md and pension-accounting.md.

## The law
### 1. Notifiable events
- Corporate transactions involving a DB scheme generally trigger a **notifiable event** needing prompt notice to the Pensions Regulator: **Pensions Act 2004 s 69** and the Pensions Regulator (Notifiable Events) Regulations 2005 (plus a direction of 6 April 2005; the materials says to look only at events notifiable by the employer).
- Prescribed employer events in the extracts include a decision to cease to carry on business in the UK, and, where the employer is a company, a decision by a controlling company to **relinquish control** of the employer company (or the relinquishing of control itself).
- The "appropriate person" for an employer event is the employer (and a person of prescribed description); notice must be in writing and given as soon as reasonably practicable after becoming aware (s 69(4)).
- The civil penalty for failing to notify was raised from up to £5,000 (individuals) / £50,000 (companies) to up to **£1 million** by the Pension Schemes Act 2021 (new s 88A of the 2004 Act inserted by s 115; consequential amendment of s 69(7) by s 108). A further new s 69A (PSA 2021 s 109) was not in force at 8 November 2025 and is to be ignored for the materials.

### 2. Section 75 debt on the employer
- Section 75 of the Pensions Act 1995 (with s 75A for multi-employer schemes) and the Occupational Pension Schemes (Employer Debt) Regulations 2005. If a trigger occurs, a very large sum may become payable to the scheme unless it can be deferred or avoided.
- **Single employer scheme triggers** (slides): winding up of the scheme begins and the trustees fix the time for calculating the debt; the employer becomes insolvent (with no scheme rescue); the employer enters a members' voluntary winding up while solvent.
- **Multi-employer schemes**: the same triggers, with each employer paying its **pro rata share** (the slides give the formula EL/TL x D), and a further trigger, the **employment cessation event**. An employment cessation event occurs when an employer ceases to employ active members (in pensionable service, accruing DB benefits) while another employer in the same scheme continues to employ active members. Examples: sale by one employer of its business (TUPE transfer of all active members), the last active member leaving or retiring, the employer ceasing to participate (ending pensionable service of all its active members). The multi-employer modification replaces the debt with the employer's share of the difference and adds cessation expenses; shares may be reduced by scheme apportionment, flexible apportionment, or approved withdrawal arrangements.
- **Ways out ("get out of jail cards")**: a period of grace notice; all employers ceasing to employ active members on the same date; a scheme apportionment arrangement; a flexible apportionment arrangement. Orphan liabilities are flagged as an issue.
- Most DB schemes are set up by a "principal employer", usually the parent, for staff of parent and participating subsidiaries. Section 75 is written for a single-employer scheme, so the Employer Debt Regulations supply the multi-employer adjustments.
- The Pension Schemes Act 2021 changed the employer debt regime (and s 75(1A) excludes the CDC section of a DB scheme).

### 3. Contribution notices and financial support directions
- The Regulator may issue a **contribution notice** requiring a payment up to the scheme deficit on the buy-out (s 75) basis, or a **financial support direction** requiring "financial support", in practice a guarantee on terms the Regulator approves up to a specified amount (if not complied with, the Regulator can direct a cash payment to the scheme). Provisions: Pensions Act 2004 ss 38-42 (contribution notices) and ss 43-51 (financial support directions); PSA 2021 changed them.
- Targets: the seller, purchaser, company sold, or directors or employees of those companies, under the "**connected or associated**" test. The key concept is the **employer covenant**: if it can be shown that the transaction has no adverse effect on the covenant, the risk of the Regulator threatening a notice or direction is usually removed. Managing the Regulator is "a material part" of planning the transaction. See pensions-regulator-powers.md.

### 4. Share sales
On a sale of shares, the buyer takes the company with its pension position intact. Check three structures: (i) the company sold participates in a DB scheme retained by the seller's group; (ii) companies retained by the seller participate in a scheme in which the company sold is the principal employer; (iii) the company sold is the only employer in its own DB scheme. In each case manage the section 75 debt (employment cessation event, flexible apportionment) and Regulator risks. Also ask: does the company sold have post-sale liabilities under the trust deed and rules, and does it retain contribution duties under the Part 3 schedule of contributions? The worked problem (a subsidiary employing 2,000 members, scheme deficit of £80m, deficit contributions of £6m a year over 12 years) also raises employee consultation, terminating active membership, whether the departing employer owes further contributions under the deed (for example participation termination clauses), and who notifies the Regulator and when. Early-retirement rights on redundancy in the scheme rules matter to employee motivation.

### 5. Asset sales
- On an asset transfer DB obligations are "much less of an issue": the purchaser does not become an employer in the seller's DB scheme, so it is not subject to the Part 3 funding obligations or s 75, and contribution notices and financial support directions generally do not arise (but check s 43A, financial support directions on transfer of members).
- **TUPE 2006**: employment contracts of employees in the undertaking transfer to the purchaser (regs 4 and 5). Regulation 10 carves out occupational pension provisions not relating to "benefits for old age, invalidity and survivors", so in practice **early retirement rights** transfer (often called **Beckmann rights**); the handbook notes this is complex and cites several cases in the reading list. The purchaser's forward obligations are governed by Pensions Act 2004 ss 257 and 258 and Regulations.
- Accrued rights are defined by Pensions Act 1995 s 67A(6) and (7): calculated as if the employee were an active member who had opted out immediately before the time in question (see section-67-protection-of-accrued-rights.md).
- The purchaser acquires no liability for pension rights accrued before the sale for old age, invalidity and survivors; those stay with the seller's scheme.

## Key authorities
- Pensions Act 1995 ss 75, 75A, 67A; Occupational Pension Schemes (Employer Debt) Regulations 2005.
- Pensions Act 2004 ss 38-51 (moral hazard), s 69 and s 88A (notifiable events), ss 257-258 (transfers).
- Pensions Regulator (Notifiable Events) Regulations 2005.
- TUPE Regulations 2006 regs 4, 5, 10.
- Pension Schemes Act 2021 ss 108, 109, 115 (and the employer debt changes).
- Pollard on Corporate Insolvency (chapters on TUPE and pension liabilities, s 75 and TPR moral hazard powers) is the suggested further reading.

## Debate and critique
the materials frames the risks practically rather than with academic debate: the question is how to manage and price them. Points to discuss: the severity of the s 75 buy-out measure for a solvent employer leaving a scheme; the use of flexible apportionment and covenant arguments to avoid it; the Regulator's reach through "connected or associated" persons and its influence on deal structure; the large increase in the penalty for non-notification; and the tension between a TUPE transfer's employee protection and the carve-out for occupational pensions (leaving early retirement rights as the main transferred rights).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Share sale or asset sale? Draw the structure (seller, target, scheme, principal employer, buyer).
2. Is the target an employer in a DB scheme? Single or multi-employer? Who is principal employer?
3. Does an employment cessation event or other s 75 trigger occur? Can a flexible apportionment, scheme apportionment, or period of grace avoid it?
4. Assess employer covenant effect: risk of contribution notice or financial support direction; who is connected or associated.
5. Notifiable events: which event (relinquishing control; ceasing business), who must notify, timing, penalty.
6. Asset sale: TUPE transfer; what transfers under reg 10 (early retirement rights) versus what stays with the seller; the purchaser's forward pension duty under ss 257-258.
7. Check scheme rules for participation, withdrawal and termination clauses and any further contributions after exit.
