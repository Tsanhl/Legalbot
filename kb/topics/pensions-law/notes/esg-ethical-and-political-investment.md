# ESG, climate change, ethical and political factors in pension investment

## Overview
Can, or must, pension trustees take account of environmental (including climate), social and governance ("ESG") factors, or ethical and political concerns, when investing? The law separates two kinds of factor. **Financial factors**, including ESG risks that are financially material, may and should be considered. **Non-financial factors** (ethical views, quality of life, disapproval of industries) may be considered only within narrow limits. The case law runs from *Cowan v Scargill* (pensions: best financial interests) through the charity cases *Bishop of Oxford* (*Harries*) and *Butler-Sloss*, to the public law decision in *Palestine Solidarity Campaign*, where Lord Carnwath endorsed the Law Commission's two-part test, and *McGaughey v USS* (fossil fuel claim). It is now overlaid by statute: Investment Regulations reg 2 on SIP disclosure of policies on financially material and non-financial matters, and the Climate Change Governance and Reporting Regulations 2021. A sceptical view is taken here of the Law Commission's position for DB schemes, where the employer pays for underperformance.

## The law

### 1. Statutory framework
1. **IORP II Art 19(1)(b)**: Member States must **allow** IORPs to consider the potential long-term impact of investment decisions on ESG factors, but **within the prudent person rule**. Recital 58 notes the importance of ESG factors (UN PRI) for investment policy and risk management and asks for disclosure.
2. **Investment Regs reg 2(3)(b)(vi)-(vii)**: the SIP must state policies on **"financially material considerations"** (defined to include ESG and climate change considerations the trustees consider financially material) and on **the extent (if at all) to which "non-financial matters"** (members' ethical views and views on social and environmental impact and quality of life) are taken into account. Reg 2(3)(c) covers **stewardship** (voting and engagement).
3. **Reg 4**: best interests (sole interest where conflicted); security, quality, liquidity and profitability; diversification.
4. **PA 2004 s255 / IORP II Art 7**: activities limited to retirement-benefit activities. The discussion asks whether non-financial investing is consistent with this, and note the Law Commission did not consider it.
5. **Occupational Pension Schemes (Climate Change Governance and Reporting) Regulations 2021**: for schemes with relevant assets of **£1 billion or more** (the regulations also refer to a £5 billion tier), trustees must have effective governance of climate-related risks and opportunities. There are associated reporting and publication duties, including a greenhouse gas emissions target.
6. **Trust deed**: may expressly require or permit ethical investing. The Law Commission says this is an exception allowing even significant financial detriment.

### 2. Case law
- ***Cowan v Scargill*** [1985] Ch 270: best interests normally means best financial interests. Trustees must set aside personal, social and political views. An investment more beneficial than others must not be refused because of those views. There is a "very rare" caveat for beneficiaries who all share strict moral views, with a heavy burden on those relying on it. Excluding overseas investment and energy competing with coal was a breach. A tie-breaker use of non-financial factors between equally beneficial investments is noted in the opinion literature as consistent with *Cowan*.
- ***Bishop of Oxford v Church Commissioners*** ("*Harries*") [1991] PLR 185 / [1992] 1 WLR 1241 (Nicholls V-C, 25 October 1991): charity investment property is held to generate money, so the starting point is the **maximum return consistent with commercial prudence**, using well-established criteria (diversification, income versus growth, risk versus return). Comparatively rare exceptions:
  1. investments that **conflict with the charity's objects** (cancer charity and tobacco), which should be avoided even at significant financial detriment, although this is unlikely in practice;
  2. investments that **hamper the charity's work** by alienating supporters or recipients, which require a balancing exercise;
  3. where the **trust deed** so provides;
  4. where supporters hold **differing moral views**, trustees may accommodate one view if there is **no risk of significant financial detriment**.
  - Trustees "must not use property held by them for investment purposes as a means for making moral statements at the expense of the charity". The Commissioners' choice to exclude about 13% of the market but not 37% was within their discretion (Law Commission 1.34). Nicholls V-C considered his views consistent with *Cowan*.
- ***Butler-Sloss v Charity Commission*** [2022] EWHC 974 (Ch) (29 April 2022; trustees of Ashden and another Sainsbury family charity with environmental and poverty purposes; *Public Trustee v Cooper* category 2): there had been a "widespread misunderstanding" of *Harries*. There is **no absolute rule** on directly conflicting investments. Setting investment policy is always a **discretionary balancing** of the likelihood and seriousness of the conflict with purposes against the likelihood and seriousness of the financial effect, including reputational and donor risk. Caution is needed on purely moral grounds. Trustees who act **honestly, reasonably and responsibly** and adopt a **reasonable and proportionate** policy comply with their duties even if others would differ. Paris-aligned exclusions were approved. A warning is needed about the **distinction between a pension scheme's purpose (s255) and an environmental charity's**.
- ***R (Palestine Solidarity Campaign) v Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government*** [2020] UKSC 16 (29 April 2020): guidance under the Public Service Pensions Act 2013 that barred LGPS administrators from investment policies (boycotts, divestment) contrary to UK foreign or defence policy was **unlawful**. The majority held it was used for an extraneous purpose (*Padfield*): the power was for scheme administration and management, not foreign policy. LGPS administrators act as **quasi-trustees**, and the fund is in substance members' remuneration ("their money"). **Lord Carnwath** said the Law Commission (Law Com No 350) appeared to have settled the long-running debate, and that trustees may take non-financial factors into account "provided that doing so would not involve significant risk of financial detriment to the scheme and where they have good reason to think that scheme members would support their decision." the discussion asks: "But is Lord Carnwath right?"
  - The **Wilberforce comment (28 May 2020)**: the decision is "quite limited". It is a public law ruling on misuse of statutory power, not *carte blanche* for ethical divestment. Whether authorities may or must take non-financial factors into account on their own initiative remains largely open. The Law Commission test rests on "dubious authority", because it extends a charity principle from *Harries* to pensions in apparent tension with *Cowan*.
- ***McGaughey v USS*** [2022] EWHC 1233 (Ch); [2023] EWCA Civ 873: the fossil fuel divestment claim failed. An investment strategy cannot be challenged "purely on the grounds of differing environmental beliefs" without showing loss. The CA called it an attempt to challenge management decisions "without any ground". (See trustee-companies-directors-duties-and-conflicts for the standing and derivative claim hurdles.)

### 3. The Law Commission's position (2014 Law Com No 350; 2017 Report No 374 "Pension Funds and Social Investment", Sch 1 guidance)
1. The primary purpose of investment powers is to secure **the best realistic return over the long term, given the need to control for risks**. This is not "maximising" returns, because risk matters as much.
2. **Financial factors**, including risks to a company's long-term sustainability such as poor governance, environmental degradation, poor treatment of stakeholders and litigation risk, may always be considered. There is "no impediment" to taking account of ESG factors where they are or may be financially material, and trustees **should** take financially material risks into account. Whether a factor is material is for trustees on proper advice, and saying trustees "must" take an ESG approach is unhelpful because the label is ill-defined.
3. **Non-financial factors** may be considered if **(1)** trustees have **good reason to think members would share the concern** (survey evidence is not always needed; for example cluster munitions banned by convention; 100% agreement is not required; where a controversial issue divides members, focus on finance; there is no duty to consider members' views) and **(2)** the decision does **not risk significant financial detriment** (take advice; sector exclusions are often acceptable, a question of degree). The two tests are applied in the round.
4. **Exceptions** allowing significant detriment: where the **trust deed** expressly permits it; and in **DC schemes where the member chooses** a specific fund. A more flexible approach applies to **affinity groups** (religious, charitable or political organisations' schemes).
5. Government responses (2017 interim, then final) led to the SIP amendments now in reg 2.

### 4. Opinions and commentary in the reading
- **Bryant QC and Rickards, abridged joint opinion (25 November 2016, for ClientEarth)**: the primary duty is members' best financial interests. Trustees **can, and must**, take climate change risks into account to the extent they carry material financial implications. They must at least **turn their minds** to whether climate change is financially material, especially when it is raised. Refusing to consider it because it is "just ESG" would be an improper exercise of powers.
- **ClientEarth letter to Tesco Pension Scheme trustees (10 August 2018)**: climate change is a material financial risk (stranded assets, transition opportunities). Ignoring it could breach duty and create legal liability.
- **Philip Bennett, "Green Gilts and the Greenium" (Pensions Institute, 7 July 2021)** and the TLI 2021 article: buying green gilts at a "greenium" (a lower yield for the same credit risk) is a known financial detriment for non-financial reasons, so it is a **breach** under *Cowan* and s33. It fails the Law Commission's first limb, because trustees cannot assume members would pay more for a "green" label, and it fails Lord Carnwath's test. Exceptions arise only if the employer funds the cost, members give fully informed consent, or the deed requires it. The proper tool for climate policy is carbon taxation, not trustees sacrificing returns.
- **FMLC paper (6 February 2024)** "Pension Fund Trustees and Fiduciary Duties: Decision-making in the context of Sustainability and the subject of Climate Change": para 4.5 records that not all legal opinion agrees with the Law Commission on non-financial factors. Footnote 30 cites the Association of Pension Lawyers Investment & DC Sub-Committee note.
- **The "thought experiment"**: the impact of x basis points of reduced return compounds over 20 years on £1m. That makes it hard to advise where "significant financial detriment" begins. The test is ex ante, not hindsight. For **DB** schemes with solvent employers, each £1 of underperformance is £1 of employer cost. Weak investment can also weaken the **employer covenant**, and conversely, investments that damage the employer's brand (prompting boycotts) could harm covenant strength.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the Law Commission's two-part test good law for pensions?** Supporters: Lord Carnwath's *PSC* endorsement (obiter); regulatory adoption in SIP rules. Critics: the Wilberforce comment ("dubious authority", taken from the charity case *Harries*); the APL sub-committee (via FMLC); Bennett's greenium analysis; *Cowan*'s insistence on financial interests; the s255 retirement-benefit limit. Appendix 1 should be read "critically".
- **DB versus DC**: in DB the employer bears the cost of detriment, and members' benefits are fixed. Why should members' ethical views (test 1) justify imposing costs on the employer? In DC, member choice of ethical funds is the clearest case (an LC exception), but the discussion asks whether trustees are absolved from s33 liability for fund design, and whether the employer has any duty there.
- **Financial materiality is expanding**: climate change has moved from an "ethical" to a "financial" factor (Bryant opinion; climate regs; Tesco letter). Most climate-related decisions can now be framed as risk management. The debate then shifts to how good the evidence is, and to time horizons.
- **Charity versus pension purposes**: *Butler-Sloss* liberalised charity investment because charities have non-financial purposes. Pension schemes' purpose is financial (retirement benefits, s255), so its reasoning transfers only partly.
- **Political investment**: *PSC* stops government from imposing foreign-policy-based restrictions. It does not authorise trustees to pursue political aims. Spending scheme money on lobbying, as Mo demands in the BBB problem, is very hard to fit within retirement-benefit activities or best interests.
- **Litigation risk on both sides**: climate activists (*McGaughey*, ClientEarth) and employers or members suffering detriment can both challenge. Good process (advice, documented materiality analysis, SIP compliance) is the trustee's main defence.

## Applying it to a problem question (theBBB problem framework)
1. **Purpose and power**: the scheme's purpose clause (retirement benefits as defined in IORP II Art 6); s255; investment powers (s34); any deed provision on ethics. Is there an employer-and-trustee amendment route to add an ethical mandate?
2. **Classify each request**:
   - Divesting high-carbon assets: is it financially material (climate risk, stranded assets), which allows or even requires consideration, or non-financial?
   - £100,000 a year to a campaign group: not an investment. It is a payment outside retirement-benefit activities (s255) and outside best interests. Note it would need extra employer contributions.
3. **Advice**: the actuary and manager say divestment raises the DB deficit by £10m (1% of liabilities) and cuts the average DC pot by £10,000 (5%). Is that "significant financial detriment"? Distinguish DB (employer cost) from DC default members (who bear the loss). Consider the green DC option already available (member choice).
4. **Members' views (LC test 1)**: one deferred member's letter is not good reason to think members generally share the concern. Members' views are divided. Consider consultation.
5. **Employer interests**: BBB's brand and net-zero marketing, the threatened social media campaign, reputational impact on the covenant. This is relevant to financial materiality through the covenant, but trustees must not act for the employer's commercial goals (proper purpose; reg 4 sole interest where conflicted). The trustee company is a BBB subsidiary, so there are conflicts for employer-appointed directors.
6. **Process**: SIP review (reg 2), written advice (s36), climate governance regs if assets are £1bn or more (liabilities here are £1,000m), stewardship and engagement as a middle course, and a documented decision (*Edge*/*Butler-Sloss* reasonableness).
7. **Likely advice**: integrate financially material climate risk; consider engagement and gradual tilts with advice; reject the lobbying payment; keep and promote the DC green option; and do not divest wholesale at a known significant cost absent deed authority or clear member consensus. Explain the risk of challenge from both Mo (*McGaughey* shows the difficulty) and BBB.
8. Traps: treating *Butler-Sloss* as directly applicable to pensions; assuming Lord Carnwath's dictum is binding; ignoring the DB/DC split; forgetting s255.
