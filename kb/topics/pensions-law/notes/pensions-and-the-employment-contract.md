# Pension rights and the employment contract

## Overview
The first principle is that "in the beginning is the employment contract". Every pension right starts as a term of the remuneration package that an employer agrees to provide in return for an employee's labour. Usually it is then delivered through a separate occupational pension scheme held on irrevocable trust. This contract-and-trust structure explains why both contract doctrines (offer, acceptance, consideration, implied terms, variation, privity) and trust doctrines apply to pensions. It also explains how an employer can sometimes change pension rights by changing the contract rather than the trust deed. Answers to problem questions should start with the employment contract and work forward in time to the date that matters.

## The law

### 1. Freedom of contract and its limits
1. The bargain between employer and employee reflects (a) what the parties agree and (b) constraints imposed by retained EU and UK legislation on that freedom.
2. Remuneration terms come in two kinds:
   - **"Hard" rights**: clear and enforceable, for example pay for hours worked, often strengthened by overriding legislation.
   - **"Soft" rights**: depend on someone exercising a discretion, for example an employer deciding whether an employee meets ill-health early retirement criteria. The law controls soft rights by implying terms that limit both the *breadth* of the discretion and the *manner* of its exercise (*Mihlenstedt*; *Braganza*).

### 2. The remuneration package
- **Cash**: regular pay including overtime and shift allowances; bonuses; pay during absence (maternity, paternity, adoption, family leave, holiday, sick pay); termination payments (voluntary or compulsory redundancy, damages for wrongful or constructive dismissal, unfair dismissal compensation under ERA 1996 ss98-104D). A question is how a one-off termination lump sum differs from a lump sum paid by a pension scheme.
- **Employee benefits** (pension rights are a subset):
  1. non-cash benefits consumed during employment (company car, gym, share options with vesting periods and good/bad leaver rules);
  2. event-conditional insured benefits other than death (injury payments, permanent health insurance, which suspends the duties to work and to pay and may also fund pension contributions);
  3. death-in-service benefits (for example a lump sum of 3-4 times basic pay, or a survivor's pension), provided either under the OPS or under a separate "life cover only" scheme, which is an OPS for FA 2004 s150 purposes but not for PSA 1993 s1;
  4. early retirement benefits (incapacity, voluntary, redundancy). For a registered scheme these cannot be paid before minimum pension age under FA 2004 s279 (currently 55; the discussion says 57 from 6 April 2028) unless the ill-health condition is met;
  5. normal retirement benefits.
- Benefits provided by third parties need a chain of contracts: employer and provider, employer and employee, and, where a trust is used, employer and trustee and trustee and member under the trust deed and rules.

### 3. How pension rights are documented: the paper trail
1. The **employment contract** usually says only that the employee may join the employer's scheme "as from time to time amended", that the employer may amend it without compensation, that contributions may be deducted, and that details are in the booklet.
2. The **scheme booklet / guide** gives more detail but typically says it confers no rights and that rights are governed solely by the Trust Deed and Rules.
3. The **Trust Deed and Rules** (for example the New Airways Pension Scheme consolidated deed) and later **deeds of amendment** set out the legally binding terms.
4. **Communications over time** may or may not vary the contract. They may be misleading or wrong, or may describe benefits that are legally invalid. The question then is whether a communication creates a separate freestanding right, for example through **negligent misstatement** or **estoppel**.
5. Because employment and retirement can be 30-40 years apart, working out entitlement means **pensions archaeology**: tracing every document from the start of employment to retirement.

### 4. Other contract-derived features
- **Tranches**: DB pensions built up over time may have different revaluation and increase rules for different slices of service. Rule improvements may not apply to earlier leavers or to later joiners.
- **Transfers**: members of OPSs and PPSs have a statutory transfer right (PSA 1993 Chapter 4ZA, outside the detailed scope). A correct transfer pays a transfer value equal to the capital value of the deferred rights, buys transfer credits in the receiving scheme, and discharges the transferring scheme. Losses of more than £30 million to transfer scams have been reported.
- **Actuarial factors**: these work like an exchange rate between pension and capital. For example, giving up £2,500 a year of pension at a commutation factor of 15 produces a £37,500 lump sum. The same idea is used to adjust a pension for early or late payment.
- **Contracting-out and the State pension**: GMPs (6 April 1978 to 5 April 1997) and "section 9(2B) rights" (6 April 1997 to 5 April 2016) replaced the second-tier State pension in return for lower NICs. Money purchase contracting-out ran from 1988 to 2012. Scheme designs often include a **bridging pension** or a **State pension offset**.

### 5. Contract law doctrines that matter
1. **Offer, acceptance, consideration**: a statement by the employer or trustee to a deferred or pensioner member that purports to confer a valuable right is enforceable in contract only if those requirements are met. Pollard (ch 15) cites *Capita ATL Pension Trustees Ltd v Gellately* [2011] EWHC 485 (Ch): an intention to create legal relations, offer, acceptance and consideration must all be shown. Merely continuing to work after an announcement will rarely be consideration for a variation (*Jones v Associated Tunnelling Co* [1981] IRLR 477, EAT).
2. **Privity**: a surviving spouse is not a party to the employment contract. The question is how such a survivor gets enforceable rights. Options are the trust (as a beneficiary), the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 (consider s6(3)), and deed polls, which can confer enforceable rights on non-signatories who gave no consideration.
3. **Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 / Consumer Rights Act 2015**: does UCTA ss2-3 apply to a trustee exoneration clause, such as clause 24 of the NAPS deed, or is a trust deed not a contract at all? This is left open.
4. **Implied terms**: the employer's duty of trust and confidence and duty of good faith (*Imperial*; *Braganza*). There are also possible implied terms requiring an employer to tell an employee about a valuable right that the employee might wish to exercise.

### 6. Contract overriding trust
- Pollard (*The Law of Pension Trusts*, 2013, ch 15) explains that an employer and member may agree, by contract outside the trust, to reduced benefits. The leading case is *South West Trains v Wightman* [1998] PLR 113 (Neuberger J), where such a contract was held binding.
- Pollard's analysis:
  - where the contract **reduces** benefits, a trustee with notice of the agreement must run the trust in line with it, even if that departs from the deed;
  - where it **increases** benefits, the agreement may need trustee consent, because augmentation affects other members.
- Pollard rejects the objection that a trustee should only follow the deed. Schemes already depend on external facts such as salary and statute, and the trustee is being asked to *comply* with the contract, not to *rely* on it.
- Simple form: making pay rises **non-pensionable**. Express agreement works (*Bradbury v BBC* [2012] EWHC 1369 (Ch)). So does collective bargaining through a union (*Tibbals v Port of London Authority* [1937] 2 All ER 413, HL; *South West Trains*). Implied acceptance by taking the pay rise also works (*Trustees of the NUS Officials' and Employees' Superannuation Fund v Pensions Ombudsman* [2002] PLR 93, Lightman J: the employee "could not pick and choose").
- Continuing to work is deemed acceptance only if the conduct is referable *only* to the new terms (Elias J in *Solectron*; applied in *FW Farnsworth Ltd v Lacy* [2012] EWHC 2830 (Ch)).

## Key authorities
- *Mihlenstedt v Barclays Bank International Ltd* [1989] PLR 91 (CA, 28 July 1987; Nourse and Nicholls LJJ, Sir John May): an ill-health early retirement rule saying retirement "will be permitted only when" the Bank formed a medical opinion was held to be language of **obligation and entitlement**, not permission. By necessary implication from the employment contract, the Bank had to perform its functions in good faith, including consulting its medical adviser and properly forming an opinion. An opinion formed dishonestly or on an erroneous basis can be set aside and a fresh decision directed. Whether the duty arises in trust or in contract does not change the remedy. Nourse LJ called references to "legitimate expectations" in contract "unnecessary and undesirable". On the facts the appeal was dismissed. Sir John May would have required the mental incapacity point to be reconsidered.
- *Braganza v BP Shipping Ltd* [2015] UKSC 17: a contractual death-in-service benefit was excluded if, in the employer's opinion, death resulted from the officer's wilful act. By a 3-2 majority (Lady Hale, Lord Kerr, Lord Hodge; Lord Neuberger and Lord Wilson dissenting), the employer's finding of suicide was irrational. A term is implied that the decision-making process must be lawful and rational in the public law sense, covering both Wednesbury limbs (process and outcome). Employment is a relational contract, which justifies more intense scrutiny. Because suicide is inherently improbable, the evidence had to be cogent. The employer could have "sat on the fence".
- *Scottish Equitable plc v Derby* [2000] PLR 1 (Harrison J, 30 September 1999): overpayment from an individual pension policy caused by a computer error (about £172,451). Money paid under a mistake of fact is prima facie recoverable, and the payer's carelessness is no bar. **Change of position** operates pro tanto. It succeeded only for about £9,661 of modest lifestyle spending. It failed for early repayment of a mortgage (no detriment) and for an investment that could be unwound. General hardship not causally linked to the payment does not count. **Estoppel** was rejected as a complete defence where the detriment was small compared with the overpayment (distinguishing *Avon CC v Howlett*; applying *Lipkin Gorman*).
- *South West Trains v Wightman* [1998] PLR 113 (Neuberger J): a union-negotiated deal limited pensionable pay for past service (about £11,950) while raising it for future service (about £18,000). The contract bound the members even though, on its face, implementing it needed a trust deed amendment.
- *Tesco v USDAW* [2024] UKSC 28: on whether an employer had contractually limited its freedom to dismiss on notice and re-hire ("retained pay").

## Debate and critique
- **Contract or trust?** Pollard describes a tripartite structure (employer, trustee, member) and warns against importing trust-like duties into the external contract, or treating the deed as the only source of rights. Commentators he cites (Maudgil; Ham QC and Hilliard; Burroughs) doubt whether an external contract should bind a trustee. Pollard calls that view "misguided".
- **Two rationales** for upholding non-pensionable pay deals (Maudgil, cited by Pollard): (a) the parties define "pay", which is an external fact the scheme takes as given; or (b) a broader principle that a person who has contracted not to claim against a third party cannot do so where the other contracting party has an interest in restraining the claim.
- **Deferred pay theory versus reality**: pensions are described as deferred pay, but death benefits, discretionary ill-health pensions and employer amendment powers make the "pay" far less secure than wages.
- **Communications risk**: booklets disclaim legal effect, yet members rely on them. The tension between protecting reliance (negligent misstatement, estoppel) and the formality of the trust deed comes up again in the amendment cases (see amendment-powers-and-employer-constraints) and in the Ombudsman's maladministration jurisdiction.
- **Restitution versus reliance**: *Derby* shows the courts preferring the flexible pro tanto change of position defence to an all-or-nothing estoppel. This matters a great deal for pension overpayments, which the Pensions Ombudsman frequently has to deal with.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the **employment contract** terms on pensions and any later variations, collective agreements or "retained pay" style promises.
2. Follow the **document chain**: contract, booklet, Trust Deed and Rules, deeds of amendment, communications. Check whether any communication created a contract (offer, acceptance, consideration, intention), an estoppel, or a claim for negligent misstatement.
3. Classify the right as **hard** (an entitlement) or **soft** (a discretion). For a discretion, apply *Mihlenstedt* and *Braganza*: was it formed in good faith and rationally, were relevant factors considered, and was the outcome perverse?
4. For a **survivor** claimant, explain the route to enforcement: trust beneficiary status, the 1999 Act, or a deed poll.
5. For an **overpayment**, apply *Derby*: mistake, prima facie recovery, change of position pro tanto, and causation.
6. For a proposed **benefit cut through the contract** (for example non-pensionable pay rises), consider *South West Trains*, *Bradbury*, *NUS Officials*, and whether conduct is unequivocally referable to the new terms. Then check trust-side constraints and section 67 (see section-67-protection-of-accrued-rights).
7. Traps: treating booklet wording as binding; assuming continuing to work equals acceptance; forgetting privity for dependants; ignoring the tranches of a DB pension.
