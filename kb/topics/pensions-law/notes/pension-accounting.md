# Accounting for pension costs - FRS 102 section 28 and IAS 19

## Overview
the materials asks lawyers to understand, at a high level, how pension promises show up in a sponsoring employer's **income statement** (profit and loss) and **balance sheet**. That matters because pension deficits affect deal pricing, covenants, dividends and investor perceptions in corporate transactions. Pension accounting uses **accruals**, not cash, and a **best-estimate** actuarial valuation with a **high-quality corporate bond** discount rate. So accounting figures differ from Part 3 funding figures and from s75 buy-out debts. UK companies not required to use IAS 19 use **FRS 102 section 28**. Groups with securities admitted to trading on a regulated market use **IAS 19** in their consolidated accounts (handbook). The two are conceptually similar.

## The law (accounting rules)

### 1. Basic accounting building blocks (handbook Part III)
1. **Income statement**: covers a period. It shows sales, cost of sales, profit or loss, tax and net profit after tax. Pension cost for the period is an **expense**. Some changes caused by assumption changes go to **other comprehensive income (OCI)** instead.
2. **Balance sheet**: a point in time. Assets minus liabilities equals shareholders' equity, so it "balances".
3. **Company versus group accounts**: consolidated accounts eliminate intra-group transactions. Pension costs often appear only in the **group** accounts, because it is unclear how to apportion a shared DB deficit between participating companies. Compare the BA plc group accounts with the subsidiary BA Avionic Engineering Ltd's individual accounts (note 2.2 and note 7 against BA plc note 28).
4. **Accounting policies and notes**: going concern versus winding-up basis. A discontinuance basis adds liabilities such as termination costs. The **pensions note** is the fastest way to see what pension arrangements a company has: do a Companies House search and read the note.

### 2. Accruals versus cash
- Income and expenditure are attributed to the period they relate to, not when cash moves. Cash is shown separately in the cash flow statement.

### 3. DC (money purchase) plans (IAS 19 paras 50-54)
- The employer recognises the contribution payable for the period's service as an expense, with any unpaid amount as a liability. **No actuarial assumptions** are needed and there are no actuarial gains or losses. IAS 19 uses the terms "defined contribution plan" and "defined benefit plan" rather than the UK statutory terms.

### 4. DB plans (IAS 19 paras 55-134; FRS 102 s28)
1. **Measurement**: the **projected unit credit method** (IAS 19 para 67 wording, "An entity shall use the projected unit credit method..."; FRS 102 para 28.18). Assumptions are **unbiased best estimates** that are mutually compatible. The **discount rate** is the market yield at the reporting date on **high-quality corporate bonds**, or government bonds where there is no deep market (IAS 19 para 83).
2. **Balance sheet**: for a trust-based funded scheme, only the **net** position appears. A **deficit** (present value of obligations above the fair value of assets) is a **provision / liability**. A **surplus** is an **asset**, capped at the **asset ceiling**, meaning the present value of economic benefits available as refunds or reductions in future contributions.
3. **Unfunded ("book reserve") schemes**: the full present value of the obligation appears on the balance sheet, because no assets are held separately. These are common in Germany, and in the UK appear for unfunded top-ups to directors (compare *Granada* in the materials).
4. **Three components of cost** (lecture summary of IAS 19):
   - **service cost** (current and past service, and settlement gains or losses) in profit or loss;
   - **net interest** (the net liability or asset × the discount rate) in profit or loss;
   - **remeasurements** (actuarial gains and losses, the return on assets excluding net interest, changes in the asset ceiling effect) in **OCI**, and never "recycled" to profit or loss.
5. **Deferred tax**: a deficit may come with a **deferred tax asset**, because paying contributions to clear it will be tax-deductible.
6. **Constructive obligations**: IAS 19 covers informal practices the entity has no realistic alternative but to honour (lecture summary).

### 5. the materials questions
- **Q1 (which income statement lines are affected by pension costs?)**: cost of sales and operating expenses (service cost within employee costs), finance costs and income (net interest), and therefore profit before tax, tax and net profit. Remeasurements bypass profit or loss and go to OCI.
- **Q2 (which balance sheet lines are affected by a DB deficit or surplus?)**: a non-current liability or provision for the net pension deficit, or an asset (debtors and prepayments in the handbook's illustration) for a surplus within the asset ceiling; the deferred tax asset; and so shareholders' equity (reserves).

### 6. British Airways example (optional reading)
- BA has two large legacy schemes, the **APS** and **NAPS**. The accounts note triennial **funding** valuations at 31 March 2021: an APS technical provisions surplus of £295m and a NAPS deficit of £1.65bn. They also note that NAPS was in an accounting surplus at 31 December 2024, so no dividend-linked payment was due under the funding agreement. The sensitivity table shows a 0.25% fall in the discount rate increasing the obligation by about £369m (per the materials summary). This contrast shows that funding and accounting positions can diverge.

## Key authorities
- IAS 19 paras 1-8, 50-54, 55-134 (especially 64 on the asset ceiling, 67 on the projected unit credit method, 83 on the discount rate, 120 on components).
- FRS 102 section 1 (scope) and section 28 (employee benefits), especially 28.18.
- BA plc report and accounts 2024 (pages 42-46, 58, 63, 66, note 28) and BA Avionic Engineering Ltd accounts.

## Debate and critique
- **Which number is "the deficit"?** Accounting (best estimate, corporate bond discount), funding (prudent) and buy-out (insurer pricing) give different answers. Lawyers drafting sale agreements, price adjustments or warranties must specify the basis. Otherwise disputes follow, as *Procter & Gamble v SCA* shows with valuation assumptions built into the sale agreement (see corporate-transactions-share-and-asset-sales).
- **Volatility and OCI**: putting remeasurements in OCI keeps reported profit stable, but balance sheet volatility (from discount rate swings) can still affect distributable reserves, credit ratings and covenant perceptions. That in turn influences employer decisions such as closing DB accrual (compare *IBM*'s earnings-per-share goals in amendment-powers-and-employer-constraints).
- **Asset ceiling and surplus**: whether a surplus is recognised depends on the employer's right to a refund or contribution holiday under the trust deed. Trust law (surplus powers, *Mettoy*) and accounting are therefore linked.
- **Group accounting**: pension deficits sit in group accounts. Subsidiaries' individual accounts may show little, even though their s75 exposure in a multi-employer scheme can be large. This is a due diligence trap.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the plan type** (DC or DB) and whether it is funded via a trust or unfunded.
2. **Find the pensions note** in the target's (and group's) accounts: schemes, assumptions, net position, sensitivities, funding valuation results, contribution commitments.
3. **Separate the bases**: accounting net liability versus Part 3 deficit versus s75 buy-out debt versus PPF s143. Explain which one drives price, which drives cash calls, and which drives exit costs.
4. **Income statement effect**: service cost and net interest in profit or loss. Remeasurements in OCI.
5. **Balance sheet effect**: net liability or asset (with the asset ceiling), deferred tax, equity.
6. **Transaction drafting**: define the valuation basis, assumptions and date in any price adjustment or indemnity.
7. Traps: assuming an accounting surplus means no funding deficit or no s75 debt; looking only at individual accounts in a group scheme.
