# Sex equality in pensions - EU law (Article 157 TFEU, Barber, Coloroll, actuarial factors)

## Overview
The discussion deals only with **sex** discrimination in pensions. Age and sexual orientation are excluded, although *Lord Chancellor v McCloud* [2018] EWCA Civ 2844 and *Walker v Innospec* [2017] UKSC 47 are flagged as important. The EU equal pay principle drove the reform. Article 157 TFEU (formerly Art 141 EC and Art 119 EEC) requires equal pay for equal work, and the ECJ held that occupational pensions are "pay". *Barber* (1990) made different pension ages for men and women unlawful, but limited the ruling's effect in time. *Coloroll* (1994) then settled levelling up, levelling down, the position of trustees and actuarial factors. The Directives (2006/54, 2000/78), the Charter and *Test-Achats* complete the EU picture. Since Brexit and the REUL Act 2023, how much of this can still be enforced depends on when the facts occurred.

## The law

### 1. Sources
1. **Art 157 TFEU**: each Member State must ensure equal pay for male and female workers for equal work or work of equal value. "Pay" includes "any other consideration, whether in cash or in kind", that the worker receives directly or indirectly from the employer in respect of employment. Note the earlier numbering (Art 141 TEC; Art 119 EEC), which appears in the case reports.
2. **Maastricht Protocol** (Protocol concerning Art 157, Treaty of Maastricht signed 7 February 1992): occupational social security benefits are not to be considered pay so far as they are attributable to periods of employment **before 17 May 1990**, except for workers (or those claiming under them) who had started legal proceedings or made an equivalent claim before that date.
3. **Directive 2006/54/EC** (Recast Equal Treatment Directive): prohibits direct and indirect sex discrimination in occupational social security schemes (scope, access, contributions, calculation of benefits). Article 9(1) lists examples of discriminatory provisions. Among them is setting different levels of benefit, except as needed to reflect sex-differing actuarial calculation factors in **defined-contribution** schemes. In **funded defined-benefit** schemes, certain elements may be unequal where the inequality results from using sex-differing actuarial factors when the scheme's funding is implemented. Recitals 15-16 give examples, reflecting *Coloroll*: conversion of pension into a capital sum, transfer of pension rights, a reversionary pension for a dependant, and early retirement reductions. Recital 17 repeats the pre-17 May 1990 limitation.
4. **Directive 2000/78/EC** (Framework Directive): age, religion or belief, disability, sexual orientation. For sex, only the general principles apply. Art 6(2) provides that fixing ages for admission or entitlement in occupational social security schemes, and using age criteria in actuarial calculations, is not age discrimination "provided this does not result in discrimination on the grounds of sex".
5. **EU Charter**: Art 21 (non-discrimination, including sex), Art 23 (equality between women and men "in all areas, including employment, work and pay"), Art 51 (field of application), Art 52 (scope and interpretation).

### 2. Barber v Guardian Royal Exchange Assurance Group (C-262/88, 17 May 1990)
- **Facts**: Mr Barber was made compulsorily redundant at 52. Under the contracted-out scheme a woman in his position would have received an immediate pension (pension age 50 for women, 55 for men, on early retirement through redundancy), while he got only a deferred pension.
- **Held**:
  1. Benefits paid in connection with compulsory redundancy are pay under Art 119, whether under contract, statute or paid voluntarily.
  2. A pension under a **contracted-out private occupational scheme is pay**.
  3. It breaches Art 119 for a man made redundant to receive only a deferred pension where a woman would get an immediate one because of a sex-based age condition. **Equal pay must be ensured for each element of remuneration**, not just through an overall comparison.
  4. Art 119 is directly effective in national courts.
  5. **Temporal limitation**: direct effect cannot be relied on to claim a pension entitlement with effect from a date before the judgment, except by those who had already started proceedings or made an equivalent claim.
- The Advocate General's opinion is required reading. The question is what the temporal limitation meant, which *Coloroll* later clarified.

### 3. Coloroll Pension Trustees Ltd v Russell (C-200/91, 28 September 1994), joined with *Ten Oever* (C-109/91), *Moroni* (C-110/91) and *Neath* (C-152/91)
Operative rulings:
1. Employees **and their dependants** can rely on Art 119 against **trustees**, who must observe equal treatment in exercising their powers and obligations under the trust deed.
2. Where national law prevents employers and trustees acting beyond their powers, they must use all available domestic means, including going to court, to eliminate discrimination.
3. **Levelling**:
   - For service **after the discrimination was found (17 May 1990) but before measures to remove it came into force**, the disadvantaged must receive the **same advantages** as the advantaged (levelling up).
   - For service **after those measures**, equality may be achieved by **reducing** the advantaged group's benefits (levelling down).
   - For service **before 17 May 1990**, EU law imposed no obligation that would justify retroactively reducing the favoured group's benefits.
4. National courts must ensure correct implementation, taking account of employers' and trustees' respective liabilities.
5. Problems caused by the fund being insufficient to equalise must be resolved under national law in light of equal pay, and do not change the answers above.
6. Under *Barber*, direct effect applies only to benefits for **service after 17 May 1990** (with the exception for earlier claims).
7. The temporal limitation applies to **survivors' pensions**.
8. For benefits not linked to length of service (for example lump sum death in service benefits), the limitation applies only where the **operative event** was before 17 May 1990.
9. The *Barber* principles cover **non-contracted-out** schemes as well as contracted-out ones.
10. **Sex-differentiated actuarial factors in funded DB schemes are outside Art 119**. So inequalities in capital or substitute benefits whose value depends on the scheme's funding arrangements (such as transfer values and commutation) are not caught.
11. Art 119 covers all benefits whether funded by employer or employee contributions, but **purely voluntary additional contributions**, where the scheme merely provides the administrative arrangements, are not covered.

### 4. Lindorfer v Council of the EU (C-227/04 P, 11 September 2007)
- An EU official transferred national pension rights into the EU staff pension scheme. The number of pensionable years credited was calculated using sex-specific actuarial values.
- The ECJ **upheld her first ground of appeal**: using sex-specific actuarial values in that calculation breached the principle of equal treatment between men and women. The contested Council decision was annulled.
- Her complaints about age and about the currency conversion formulae failed. A sum of money and a period of service are not comparable values. Conversion variant (ii) was optional, so officials could choose the more favourable formula.
- The summary contrasts the EU scheme's collective pay-as-you-go character (identical contributions, formula benefits) with funded schemes, where *Coloroll* para 10 had allowed sex-based actuarial factors.

### 5. Test-Achats (C-236/09, 1 March 2011)
- **Held**: Art 5(2) of Directive 2004/113/EC (goods and services), which let Member States keep sex-based differences in insurance premiums and benefits indefinitely, is **invalid with effect from 21 December 2012**, because it conflicts with equal treatment (Charter Arts 21 and 23).
- The question is why this matters, given that the goods and services Directive does not apply to occupational pension schemes. The point is the reasoning: a permanent derogation allowing sex-based actuarial factors undermines the equality goal. That casts doubt on the *Coloroll* para 10 approach and on UK exceptions that allow sex-based commutation factors (see sex-equality-uk-equalisation, Q5).

### 6. Brexit and enforceability
- **EUWA 2018 s4(1)** kept Art 157's direct effect for the time being. **s5(4)** removed the Charter. **s5(5)** kept its codified general principles for interpretation. **Sch 1 para 3** removed disapplication and freestanding claims.
- **REUL Act 2023** (ss2, 3, 22(5)): for anything occurring **after 31 December 2023**, claims based on directly effective Treaty provisions such as Art 157 can no longer be brought. Claims about earlier events based on retained Art 157 can still be brought. Directive-based claims against the State for non-transposition are similarly limited to rights recognised before 11pm on 31 December 2020.
- Pre-2021 ECJ case law (*Barber*, *Coloroll*, *Lindorfer*, *Test-Achats*, *Safeway*) remains relevant to interpreting retained law, subject to the higher courts' power to depart from it.

## Key authorities
- Art 157 TFEU (ex 141 TEC; ex 119 EEC); Maastricht Protocol.
- *Barber v GRE* C-262/88 [1990]: pensions are pay; equal pay element by element; temporal limitation.
- *Coloroll v Russell* C-200/91 [1994] (with *Ten Oever*, *Moroni*, *Neath*): trustees bound; levelling up and down; survivors; non-contracted-out schemes; actuarial factors; AVCs.
- *Lindorfer v Council* C-227/04 P [2007]: sex-specific actuarial values on transfer breach equal treatment.
- *Test-Achats* C-236/09 [2011]: Art 5(2) of Directive 2004/113 invalid from 21 December 2012.
- *Safeway v Newton* C-171/18 [2019] (see the UK note): no retroactive levelling down for the period between announcement and adoption.
- Directives 2006/54/EC, 2000/78/EC; Charter Arts 21, 23, 51, 52.
- *Walker v Innospec* [2017] UKSC 47 and *Lord Chancellor v McCloud* [2018] EWCA Civ 2844 (outside the scope).

## Debate and critique
- **Legal certainty versus equality**: *Barber*'s temporal limitation and the Maastricht Protocol protected schemes from huge retrospective costs. The price was that discrimination in pre-1990 service continues to affect pensions paid today. Is a fundamental right properly limited by cost? *Coloroll* para 5 says funding shortfalls cannot change the equal pay answer, which sits awkwardly with the *Barber* limitation itself being justified by financial consequences.
- **Actuarial factors**: *Coloroll* (1994) treated sex-based actuarial factors in funded DB schemes as outside Art 119, because they reflect funding, not pay. *Lindorfer* and *Test-Achats* moved towards unisex treatment. The tension between *Coloroll* and these later cases is unresolved for UK occupational schemes and is a natural essay topic.
- **Element-by-element comparison**: *Barber* rejects an overall package comparison, which produces strict outcomes (for example bridging pensions need specific statutory exceptions).
- **Levelling down**: EU law requires equality, not a particular level of benefit. So once compliant, schemes may lower the advantaged group's benefits. Formal equality can therefore leave both sexes worse off, which raises the question of whether that serves the substantive equality aims of Charter Art 23.
- **Post-Brexit erosion**: the end of direct Art 157 claims for post-2023 events means future sex equality enforcement relies on the Equality Act 2010 alone. How fully the Act reflects the EU case law becomes critical.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is it "pay"?** Occupational pension (contracted-out or not), redundancy benefits, survivors' pensions: yes. Purely voluntary AVCs: no (*Coloroll* 11).
2. **Is there sex discrimination in an element of pay?** Compare element by element (different NPAs, different early retirement terms, survivor rules).
3. **Date the service**: before 17 May 1990 (no EU obligation, unless proceedings were started earlier); from 17 May 1990 until equalising measures took effect (level up); after effective measures (levelling down permitted). For non-service-related benefits, use the date of the operative event.
4. **Who is liable?** Employers and trustees, who must use all domestic means, including court applications, to equalise (*Coloroll* 1-2, 4).
5. **Actuarial factors**: is this a funded DB scheme (*Coloroll* 10, outside Art 119), an EU staff-type scheme (*Lindorfer*), or affected by *Test-Achats* reasoning? Then check the UK exceptions.
6. **Enforcement route and timing**: Art 157 directly for events up to 31 December 2023; otherwise the Equality Act 2010 interpreted in line with the retained case law.
7. Traps: treating 17 May 1990 as the end rather than the start of the Barber window; assuming any levelling down is unlawful; ignoring the survivors' pension limitation; overlooking the *Test-Achats* reasoning point.
