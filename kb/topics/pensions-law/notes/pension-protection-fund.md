# The Pension Protection Fund - eligibility, compensation and the EU law dimension

## Overview
The Pension Protection Fund (PPF) is the UK's statutory lifeboat for **defined benefit** occupational pension schemes whose sponsoring employer becomes insolvent while the scheme is underfunded on the PPF basis and no rescue is possible. It was created by the Pensions Act 2004 (Board under s107; Part 2). As the handbook stresses, it exists because of **Article 8 of the Insolvency Directive (80/987/EEC, now 2008/94/EC)** and the ECJ's ruling in *Robins*. ECJ case law (*Robins*, *Hogan*, *Hampshire*, *Bauer*) and UK litigation (*Hughes*) have since reshaped the level of compensation. The PPF also affects trustee decisions in distressed schemes: *Brass Trustees v Goldstone* and the the materials CFCF problem concern "scheme drift", "PPF drift" and whether trustees may factor in the PPF.

## The law

### 1. When does the PPF take over?
1. **Eligible scheme** (PA 2004 s126): an occupational pension scheme that is **not a money purchase scheme** and not prescribed. Collective money purchase sections are treated as separate money purchase schemes (s126(1A)). Schemes already winding up before the appointed day are excluded.
2. **Qualifying insolvency event** affecting the employer (s127), or a s129 application or notification (s128) where the employer is unlikely to continue as a going concern.
3. **Underfunding**: the value of scheme assets immediately before the event is **less than the protected liabilities** (ss131, 143). Protected liabilities are the cost of securing benefits equal to PPF compensation, plus liabilities to non-members and wind-up expenses. Money purchase assets and liabilities are excluded.
4. **No scheme rescue**: a scheme failure notice becomes binding and no withdrawal event is possible (s149).
5. During the **assessment period** (s132), benefits are reduced to PPF levels (s138(2)). If the assets turn out to cover protected liabilities, the trustees must wind up the scheme outside the PPF (**s154**), as happened to Mr Hampshire's T&N scheme. Otherwise a **transfer notice** (s160) passes the scheme's property, rights and liabilities to the Board, discharges the trustees' pension obligations, and treats the scheme as wound up (**s161**). The Board then pays compensation under the **pension compensation provisions** (s162, Sch 7).
6. **Valuation**: PA 2004 ss143-145 (the s143 valuation on PPF assumptions).
7. **Priority on winding up outside the PPF**: PA 1995 **s73** applies to non-money-purchase schemes in this order: (a) certain pre-1997 insurance policies that cannot be surrendered; (b) liabilities up to the **corresponding PPF liability**; (c) benefits from members' voluntary contributions; (d) everything else. Within a category that cannot be met in full, liabilities abate proportionately.
8. Multi-employer and "last man standing" schemes add complexity that is outside the module.

### 2. How the PPF is financed
- Mainly through the **pension protection levy** on eligible schemes. The handbook says it is in effect borne by employers, since every £1 of levy paid from scheme assets raises the employer's Part 3 funding obligations by £1.
- Shortfalls are met from levies and from investment returns above the discount rate assumed for its liabilities. As a last resort, compensation can be reduced to rebalance.
- **Position at 31 March 2025** (handbook): levies £104m in the year; compensation paid £1.2bn; assets £31.2bn against liabilities with a present value of £17bn (so a surplus of £14.2bn); 289,469 members. Future claims and discount-rate movements are uncertain.
- The Board also runs the **Fraud Compensation Fund**. There is a **PPF Ombudsman** (from 6 April 2006), and the **Financial Assistance Scheme** covers some earlier failures (CMS Handbook).

### 3. Level of compensation (handbook summary of Sch 7)
1. **At or above PPF normal pension age**: **100%** of the accrued pension. No indexation for pre-6 April 1997 service. Post-5 April 1997 service increases at CPI capped at **2.5%** (so there is a loss where scheme increases were up to 5% for 1997-2005 service, or higher).
2. **Below PPF normal pension age**: **90%** of the accrued pension; increases reduced as above; deferred revaluation limited to CPI over complete years of deferral, or if lower 5% a year (service before 6 April 2009) or 2.5% a year (service after 5 April 2009). Special rules apply where service ended between 1 January 1986 and 31 December 1990. There is no revaluation if service ended before 1 January 1986.
3. **Compensation cap**: historically applied to the under-NPA group (Sch 7 para 26). It was £41,461 at age 65 from 1 April 2020, actuarially reduced for younger ages, with a long-service uplift of 3% per year over 20 years up to double the standard amount. **It was disapplied as unlawful age discrimination in *Hughes*.**
4. **Hampshire underpin**: every individual must receive at least 50% of the value of accrued old-age benefits (see below).
5. **Bauer "at risk of poverty" underpin**: not applicable where the employer insolvency event occurs **after 31 December 2023**.

### 4. EU law: Article 8 of Directive 2008/94/EC
Member States must take the measures necessary to protect employees' and former employees' interests in respect of immediate or prospective entitlement to old-age benefits, including survivors' benefits, under supplementary schemes outside statutory social security, when the employer is insolvent.
- ***Robins v SSWP*** (C-278/05, 25 January 2007): ASW employees' schemes wound up underfunded. Under priority rules some claimants would get 20% or 49%. **Held**: (1) Art 8 does not require full funding, and States have considerable latitude; (2) a system that can deliver **less than half** of accrued entitlement does not "protect", so the UK was in breach (the Financial Assistance Scheme helped fewer than 13%); (3) State liability requires a **sufficiently serious breach**. Given the imprecise wording and a 1995 Commission report suggesting compliance, liability needed a "manifest and grave disregard". The handbook says that but for Art 8 and *Robins* the UK would not have had to set up the PPF.
- ***Hogan v Ireland*** (C-398/11, 25 April 2013): Waterford Crystal employees were to recover 16-41%. **Held**: the Directive applies where scheme membership was a condition of employment; the **State pension cannot be counted**; the **cause of underfunding is irrelevant**; an **economic crisis is no excuse** to fall below the minimum; and after *Robins* a failure to secure more than 49% is **in itself a sufficiently serious breach**.
- ***Hampshire v Board of the PPF*** (C-17/17, 6 September 2018): T&N early retiree whose £48,781.80 pension fell to £19,819 because of the cap (about 25% of total value, given lost increases). **Held**: Art 8 gives an **individual** minimum guarantee of at least **50% of the value of accrued old-age benefits** for each employee, not an average. Art 8 is **directly effective** (unconditional and sufficiently precise: beneficiaries, content after *Robins*, and liable body once the State designated the PPF). The **PPF is an emanation of the State** (a public-interest task, special powers such as levies and directions), so it can be relied on against the PPF. An adverse knock-on effect on the scheme does not prevent this.
- ***PSV v Bauer*** (C-168/18, 19 December 2019): a German Pensionskasse cut benefits, and the guaranteeing employer then became insolvent. **Held**: Art 8 applies. Even above 50%, a loss is **manifestly disproportionate** if the former employee lives, or would have to live, below the Eurostat **at-risk-of-poverty threshold**. Art 8 is directly effective against a body like PSV, within the scope of the tasks entrusted to it. The ECJ rejected the Advocate General's (8 May 2019) suggestion that substantially higher protection was required.

### 5. UK litigation: Hughes
- ***Hughes v Board of the PPF*** (HC, 22 June 2020): BALPA and 24 claimants. Mr Hughes's £66,245 pension became £17,481 after the age-adjusted cap and 90% reduction.
  - **Held**: (1) the **cap is unlawful age discrimination** and must be **disapplied**. The difference in treatment is age-based (under or over NPA). The moral hazard justification was "so weak as to be without reasonable foundation": the 90% rule already deters, the cap hit only about 0.5% of members, and it also hurt long-serving non-influential staff. (2) A **lifetime cumulative** approach to the 50% test is permissible, but the HC required a check that actual lifetime payments would not fall below 50%, and held that survivors needed a separate 50% guarantee. (3) Arrears claims are subject to a **6-year limit** (Limitation Act 1980 s9: a statutory sum, not trust property under s21(1)(b)). (4) Interest was left undecided. (5) During assessment, trustees must pay up to the **uncapped** level, reading s138(2) consistently with directly effective EU law.
- ***SSWP and Board of the PPF v Hughes*** [2021] (CA, 19 July 2021): **cap ruling upheld**. The justification is measured against Art 8's aim of protection, and the cap had to be justified from enactment. The **PPF's one-off actuarial valuation ("Value Test") is lawful**: the HC's distinction between predicted and actual value was a "chimera". **Survivors' benefits** are part of the employee's bundle of rights and may be valued within it.
- **Brexit**: pre-2021 ECJ case law is retained EU law. The REUL Act 2023 changes the position for acts or omissions after 31 December 2023. Leaving aside the Bauer underpin, the effect of *Hughes* is retained (handbook).

### 6. Trustees, distressed employers and the PPF
- ***Brass Trustees Ltd v Goldstone and the PPF*** (HC, Richard Smith J, 31 July 2023): the Biwater scheme had a £28.3m deficit, and the employer owed over £39.74m with arrears since March 2020. The trustee decided to **petition to wind up the employers** and sought the court's blessing (*Public Trustee v Cooper* category 2; CPR Part 64).
  - **Approved**: the trustee genuinely formed the view; it was one a reasonable body of trustees, properly directed, could reach (*Edge*); and it was not vitiated by conflict. The representative beneficiary found no grounds to oppose, and the PPF supported it.
  - **The PPF point**: the PPF is not a beneficiary. Trustees owe duties to their members and need not consider the PPF's interests ("PPF drift"). But they cannot use the PPF's existence to justify otherwise improper inaction, such as letting **scheme drift** erode assets, because that would be "gaming the system" (following *Independent Trustee Services v Hope*). Whether the PPF is relevant depends on the context and power (*Granada UK Rental v TPR*).
- **the materials CFCF problem**: a conflicted trustee board agrees to suspend deficit and future service contributions on the basis that members will be better off in the PPF if CFCF later fails. Apply *Brass*/*ITS v Hope*: relying on higher PPF compensation is a questionable consideration and risks gaming the system. Weigh scheme drift, the employer covenant, the declared conflicts (authorised by the deed and articles), and whether a reasonable body of trustees could reach the decision.

## Debate and critique
- **EU law as the engine of member protection**: the PPF, the 50% floor and the removal of the cap all come from EU law (Art 8 and age discrimination principles). After Brexit and the REUL Act 2023, future development depends on Parliament. The Bauer underpin has already been switched off for insolvencies after 2023. Is that a backward step in protection?
- **Moral hazard versus fairness**: the cap's rationale (incentivising "fat cat" decision-makers to fund schemes) was found too weak to justify its age-based effects. The handbook calls the age discrimination route an "elegant" alternative to the 50% attack. Essay angle: is 90% for under-NPA members, and the loss of pre-1997 indexation, itself fair?
- **Individual versus collective guarantees**: *Hampshire* rejected averaging. The CA in *Hughes* then accepted an ex ante actuarial "value" test, which shifts longevity and inflation risk back onto members whose outcomes differ from the assumptions.
- **Levy and cross-subsidy**: healthy schemes, and so their employers, pay for failures. The current large PPF surplus raises questions about levy levels and possibly better benefits. The handbook notes the uncertainty of future claims.
- **Trustee strategy in distress**: *Brass* shows that trustees can be required to act decisively (petition) rather than drift. That is uncomfortable where members may personally gain from delay (PPF drift). The law prioritises the scheme's own integrity over maximising transfers from the PPF.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Eligibility**: is the scheme DB, or does it have DB sections? Is it within s126? Has there been a qualifying insolvency event (s127), or is there a s129 route?
2. **Funding test**: are assets below protected liabilities on the s143 basis (excluding money purchase)? If not, s154 wind-up applies with s73 priorities.
3. **Assessment period**: benefits are capped at PPF levels (s138), now uncapped following *Hughes*.
4. **Compensation**: is the member over or under PPF NPA? Apply 100% or 90%, pre/post-1997 indexation, revaluation limits, no cap after *Hughes*, the Hampshire 50% value underpin, and the Bauer underpin only for insolvency events before 1 January 2024.
5. **EU route**: is Art 8 directly effective against the PPF (*Hampshire*)? What is the Brexit timing? Are State liability arguments available (*Robins*/*Hogan*)? Note the limitation period for arrears (6 years, *Hughes*).
6. **Trustee decisions near insolvency**: proper purpose; relevant considerations (scheme drift, covenant, recoveries); irrelevance of PPF drift and the "gaming" prohibition (*Brass*, *ITS v Hope*); conflicts (authorisation and declarations); the option of a court blessing (*Public Trustee v Cooper* category 2).
7. Traps: assuming the PPF covers DC benefits; forgetting s73 priority when a scheme is wound up outside the PPF; applying the Bauer underpin to a 2024 insolvency; treating the PPF as a scheme beneficiary.
