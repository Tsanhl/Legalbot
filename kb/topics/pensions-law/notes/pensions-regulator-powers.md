# The Pensions Regulator - objectives, powers, codes and controls

## Overview
The Pensions Regulator ("TPR", "tPR", "the Regulator") is the statutory body that supervises work-based pension schemes in the UK. It was created by PA 2004 s1 (Part 1) and replaced Opra from April 2005, according to the CMS *Pensions Law Handbook* ch 2. Trustees and employers deal with it all the time: annual scheme returns, whistleblowing reports, clearance applications on corporate deals, and DB valuations under PA 2004 Part 3. It is important to know where its powers are found, how much weight its guidance and Codes of Practice carry, and how its enforcement powers are controlled. The moral hazard powers (contribution notices and financial support directions) are developed.

## The law

### 1. Where the powers come from
- **PA 1995 ss3-26** and **PA 2004 ss1-101**. Further powers are in other Acts, such as the Pensions Act 2008 (auto-enrolment) and the Pension Schemes Act 2017 (master trusts), which are outside the discussion.
- **PA 2004 s6**: supplementary power to do anything (except borrow money) calculated to facilitate, or incidental or conducive to, its functions.

### 2. Key PA 1995 powers (skim-read level)
1. **Control of trustees (ss3-9)**: suspend, prohibit, remove and appoint trustees. The question is how this works where the only trustee is a company.
2. **Civil penalties (s10)**: up to £5,000 for individuals (including directors of a trustee company) and £50,000 for companies.
3. **Winding up (s11)**: TPR can order a scheme to be wound up, including where that is in the members' interests. This links to **PA 1995 s75**: starting a winding up can trigger the employer debt in a DB scheme.

### 3. PA 2004 s5: TPR's main objectives
(a) protect the benefits of members of occupational pension schemes; (b) protect the benefits of members of certain personal pension schemes; (c) reduce the risk of situations arising that may lead to PPF compensation; **(cza) in relation to its Part 3 (scheme funding) functions only, minimise any adverse impact on the sustainable growth of an employer**; (ca) maximise compliance with the auto-enrolment employer duties (Pensions Act 2008 Part 1 Chapter 1, plus ss50 and 54); (d) promote and improve understanding of good administration of work-based pension schemes. Section 5(1)(cza) returns in the discussion and the discussion.

### 4. PA 2004 s70: whistleblowing
- **Who must report**: trustees and managers, public service pension board members, anyone otherwise involved in administration, the employer, professional advisers, anyone else advising the trustees, and master trust scheme strategists and funders.
- **Trigger**: the person has **reasonable cause to believe** that (a) a duty relevant to administering the scheme, imposed by enactment or rule of law, has not been or is not being complied with, and (b) the failure is **likely to be of material significance** to TPR.
- **What**: a written report as soon as reasonably practicable.
- **Protection**: reporting breaches no other duty (s70(3)), subject to **s311 (protected items)**. Communications between a legal adviser and client made in connection with legal advice, or with actual or contemplated proceedings, need not be disclosed, unless held to further a criminal purpose. This answers the question of a legal adviser's position.
- **Sanction**: s10 PA 1995 civil penalties for failure without reasonable excuse. The Code and guidance ask: (i) is there a tangible reason to believe a breach occurred, not mere suspicion, and (ii) is it materially significant, judged by its cause, effect, the reaction to it and its wider implications.

### 5. Guidance versus Codes of Practice (PA 2004 ss90-92)
1. **TPR guidance** "is not without its mistakes", should be read critically, and has limited evidential value. Warren J said he could attach "only the slightest weight" to TPR's views and would feel no anxiety if his conclusion differed (the case is not named in the source).
2. **Codes of Practice** (s90) give practical guidance on exercising functions and on expected standards of conduct.
   - s90(4): failing to observe a Code does not *of itself* make a person liable to proceedings. This is subject to s13 improvement notices, which can direct compliance with a Code and are backed by civil penalties.
   - s90(5): a Code is **admissible in evidence** and, if relevant, **must be taken into account** by the court or tribunal, including the Ombudsman.
   - **Parliamentary oversight**: TPR must consult. The draft is laid before Parliament for 40 days under the negative resolution procedure (s91).
3. The **General Code** replaced 10 earlier Codes. Its language tends to be "woolly". Other Codes remain, including the **DB Funding Code** (November 2024 version).

### 6. Controls on TPR's powers
- There are natural justice concerns: is TPR "judge and jury"?
- PA 2004 builds in a **separation of powers**:
  - **Determinations Panel** (s9) exercises the "reserved regulatory functions" listed in Sch 2, including prohibition and suspension orders (s4), contribution notices and FSDs (ss38, 43), restoration orders (s52), s71 report notices, and various orders and directions.
  - **Non-Executive Committee** (s8) monitors TPR internally.
  - Appeals from determinations go to the **Upper Tribunal** (Transfer of Tribunal Functions Order 2010).

### 7. Moral hazard powers (summary)
- **Contribution notices (PA 2004 ss38-42)**: TPR may require a person who was the employer, or connected with or an associate of it, at any time in the relevant period, and who was a party to an act or a deliberate failure to act, to pay a specified sum to the trustees (or to the PPF Board). Conditions:
  - the act meets the main purpose test (preventing recovery of, avoiding, compromising or reducing a s75 debt) **or** the material detriment test, employer insolvency test or employer resources test (ss38A, 38C, 38E);
  - it occurred within six years before the warning notice;
  - TPR considers it **reasonable** to impose liability, having regard to listed factors (degree of involvement, relationship and control, connection with the scheme, failure to notify under s69, purposes, benefits received, the likelihood of relevant creditors being paid, effect on scheme assets and liabilities, financial circumstances).
  - Insolvency practitioners acting in that capacity are excluded. Knowing assistants count as parties.
- **Financial support directions (ss43-51)**: for service companies and insufficiently resourced employers.
- The **Pension Schemes Act 2021** (in force 1 October 2021 for these parts) widened contribution notice powers, added criminal offences and civil penalties for avoidance of employer debt and for conduct risking accrued benefits, and expanded information-gathering on notifiable events. Criticism has been noted of TPR's lack of "teeth" (for example after BHS), which led to the 2018 White Paper "Protecting defined benefit pension schemes - a stronger Pensions Regulator".

## Key authorities
- PA 1995 ss3-11 (trustee control, civil penalties, winding up); s10 penalty levels.
- PA 2004 s1 (TPR), s5 (objectives), s6, s8, s9 and Sch 2 (Determinations Panel), s13 (improvement notices), s69 (notifiable events), s70 (whistleblowing), s311 (protected items), ss90-92 (Codes), ss38-51 (moral hazard).
- Pension Schemes Act 2021 (expanded powers).
- *Granada UK Rental & Retail Ltd v The Pensions Regulator* [2019] EWCA Civ 1032 ("Box Clever"): FSD powers considered by the Court of Appeal; still unresolved as at 2023.
- Warren J's remark on the weight of TPR guidance.

## Debate and critique
- **Soft law with hard effects**: Codes are not binding in themselves but must be taken into account and can be enforced through improvement notices. Parliamentary scrutiny is only by negative resolution. Is that enough democratic control over rules that in practice govern how trillions of pounds of DB liabilities are funded? (the question: how would you advise trustees who do not want to follow the DB Funding Code?)
- **Guidance can be wrong**: Warren J's remark is a reminder that guidance is not law. Trustees should take legal advice rather than treat TPR statements as decisive.
- **Competing objectives**: s5(1)(cza) (employer sustainable growth) can pull against member protection (s5(1)(a)) and PPF protection (s5(1)(c)). Funding negotiations sit in that tension.
- **Teeth versus deterrence**: moral hazard powers are used relatively rarely, but the threat of them drives settlements (for example the BHS settlement of up to £363m from Sir Philip Green). The PSA 2021 criminal offences remain largely untested.
- **Judge and jury**: the Determinations Panel and Upper Tribunal appeal respond to natural justice concerns. Whether that internal separation is enough is a fair essay point.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the issue one for TPR at all? Check the scheme type and the power in point (PA 1995 ss3-11; PA 2004).
2. **Whistleblowing**: who is the potential reporter (s70(1) list)? Is there reasonable cause to believe a breach occurred, and is it of material significance? Are any communications protected items (s311)?
3. **Codes**: identify the relevant Code. Explain s90(4)-(5): no automatic liability, but evidential weight and the improvement notice risk. Advise documenting reasons for any departure.
4. **Guidance**: treat it as persuasive only, and check it against the legislation.
5. **Enforcement**: Determinations Panel procedure (warning notice, determination), then Upper Tribunal appeal.
6. **Corporate activity**: flag contribution notice and FSD risk, notifiable events (s69) and the possibility of clearance.
7. Traps: treating guidance as binding; overlooking s311 privilege; forgetting that s11 winding up can trigger s75.
