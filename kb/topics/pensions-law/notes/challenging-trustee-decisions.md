# Validity of trustee (and employer) decisions and contracting with trustees

## Overview
Most UK occupational pension schemes are irrevocable trusts. The trustee makes high-value decisions on investment, benefit discretions, death benefits, amendments, transfers and funding. Members can challenge those decisions in court, or for free before the Pensions Ombudsman, so "getting it right" matters especially in pensions. The discussion gives a checklist of grounds for challenging a trustee decision (the same list, reversed, tells a trustee how to make a decision safe). It adds cautionary points on void and voidable decisions, on trustees' duties to employers, and on the practical risks of contracting with trustees. Later topics build on this: proper purpose in amendments, Ombudsman review, and trustee company governance and investment.

## The law

### Grounds for challenging a trustee decision
1. **The "trustee" is not the trustee.** A person who has not been validly appointed has no power, so the decision has no effect. Take particular care where individual trustees have been replaced by a sole trustee that is not a trust corporation.
2. **No power to take the decision (ultra vires).** Either the power is missing and no overriding legislation supplies it, or an express or implied restriction applies, whether in the deed or imposed by overriding legislation.
3. **Insufficient number of trustees.** The deed's required minimum, majority or unanimity was not met.
4. **Non-compliance with the deed's procedure**: notice of meetings, quorum, declarations of interest, and whether a conflicted trustee may count in the quorum or vote. Deeds sometimes include "cure" provisions, for example for notice sent to the wrong address, or allow shorter notice by agreement. For a **sole corporate trustee**, the same questions arise under the trustee company's articles (see trustee-companies-directors-duties-and-conflicts).
5. **Unauthorised conflicts of interest or duty.** A decision by conflicted trustees can be set aside unless the conflict was authorised: expressly in the deed, by statute (for example PA 1995 s39 for member-nominated trustees, the discussion), or by implication.
6. **Improper purpose.** Even if every formality is met, a power used for an improper purpose is invalid. The duty to act in the beneficiaries' best interests is now seen as shorthand for the rule that a power must be used for its proper purpose. Authority: *British Airways v Airways Pension Scheme Trustee* [2018] EWCA Civ 1533, and in relation to employer-side powers *Re Courage Group's Pension Schemes* [1987] 1 WLR 495.
7. **Failure to consider relevant factors, or taking account of irrelevant ones.** Such a decision can be set aside (see also *Braganza*).

### Cautionary notes
- The commentary does not develop (a) whether a flawed decision is **void or voidable**, or (b) the effect on beneficiaries and on third parties who have contracted with the trustees and acquired trust assets as bona fide purchasers for value without notice. These questions need to be kept in mind.
- A summary adds *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26. On that summary's account, the Supreme Court held that inadequate deliberation makes a decision voidable only if it amounts to a **breach of fiduciary duty**. A trustee who relies on apparently competent professional advice will usually have done its duty. A separate equitable jurisdiction allows a voluntary disposition to be set aside for a **causative mistake of sufficient gravity**.

### Standard of review: the trustee cases
- *Edge v Pensions Ombudsman* [2000] Ch 602, [2000] ICR 748 (CA, 29 July 1999). Trustees used surplus to reduce active members' contributions and improve their benefits, with nothing for pensioners. The Ombudsman held that this breached a duty of impartiality. The Court of Appeal disagreed. The so-called duty to act impartially is "no more than the ordinary duty" of a person exercising a discretionary power: to use it for its purpose, considering relevant matters and excluding irrelevant ones. Trustees who do that cannot be criticised for preferring one class (employers, current employees or pensioners) over another. They must ask what is fair and equitable in all the circumstances, and the weight given to each factor is for them. The court adopted the *Harris v Lord Shuttleworth* [1995] OPLR 79 tests: ask the correct questions; direct yourself correctly in law (including the correct construction of the rules); do not reach a perverse decision; consider all relevant and no irrelevant factors. Trustees who are members are not required to prove their decision is fair merely because they have a conflict.
- *Mihlenstedt v Barclays Bank International Ltd* [1989] PLR 91 (CA): an opinion formed dishonestly or on an erroneous basis can be set aside by a court of equity, with a direction to reconsider. A medical adviser's imperfect understanding of the rule is not fatal if the adviser supplied the facts and opinions on which a proper decision could be made.
- *Braganza v BP Shipping Ltd* [2015] UKSC 17: contractual discretions must be exercised lawfully and rationally in the public law sense, under both Wednesbury limbs. Rationality is a minimum objective standard applied to the decision-maker's reasoning, while reasonableness is an external standard applied to the outcome (Lord Sumption, quoted by Lady Hale). A lay decision-maker is not expected to produce an "expert, professional and almost microscopic investigation" (*The Vainqueur José*, cited).

### Employer powers: the three categories
Employer powers under the employment contract and the trust deed fall into three groups. Which group a power belongs to is a **matter of construction**:
1. **Own-interest powers with no implied constraint**, for example terminating employment on full notice, or a unilateral power to start winding up.
2. **Own-interest powers subject to the implied duty of mutual trust and confidence / good faith** (the "Imperial duty"). Review is for rationality (*Imperial*; *IBM v Dalgleish* [2017] EWCA Civ 1212).
3. **Fiduciary powers**, which must be used for their purpose and not in the employer's own interest. Example: *Mettoy Pension Trustees Ltd v Evans* [1990] 1 WLR 1587, where the employer's power over surplus on winding up was held fiduciary and so could not be exercised by the liquidator for creditors.

### Contracting with trustees
1. A trust has **no separate legal personality**. The trustees contract personally.
2. Example: trustees agree to buy Greenacre Towers for £50m (8% net yield). Before completion a fraud wipes out the trust fund.
   - **Contract 1** contains a **limitation of liability clause**: the trustees take on no personal liability, and the seller's claim is limited to the trustees' right of indemnity out of the trust assets.
   - **Contract 2** has no such clause. The trustees are personally liable for any shortfall, subject to any claim against their solicitors.
   - *Learning point*: never let trustees sign a contract without limitation-of-liability wording.
3. Other risks: the **beneficiaries' right of set-off** against the trustee's indemnity; and, on a trustee's bankruptcy or insolvency, whether amounts recovered under the indemnity go to all the trustee's unsecured creditors pro rata, including personal creditors.
4. The exercise asks for at most three risks of contracting with OPS trustees and how to mitigate them. The points above are the raw material: personal liability (mitigated by a limitation clause), limited trust assets or indemnity (credit support or due diligence), adequacy of powers (check the deed), and insolvency or set-off issues.

### Exoneration and overriding statute
- Trust deeds commonly disapply the no-conflict and no-profit rules (for example NAPS clause 27) and limit trustee liability (NAPS clause 24). Every general trust rule should be read as "unless otherwise disapplied or authorised by an express provision of the trust deed".
- But **PA 1995 s33** prevents exclusion or restriction of liability for breach of an obligation of care in performing investment functions (with s117 as the general override).

## Key authorities
- *Edge v Pensions Ombudsman* [2000] Ch 602 (CA): the "impartiality" duty reduced to the ordinary proper purpose / relevant considerations duty. The Ombudsman has no greater powers than a court and cannot make determinations that adversely affect people not bound by them.
- *Harris v Lord Shuttleworth* [1995] OPLR 79 (CA): the four-part test for trustee discretions, approved in *Edge*.
- *Mihlenstedt v Barclays Bank International Ltd* [1989] PLR 91 (CA): relief for opinions formed dishonestly or on a wrong basis; contractual good faith.
- *Braganza v BP Shipping Ltd* [2015] UKSC 17: rationality, both limbs, relational contracts.
- *Mettoy Pension Trustees Ltd v Evans* [1990] 1 WLR 1587: a fiduciary employer power over surplus.
- *British Airways v Airways Pension Scheme Trustee* [2018] EWCA Civ 1533: improper purpose in a trustee's unilateral amendment (see amendment-powers-and-employer-constraints).
- *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26: the narrowed *Hastings-Bass* rule and equitable mistake.
- PA 1995 ss33, 39, 117.

## Debate and critique
- **Public law creeping into private law.** The summary traces a line from *Mettoy* through *IBM* and *Braganza*. Courts have imported public law rationality standards into the review of pension decisions, treating trustees and employers almost as quasi-public decision-makers. Supporters say this protects members with little bargaining power. Critics say it blurs the line between fiduciary and contractual duties, and *Braganza*'s dissenters warned against expecting lay decision-makers to meet unrealistically high standards.
- **Impartiality versus discretion.** *Edge* is a pro-trustee decision. Members who see surplus go to actives but not pensioners have little remedy if the process was sound. Is that adequate protection where trustees are themselves members (a structural conflict)? The court said a conflict alone does not reverse the burden of proving fairness.
- **Contracting out of constraints.** A question is whether an employer could, by drafting, make all its powers own-interest powers free of implied constraint. The discussion cites deed clauses saying the principal company "shall owe no duty" when exercising powers. How far such clauses can exclude the Imperial or Braganza duties remains open.
- **Void or voidable, and third parties.** The commercial consequences for buyers of trust assets and for counterparties are under-explored but important in practice. They are why limitation-of-liability and power checks matter.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Who decided?** Check appointment validity (individual or corporate trustee) and, for a company, the board's quorum and articles.
2. **Under what power?** Find it in the deed or statute. Check express restrictions (consents, actuarial certificates, form such as a deed) and statutory overrides (s67, s33, s117).
3. **Procedure**: notice, quorum, declarations of interest, conflict authorisation (deed, s39, implication), majority required.
4. **Purpose**: is the decision aimed at the scheme's purpose (providing benefits), or at an ulterior goal such as surplus capture or employer benefit? (*Courage*, *BA v APST*)
5. **Process**: relevant and irrelevant considerations; proper advice; correct construction of the rules; no perverse result (*Edge* / *Harris v Shuttleworth*; *Braganza*). For an **employer** power, first classify it (own-interest, Imperial-constrained or fiduciary), then apply the matching standard.
6. **Consequence**: void or voidable? Is relief available under *Pitt v Holt* (breach of duty) or for mistake? What happens to third parties?
7. **Forum**: court or Pensions Ombudsman (no greater powers than a court, *Edge*; see pension-dispute-resolution-and-ombudsman).
8. **Contracts with trustees**: insist on limitation-of-liability wording; check powers; consider set-off and insolvency risk.
9. Traps: arguing a freestanding "duty of impartiality"; assuming a conflicted decision is automatically invalid when the deed or statute authorises the conflict; forgetting that the deed may have excluded general trust rules.
