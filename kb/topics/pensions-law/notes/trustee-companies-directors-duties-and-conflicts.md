# Pension scheme trustee companies - directors' duties, conflicts and liability

## Overview
Registered schemes within the PA 2004 funding regime must be held on irrevocable trust, which keeps the assets "bankruptcy remote" from the employer. The trustee is usually a **single-purpose trustee company**, often a subsidiary of the employer. That adds a company law layer. The company is a separate legal person and acts through directors appointed and governed by its articles, which are usually based on Table A or model articles (GM (UK) Pension Trustees Ltd's articles are a typical example). Directors owe their duties under the **Companies Act 2006** to the **company**, not directly to the scheme members. The questions for the materials are: how directors' duties and conflicts work alongside trust law, how far liability can be excluded or indemnified, and what routes members have against directors (accessory liability, "dog-leg" claims, derivative claims).

## The law

### 1. Board composition
- **PA 2004 s242**: at least one third of the trustee company's directors must be **member-nominated directors** (MNDs) selected in accordance with the section, **or** all directors must be independent (PA 1995 s23(3)). There are exceptions, for example a "relevant centralised scheme" such as the USS. Section 242 overrides the articles if they conflict. **s241** is the equivalent for member-nominated trustees (MNTs) of individual-trustee schemes.
- The articles set out appointment and removal of directors and decision-making (notice, quorum, majorities). Board powers are usually delegated to the directors (Table A reg 70 in the GM example).

### 2. Directors' general duties (CA 2006 ss170-181)
- **s170**: the duties are owed **to the company**.
- **s171**: act within the constitution and use powers only for the purposes for which they are conferred (proper purpose, compare *BA v APST* for trustees).
- **s172**: promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, having regard to long-term consequences, employees, community and environment, reputation and so on. The question is how you "promote the success" of a company whose only role is acting as trustee, and points to **s172(2)** (where the company's purposes are not for the members' benefit, success means achieving those purposes).
- **s174**: reasonable care, skill and diligence, judged by the general knowledge expected of the role and the director's actual knowledge.
- **s175**: avoid conflicts of interest. **s177**: declare an interest in proposed transactions.
- There is considerable overlap between trustee duties and directors' duties.

### 3. Conflicts
1. An MND who is a scheme member has a prima facie **interest/duty conflict**. An employer-appointed director who is also an employer executive has a **duty/duty conflict**, which is often harder to spot but more common and more serious.
2. **CA 2006 s182**: declare an interest in an **existing** transaction as soon as reasonably practicable (with exceptions such as interests already declared under s177, interests the director is unaware of, those not reasonably likely to give rise to a conflict, or those the other directors already know about). **s183**: failure is a **criminal offence**.
3. **Authorisation**: under both trust law and company law, a conflicted trustee or director may not take part unless authorised expressly or by necessary implication.
   - **PA 1995 s39** gives a limited statutory authorisation for **member-nominated trustees** that overrides the deed (with s117). The question is whether s39 covers MNDs of a trustee company.
   - Articles usually allow conflicted directors to act once they have declared.
   - Authorisation does not mean the director **should** act, or may prefer their own interest or other duty. If the conflict is acute, the burden of showing proper performance shifts in practice to the director, and reputational issues arise. The practical solution is to **recuse or resign**.
4. *Edge v Pensions Ombudsman* (CA): trustees who are members are not required to prove fairness merely because they are conflicted. The argument that member trustees must account for benefits was settled for the future by s39.

### 4. Liability, exemption, indemnity and insurance
- **CA 2006 s232**: any provision exempting a director from liability for negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust in relation to the company is **void**, as is a company indemnity for such liability, subject to:
  - **s233**: insurance (D&O);
  - **s234**: qualifying third party indemnity provisions;
  - **s235**: **qualifying pension scheme indemnity provision (QPSIP)**. A company that is trustee of an OPS may indemnify its directors against liability incurred in connection with its activities as trustee, but not criminal fines or regulatory penalties.
- **s239**: ratification by a members' resolution, disregarding the votes of the director concerned and connected persons.
- **PA 2004 s256**: no reimbursement from scheme assets of a trustee's or manager's **criminal fines or civil penalties** (PA 1995 s10 and others), including through insurance premiums. Breach exposes trustees to civil penalties, and knowingly being reimbursed is an offence.
- **PA 1995 s33**: liability for breach of a duty of care in **investment functions** cannot be excluded (see trustee-investment-duties).

### 5. Can members sue directors of the trustee company?
1. **Direct fiduciary duty**: no. Under *Bath v Standard Land Co* (applied in *HR v JAPT* and *Gregson*), directors are not fiduciaries to the beneficiaries just because of their office.
2. **Direct duty of care in tort**: not arguable in *HR v JAPT*. The corporate trustee is the party that assumes responsibility, and there is no gap of the *White v Jones* kind because beneficiaries can sue the trustee company.
3. **Accessory liability (dishonest assistance)**: yes, if dishonest. *Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan* [1995] 2 AC 378 (PC): a person who **dishonestly procures or assists** a breach of trust is liable. The trustee's own state of mind is irrelevant. Dishonesty is objective, judged in light of what the person actually knew. "Carelessness is not dishonesty." It includes deliberately shutting one's eyes and reckless disregard of others' rights. In *HR v JAPT* the pleaded dishonesty claim against the director was allowed to go to trial.
4. **"Dog-leg" claim**: the director breaches a duty owed to the trustee company; the company's claim against the director is said to be trust property; the beneficiaries enforce it.
   - *HR v JAPT* [1997] PLR 99 (Lindsay J): **arguable** for a "one-trust, no-asset" trustee company whose only business was one scheme.
   - *Gregson v HAE Trustees* [2008] EWHC 1006 (Ch): **struck out** where the trustee company administered many trusts and had outside creditors. The duty is owed to the company generally, the claim is a general company asset, and there is no mechanism that turns it into trust property (following *Young v Murphy*). *Royal Brunei*'s dishonesty threshold also weighs against making directors liable for mere negligence through this route.
5. **Piercing the veil**: "quite hopeless" in *HR v JAPT*, because nothing was concealed.
6. **Derivative claims**: *McGaughey v USS* [2022] EWHC 1233 (Ch) (Leech J); [2023] EWCA Civ 873 (CA). Members sought permission to continue a multiple derivative claim against USS Ltd's directors (valuation timing, benefit cuts, discrimination, fossil fuel investment, costs).
   - **Refused**. Standing requires a loss to the company and a **reflective loss** to the claimant (*Waddington v Chan*). The company suffered none: the valuation and benefit changes reduced its liabilities. The CA said members are **not analogous to shareholders**, because their interests differ widely.
   - The "fraud on the minority" exception under *Foss v Harbottle* requires deliberate or dishonest breach or **improper personal benefit** to the directors (*Harris v Microfusion*). There was no evidence of either.
   - Discrimination claims belong as direct claims against the trustee. A challenge to investment strategy "purely on the grounds of differing environmental beliefs" fails without proof of loss (applying *Cowan v Scargill*).
7. **Equitable compensation and profits**: *Gwembe Valley Development Co v Koshy* [2003] EWCA Civ 1048 (paras 57-68 set as reading). A director who breaches fiduciary duty may have to pay equitable compensation for loss caused (causation needed; in that case there was no proof of loss) or account for unauthorised profits. Dishonesty for a "fraudulent breach" is discussed by reference to *Armitage v Nurse* and *Twinsectra* (as summarised in the materials folder).

### 6. the materials problem points (Tontine)
- **Q2 (corporate trustee)**: the trustee company bears liability to members. Directors are exposed only through dog-leg claims (arguable under *HR v JAPT*, since Tontine Pension Trustee Ltd appears to be a single-scheme subsidiary), dishonest assistance (*Royal Brunei*), or a derivative claim (*McGaughey* hurdles). Exoneration for directors is limited by CA s232. The deed indemnity must fit s235 (QPSIP). PA 1995 s33 bites on the trustee company's investment functions.
- **Q3 (undisclosed interests: Anne's husband and Bert's brother are directors of the investment manager)**: s177 and s182 declaration duties (with criminal liability under s183 for existing transactions); s175 conflict; whether family connections are "interests"; the effect on the validity of the IMA decision; and remedies such as equitable compensation, which depends on proving loss caused (*Gwembe*), or rescission.
- **Q4 (interests declared, majority approves over the MND's objection)**: authorisation under the articles lets them vote, but they must still act in the company's (and so the trust's) interests. For an acute conflict, the practical burden shifts and recusal is advisable. Also consider proper purpose and the investment law requirements (proper advice, PA 1995 s36).

- PA 2004 ss241-242, 256; PA 1995 ss23(3), 33, 39, 117.
- *HR v JAPT* [1997] PLR 99; *Gregson v HAE Trustees* [2008] EWHC 1006 (Ch); *Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan* [1995] 2 AC 378; *Gwembe Valley v Koshy* [2003] EWCA Civ 1048; *McGaughey v USS* [2022] EWHC 1233 (Ch), [2023] EWCA Civ 873; *Edge v Pensions Ombudsman* [2000] Ch 602; *Bath v Standard Land Co*.

## Debate and critique
- **Accountability gap**: the corporate trustee model, often an assetless one-scheme subsidiary, shields directors. Members must rely on the trustee company's indemnity or insurance, dishonesty-based accessory claims, or uncertain dog-leg arguments. *HR v JAPT* and *Gregson* point in different directions. *McGaughey* shows derivative claims are nearly closed to members because they do not fit the shareholder model.
- **Conflicts are built in**: statute requires member-nominated directors, and employers appoint executives. Authorisation is necessary for schemes to work, but authorisation is not absolution: acute conflicts call for recusal. Is s39's limited override (for MNTs) properly extended to MNDs? This is left open.
- **s172 for a trustee company**: "success" for a company that exists to run a trust is really achieving the trust's purposes (s172(2)). This merges company law duties with trust duties and raises questions about how s172(1)(d) environmental considerations fit with *Cowan* (see esg-ethical-and-political-investment).
- **Indemnities and moral hazard**: QPSIPs and D&O insurance protect volunteer lay directors. Without them, few people would serve. But broad "personal conscious bad faith" exoneration clauses, such as the one in the Tontine deed, are cut back by s33 PA 1995 and s232 CA 2006, which shows Parliament's distrust of contractual exclusion in this field.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the trustee**: individuals or a company? Single-scheme and assetless? Who owns it? What do the articles say about quorum, conflicts and appointment? Is s242 met?
2. **Duties engaged**: CA ss171, 172(2), 174, 175, 177, 182; trust duties (proper purpose, relevant considerations); investment statute (PA 1995 ss33-36).
3. **Conflicts**: interest or duty? Declared (s177/182)? Authorised (articles; s39 for MNTs; the deed)? Acute enough that recusal is needed?
4. **Liability of the trustee company to members** (breach of trust), and the effect of exoneration and indemnity clauses (s33; s256; QPSIP limits).
5. **Liability of directors**: to the company (s170), with enforcement by the company, a liquidator or possibly members by derivative claim (*McGaughey* tests). To members: dog-leg (*HR v JAPT* versus *Gregson*), dishonest assistance (*Royal Brunei*), no direct fiduciary duty or duty of care (*HR v JAPT*).
6. **Exemptions**: s232 voids exemptions; s233 insurance; s235 QPSIP; s239 ratification (with conflicted votes excluded).
7. **Remedies**: equitable compensation (causation, *Gwembe*), account of profits, setting aside the transaction.
8. Traps: assuming directors owe duties directly to members; treating declaration of an interest as curing everything; forgetting the criminal offence in s183; forgetting s256 on fines and penalties.
