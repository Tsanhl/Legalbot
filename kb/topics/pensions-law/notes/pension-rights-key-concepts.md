# Pension rights - key concepts, scheme types and members

## Overview
Pensions law (UK) starts with classification. Before you can say which statute protects a benefit, you have to know what kind of "pension right" it is, whether the employer has funded it, whether it is a money purchase or a defined benefit, and what kind of "scheme" provides it. The discussion callss this the **logic hierarchy**. The same classification also decides who the "employer" is, which category of member someone falls into, and whether an arrangement is pensions business, insurance business or a collective investment scheme. Large sums can turn on these labels. For example, whether someone is still an "active member" can decide whether a section 75 employer debt worth tens of millions is triggered.

## The law

### 1. What a "pension right" is
In broad terms, a pension right is a right an employee gets under the employment contract to benefits that become payable when one of these conditions is met:
1. **Normal retirement**: reaching normal retirement or pension age and retiring. The benefit is either periodic payments for life (sometimes with a guaranteed minimum period such as 5 or 10 years), or a lump sum that can be taken as cash or converted into periodic income. Payments can be fixed or increasing (for example in line with CPI, capped at 2.5%). They can also vary with investment return and with how fast the member draws the fund down ("income drawdown").
2. **Early retirement**: before normal retirement age but not before a minimum age (usually 55; the discussion notes it rises to 57 from 6 April 2028). Under the Finance Act 2004 a registered scheme cannot pay before the "minimum pension age" (FA 2004 s279; see also s165(1)) unless the ill-health condition is met.
3. **Incapacity / ill-health retirement**: at any age.
4. **Death**: benefits paid to a surviving spouse, civil partner, adult dependant or eligible children.

A question to think about: if pensions are deferred pay, what does an employee get who dies before retirement? Death benefits show why the "deferred pay" label does not fit neatly.

### 2. Statutory definitions to test against
- **PSA 1993 s1(5), "pension scheme"**: a scheme or other arrangements, in one or more instruments or agreements, that has or can have effect to provide benefits to or in respect of people (a) on retirement, (b) on reaching a particular age, or (c) on termination of service in an employment. **Death is not on the list.**
- **PSA 1993 s1(1), "occupational pension scheme"**: a pension scheme set up to provide benefits to or in respect of people with service in employments of a description (with or without other people), established by or with a person to whom s1(2) applies. That means an employer of such people, a person in such employment, or a person representing those interests. Prescribed schemes are also included. The definition was amended by the Pensions Act 2004 s239 from 6 April 2006, and the versions before and after that date should be compared before and after that date.
- **PSA 1993 s1(1), "personal pension scheme"**: a pension scheme that is not an occupational pension scheme and is established by a person within FA 2004 s154(1) (in practice an FSMA-authorised provider, most commonly an insurer).
- **FA 2004 s150(1), "pension scheme" for tax purposes**: wider. It covers benefits on retirement, **on death**, on reaching a particular age, on the onset of serious ill-health or incapacity, or in similar circumstances.
- **FA 2004 s150(5), "occupational pension scheme"**: a pension scheme established by an employer or employers that provides benefits to any or all of the employees of that employer or any other employer.
- **IORP II Directive (2016/2341), Articles 2 and 6**: the EU scope and definition of an "institution for occupational retirement provision", retained in UK law after Brexit.

**Key boundary point.** A scheme that pays only death benefits (a "life cover only" scheme, or the contractual death benefit in *Braganza v BP Shipping*) is **not** an occupational pension scheme under PSA 1993 s1, because "death" is not one of the s1(5) triggers. It can still be an occupational pension scheme under FA 2004 s150, which expressly includes death. This is an example of an arrangement in the "other" category.

### 3. The logic hierarchy
**Level 1: is the benefit funded by the employer or not?**
- *Funded* means the employer has set aside assets from which the benefit can be paid, or has arranged for a third party to provide it.
- *Unfunded* means the employee carries a credit risk on the employer. Security can reduce that risk, for example a bank or insurer guarantee, a performance bond, or a charge over employer assets.
- Check against IORP II Arts 2 and 6, PA 2004 s252 (a UK-based scheme that takes payments must be a trust with effective rules) and the facts of *Granada Group Ltd v Law Debenture Pension Trust Corporation plc* [2015] EWHC 1499 (Ch). *Granada* concerned a secured unfunded unapproved retirement benefit scheme (a "SUURBS") for directors, secured by a charge over gilts.
- What is an irrevocable trust, and how would you recognise one?

**Level 2: money purchase or not?** There are three concepts of "guarantee":
1. *Concept 1*: a fixed or increasing amount promised once the payment condition is met, for example 1/60th of final pensionable pay for each year of pensionable service. With 40 years' service and £60,000 final pay, that gives £40,000 a year. This is a **defined benefit (DB)** or "not money purchase" benefit.
2. *Concept 2*: the benefit depends on how the contributions have been invested, net of fees. The member bears investment and longevity risk ("will I outlive my pot?"). This is a **money purchase / defined contribution (DC)** benefit.
3. *Concept 3*: like concept 1, but payment (for example of increases) depends on conditions continuing to be met, such as employer profitability or the scheme's funding level. This was previously Dutch practice. From 1 August 2022 UK law uses it through **collective money purchase benefits** (Pension Schemes Act 2021 s1).

UK legislation sorts benefits into two buckets: "money purchase benefits" (concepts 2 and 3) and everything else (concept 1, usually called DB). See PSA 1993 s181 read with s181B (as amended from 1 August 2022 to add collective money purchase benefits as a second limb), PA 1995 s75(1) and PA 2004 s221. "Defined contribution" is not a statutory term. The only statutory use known was PSA 2015 s4, which never came into force and was repealed by PSA 2021 s128.

*Underpins.* A benefit that looks like money purchase but carries a minimum return guarantee (for example contributions rolled up at 3% a year if that beats actual return) is a minimum-benefit guarantee, and needs checking against s181/181B and IORP II Art 13(2). Older personal pension policies may carry a **guaranteed annuity rate (GAR)**.

**Level 3: what type of scheme is it?**
1. **Occupational pension scheme (OPS)**: established by an employer. It is generally regulated by statutes with "Pension" in the title (PSA 1993, PA 1995, PA 2004, PSA 2021).
2. **Personal pension scheme (PPS)**: established by an FSMA-authorised provider. The provider is regulated under FSMA 2000. Some pensions legislation still applies, for example the statutory transfer right in PSA 1993 Part IVZA.
3. **"Other"**: an employer arrangement that falls outside PSA 1993 s1, such as death-benefit-only arrangements.

Hybrid labels:
- **Group personal pension**: not a statutory term. The employer agrees to use a provider's PPS for its staff and to pay contributions, and employees get direct contract or trust rights against the provider. The branded "plan" name has no separate legal existence. It is just part of the provider's single registered PPS.
- **Master trust**: legally an OPS that provides money purchase benefits, but commercially close to a PPS. It is subject to extra regulation under the Pension Schemes Act 2017 (and the Master Trusts Regulations 2018). Master trusts are outside the scope.

**Benefit mix within an OPS.** An OPS may provide DC benefits, DB benefits or hybrid benefits (for example the better of DB and DC). It may give DC to one group and DB to another, or DB up to a ceiling and DC above it. The USS provides context.

### 4. Categories of member
A member moves through stages, defined in PA 1995 s124 and PA 2004 s318:
- **active member**: in pensionable service under the scheme;
- **deferred member**: has accrued rights but is neither active nor a pensioner;
- **pensioner member**: receiving a pension;
- **postponed pensioner**: has stayed on beyond normal pension age without drawing benefits;
- **pension credit member**: holds rights from a pension sharing order on divorce;
- **survivors**: dependants entitled on a member's death.

Compare FA 2004 s151, which **folds survivors into the pensioner member category**. This causes conceptual confusion. Whether someone is "active" matters a great deal. In a multi-employer DB scheme, an employer ceasing to employ any active member can trigger a debt under PA 1995 s75.

### 5. Protection of deferred members (summary only)
- Before 6 April 1975 a scheme could give an early leaver nothing. Some rules even forfeited the pension of a leaver who joined a competitor, a form of "golden handcuffs".
- From 6 April 1975, a leaver who satisfies the **vesting condition** must be given a pension. For DB this is a pension proportionate to what full service to normal pension age would have produced, ignoring future pay rises. This is the "preservation" legislation: PSA 1993 ss69-82 and the Occupational Pension Schemes (Preservation of Benefit) Regulations 1991.
- **Revaluation** of DB deferred pensions protects against inflation (at 5% inflation purchasing power halves in about 14 years): PSA 1993 ss83-86 and Sch 3, originally Social Security Act 1985.
- **Increases to DB pensions in payment** for service after 5 April 1997: PA 1995 ss51-54.
- **Money purchase benefits** have no statutory revaluation or increase requirement. The only rule is no discrimination between active and deferred members in how investment return and charges are allocated.
- "Normal pension age" has a statutory meaning in PSA 1993 s180. It is the earliest age at which the member is entitled to benefits (or, for a GMP-only member, the GMP) on retirement, disregarding special early retirement rules (s180(2)).

### 6. Who is "the employer"?
- **Single-employer scheme**: the employer under the employment contract establishes the trust. The trust deed may call it "the Company", "Principal Employer", "Founder" and so on. The label does not matter, as long as it is the same legal person. A change of company name does not create a new legal person.
- **Group scheme**: the parent establishes the scheme, and subsidiaries join either as parties to the original deed or by a **deed of participation / adherence**. Check where the power to admit lies, what formalities apply (including whether the trustee must sign), and when participation ends. The four main exit triggers are: notice by the participating company; notice by the principal employer; the participating company leaving the group (for example on a sale); and default that is not cured within any grace period.
- **Multi-employer, non-group schemes**: for example the USS, where Rule 44 governs admission of institutions. If such a scheme provides money purchase benefits it may be a "master trust" under PSA 2017 s1.
- **Change of principal employer**: this needs a power (for example clause 19 of the New Airways Pension Scheme deed) and compliance with its formalities, and the power must be used for a proper purpose. If there is no such power, take care before adding one by amendment. In a corporate deal, changing the principal employer "completely changes the pension shape" of the transaction.

### 7. Boundaries with insurance and collective investment schemes
- **Insurance**: carrying on insurance business needs FSMA 2000 authorisation (s19 general prohibition; s22 and Sch 2; Regulated Activities Order 2001). "Annuities on human life" are long-term insurance, but the definition excludes superannuation allowances and annuities paid from a fund applicable solely to people engaged in a particular profession, trade or employment and their dependants. That excludes most of what a funded DB OPS pays, apart from death lump sums, which are not annuities anyway. There are two further arguments: (i) benefits that depend on a trustee/beneficiary relationship are not provided under a *contract*, so they are not insurance contracts; (ii) as a matter of construction, an OPS should not be double-regulated when a separate pensions regime already exists. The RAO defines "occupational pension scheme" as in PSA 1993 s1 but without para (b).
- **Collective investment schemes** (FSMA s235, for example unit trusts): a DC trust in which each member's share is proportionate to their account balance looks like a CIS. However, the FSMA (Collective Investment Schemes) Order 2001, Schedule para 20, excludes both OPSs and PPSs. Establishing, operating or winding up a PPS is a separate regulated activity (RAO art 52). RAO art 3(1) defines a PPS for those purposes.

## Key authorities
- **PSA 1993 s1**: definitions of pension scheme, occupational pension scheme, personal pension scheme and public service pension scheme. This is the gateway to the pensions statutes.
- **PSA 1993 s180**: normal pension age. **s181/181B**: money purchase benefits, including collective money purchase benefits from 1 August 2022.
- **FA 2004 ss150-154**: tax definitions. These are wider than PSA 1993 because they include death benefits. Registered schemes are dealt with in s153.
- **PA 1995 s124 / PA 2004 s318**: member categories and "employer". **FA 2004 s151**: tax member categories, which fold survivors into pensioners.
- **PA 1995 s75(1) and PA 2004 s221**: used only to show the money purchase / non-money purchase boundary.
- **PA 2004 s252**: a UK OPS that receives payments must be established under irrevocable trust (subject to the exemptions in the Trust and Retirement Benefits Exemption Regulations 2005).
- **IORP II Directive arts 2, 6, 7 and 13(2)**: scope, definitions, activities, technical provisions.
- *Granada Group Ltd v Law Debenture Pension Trust Corporation plc* [2015] EWHC 1499 (Ch) (Andrews J): a secured unfunded unapproved scheme for directors, a top-up arrangement prompted by the Finance Act 1989 earnings cap. The employer argued that the charge over gilts was voidable under Companies Act 1985 s320 (substantial property transactions with directors). Held: (a) the directors acquired no non-cash asset, because their right to compel the trustee to administer the trust is a personal right, not a "right over property", and is too nebulous to value; (b) the trustee fell within the absolute pension scheme exemption in s346(3)(b), because the security was an integral part of a composite pension scheme. The scheme was not a bare trust because there were contingent beneficiaries, so *Saunders v Vautier* did not apply. In the discussion notes the case is used for classification: it is an example of an unfunded but secured employer arrangement.
- *Braganza v BP Shipping Ltd* [2015] UKSC 17: a contractual death-in-service benefit in an employment contract. It is used as an example of a death benefit scheme, and later for review of contractual discretions (see challenging-trustee-decisions).

## Debate and critique
- **Fragmented definitions.** The same arrangement can be an OPS for tax purposes (FA 2004 s150) but not for regulation (PSA 1993 s1). This is a trap, and it shows that pensions regulation and pensions tax developed separately, without one shared vocabulary.
- **Labels mislead.** Commercial branding (a "group personal pension", a "Flexible Retirement Benefits Plan") hides the true legal structure. A lawyer has to look at who established the arrangement and who owes the obligations.
- **Risk allocation.** The DB / DC split is really about who bears investment and longevity risk. Collective money purchase benefits (PSA 2021) are a middle path in which risk is pooled among members rather than placed on the employer. They are outside the scope here but changed the statutory definition.
- **Deferred pay theory.** The question of death before retirement challenges the idea that pensions are simply deferred wages. The same theme appears in the EU "pay" cases (see sex-equality-eu-article-157).
- **Pensions archaeology.** Rights build up over 30 to 40 years, across several employers and schemes, under regimes that keep changing. A member's pension may need to be split into tranches with different revaluation and increase rules. This complexity raises cost and the risk of error.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Start with the employment contract** and work forward in time to the date the question asks about.
2. **What is the benefit and what triggers it?** Retirement, a particular age, termination of service, ill-health, or death only? If it is death only, it falls outside PSA 1993 s1 but may be within FA 2004 s150.
3. **Funded or unfunded?** Are assets set aside or is a third party contracted to pay? Is any security given (as in *Granada*)? Is it held on irrevocable trust (PA 2004 s252)?
4. **Money purchase or DB?** Apply PSA 1993 s181/181B. Look for underpins, conditional increases (collective money purchase) and GARs.
5. **Who established it?** An employer means an OPS. An FSMA-authorised provider means a PPS. Look behind the branding of group personal pensions and master trusts.
6. **Which member category?** Check PA 1995 s124 against FA 2004 s151, especially for survivors and for the last active member of an employer (s75 risk).
7. **Who is the employer / principal employer?** Check participation documents, any change of principal employer, and how participation ends.
8. **Regulatory boundary?** Is it pensions, insurance (FSMA, RAO) or a CIS? Apply the "annuities on human life" exclusion and the CIS Order exclusion.
9. **Typical traps**: forgetting that "death" is missing from PSA 1993 s1(5); treating "DC" as a statutory term; confusing a change of company name with a change of principal employer; assuming a scheme booklet's branding reflects the legal reality.

The exercises that use these points include: whether a scheme giving retired employees free firewood is an OPS; whether a scheme can be amended to provide kilos of firewood per year of service when its purpose clause refers to IORP II Art 6 "retirement benefits"; and whether the *Granada* arrangement is funded or unfunded and inside or outside IORP II.
