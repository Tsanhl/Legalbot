# Confidential information and trade secrets - breach of confidence, the 2018 Regulations and employees

## Overview
The law of confidence is a judge-made, equity-based action restraining the misuse of information that has the necessary quality of confidence and was imparted in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence. Since 9 June 2018, the Trade Secrets (Enforcement, etc.) Regulations 2018 add a statutory definition of "trade secret" and a set of procedural protections and remedies, but the Regulations leave the underlying liability to the law of confidence: the acquisition, use or disclosure of a trade secret is unlawful where it constitutes a breach of confidence. For employees, the courts distinguish ordinary know-how (which the employee can use after leaving), trade secrets (protected after employment ends) and information protected only by express restrictive covenants.

## The law
**The equitable action: *Coco v Clark*.** In *Coco v A N Clark (Engineers) Ltd* [1969] RPC 41 (Ch, Megarry J), a claimant must show: (1) the information itself has the necessary quality of confidence (it is not public property or public knowledge, and is more than trivial); (2) it was imparted in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence (contract, relationship, or circumstances in which a reasonable person would realise it was given in confidence); and (3) there was an unauthorised use of that information to the detriment of the party communicating it. (The detriment requirement is treated flexibly; a detriment may be nothing more than the loss of exclusivity or an invasion of privacy.) In *Attorney General v Guardian Newspapers Ltd (No 2)* [1990] 1 AC 109 (HL) ("Spycatcher"), Lord Goff set out three limiting principles: the principle of confidentiality does not apply to information that is in the public domain (to the extent it is); it does not apply to useless or trivial information; and the public interest in maintaining confidence may be outweighed by a countervailing public interest in disclosure (the court balances, with the iniquity defence a part of it); a third party who receives information knowing that it is confidential can be bound. The action can be brought against a third party who knows (or comes to know) the information is confidential But liability requires knowledge: in *Vestergaard Frandsen A/S v Bestnet Europe Ltd* [2013] UKSC 31 (Supreme Court, Lord Neuberger), a former employee (Mrs Sig) was not liable for breach of confidence where she did not know (and had not shut her eyes to the fact) that the rival product sold by the business she had helped to set up had been developed using the claimant's confidential information; she could not be liable as an accessory under a common design either, because that too requires knowledge of the facts that make the conduct wrongful. The claimant's appeal was dismissed.

**Quality of confidence.** Examples: customer lists in some cases, pricing formulas, designs and specifications, manufacturing processes, source code, business plans, undisclosed financial data. Information that is public (even if in obscure sources) or "general knowledge" is not confidential; a compilation of public information may be confidential (*Coco*; the "spring-board" doctrine). An idea must be sufficiently developed to be capable of confidence (*Fraser v Thames Television Ltd* [1984] QB 44 (Hirst J): an idea for a television series about a female rock group, disclosed orally in confidence, was protected because it was clearly identifiable, original, of potential commercial attractiveness and capable of being realised).

**Remedies.** Injunction (including a springboard injunction preventing a defendant from benefiting from a head start gained by misuse: a person who has obtained information in confidence may not use it as a springboard for activities detrimental to the confider, *Terrapin Ltd v Builders Supply Co (Hayes) Ltd* [1967] RPC 375), delivery up or destruction, an account of profits or damages (including equitable compensation measured by a reasonable royalty where the information has been used without liberty: *Seager v Copydex Ltd* [1967] 1 WLR 923 (CA)), and where appropriate a declaration. Interim injunctions are often decisive (*American Cyanamid* principles), subject to a restriction on use with careful drafting so that it is specific about the information. Orders may be made against third parties.

**The Trade Secrets (Enforcement, etc.) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/597).** These implement Directive (EU) 2016/943 in the UK; they remain in force after Brexit as retained (now assimilated) law.
- *Definition (reg 2):* "trade secret" means information which (a) is secret in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among, or readily accessible to, persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question; (b) has commercial value because it is secret; and (c) has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret. "Trade secret holder" means any person lawfully controlling a trade secret; "infringing goods" are goods whose design, functioning, production process, marketing or a characteristic significantly benefits from a trade secret unlawfully acquired, used or disclosed.
- *Wider protection (reg 3):* the acquisition, use or disclosure of a trade secret is unlawful where it constitutes a breach of confidence in confidential information (reg 3(1)); the holder may also seek the measures, procedures and remedies available in an action for breach of confidence, where they give wider protection than the Regulations and comply with the safeguards of Article 1 of the Directive (reg 3(2)-(3)). The Regulations therefore do not create a new substantive cause of action with its own list of unlawful acts; they add the definition and the procedural toolkit.
- *Limitation (regs 4-9):* six years in England and Wales and Northern Ireland (a five-year prescriptive period in Scotland) (reg 5), running from the later of the day the unlawful acquisition, use or disclosure ceases and the day the holder knew or could reasonably be expected to know of the activity, that it was unlawful and the infringer's identity (reg 6). Section 36 of the Limitation Act 1980 is disapplied (reg 4(3)), so the six-year period applies to equitable remedies too rather than only by analogy.
- *Procedure (regs 10-19):* preservation of confidentiality of trade secrets during proceedings (reg 10: orders restricting access to documents and hearings, limits on the persons who may see the secret, and redaction of judgments); interim measures (reg 11: interim injunctions, seizure and delivery up of suspected infringing goods; reg 12 lists the matters to be considered; reg 13 revocation); injunctions and corrective measures (reg 14: prohibiting use or disclosure, prohibiting the production or marketing of infringing goods, recall, destruction or delivery up; reg 15 sets the proportionality factors); compensation instead of an injunction (reg 16) where, at the time of use or disclosure, the person neither knew nor ought to have known that the secret came from someone using or disclosing it unlawfully, the order would cause that person disproportionate harm, and compensation appears reasonably satisfactory (capped at the licence fee for the period the use could have been prohibited); damages (reg 17: appropriate to actual prejudice, taking account of the negative economic consequences, the infringer's unfair profits and non-economic factors such as moral prejudice, or a royalty basis); publication of judicial decisions (reg 18). Reg 19 sets out the proceedings to which they apply.
- The Regulations do not expressly transpose the Directive's provisions on lawful acquisition (independent discovery, reverse engineering of a lawfully obtained product) or its exceptions for freedom of expression and whistleblowing (arts 3 and 5); the UK relied on the existing law of confidence, under which such acquisition is not a breach and the public interest defence applies, and reg 3(2)(b) requires any wider breach-of-confidence remedies to comply with the Directive's art 1 safeguards.

**Employees and former employees.** *Faccenda Chicken Ltd v Fowler* [1987] Ch 117 (CA, Neill LJ giving the judgment of the court): (1) During employment, the implied duty of good faith (fidelity) bars the employee from using or disclosing confidential information against the employer's interests, and from, for example, making or copying a list of customers for use after leaving. (2) After employment ends, the implied term is narrower: it protects only trade secrets and information of so highly confidential a nature as to require the same protection as a trade secret; it does not cover information which is "confidential" only in the sense that disclosing it during employment would have been a breach of good faith, nor the employee's general skill, knowledge and experience. (3) Wider protection depends on an express restrictive covenant (non-compete, non-solicitation or non-dealing), which is enforceable only if it protects a legitimate business interest (such as trade secrets or customer connection) and is no wider than reasonably necessary (*Herbert Morris Ltd v Saxelby* [1916] 1 AC 688 (HL); severance under *Tillman v Egon Zehnder Ltd* [2019] UKSC 32 (Supreme Court)). The Court of Appeal in *Faccenda* identified the factors to decide whether information is a trade secret: the nature of the employment (confidential material habitually handled); the nature of the information (does it have the character of a trade secret or its equivalent); whether the employer impressed on the employee the confidentiality; and whether the relevant information can be isolated from other information which the employee is free to use. A person may be liable if they memorise or copy customer details with the plan of using them (copying a list is more serious than remembering). *Lansing Linde Ltd v Kerr* [1991] 1 WLR 251 (CA): the term "trade secrets" is not confined to secret processes and can include highly confidential information of such a nature that its disclosure would be liable to cause real or significant harm to the owner (Staughton LJ). The 2018 Regulations' definition now applies in addition when pleading trade secrets: an employer who fails to take reasonable steps to keep information secret (marking documents, limiting access, contractual confidentiality and policies) may fail the third limb of reg 2.

**Public interest, whistleblowing and misuse of private information.** A defendant may justify disclosure where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the interest in confidence (revealing serious wrongdoing: *Gartside v Outram* (1856) 26 LJ Ch 113, the "iniquity" principle, now part of a balancing exercise). Protected disclosures by workers are protected by the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 (Employment Rights Act 1996 Part IVA). Private information about individuals is protected by the distinct tort of misuse of private information (*Campbell v MGN Ltd* [2004] UKHL 22) and not merely by confidence.

## Key authorities
- *Coco v A N Clark (Engineers) Ltd* [1969] RPC 41 (Ch): three-element test.
- *Attorney General v Guardian Newspapers (No 2)* [1990] 1 AC 109 (HL): limiting principles; public interest.
- *Faccenda Chicken Ltd v Fowler* [1987] Ch 117 (CA): employees' duties.
- *Lansing Linde v Kerr* [1991] 1 WLR 251 (CA): meaning of trade secret.
- *Vestergaard Frandsen v Bestnet Europe* [2013] UKSC 31 (Supreme Court): accessory liability; knowledge.
- *Seager v Copydex Ltd* [1967] 1 WLR 923 (CA): remedy by damages for use of confidential idea.
- *Tillman v Egon Zehnder* [2019] UKSC 32 (Supreme Court): severance of restrictive covenants.
- *Campbell v MGN* [2004] UKHL 22 (HL): misuse of private information.

## Applying it
Steps: (1) What is the information? Is it identifiable, secret, and valuable? Is it a "trade secret" under reg 2 (secret, value because secret, reasonable steps taken)? (2) How did the defendant acquire it: in confidence (contract, employment, negotiations), or by improper means, or did they receive it from another (notice)? (3) Misuse: use or disclosure beyond the permitted purpose. (4) For employees: which phase (during or after employment), is the information a trade secret or only general skill and knowledge (*Faccenda*), is there an express covenant that is enforceable. (5) Defences: public domain, independent development, public interest, lawful reverse engineering, consent. (6) Remedies: interim injunction (act quickly, since delay may defeat it), springboard, delivery up, damages or account; use the Regulations to ask for confidentiality in court (reg 10). (7) Time: six years. Traps: assuming all information about a business is protected; neglecting to prove reasonable steps; treating the customer list as automatically a trade secret; and ignoring the need to plead restrictive covenants separately.
Everyday: "An employee can use their general experience in a new job, but not take or use customer lists, pricing models or other trade secrets, even without a non-compete in the contract, if the information really is a trade secret."

## Debates and reform
- Whether to create a distinct statutory tort of misappropriation of trade secrets: the 2018 Regulations were a minimal implementation of the Directive, and critics argue that the dual regimes (equitable and statutory) create complexity.
- Non-compete clauses: the 2023 announcement of a three-month statutory cap was never implemented. On 26 November 2025 the Government published a working paper on options (a statutory cap such as three months, an outright ban, or a ban below a salary threshold), with responses closing on 18 February 2026, and on 9 October 2026 it announced that it intends to legislate to curb non-competes, without giving details. No Bill had been introduced by November 2026, so the common law restraint of trade doctrine still governs.
- The balance between employee mobility and employer protection, particularly in technology and finance, and the use of garden leave and springboard injunctions.
- Whistleblowing and journalism: how the Regulations' safeguards apply.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The 2018 Regulations came into force on 9 June 2018 (reg 1).
- Non-competes: no statutory restriction was in force at November 2026; the Government has said it will legislate (see Debates and reform), so check for a Bill before advising on a covenant.
