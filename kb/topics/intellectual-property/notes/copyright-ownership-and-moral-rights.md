# Copyright ownership - authors, employees, commissioned works, assignment and moral rights

## Overview
The author is the first owner of copyright (CDPA 1988 s 11(1)), except that an employer owns works made by an employee in the course of employment unless the parties agree otherwise (s 11(2)). A person who commissions a work does not own it merely by paying for it: ownership stays with the author unless there is a written assignment, but the commissioner may be given an implied licence or an equitable interest, as in *Griggs Group v Evans*. Authors also have moral rights (attribution, integrity) that cannot be assigned but can be waived.

## The law
**Who is the author (s 9).** The author is the person who creates the work (s 9(1)); special rules: the producer of a sound recording, the producer and principal director of a film, the person making a broadcast, the publisher of a typographical arrangement (s 9(2)); and for computer-generated works, the person by whom the arrangements necessary for creation are undertaken (s 9(3): see the AI note). Someone who merely has the idea, gives instructions or does skilled but non-creative work is not the author.

**Joint authorship (s 10).** A work of joint authorship is a work produced by the collaboration of two or more authors where the contribution of each is not distinct from that of the others. Joint authors co-own, and each must join in licensing or assigning. Test: *Kogan v Martin* [2019] EWCA Civ 1645 (Court of Appeal, summary at [53]): there must be collaboration (a common design as to the general outline and a sharing of the labour of working it out); each joint author must have made a significant contribution of the right kind, meaning an authorial contribution reflecting free and expressive choices (the *Infopaq* standard), which in a literary or dramatic work can include plot, characters and structure, not merely the words; and the contribution must not be distinct from the others'. "Who did the writing?" is never the whole question. There is no further requirement that the parties subjectively intended to create a work of joint authorship; having the final say is relevant to collaboration but not conclusive; and shares need not be equal. The Court of Appeal set aside the first-instance decision and ordered a retrial. *Fisher v Brooker* [2009] UKHL 41 (the "Whiter Shade of Pale" organ solo): the lower courts' finding that the organist was a joint author of the musical work (his organ melody being a significant contribution) was not challenged in the House of Lords, which held that his long delay in bringing the claim did not bar a declaration of his co-ownership. Co-authorship (s 10A): where music and lyrics are written by different authors in collaboration to be used together, they remain separate musical and literary works, but for duration each lasts until 70 years after the death of the last of the co-authors to die (s 12(8)).

**Employees: s 11(2).** Where a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, or a film, is made by an employee in the course of his employment, the employer is the first owner of any copyright in the work, subject to any agreement to the contrary. Two questions:
- *Employee or independent contractor?* The courts look at the overall relationship (control, integration, economic reality, mutuality of obligation): *Stephenson Jordan & Harrison Ltd v MacDonald & Evans* (1952) 69 RPC 10 (CA) (Denning LJ: a contract of service is one under which the work is done as an integral part of the business, compared with a contract for services, where it is only accessory to it); *Beloff v Pressdram Ltd* [1973] 1 All ER 241 (a political journalist's memorandum: made in the course of employment, as it was the sort of work she was employed to do). Freelancers and consultants are therefore not "employees" for s 11(2), and own the copyright in the works they create unless assigned.
- *In the course of employment?* The work must be what the employee was employed to do or was done in the course of duties, fairly regarded; work done in their own time on their own initiative is generally outside (compare *Noah v Shuba* [1991] FSR 14). Contractual terms in the employment contract may make clear which works belong to the employer. A contract can modify s 11(2) ("subject to any agreement to the contrary").
- Crown and Parliamentary copyright are governed by ss 163-166; s 11(2) does not apply to them (s 11(3)).

**Assignment and licences (ss 90-92, 101).** Copyright is transmissible by assignment, testamentary disposition or operation of law as personal property (s 90(1)). An assignment must be in writing signed by or on behalf of the assignor (s 90(3)); an assignment of future copyright takes effect automatically when the work comes into existence if signed by the prospective owner (s 91(1)). A non-exclusive licence need not be in writing and may be implied. An exclusive licence must be in writing and signed by or on behalf of the copyright owner (s 92(1)); an exclusive licensee has concurrent rights of action with the owner (s 101), and needs to join the owner in proceedings where required (s 102).

**Commissioned works: implied terms and equitable ownership.** Without agreement the contractor (author) keeps the copyright; the commissioner has at least an implied licence to use the work for the purpose for which it was commissioned. *Griggs Group Ltd v Evans* [2005] EWCA Civ 11 (Court of Appeal, Jacob LJ giving the leading judgment; the work was created in 1988, so the Copyright Act 1956 s 4(1) governed first ownership): the Griggs group, makers of Dr Martens footwear, decided in 1988 to combine two of its marks into a combined logo and commissioned an advertising agency, which in turn commissioned a freelance designer, Mr Evans, paid at an hourly rate. Evans did not know the logo would be used as the group's trade mark worldwide. In 2002 Evans assigned the legal title to a company connected with the defendants, which had notice of the claim. The Court of Appeal upheld the Deputy Judge's decision that Griggs was the owner of the copyright in equity (the appeal on the court's power to affect foreign rights was abandoned). It applied the principles in *Robin Ray v Classic FM plc* [1998] FSR 622 (Lightman J), which include: the contractor retains the copyright absent an express or implied term to the contrary; the court will imply a term only to the extent necessary to give business efficacy; the minimum necessary is a licence, but where the client needs to exclude the contractor from using the work (as with a logo identifying the client's trade mark) it will be necessary to imply a term that the client owns the copyright in equity. In *Griggs* the court rejected the argument that Evans's lack of knowledge of the wider use mattered, since the judge had found he was paid the proper rate for the job and the nature of a logo means the client must be able to exclude others. Practical lessons: a commissioner should obtain a written assignment (with future copyright and a waiver of moral rights) before the work is created.

**Moral rights (CDPA ss 77-89).** Moral rights belong to the author (or director) and, apart from the right against false attribution, to certain classes of work:
- *Right to be identified (s 77):* the author of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, and the director of a film, has the right to be identified in the circumstances listed in s 77 (for example when the work is published commercially, performed or communicated to the public, or exhibited in public); the right is not infringed unless it has been asserted (ss 77(1), 78), usually in a written assertion or in an assignment. Exceptions (s 79): computer programs, typeface designs and computer-generated works (s 79(2)); anything done by or with the authority of the copyright owner where copyright first vested in the employer under s 11(2) (s 79(3)); acts permitted by fair dealing for reporting current events by sound recording, film or broadcast, incidental inclusion, s 51 and certain other permitted acts (s 79(4)); works made for reporting current events (s 79(5)); and works made for, or made available with consent for, publication in newspapers, magazines and works of reference (s 79(6)).
- *Right to object to derogatory treatment (s 80):* a treatment is derogatory if it amounts to distortion or mutilation of the work or is otherwise prejudicial to the honour or reputation of the author or director. The right applies without assertion. Exceptions (s 81): computer programs and computer-generated works, works made for reporting current events, and contributions to periodicals and works of reference; for works first owned by an employer under s 11(2), the right applies to acts of the copyright owner only if the author is or has been identified, and a sufficient disclaimer prevents infringement (s 82). Test: *Confetti Records v Warner Music UK* [2003] EWHC 1274 (Ch) (Lewison J): distortion or mutilation is not enough on its own; the author must show the treatment is prejudicial to his honour or reputation, not merely that he dislikes it, and the claim failed for want of evidence of such prejudice.
- *False attribution (s 84)* and *the right to privacy of certain photographs and films (s 85)*, the latter for commissioned private photographs.
- Duration: for ss 77, 80 and 85, the same as copyright; for s 84, 20 years after death (s 86).
- Moral rights cannot be assigned (s 94) but pass on death; they can be waived by a signed written instrument (s 87(2)) or by informal agreement or conduct (s 87(4)). Breach is actionable as a breach of statutory duty (s 103). 
- Performers have separate moral rights (ss 205C-205N).

## Key authorities
- *Griggs Group Ltd v Evans* [2005] EWCA Civ 11 (CA): implied assignment/equitable ownership of a commissioned logo.
- *Robin Ray v Classic FM plc* [1998] FSR 622 (Ch): principles on commissioned works.
- *Kogan v Martin* [2019] EWCA Civ 1645 (CA): joint authorship test.
- *Fisher v Brooker* [2009] UKHL 41 (HL): joint authorship of a musical work; delay.
- *Stephenson Jordan & Harrison v MacDonald & Evans* (1952) 69 RPC 10 (CA): contract of service versus for services.
- *Beloff v Pressdram* [1973] 1 All ER 241 (Ch): employment of a journalist; course of employment.
- *Confetti Records v Warner Music UK* [2003] EWHC 1274 (Ch): derogatory treatment.

## Applying it
Ownership problem: (1) Identify the work and the actual creator (and joint authors). (2) Was the creator an employee (contract of service), creating the work in the course of employment? If so, employer owns (s 11(2)), subject to contrary agreement. (3) If a freelancer or consultant, the creator is the first owner; was there a signed written assignment (s 90(3))? (4) If not, what term can be implied: a licence (what scope?), or an equitable assignment where the commissioner needs exclusivity (*Griggs*)? The client can ask for a declaration and an order for legal title. (5) Moral rights: assertion; waiver clauses; whether any exceptions apply. (6) Remedies: declaration of ownership; infringement claims by assignee or exclusive licensee; account of profits. Traps: assuming the person who pays owns the work; forgetting that s 11(2) applies only to employees; treating moral rights as automatically assigned; forgetting that the paternity right must be asserted. Everyday: "If you hire a freelancer to design your logo, get an assignment in writing. Otherwise, you may only have a licence, or may need to go to court to establish your rights."

## Debates and reform
- Whether the default rule for commissioned works should favour commissioners (some jurisdictions' "work made for hire"), and the position of freelancers in creative industries; contract terms requiring blanket assignments and moral rights waivers.
- Moral rights: weak enforceability (assertion requirement, wide waivers), and their treatment in the digital environment.
- Joint authorship and music (the "split sheet" disputes, *Kogan* and *Fisher*): whether "significant contribution of the right kind" is too uncertain.
- Employee and freelance creators in the gig economy, and the AI consultation (see the AI note) about authorship of AI-assisted work.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No change to ss 9-11 or 77-89 was made in 2024-26. The Government's March 2026 report on copyright and AI proposed removing protection for wholly computer-generated works (s 9(3)), but no legislation had been introduced by November 2026 (see the AI note).
