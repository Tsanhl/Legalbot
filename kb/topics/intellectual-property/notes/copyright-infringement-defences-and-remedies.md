# Copyright infringement - primary and secondary, fair dealing, remedies and online platforms

## Overview
Copyright is infringed by doing, or authorising another to do, any of the restricted acts in relation to the whole or a substantial part of a protected work without licence (CDPA 1988 s 16). Liability for the primary acts is strict; secondary infringement needs knowledge. Defences are the "permitted acts" in Chapter III, notably the fair dealing provisions (criticism, review, quotation, news reporting; caricature, parody, pastiche; non-commercial research and private study), and the public interest. Remedies include damages, an account of profits, injunctions and delivery up. Online platforms have a limited hosting defence under the E-Commerce Regulations 2002 and may be subject to blocking injunctions.

## The law
**Restricted acts (s 16(1)):** (a) copy the work (s 17); (b) issue copies to the public (s 18); (c) rent or lend (s 18A); (d) perform, show or play the work in public (s 19); (e) communicate the work to the public (s 20); (f) make an adaptation, or do any restricted act in relation to an adaptation (s 21). Section 16(2): copyright is infringed by a person who without licence does or authorises another to do a restricted act; s 16(3): the act must be done in relation to the work as a whole or any substantial part, and doing so directly or indirectly. Copying includes storing the work in any medium by electronic means and making transient or incidental copies (s 17(2), (6)); in the case of an artistic work, making a three-dimensional copy of a two-dimensional work, and vice versa (s 17(3)).

**Elements of primary infringement.** (1) Copyright subsists in the claimant's work and the claimant owns it (or is exclusive licensee); (2) the defendant has done a restricted act in the UK (or, for online acts, targeted the UK: see below); (3) there is copying, meaning a causal connection between the claimant's work and the defendant's work: similarity plus access leads to an inference of copying, which the defendant can rebut by proving independent creation (*Francis Day & Hunter v Bron* [1963] Ch 587 (CA): subconscious copying counts); (4) the copied part is a substantial part. "Substantial" is a qualitative, not a quantitative, test: it asks whether the part taken is an expression of the intellectual creation of the author: *Designers Guild v Russell Williams* [2000] 1 WLR 2416 (HL) (existing note: idea/expression, and the importance of the part taken), applying *Infopaq* (C-5/08): even very small extracts may be protected if they express the author's own intellectual creation. *Newspaper Licensing Agency v Meltwater Holding* [2011] EWCA Civ 890 (CA): headlines and extracts of newspaper articles supplied by a media-monitoring service were capable of being a substantial part, and end users who received the monitoring reports by email needed a licence. On the separate question of copies made merely by viewing the service's website, the Supreme Court and the CJEU later held that on-screen and cache copies fall within the temporary copies exception in s 28A (*Public Relations Consultants Association v Newspaper Licensing Agency* [2013] UKSC 18; C-360/13). *SAS Institute v World Programming* [2013] EWHC 69 (Ch) (and CJEU C-406/10): the functionality of a computer program, programming language and data file formats are not protected by copyright; copying of the manual expressions was not substantial. *Hyde Park Residence v Yelland* [2001] Ch 143 (CA): publishing stills from security footage was not fair dealing for reporting current events, and the public interest defence is narrow.

**Authorisation.** A person who sanctions, approves or countenances the infringement (more than facilitating it) authorises it; *CBS Songs v Amstrad Consumer Electronics* [1988] AC 1013 (HL): selling a twin-tape recorder did not amount to authorisation, since the seller did not have control over use after sale.

**Secondary infringement (ss 22-26)** requires knowledge or reason to believe: s 22 importing an infringing copy otherwise than for private and domestic use; s 23 possessing in the course of business, selling or hiring, exhibiting or distributing an infringing copy; s 24 providing the means for making infringing copies (articles specifically designed or adapted) or transmitting a work by a telecommunications network; s 25 permitting premises to be used for an infringing performance; s 26 supplying apparatus for an infringing performance. "Infringing copy" (s 27) is a copy whose making constituted an infringement, or would have if made in the UK; articles imported are infringing copies if their making in the UK would have infringed (s 27(3)). In *Getty Images v Stability AI* [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch) the High Court held that an "article" can be intangible, but that an AI model which does not store or reproduce the works is not an "infringing copy"; that point was argued in the Court of Appeal in November 2026 and judgment is awaited (see the AI note).

**Territoriality and online acts.** UK copyright is infringed only by acts in the UK. For internet communications, the courts ask whether the act is targeted at the public in the UK (an approach derived from trade mark cases and applied to copyright, for example *Football Dataco v Sportradar* (C-173/11) on database right; *Lifestyle Equities v Amazon UK* [2024] UKSC 8 (Supreme Court) on trade marks, which applies the targeting test to online sales into the UK). Communication to the public (s 20) covers making available on demand; the courts treat linking, embedding and platform liability by applying EU case law developed before 2021 (*Svensson* (C-466/12), *GS Media* (C-160/15): knowledge, profit and the "new public"); these are now assimilated case law.

**Defences and permitted acts.**
- *Fair dealing (the dealing must be fair, and a sufficient acknowledgement is usually needed):* s 29: fair dealing for the purposes of research for a non-commercial purpose and private study; s 29A: text and data analysis copies for non-commercial research (see the AI note); s 30(1): criticism or review (of the work or another work), with sufficient acknowledgement; s 30(1ZA): quotation (the extent must not be more than required by the specific purpose); s 30(2): reporting current events (not by photograph, so photographs are not covered by this dealing); s 30A: caricature, parody or pastiche (from 1 October 2014). Fairness depends on: whether the dealing is commercial and competes with the original (the "market substitution" factor), whether the work has been published, the amount taken and the importance of the part, and the motive for the dealing (*Hubbard v Vosper* [1972] 2 QB 84 (CA): fair dealing is a matter of fact, degree and impression; *Ashdown v Telegraph Group* [2001] EWCA Civ 1142 (CA): the Court of Appeal held that, in rare cases, freedom of expression under art 10 ECHR may justify a use that would otherwise infringe, but that the newspaper's extensive verbatim use of a confidential minute was not within fair dealing; and *Pro Sieben Media v Carlton UK Television* [1999] 1 WLR 605 (CA) on fairness in criticism and current events reporting). On parody: *Deckmyn v Vandersteen* (C-201/13) said a parody must evoke an existing work while being noticeably different and constitute an expression of humour or mockery, and its application must strike a fair balance between the rights of the author and freedom of expression; the English courts apply this to s 30A. 
- *Other permitted acts:* incidental inclusion (s 31), time-shifting (s 70), fair dealing for education and illustration for instruction (s 32), libraries and archives (ss 40A-43), temporary copies (s 28A). The proposed private copying exception (s 28B) was quashed in *R (British Academy of Songwriters, Composers and Authors) v Secretary of State for Business, Innovation and Skills* [2015] EWHC 2041 (Admin), so there is no general exception for copying CDs for personal use.
- *Public interest (s 171(3)):* a court may refuse to enforce copyright if enforcement would be contrary to the public interest (*Hyde Park v Yelland*; *Ashdown*), a narrow defence to be applied in rare cases.
- *Other defences:* licence (express or implied), consent, estoppel, acquiescence and delay.

**Remedies.**
- *Injunction and delivery up:* final and interim injunctions; delivery up and destruction of infringing copies (ss 99-100); technical solutions such as injunctions against service providers who have actual knowledge (s 97A: blocking injunctions against websites: *Twentieth Century Fox v British Telecommunications* [2011] EWHC 1981 (Ch), Arnold J; *Cartier International v British Telecommunications* [2018] UKSC 28 concerned trade mark blocking orders under the Senior Courts Act 1981 s 37(1) and held that rights holders must bear the reasonable costs of implementation; the reasoning is relevant by analogy to s 97A).
- *Damages (s 96(2)):* all such relief by way of damages as is available for any other property right. The measure: for lost sales, or a reasonable royalty or user principle; under s 97(1), if the defendant did not know and had no reason to believe that copyright subsisted, the claimant is not entitled to damages (but can have other remedies); under s 97(2), additional damages may be awarded if justified by flagrancy of the infringement and benefit accruing to the defendant, with regard to all circumstances.
- *Account of profits:* an alternative to damages, at the claimant's election after disclosure (innocence is no bar).
- *Limitation:* six years (Limitation Act 1980 s 2 for tort), applied to breaches. 
- *Procedure:* The Intellectual Property Enterprise Court (IPEC) deals with lower-value claims: damages are capped at £500,000 and recoverable costs at £60,000 for the liability stage and £30,000 for an inquiry as to damages or account (the costs caps since 1 October 2022), with a small claims track for claims up to £10,000. The High Court (Business and Property Courts) deals with larger claims.
- *Criminal liability:* s 107 creates offences of making or dealing in infringing copies with knowledge or reason to believe, and s 107(2A) an offence of infringing by communicating a work to the public with knowledge and intent to make a gain or cause loss; the main s 107(1) offences and the s 107(2A) offence carry up to ten years' imprisonment on indictment (s 107(4), (4A)).

**Online marketplaces and takedown.** There is no UK statutory notice-and-takedown regime for copyright. A host of user content is not liable for damages or criminal sanctions for unlawful content if it does not have actual knowledge of unlawful activity or information (and, for damages, awareness of facts from which it would have been apparent) and, on obtaining such knowledge, acts expeditiously to remove or disable access (Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 reg 19 (hosting)); in deciding whether the host had actual knowledge, the court considers among other things whether it received a notice through its published contact details and how far the notice identified the sender, the location of the material and why it is unlawful (reg 22). Rights holders therefore send detailed notices of infringement to the platform, which generally takes the content down under its own policy. A marketplace that itself uses or promotes infringing goods or has an active role may be liable as a joint tortfeasor or under secondary infringement. Trade mark principles from *L'Oréal v eBay* (C-324/09) apply to marketplaces for mark infringement (see the trade mark note). Courts may order intermediaries to block access to infringing websites (s 97A for copyright).

- *Deckmyn v Vandersteen* (C-201/13): parody.

## Applying it
Steps: (1) Subsistence and ownership; (2) Which restricted act (copy, communicate, adapt)? Did the defendant do it in the UK? (3) Copying: access and similarity; independent creation. (4) Substantial part: what did the defendant take, and does it express the author's intellectual creation (not merely ideas or functionality)? (5) Secondary infringement if the defendant dealt with copies: knowledge. (6) Defences: fair dealing (purpose listed? sufficient acknowledgement? fair?), s 30A parody, licence, public interest. (7) Remedy: injunction, damages (s 97(1) innocence, s 97(2) additional damages), account of profits, delivery up; site blocking for infringing sites; notice to platform. Traps: saying copyright protects ideas; saying "I changed it by 30 per cent so it's allowed"; assuming fair dealing applies for any purpose (only listed purposes); forgetting acknowledgment. Everyday: "Quoting a short extract in a review with credit is generally fine; copying a whole photo or article for your own commercial use is not."

## Debates and reform
- The fair dealing purposes are closed (not open "fair use"); the 2014 reforms (parody, quotation) gave limited flexibility; arguments for a more flexible approach and for protecting text and data mining (see the AI note).
- Online intermediaries: whether the UK should implement a notice-and-takedown scheme with counter-notice; the effect of leaving the EU's DSM Directive art 17 (user-uploaded content licensing) which the UK did not implement.
- Remedies and costs for small creators: the IPEC small claims track for claims up to £10,000.
- The liability of AI developers for training and outputs (see the AI note).

## Recent changes and points to check
- IPEC: damages cap £500,000; costs caps £60,000 (liability) and £30,000 (quantum) since 1 October 2022; no later change was identified as at November 2026.
- The Government's March 2026 report on copyright and AI (see the AI note) proposed no change to the exceptions for now; none is in force.
