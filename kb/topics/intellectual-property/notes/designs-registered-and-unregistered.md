# Designs - registered designs, UK design right, supplementary unregistered design and CDPA s 51

## Overview
Product appearance is protected in the UK by several overlapping rights: UK registered designs (Registered Designs Act 1949, "RDA"), UK unregistered design right for shape and configuration (CDPA 1988 Part III), the "supplementary unregistered design" (SUD) that replaced the EU unregistered Community design for UK disclosures from 1 January 2021, and, for artistic works, copyright, restricted by CDPA s 51 so that copyright cannot be used to stop copying of the shape of articles other than artistic works. A registered design gives a monopoly for up to 25 years; UK design right lasts at most 15 years (10 on marketing); the SUD lasts three years. A design question should ask which right applies and what each protects.

## The law
**Registered designs (RDA 1949).**
- *What is registrable (s 1):* "design" means the appearance of the whole or a part of a product resulting from the features of, in particular, the lines, contours, colours, shape, texture, materials of the product or its ornamentation (s 1(2)); "product" includes packaging, get-up, graphic symbols and typographic typefaces but not computer programs (s 1(3)). 
- *Requirements:* the design must be new and have individual character (s 1B(1)). It is new if no identical design, or design differing only in immaterial details, has been made available to the public before the relevant date (s 1B(2)). It has individual character if the overall impression it produces on the "informed user" differs from that produced on such a user by any earlier design (s 1B(3)); the designer's freedom in creating the design is taken into account (s 1B(4)). Disclosures by the designer (or successor in title) in the 12 months before the filing or priority date do not count (the grace period: s 1B(6)(c)), so an application must be made within 12 months of the designer's first disclosure. Nor do disclosures that could not reasonably have become known in the normal course of business to persons in the UK and the EEA specialising in the sector concerned (s 1B(6)(a)), or disclosures made in confidence (s 1B(6)(b)). A component of a complex product must remain visible in normal use (s 1B(8)).
- *Exclusions (ss 1C, 1D):* features solely dictated by technical function (s 1C(1)); features which must necessarily be reproduced in exact form and dimensions so that the product can be mechanically connected to, or placed in, around or against, another (the "must fit" exclusion: s 1C(2)), except for designs allowing multiple assembly within a modular system (s 1C(3)); designs contrary to public policy or accepted principles of morality (s 1D); and certain protected emblems. There is no "must match" exclusion from registrability, but there is a repair defence: the right in a registered design of a component part which may be used to repair a complex product so as to restore its original appearance is not infringed by use for that purpose (s 7A(5)).
- *Ownership:* the designer is the first proprietor unless the design was created in the course of employment, in which case the employer is (s 2, as amended by the Intellectual Property Act 2014, which removed the former commissioner rule from 1 October 2014).
- *Right and duration:* registration gives the exclusive right to use the design and any design which does not produce on the informed user a different overall impression (s 7(1)); use includes making, offering, putting on the market, importing, exporting or using a product incorporating the design, or stocking it for those purposes (s 7(2)). The right lasts for five years from registration and may be renewed for four further five-year periods, a maximum of 25 years (s 8). There is no substantive examination for novelty before registration: an invalidity action can be brought later (s 11ZA). Infringement is an act within s 7 without consent (s 7A), subject to the exceptions in s 7A (private non-commercial acts, experiments, teaching and citation, repair under s 7A(5), and exhaustion for products put on the market in the UK or EEA with consent: s 7A(4)); liability does not depend on copying; damages are not awarded against a defendant who proves they were not aware, and had no reasonable ground for supposing, that the design was registered (s 24B), though other remedies such as an injunction remain. Section 35ZA makes intentional copying of a registered design in the course of business a criminal offence (from 1 October 2014).

**UK unregistered design right (CDPA 1988 Part III).**
- *Subsistence (s 213):* property right in an original design, meaning the design of the shape or configuration (internal or external) of the whole or part of an article (s 213(1)-(2)). It is not subsisting in a method or principle of construction; "must fit" or "must match" features (features of shape or configuration that enable the article to be connected to another so that either can perform its function, or that depend on the appearance of another article of which it is intended to form an integral part: s 213(3)(b)); or surface decoration (s 213(3)(c)). A design is not original if it is commonplace in the design field in a qualifying country at the time of its creation (s 213(4)). The design must be recorded in a design document or an article made to it (s 213(6)), and the design must qualify by reference to the designer or the first marketer (s 213(5)).
- *Ownership (s 215):* the designer, or the employer where the design is created in the course of employment; the commission rule was repealed in 2014 (so a commissioner owns only by assignment), and the person who first markets articles for designs qualifying by marketing (s 215(4)).
- *Duration (s 216):* 15 years from the end of the calendar year in which the design was first recorded or an article first made, but if articles made to the design are made available for sale or hire within five years of that, ten years from the end of the calendar year of first marketing. 
- *Infringement (ss 226-228):* reproducing the design for commercial purposes by making articles to the design (or a design document for making them) or by importing, selling or dealing with such articles with knowledge; only copying is infringement (it is not a monopoly right, so independent design is a defence); the right extends to articles made to a design that is a copy, not merely similar. A defendant who did not know and had no reason to believe that design right subsisted is not liable in damages (s 233). 
- *Qualifying persons:* design right only subsists if the designer (or employer) or the first marketer is a qualifying person (ss 218-220): an individual habitually resident in a qualifying country, or a body formed under the law of the UK or another qualifying country with a place of substantial business activity in a qualifying country (s 217(1)). Qualifying countries are the UK, countries to which Part III is extended, and countries designated by Order as giving reciprocal protection (s 217(3)). EU member states ceased to be qualifying countries in their own right from 1 January 2021 (s 217(3)(c) repealed by the 2019 Regulations, with transitional provisions for designs that already qualified), so later designs by EU-based designers must qualify by another route, such as first marketing in a qualifying country.

**Supplementary unregistered design (SUD).** Since 1 January 2021 the UK has had its own unregistered design right replacing, for the UK, the EU unregistered Community design (which no longer covers the UK). A SUD arises automatically on first disclosure of the design in the UK (not just anywhere in the world; first disclosure in the EU alone does not give a SUD) and protects the appearance of the whole or part of a product, with the same novelty and individual character requirements as for registered designs and without the UK design right exclusions for surface decoration, and gives the right to stop copying for three years from first disclosure. The Government proposed in November 2026 to make the SUD available after first disclosure anywhere in the world (legislation needed; not yet made). Existing EU unregistered designs that arose before 2021 continue as "continuing unregistered designs" for the remainder of their three-year term. The Regulations also deemed existing registered Community designs to have comparable UK registered designs (with the same filing and priority dates, subject to opt-out).

**Copyright and design: CDPA s 51 and s 236.** It is not an infringement of any copyright in a design document or model recording or embodying a design for anything other than an artistic work or a typeface to make an article to the design or to copy an article made to the design (s 51(1)); "design" means the design of the shape or configuration (internal or external) of the whole or part of an article, other than surface decoration (s 51(3)). The result is that someone who manufactures a functional article copying a drawing for it does not infringe the copyright in that drawing (the claimant's remedy lies in design right or registered design), but surface decoration (patterns on wallpaper or fabric) and artistic works (such as sculptures and logos) remain protected by copyright. A work that is primarily utilitarian, such as a film prop helmet, is not a sculpture and so not an "artistic work": *Lucasfilm Ltd v Ainsworth* [2011] UKSC 39. The former CDPA s 52 (which cut copyright in industrially exploited artistic works to 25 years) was repealed by the Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, with effect from 28 July 2016. Under s 236, where copyright subsists in a work that includes a design in which design right subsists, doing something that infringes the copyright is not an infringement of the design right.

## Key authorities
- *Magmatic Ltd v PMS International Ltd* [2016] UKSC 12 (Supreme Court): "Trunki" ride-on suitcase; the Supreme Court dismissed the claimant's appeal and held that PMS's Kiddee case did not infringe the registered design, because the design had to be assessed as registered (on the filed images, including features such as the absence of surface decoration and the way colour was shown) and on that basis the overall impression on the informed user differed.
- *Samsung Electronics (UK) v Apple Inc* [2012] EWCA Civ 1339 (CA): the Galaxy Tab did not infringe Apple's registered design; the informed user is a person with experience of the product and design corpus; the overall impression differed (the trial judge's phrase was that the Samsung tablets were "not as cool").
- *Dyson Ltd v Vax Ltd* [2011] EWCA Civ 1206 (CA): the informed user, design freedom and the overall impression; the Vax vacuum cleaner did not infringe Dyson's registered design.
- *Lucasfilm Ltd v Ainsworth* [2011] UKSC 39 (Supreme Court): film prop helmets were not sculptures, so there was no UK copyright in them (the case also held that an English court could hear a claim for infringement of US copyright).
- *Ocular Sciences Ltd v Aspect Vision Care Ltd* [1997] RPC 289 (Ch): design right: "must match" and commonplace and infringement principles.
- *Designers Guild* (existing note): for copyright, idea/expression.

## Applying it
Steps: (1) What is the claimant's right: registered design (check the registration and its representations); UK design right; SUD; copyright (artistic work or surface decoration)? (2) If registered: is the design new and of individual character on the filing date or priority date (and was any disclosure within 12 months)? Does the technical function or must-fit exclusion apply? (3) Infringement of registered design: does the defendant's product produce the same overall impression on the informed user? Take account of design freedom, similarities and differences, the design corpus; the copyist's intention is irrelevant (monopoly right). (4) UK design right: was the design original and not commonplace; was it a shape or configuration (not surface decoration); is any part a "must fit" or "must match"; did the defendant copy? (5) SUD: first disclosure in the UK; copying; three years. (6) Copyright: s 51 limits functional articles, but surface decoration and artistic works remain protected; consider *Lucasfilm* for sculptures. (7) Remedies: injunction, damages (not for innocent infringers), delivery up, criminal offence (for registered designs, intentional copying in the course of business is an offence under RDA s 35ZA). Common errors: forgetting the 12-month grace period; believing a registered design protects the idea or function; applying the "must match" exclusion to registered designs (they have instead the s 7A(5) repair defence); stating that copying is needed for registered design infringement (it is not).

## Debates and reform
- Whether the UK should align or diverge from the EU design reforms: the EU has reformed its own design law (Regulation (EU) 2024/2822 and Directive (EU) 2024/2823), including a repair clause for spare parts. In the UK, the IPO's 2022 call for views was followed by a 2025 consultation and a Government response in November 2026 committing, "when parliamentary time allows", to targeted reforms: registrar search and examination of some applications, a statutory 30-month deferment period, express protection for graphical user interfaces and animated designs, and SUD protection after disclosure anywhere in the world. UK unregistered design right will be kept and computer-generated designs stay under the existing framework for now. None of this had been legislated by November 2026.
- The "spare parts" and "must-match" debates: how far repair and replacement parts should be protected, and the relationship between the "must match" exclusion for UK design right (CDPA s 213(3)(b)(ii)) and the repair defence for registered designs (RDA s 7A(5)).
- Overlap and complexity of rights: the relationship between design right, copyright and registered designs, and the confusing result after the 2013 repeal of s 52.
- The duration of unregistered design right and the "licences of right" provisions.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 January 2021: SUD introduced; continuing unregistered designs preserved; comparable registered designs for Community designs.
- November 2026: Government response to the 2025 designs consultation (see Debates and reform); the reforms need legislation and none had been introduced by November 2026.
