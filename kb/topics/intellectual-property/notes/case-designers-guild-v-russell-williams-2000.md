# Designers Guild Ltd v Russell Williams (Textiles) Ltd [2000] UKHL 58 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. The citation "[2000] 1 WLR 2416" given in the request
> was not found in the sources used; the sources give [2001] FSR 113 and [2000] UKHL 58. The House of Lords panel and the
> author of the leading speech are not confirmed and are omitted. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, November 2000 (one source gives 23 November, another listing suggests 28 November, so only the month is
stated). The case deals with when copying from a textile design amounts to copying a **substantial part** of the
claimant's copyright work under section 16(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, and the proper approach
to non-literal copying.

## Facts
Designers Guild and Russell Williams (Textiles) Ltd (trading as Washington DC) both designed and sold fabrics and
wallpapers. Designers Guild held copyright in a fabric design called "Ixia". It claimed that the defendant had copied it
in creating a design called "Marguerite". The defendant's designers' copying and what was copied were the main issues.

## Decisions
- At first instance Lawrence Collins J found that the Marguerite design had copied a substantial part of Ixia and
  that copyright was infringed.
- The Court of Appeal reversed that finding.
- The House of Lords **allowed Designers Guild's appeal and restored** the first-instance finding of infringement.

## Principles (as reported)
- Once the copied features are identified, the remaining question is whether what was taken is the whole or a
  **substantial part** of the claimant's work.
- Non-literal copying is assessed by comparing the designs and looking at the cumulative effect of the features
  taken, not by dissecting individual elements in isolation.
- One source reports that substantiality depends on the quality or importance of what was taken, not merely the
  amount, and that copyright protects the way an idea is expressed, not the general idea or technique itself. It also
  reports that appeal courts should be slow to disturb a trial judge's evaluation. These last points come from a
  single source and should be checked.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Ask first whether there was copying (access and similarity), then whether what was copied is a substantial
   part.
2. Do not count lines or percentages; consider the importance of the features to the original work as a whole.
3. Distinguish ideas and general styles (unprotected) from the particular expression and combination of features
   (protected).
4. Compare the works as a whole and the cumulative effect of the similarities.
