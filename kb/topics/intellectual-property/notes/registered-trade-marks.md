# Registered trade marks - registrability, infringement, defences and bad faith (Sky v SkyKick)

## Overview
A registered UK trade mark (Trade Marks Act 1994, "TMA") gives its owner an exclusive right to use the sign for the goods and services for which it is registered, and to stop others using identical or similar signs in the ways set out in s 10. A sign is registrable if it can be represented on the register, distinguishes the goods or services and is not caught by the absolute grounds in s 3; it can be refused or opposed under the relative grounds in s 5 if it conflicts with earlier rights. The Supreme Court's decision in *SkyKick* [2024] UKSC 36 makes clear that an application made without any intention to use the mark for the goods or services covered can be made in bad faith; breadth of specification does not establish bad faith by itself, but may support an inference of it depending on the circumstances.

## The law
**What is a trade mark (s 1(1)).** Any sign capable of being represented on the register in a manner which enables the registrar and other competent authorities and the public to determine the clear and precise subject matter of the protection, and capable of distinguishing goods or services of one undertaking from those of other undertakings. Words, designs, letters, numerals, colours, the shape of goods or packaging, sounds, and other signs may qualify if they can be so represented (the former "graphic representation" requirement was removed in January 2019).

**Absolute grounds (s 3).** Refusal if the mark: (a) does not satisfy s 1(1); (b) is devoid of distinctive character; (c) consists exclusively of signs which designate the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin, time of production or other characteristics of goods or services; (d) consists exclusively of signs customary in the language or established practice of the trade. Marks falling under (b)-(d) may be registered if, before the application date, they have acquired a distinctive character as a result of the use made of them (s 3(1) proviso). Section 3(2): signs consisting exclusively of a shape (or another characteristic) which results from the nature of the goods themselves, is necessary to obtain a technical result, or gives substantial value to the goods cannot be registered (and cannot become registrable through use). Section 3(3): deceptive marks and marks contrary to public policy or accepted principles of morality; s 3(4): contrary to law; s 3(6): the application was made in bad faith. For shape marks, acquired distinctiveness must be shown for the mark as registered, with evidence that the relevant public perceive the shape itself as indicating origin: see *Société des Produits Nestlé SA v Cadbury UK Ltd* [2017] EWCA Civ 358 (CA) (the four-finger KitKat shape: the Court of Appeal dismissed Nestlé's appeal against refusal; recognition of the shape and its association with KitKat were not enough, because the applicant must show that a significant proportion of the relevant class perceive the goods as originating from a particular undertaking because of the shape, so that they would rely on it as an indication of origin).

**Relative grounds (s 5).** (1) A mark identical to an earlier mark for identical goods or services; (2) identical or similar to an earlier mark for identical or similar goods or services where there is a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public, including a likelihood of association; (3) a mark identical or similar to an earlier mark with a reputation in the UK where use of the later mark without due cause would take unfair advantage of, or be detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the earlier mark; (4) use of the mark is liable to be prevented by an earlier unregistered mark or sign protected by passing off (s 5(4)(a)), by a protected designation of origin or geographical indication (s 5(4)(aa)), or by another earlier right such as copyright or design right (s 5(4)(b)); (5) the proprietor of the earlier mark or right may consent to registration (s 5(5)). Likelihood of confusion is assessed globally, from the viewpoint of the average consumer, who is reasonably well informed, observant and circumspect, taking into account visual, aural and conceptual similarities, the distinctiveness of the earlier mark and imperfect recollection (*Sabel v Puma* (C-251/95); *Canon v MGM* (C-39/97); *Lloyd Schuhfabrik Meyer v Klijsen* (C-342/97)).

**Infringement (s 10).**
- s 10(1) "double identity": use in the course of trade of a sign identical to the registered mark in relation to goods or services identical to those for which it is registered. No confusion needed, but the use must affect one of the functions of the mark (above all, indicating origin): *Arsenal Football Club v Reed* (C-206/01). Keyword advertising using a competitor's mark infringes only where the advertisement does not enable, or enables only with difficulty, reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant internet users to ascertain whether the goods or services come from the proprietor or a third party (*Interflora v Marks & Spencer* (C-323/09)). Arnold J found that M&S infringed on that test, but the Court of Appeal set the finding aside and ordered a retrial ([2014] EWCA Civ 1403), holding among other things that the burden of proof remained on Interflora.
- s 10(2): use of an identical sign for similar goods or services, or a similar sign for identical or similar goods or services, where there exists a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public, including a likelihood of association.
- s 10(3): use of an identical or similar sign, in relation to any goods or services (similar or not), where the registered mark has a reputation in the UK and use of the sign, being without due cause, takes unfair advantage of, or is detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the mark. *L'Oréal SA v Bellure NV* [2010] EWCA Civ 535 (CA) (following the CJEU C-487/07): taking a free ride on the reputation of a famous mark (unfair advantage) does not require confusion.
- s 10(4) lists acts which constitute use of a sign (affixing it to goods, offering goods under it, importing or exporting, using it as a trade or company name, using it in business papers and advertising, and using it in comparative advertising contrary to the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008: s 10(4)(e)); the former s 10(6) on comparative advertising has been repealed.
- *Territory and online sales:* *Lifestyle Equities CV v Amazon UK Services Ltd* [2024] UKSC 8 (Supreme Court, Beverly Hills Polo Club marks): for an online listing on a non-UK website to infringe a UK mark, the website must have targeted UK consumers, which is judged objectively from the perspective of the average consumer in the UK; the court looked at the whole context (including the delivery options, the use of the mark, and the language). On the facts, the court found the sales via a US-based website to UK customers infringed.

**Defences and limits (s 11, 11A, 12).** A registered mark is not infringed by (s 11(2)), provided the use is in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters: (a) the use by an individual of his own name or address (since 14 January 2019 the defence is confined to individuals, so companies cannot rely on it for their trade names); (b) signs or indications which are not distinctive or which concern kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin or other characteristics (descriptive use); (c) use of the mark to identify or refer to the proprietor's goods or services, in particular where necessary to indicate intended purpose (such as accessories or spare parts). Nor is it infringed by use of an earlier right in a particular locality (s 11(3)). The proprietor cannot prevent use of the mark in relation to goods put on the market in the UK or the European Economic Area under the mark by him or with his consent, unless there are legitimate reasons to oppose further dealings, in particular where the condition of the goods has been changed or impaired (exhaustion: s 12). This "UK+" regime is one-way (goods first marketed in the EEA are exhausted in the UK, not vice versa; goods first marketed outside the UK and EEA can still be blocked); adopted as an interim measure from 1 January 2021, it was confirmed as permanent on 15 May 2025. Section 11A: a defendant may rely on non-use as a defence if the registration has been completed for five years or more before the date of the claim and the mark has not been put to genuine use in relation to the goods or services relied on in the preceding five years (without proper reasons). Additionally, revocation for non-use (s 46(1)(a)-(b)) and invalidity (s 47) can be raised by counterclaim. On honest practices, *Gerolsteiner Brunnen v Putsch* (C-100/02) (KERRY Spring against GERRI): use of an indication of geographical origin is not excluded merely because it is aurally similar to the mark; the national court must assess all the circumstances, including the presentation of the goods.

**Bad faith: *SkyKick*.** *SkyKick UK Ltd v Sky Ltd* [2024] UKSC 36 (Supreme Court, 13 November 2024): Sky (broadcaster, owner of SKY marks registered for very wide ranges of goods and services, including computer software) sued SkyKick (a cloud migration software provider) for infringement and SkyKick counterclaimed for invalidity. Arnold J found the SKY marks partly invalid for bad faith and cut down their specifications, but held that SkyKick infringed; the Court of Appeal reversed the bad faith finding. The Supreme Court unanimously allowed SkyKick's appeal in part. Held: (1) applying the CJEU's ruling on the reference in the case (C-371/18, 29 January 2020), an application made without any intention to use the mark for specified goods or services can be in bad faith (s 3(6)) where the applicant intended to undermine the interests of third parties in a manner inconsistent with honest practices, or to obtain an exclusive right for purposes other than those falling within the functions of a trade mark (for example as a weapon against others); lack of intention to use is not bad faith in itself; (2) the Court of Appeal was wrong to hold that the overall width of a specification can never support an inference of bad faith: whether it can depends on all the circumstances, and the use of broad general terms rather than sub-categories does not rule it out; (3) Arnold J was entitled to find that the marks were applied for in bad faith to the extent he did, and his modified specifications were restored, so the registrations are partly invalid and the unaffected parts remain; (4) on infringement, SkyKick's cloud backup service infringed the marks as cut down, but infringement by its cloud migration service had not been established, because "electronic mail services" does not cover all services related to email. Lack of clarity and precision is not itself a ground of invalidity (CJEU ruling on the same reference; not reopened in the Supreme Court).

## Key authorities
- *SkyKick UK Ltd v Sky Ltd* [2024] UKSC 36 (Supreme Court): bad faith and broad specifications; partial invalidity; cloud backup infringed, cloud migration did not.
- *Lifestyle Equities CV v Amazon UK Services Ltd* [2024] UKSC 8 (Supreme Court): targeting.
- *Interflora Inc v Marks & Spencer plc* (C-323/09) and [2014] EWCA Civ 1403 (CA): keyword advertising and the origin function; the first-instance finding of infringement was set aside and a retrial ordered.
- *L'Oréal v Bellure* [2010] EWCA Civ 535 (CA): s 10(3) unfair advantage.
- *Société des Produits Nestlé v Cadbury UK* [2017] EWCA Civ 358 (CA): shape mark and acquired distinctiveness.
- *Arsenal v Reed* (C-206/01): the function-of-origin test for double identity.
- *Sabel v Puma* (C-251/95): global appreciation of confusion.

## Applying it
Registrability: (1) is it a sign capable of representation and distinguishing? (2) Absolute grounds: descriptive? non-distinctive? shape? Has distinctiveness been acquired by use? (3) Relative grounds: earlier marks in the same area, and earlier rights (passing off). (4) Bad faith (s 3(6)): did the applicant intend to use the mark for all items in the specification, or intend to block others? Infringement: (1) Is the mark validly registered and in use (non-use defence)? (2) Is the defendant's use in the UK, in the course of trade, as a trade mark? (3) Choose the right provision: identical sign/goods (s 10(1)); similar, confusion (s 10(2)); reputation (s 10(3)). (4) Apply the average consumer test; global appreciation. (5) Defences: own name, descriptive, exhaustion, non-use, invalidity counterclaim. (6) Remedies: injunction, damages or account of profits, delivery up, publicity. Traps: confusing registered marks with passing off, requiring confusion under s 10(1) or s 10(3); forgetting to ask whether the use is "as a trade mark"; ignoring proof of use; treating *SkyKick* as saying broad specifications are always bad faith.

## Debates and reform
- Specifications and clutter: *SkyKick* addresses defensive over-claiming; the UKIPO and the courts continue to push for narrower specifications; whether more safeguards are needed against "squatting" and trade mark trolling.
- Keyword advertising and online marketplaces: how the *Interflora* approach applies when the advertiser uses third-party brands; the position of marketplaces (see the copyright infringement note for hosting).
- Post-Brexit divergence: the UK has "comparable" marks for former EU marks and diverges on exhaustion (UK/EEA regime) and the territorial reach of EU marks; since 1 January 2024 pre-2021 CJEU case law is "assimilated" and can be departed from.
- Whether the UK should have a system that protects well-known marks without use, or one that requires use more strictly.
