# Artificial intelligence and intellectual property - inventorship, computer-generated works, training and Getty v Stability AI

## Overview
UK intellectual property law has no AI-specific regime. An AI system cannot be an inventor (*Thaler v Comptroller-General* [2023] UKSC 49). The CDPA 1988 unusually provides copyright in "computer-generated works" (s 9(3)), but its operation is uncertain and the Government proposed in March 2026 to remove that protection for wholly computer-generated works (not yet legislated). Whether training generative AI models on copyright works infringes in the UK is unresolved: the only substantive exception is for non-commercial text and data analysis (s 29A), and the first UK judgment on the issue, *Getty Images v Stability AI* (High Court, 4 November 2025), rejected the secondary infringement claim, but an appeal was heard in November 2026 and judgment was awaited at the date of writing. The Government's reform process has moved away from its preferred "opt-out" exception towards gathering more evidence, and no legislative change is in force.

## The law
**Patents: AI as inventor.** *Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks* [2023] UKSC 49 (Supreme Court, 20 December 2023): Dr Thaler applied for patents naming as inventor "DABUS", an AI system, and claimed the right to the patents as owner of the machine. The Supreme Court unanimously held that under the Patents Act 1977 (ss 7 and 13) an "inventor" must be a natural person; DABUS is not a person and so could not be an inventor; and Dr Thaler had no entitlement to apply for a patent merely by owning the machine, since the doctrine of accession did not give him the rights of the inventor. The Court expressly did not decide whether an invention generated autonomously by AI is patentable, or whether the law should change; it said those questions were for Parliament. Patents for inventions made with AI assistance remain available where a human is the inventor. Separately, in *Emotional Perception AI Ltd v Comptroller-General of Patents* [2026] UKSC 3 (11 February 2026; Lord Briggs and Lord Leggatt giving the judgment of a unanimous court) the Supreme Court held that the *Aerotel* approach to excluded subject matter should no longer be followed and adopted the EPO Enlarged Board's "any hardware" approach (G 1/19). An artificial neural network is a "program for a computer", but a claim involving hardware is an invention and is not excluded as a program "as such". Features that do not contribute to the technical character of the invention are then disregarded when assessing novelty and inventive step (the G 1/19 "intermediate step"), and the application was remitted to the hearing officer.

**Copyright: computer-generated works.** CDPA s 178 defines a "computer-generated" work as one generated by computer in circumstances such that there is no human author of the work. Section 9(3): in the case of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work which is computer-generated, the author shall be taken to be the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken. Section 12(7): copyright in a computer-generated work expires 50 years from the end of the calendar year in which it was made. The paternity and integrity moral rights do not apply to computer-generated works (ss 79(2)(c), 81(2)). Questions: (i) what is a "computer-generated" work, as against a work created by a human using a software tool (the latter has a human author and ordinary rules apply; in the *Nova Productions v Mazooma* litigation the player of a video game was held not to be the author of the screen images, the programmer was); (ii) who undertakes the "arrangements necessary": the developer of the AI, the person entering prompts, the training-data provider, or a combination; (iii) the originality requirement: after *Infopaq* and *THJ Systems v Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354, originality is the author's own intellectual creation, which presupposes a human author making free and creative choices, so it is doubtful that a work with no human author can meet it; *THJ* did not address the point, because authorship under s 9(3) was not in issue on appeal. In practice, a large part of the debate is whether s 9(3) offers any protection for AI output, or whether such output falls into the public domain, or whether a human prompting with sufficient creative control is the author of the output under s 9(1).

**Copyright: training data and text and data mining.** Training a generative model usually involves copying works, which is a restricted act (s 17) unless licensed or within an exception. The only UK exception directed at computational analysis is s 29A (inserted in 2014): copying by a person with lawful access to the work for the sole purpose of research for a non-commercial purpose, with sufficient acknowledgement; the copy may not be transferred to others or used for another purpose; contract terms purporting to restrict copying permitted by the section are unenforceable (s 29A(5)). The section does not cover commercial training. The temporary copies exception (s 28A) is narrow. Territoriality matters: UK copyright is infringed only by acts in the UK; training done abroad is not an infringement under UK law (though importing or dealing in the resulting model might be argued to be secondary infringement). 

**Getty Images (US) Inc v Stability AI Ltd** [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch) (Joanna Smith J, 4 November 2025), the first UK judgment on generative AI and copyright. Getty sued Stability AI (developer of the Stable Diffusion image model) alleging that it had scraped millions of Getty's images to train the model, that the model's outputs reproduced Getty's works and sometimes Getty watermarks, and claims of copyright, database right and trade mark infringement and passing off. By the end of the trial (June 2025) Getty had abandoned its primary infringement claim based on training (there was no evidence that training took place in the UK), its claim based on outputs, and its database right claim. The main points decided: (1) *Secondary infringement of copyright (importing or dealing with an "infringing copy", CDPA ss 22-23, 27):* the judge held that an intangible model can be an "article" for these purposes, but that the Stable Diffusion model weights are not an "infringing copy" because they do not store or reproduce the training images; an infringing copy must itself be a copy of the work (or made by an act which would infringe), so the claim failed. (2) *Trade marks:* Getty succeeded only to a very limited extent: iStock watermarks generated by users of early versions (v1.x, accessed through Stability's own DreamStudio and Developer Platform) infringed under s 10(1) and s 10(2) TMA 1994, and a Getty Images watermark generated by v2.1 infringed under s 10(2), each finding resting on specific examples; there was no evidence of UK users generating watermarks with the later SD XL and v1.6 models; the s 10(3) claim failed; and Stability bore no direct liability for the release of v1.x models via the CompVis GitHub and Hugging Face pages. (3) *Passing off:* the judge declined to address it. She described her trade mark findings as "both historic and extremely limited in scope". The judgment does not decide whether using copyright works to train a model in the UK would infringe. After a consequentials hearing, Joanna Smith J granted Getty permission to appeal the dismissal of the secondary infringement claim (order of 17 December 2025; [2025] EWHC 3343 (Ch)), as a pure question of statutory construction on which reasonable lawyers may differ, and refused Stability permission to appeal the trade mark findings. The Court of Appeal heard the appeal on 11 and 12 November 2026 and judgment had not been given by 24 November 2026, so the High Court decision is the current authority, subject to the appeal. (A raw interim judgment in this case on representative claims is in the knowledge base; the 4 November 2025 judgment is the trial judgment.)

**Government policy and legislation.**
- *Consultation (17 December 2024 to 25 February 2025):* "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence" proposed (as the Government's preferred option) a data mining exception for commercial AI training with a rights reservation (opt-out) mechanism, supported by transparency requirements and technical standards, and asked about computer-generated works under s 9(3) (including whether to remove protection) and about digital replicas. It attracted a very large number of responses from creative industries and AI developers, with creative sector bodies strongly opposed to an opt-out.
- *Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Royal Assent 19 June 2025):* after a campaign by creative industry peers during its passage, the Act did not contain AI copyright reforms but required the Government to publish, within nine months, an economic impact assessment (s 135) and a report on the use of copyright works in the development of AI systems (s 136), covering technical standards, transparency, licensing and enforcement, and a progress statement within six months (s 137).
- *House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee report (6 March 2026):* urged the Government to rule out a commercial text and data mining exception with an opt-out, to adopt a licensing-first approach, and to make training-data transparency a statutory duty.
- *Report and impact assessment (18 March 2026):* the Government's report said the consultation's preferred option, a broad commercial text and data mining exception with an opt-out, is no longer its preferred way forward; found the evidence on the economic effects limited and uncertain; said it will not introduce copyright reforms until confident they meet its objectives and proposed no intervention in the licensing market for now; committed to further work on transparency best practice, technical standards, enforcement and the monitoring of litigation and other jurisdictions; proposed removing protection for wholly computer-generated works while keeping protection for AI-assisted works with a human author; and said it is considering a new right covering digital replicas.
- No change to ss 9(3), 12(7) or 29A had been enacted at the date of writing.

## Key authorities
- *Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents* [2023] UKSC 49 (Supreme Court): inventors must be natural persons.
- *Getty Images (US) Inc v Stability AI Ltd* [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch): secondary infringement failed; limited trade mark success; appeal pending (heard 11-12 November 2026).
- *Emotional Perception AI v Comptroller-General* [2026] UKSC 3: *Aerotel* abandoned for the "any hardware" approach; a neural network claim involving hardware is not excluded as a program "as such".
- *THJ Systems Ltd v Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354: originality as the author's own intellectual creation (see the subsistence note).
- *Nova Productions v Mazooma Games* [2007] EWCA Civ 219 (CA): video game screens as artistic works; the player was not the author.
- CDPA ss 9(3), 12(7), 29A, 178.

## Applying it
For a question on AI output: (1) Is there a human author who made free and creative choices (prompts that specify composition, selection and arrangement, editing)? If so, ordinary copyright (s 9(1)) and that person is the author; (2) if no human author, s 9(3) may apply (the person undertaking the arrangements), though originality is uncertain; duration 50 years; no moral rights. (3) Ownership: contract terms of the AI service may allocate rights. For a question on training: (1) Where did the copying occur (UK or elsewhere)? (2) Is there a licence? (3) Does s 29A apply (non-commercial research, lawful access)? (4) If an AI company imports or provides a model, consider secondary infringement and the *Getty* first-instance reasoning (model weights are not infringing copies) but note the appeal. (5) Outputs that reproduce a substantial part of a work infringe as ordinary copying (*Designers Guild*, existing note). (6) Watermarks and brand reproduction: trade marks (s 10) and passing off. For an inventor question: a natural person must be named (*Thaler*). Everyday: "AI tools do not make you the owner of everything they output in the UK: the law is unclear, so check the tool's terms and keep records of your own creative input."

## Debates and reform
- *Whether to create an exception for training:* the argument that a broad text and data mining exception (as in the EU's DSM Directive art 4 with opt-out, and Japan and Singapore's broader provisions) is needed for UK AI investment, against the argument that creators should be remunerated and consent should be required; a licensing market is developing.
- *Opt-out versus opt-in and transparency:* technical feasibility of rights reservation, and the scope of transparency obligations about training data.
- *Section 9(3):* whether to repeal (as the 2024 consultation floated and the March 2026 report proposed for wholly computer-generated works), retain, or reform the provision on the basis that AI output without human creativity should not be protected; the UK, Ireland, New Zealand, India and some others have computer-generated work provisions, though most jurisdictions require human authorship.
- *Liability of model providers:* whether a model is an infringing copy (the *Getty* appeal), and the position of outputs.
- *AI inventorship and patentability:* whether the patent system should accommodate AI-generated inventions; the *Thaler* decision leaves it for Parliament.
