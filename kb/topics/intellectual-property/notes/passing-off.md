# Passing off - goodwill, misrepresentation, damage and get-up

## Overview
Passing off is a common law tort that protects the goodwill of a business against misrepresentation by others. There is no property in a name or get-up as such; the claimant must prove the "classical trinity": goodwill, misrepresentation (likely to deceive a substantial number of the claimant's customers or potential customers) and resulting damage or likely damage. It protects unregistered marks and get-up, and works alongside the registered trade mark system (and is itself a ground on which a later registration can be opposed or invalidated).

## The law
**The classical trinity.** In *Reckitt & Colman Products Ltd v Borden Inc* [1990] 1 WLR 491 (HL) ("Jif Lemon"), Lord Oliver restated the tort in three elements: (1) goodwill or reputation attached to the goods or services in the minds of the purchasing public, identified by a "mark, name, get-up" or other distinctive indicia; (2) a misrepresentation by the defendant to the public (whether or not intentional) leading or likely to lead the public to believe that goods or services offered by the defendant are those of the claimant; (3) damage, or a real likelihood of damage, to the claimant's goodwill by the erroneous belief caused by the misrepresentation. Lord Diplock's five-point formulation in *Erven Warnink BV v J Townend & Sons (Hull) Ltd* [1979] AC 731 (HL) ("Advocaat") is an earlier statement: (1) a misrepresentation (2) made by a trader in the course of trade (3) to prospective customers or ultimate consumers (4) which is calculated to injure the business or goodwill of another trader (in the sense that this is a reasonably foreseeable consequence) and (5) which causes actual damage to the claimant's business or goodwill or will probably do so. Lord Oliver's version is now the usual one.

**Goodwill.** Goodwill is "the attractive force which brings in custom" (Lord Macnaghten, *IRC v Muller & Co's Margarine Ltd* [1901] AC 217 (HL)). It must exist in the UK: *Starbucks (HK) Ltd v British Sky Broadcasting Group plc* [2015] UKSC 31 (Supreme Court): a foreign business with a reputation among UK residents who had never been customers did not have UK goodwill; goodwill requires customers in the jurisdiction, so mere reputation or recognition (for example among travellers or from advertising abroad) is not enough. The claimant (Hong Kong-based "Now TV" operator) had no UK customers, so Sky's NOW TV did not pass off. Goodwill must be more than trivial: the law of passing off does not protect goodwill of trivial extent (*Hart v Relentless Records Ltd* [2002] EWHC 1984 (Ch), Jacob J). Exceptionally, substantial pre-launch publicity may generate protectable goodwill before trading begins (*BBC v Talbot Motor Co* [1981] FSR 228). It may attach to a name, a descriptive name with secondary meaning, a get-up (packaging, shape, colours), a logo or slogan, a person's persona (see below), or a product shape. Descriptive names (such as "Greek yoghurt") may be protected where the claimant shows that the descriptive term has become distinctive of their goods only, or where the class of traders sharing the goodwill is identifiable (*Fage UK Ltd v Chobani UK Ltd* [2014] EWCA Civ 5 (CA): "Greek yoghurt" denoted yoghurt made in Greece and a class of traders; the claimants' goodwill in the product class was protected as an extended passing off claim).

**Misrepresentation.** The misrepresentation must be likely to deceive a substantial number of the claimant's customers or potential customers (not necessarily a majority; "more than de minimis"). It can be of origin (the goods are the claimant's), or of connection or association (the goods are licensed or endorsed by the claimant), or as to quality. Intention is not required but strengthens the inference; the court looks at the similarity of the names or get-ups, the market, the channels and the evidence of actual confusion (though actual confusion evidence is not necessary). Conditions such as differences in the labelling may not dispel the misrepresentation. In *Reckitt v Borden*, the defendant's plastic lemon-shaped container was a get-up associated with the claimant's JIF lemon juice; the trial judge found a misrepresentation, and the House of Lords upheld the finding on the evidence, even though the defendant's label carried its own name, because many customers would believe the products were the same or connected.

**Damage.** The claimant must show damage or likelihood of damage to goodwill, typically loss of sales (diversion of trade), loss of control over reputation (association with inferior goods), or erosion of distinctiveness and exclusivity (dilution of the goodwill's singularity). Damage to goodwill by erosion of distinctiveness was recognised in *Taittinger SA v Allbev Ltd* [1993] FSR 641 (CA) ("Elderflower Champagne"). In some cases, damage in the form of loss of the ability to license is enough (*Irvine v Talksport Ltd* [2002] EWHC 367 (Ch), [2003] EWCA Civ 423: false endorsement; a photograph of the Formula 1 driver Eddie Irvine was doctored to show him holding a radio bearing the station's name, suggesting he endorsed the station; he had goodwill in his image for endorsement purposes; the Court of Appeal increased the damages to £25,000, the fee he would reasonably have charged). *Harrods Ltd v Harrodian School Ltd* [1996] RPC 697 (CA): no passing off; the majority held that the public would not think Harrods was responsible for or connected with the school, so there was no relevant misrepresentation, and in any event no real likelihood of damage to Harrods' goodwill (Sir Michael Kerr dissented).

**Get-up and shape.** Product get-up (packaging, bottle shape, colour combinations) can be protected where the claimant shows goodwill in the get-up and a misrepresentation that goes beyond a similar product-category look: courts are reluctant to protect functional or common features of a product class. *Reckitt v Borden* (the Jif plastic lemon) is the classic get-up case. Look-alike products with different brand names can still pass off if the get-up as a whole is likely to deceive, but the claimant must show deception rather than mere resemblance, and courts are slow to give a monopoly over a product class look.

**Defences.** Lack of goodwill in the UK, no misrepresentation (differences in presentation; disclaimers rarely work if the misrepresentation remains likely), honest concurrent use (a share of goodwill where two traders have used a name honestly), delay and acquiescence, and descriptive use of one's own name in good faith (a person may use their own name honestly, but not if the use is calculated to deceive; *Parker-Knoll Ltd v Knoll International Ltd* [1962] RPC 265 (HL)).

**Remedies.** An injunction (sometimes qualified, for example by a requirement of a distinguishing label, where two traders share a name), damages (or an account of profits at the claimant's election, in line with the equitable relief), delivery up or destruction of passing-off goods, and, for company names registered opportunistically, a complaint to the Company Names Adjudicator. Interim relief is often decisive (*American Cyanamid* principles).

**Relationship with trade marks.** Passing off protects goodwill (UK trading reputation), not a registered monopoly; it covers unregistered signs and can be relied on as an earlier right against a later registration (s 5(4)(a) TMA 1994). Registered trade mark infringement does not require goodwill, and does not need proof of confusion in some cases. Where both are available, a claim is usually brought under both.

## Key authorities
- *Reckitt & Colman Products v Borden* [1990] 1 WLR 491 (HL): classical trinity, get-up.
- *Erven Warnink v Townend* [1979] AC 731 (HL): extended passing off (advocaat).
- *Starbucks (HK) v British Sky Broadcasting* [2015] UKSC 31 (Supreme Court): goodwill needs customers in the UK.
- *Fage UK v Chobani UK* [2014] EWCA Civ 5 (CA): descriptive names; extended passing off.
- *Harrods v Harrodian School* [1996] RPC 697 (CA): misrepresentation must be of a connection that matters to the public, and damage must be real; mere association is not enough.
- *Irvine v Talksport* [2003] EWCA Civ 423 (CA): false endorsement; damages.
- *Taittinger v Allbev* [1993] FSR 641 (CA): erosion of distinctiveness.
- *Parker-Knoll v Knoll International* [1962] RPC 265 (HL): use of own name.

## Applying it
For a problem question: (1) Who is the claimant, what business, and where is its trade? Prove goodwill in the UK and identify the sign (name, get-up, logo) on which it relies and its distinctiveness in the claimant's field (secondary meaning if descriptive). (2) What does the defendant do? Does the use represent, or would a substantial number of the claimant's customers believe, that the defendant's goods or services are the claimant's or connected with it? Consider similarity, field of activity, trade channels, price, and evidence of confusion. (3) Damage: diversion of sales, loss of control, dilution, injury through inferior goods, loss of licensing opportunity. (4) Defences. (5) Remedies. Also consider alternative claims: registered trade marks, copyright, and false advertising. Everyday: "If you're building a brand without a registered trade mark, passing off may still protect your name once you've built goodwill, but you must prove goodwill, confusion and damage, which is harder than relying on a registered mark."

## Debates and reform
- Whether the UK should recognise a distinct tort of unfair competition or misappropriation (rejected in English law; *Hodgkinson & Corby Ltd v Wards Mobility Services Ltd* [1994] 1 WLR 1564 (Ch, Jacob J) refused to recognise a general tort of copying or unfair competition and insisted on proof of deception); and the reliance on registration.
- The scope of extended passing off (champagne and "Spanish champagne"); the 2014 *Fage* decision and the potential for descriptive names to attract protection.
- Character merchandising and personality rights: the English courts do not recognise a general image right; *Irvine v Talksport* treats it as false endorsement; the *Fenty v Arcadia Group* [2015] EWCA Civ 3 (CA) decision (Rihanna T-shirt) held that sale of a T-shirt bearing her image was passing off because customers would believe she had authorised it.
- The territorial goodwill test after *Starbucks* and the digital economy: international brands without UK customers.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No major statutory change; check recent decisions applying *Starbucks* to online or app-based businesses.
