# Refusal of Treatment in Pregnancy - Court-Authorised Caesarean Sections

## Overview
Obstetric refusals test the principle that a capacitous adult may refuse treatment for any reason. Early cases suggested a "viable fetus" exception and authorised caesareans without properly considering capacity. The Court of Appeal in *Re MB* (1997) and *St George's v S* (1998) firmly rejected any such exception: a pregnant woman with capacity may refuse, even if she or the fetus dies, and the fetus has no separate legal interests before birth. The live problem now lies in capacity and best interests: in cases involving serious mental illness, the presumption of capacity is easily rebutted and "best interests" has been criticised as a route to enforcing compliance (Halliday; Walmsley).

## The law
1. **The Re T dictum (now discredited).** *Re T (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* [1992] EWCA Civ 18, Lord Donaldson MR: a capacitous adult's right to refuse is absolute; "the only possible qualification" is where the choice may lead to the death of a viable fetus.
2. **Early first-instance cases.**
   - *Re S (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment)* [1992/1993]: caesarean authorised with no consideration of the woman's capacity (see M Thomson, 'After Re S' (1994) 2 Med Law Rev 127, optional).
   - *Tameside & Glossop Acute Services Unit v CH* [1996] 1 FLR 762: woman held to lack capacity to refuse a caesarean.
3. **The Court of Appeal's intervention (pre-MCA).**
   - *Re MB (Caesarean Section)* [1997] EWCA Civ 1361 / [1997] 2 FLR 426: MB temporarily lacked capacity because of needle phobia, so the caesarean was in her best interests. But the court affirmed that a competent woman may, for religious reasons, other reasons or no reason, refuse intervention even if the consequence is death or serious handicap of the child or her own death. The fetus up to birth has no separate interests that can be weighed, and the court has no jurisdiction to declare intervention lawful to protect the unborn child (Butler-Sloss LJ). Also set the test: incapacity only where an impairment or disturbance of mental functioning makes the person unable to decide (Re C test).
   - *St George's Healthcare NHS Trust v S* [1998] 3 All ER 673 / [1999] Fam 26: S was detained under the Mental Health Act and a caesarean was performed against her will. Held unlawful: the grounds for admission and for dispensing with consent were not made out ("victory after the event"). Judge LJ: the unborn child's need for medical help does not prevail over her rights; she is entitled not to be forced to submit to bodily invasion whether her life or the child's depends on it; her right is not reduced because her decision may seem morally repugnant.
4. **Post-St George's and post-MCA cases.** the materials's point: the presumption of capacity is easily rebutted where a woman has serious mental illness and her decision might harm the fetus or child.
   - *Bolton Hospitals Trust v O* [2002] EWHC 2871 (Fam).
   - *Re AA (Mental Capacity: Enforced Caesarean)* [2012] EWHC 4378 (COP) (Mostyn J): urgent application at 39 weeks for a woman detained under the Mental Health Act with bipolar disorder, who had had two previous elective caesareans. The Trust's main concern was uterine rupture in vaginal birth (risk put at up to 1%, treated as "significant"). Declared that she lacked capacity regarding the birth and that a caesarean (with reasonable force if necessary) was in her best interests, including her "mental health best interests" in a live, healthy birth. Mostyn J saw himself applying the conventional Re MB principles. The case attracted media controversy and comment on transparency (Sir James Munby P in later reporting-restrictions proceedings).
   - *Shrewsbury and Telford Hospital NHS Trust v T* [2023] EWCOP 20: the judge **refused** an anticipatory declaration on obstetric care; there was no more than a "small risk" that T might lose capacity, which was insufficient. Halliday (2024, as quoted on the slides) notes T was compliant: she agreed with clinical advice and had agreed to be induced.
5. **Applying the MCA today.** Capacity is assessed under ss.1-3 (presumption, practicable steps, unwise decisions; functional then causal test per JB). If P lacks capacity and has no valid advance refusal, a caesarean may be authorised under s.5/s.4 best interests or by declaration under s.15. The fetus's interests are not legally weighed in their own right; but courts have counted the *woman's* interest in a healthy birth within her best interests (Re AA).

## Key authorities
- *Re T* [1992] EWCA Civ 18: viable fetus qualification (obiter, later rejected).
- *Re S* [1993] / [1992] All ER 649: authorised without capacity analysis; criticised.
- *Tameside & Glossop v CH* [1996] 1 FLR 762: incapacity finding.
- *Re MB* [1997] EWCA Civ 1361: competent woman's absolute right; fetus has no separate interests; no jurisdiction to protect unborn child; temporary incapacity through phobia.
- *St George's v S* [1998] 3 All ER 673; [1999] Fam 26: forced caesarean unlawful; autonomy not diminished by moral repugnance.
- *Bolton Hospitals v O* [2002] EWHC 2871 (Fam).
- *Re AA* [2012] EWHC 4378 (COP): post-MCA enforced caesarean; best interests incl. mental health benefit of live birth.
- *Shrewsbury and Telford v T* [2023] EWCOP 20: anticipatory declaration refused.
- Cases discussed by Halliday (2023): *X & Y v Ms A* [2021] EWCOP 17 (Cohen J found incapacity and ordered planned caesarean for breech presentation, reading her general wish for her and the baby to be safe as her "true" wish); *University Hospitals Dorset v Miss K* [2021] EWCOP 40 (Lieven J; reliance on earlier "consent" given without knowing the baby would be removed); *Guys and St Thomas' v R* [2020] EWCOP 4 (Hayden J granted an anticipatory, contingent declaration for a woman who *had* capacity and said a caesarean was the last thing she wanted).

## Debate and critique
- **Pregnancy exceptionalism (Halliday).** Samantha Halliday ('Pregnancy and severe mental illness: Birth choices, best interests and the untapped potential of advance decisions' (2023) Medical Law International 14; and *Autonomy and Pregnancy* (Routledge, 2016)) argues that obstetric cases give women's wishes minimal weight and treat best interests largely as a conduit to a healthy baby, in contrast to the patient-centred approach of Aintree and Briggs. She is especially critical of the anticipatory declaration in *GSTT v R*, which she says threatens to subvert the ideology of the MCA, and of R lacking Official Solicitor representation. Her conclusion: for women with serious mental illness, an incapacity finding becomes a "choke-hold" rather than a protective embrace.
- **Halliday's reform proposal: advance decisions.** Pregnancy suits advance decisions (ss.24-26 MCA) because it is time-limited and scenarios are predictable. To make them robust: obtain a formal capacity assessment when drafting (cf. *Re E (Anorexia)*, where doubt over capacity undermined an AD); be very specific (e.g. refusing a caesarean even if needed to avert harm or death to herself or the fetus); comply with the enhanced formalities (writing, signature, witness) even if not strictly required. The drafting process also fosters dialogue with clinicians.
- **Walmsley on Re AA.** Emma Walmsley ('Mama mia! Serious shortcomings with another "(en)forced" caesarean section case' (2015) 23(1) Med L Rev 135) argues neither the Trust nor the court properly engaged with the functional elements of capacity or the participation principle; the woman's past wishes (e.g. trauma from previous caesareans) were not considered; best interests focused on the 1% medical risk and was used as a "tool for maternal compliance"; and her interests ceased to matter after birth (rapid discharge). Lessons: transparency, robust s.4 engagement including post-birth care, strict application of the capacity provisions; she too supports advance directives for women with fluctuating capacity.
- **Is a fetal-protection exception defensible?** A full-status view (Finnis/Keown) might support protecting a viable fetus; rights-based views (e.g. Thomson's argument that even a person cannot use another's body without consent) support St George's. The law's position that the fetus has no legal personality before birth (*Paton v BPAS*) underpins Re MB.
- **Capacity as the battleground.** Since the law cannot override a capacitous refusal, the pressure moves to findings of incapacity. Brazier & Cave warn generally against confusing capacity with the gravity of the decision.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the woman an adult with capacity? Presume capacity; apply s.3 then s.2 (JB order); consider temporary factors (pain, fear, phobia: Re MB) and mental illness (not automatically incapacity: Re C).
2. If capacity: her refusal binds, even if she or the fetus will die (Re MB; St George's). Treating her is battery and assault.
3. If no capacity now but there is a valid and applicable **advance decision** refusing a caesarean, it binds (s.26(1)); check formalities and specificity.
4. If no capacity and no AD: s.5 GLA / s.4 best interests: weigh her past and present wishes and beliefs (s.4(6)), consultation (s.4(7)), participation (s.4(4)), less restrictive options (s.1(6)). The fetus has no independent legal interests, but courts have treated a healthy birth as part of her best interests (Re AA).
5. **Anticipatory declarations** where she currently has capacity are controversial; Shrewsbury v T refused one where the risk of losing capacity was small.
6. **Mental Health Act detention** does not authorise treatment unrelated to the mental disorder (St George's) – mental health law is otherwise outside this module.
7. Traps: citing Re T's viable fetus qualification as current law; relying on Re S; ignoring the woman's post-birth interests.
