# Medical Ethics (moral theories, dignity and their role in medical law)

## Overview
Medical ethics is the ethical toolkit used throughout. Every later area (consent, end of life, transplantation, abortion, reproductive medicine) is "ethically controversial", and essays are expected to take and defend a viewpoint. Commentary sorts moral positions into five groups (utilitarian, duty-based, rights-based, virtue-based and mixed) and shows how each handles moral status, consent to harm and human dignity. A strong essay uses these positions to explain *why* a rule is defensible or needs reform, rather than just describing the law.

## The law
This topic is not about legal rules as such, but several framing points are examinable:

1. **Medical ethics is not professional ethics.** Bodies such as the General Medical Council (GMC) and the Nursing and Midwifery Council issue guidance on professional conduct. That guidance does not settle what is ethically defensible: the question "is this right?" stays open even where guidance answers "is this permitted by the regulator?" .
2. **Meaning of "ethics" and "morality".** Here the two words are used interchangeably for positions on what is permissible and what duties are owed to *others* (other-regarding duties).
3. **Moral relativism v moral objectivism.**
   - *Relativism (subjectivism)*: basic moral views cannot be true or rational; all values are ultimately opinion. This is a philosophical claim, and it is not proved by the mere fact that people disagree.
   - *Objectivism (universalism)*: basic moral views can be true or rationally required. This does not mean moral questions are easy or undisputed.
   - the summary point: if relativism were right, nothing would be objectively immoral, so the critique of any law would collapse into preference.
4. **Who decides?** Commentary ends with the point that ethical decisions must be made by *someone*, within the limits of their expertise. This links to recurring themes: should doctors, judges, Parliament, patients or parents decide? (e.g. Bell v Tavistock on doctors deciding Gillick competence; Conway on Parliament deciding assisted suicide; s.13(5) HFEA 1990 leaving welfare-of-the-child judgments to clinicians).

## Key authorities (theorists and positions)
The five groups below are each tied to a "value statement".

**(1) Utilitarianism** – value statement: "we ought to maximise happiness in the population as a whole".
- We must seek the best balance of utility. Classical utilitarians maximise pleasure over pain; preference utilitarians maximise overall satisfaction of preferences.
- To count at all you must be sentient. No one counts for more than one, but the interests of different individuals *can be added together* (aggregation).
- Jeremy Bentham: the greatest happiness of the greatest number is "the foundation of morals and legislation".
- Peter Singer is the main modern example and has written on every topic (e.g. no status for the early fetus until it can feel pain).

**(2) Duty-based theories** – value statement: "we ought to respect the most important interests of individuals".
- We have duties to protect individuals' important interests. Those interests are *objective*, so they do not always track what the person wants.
- The person to whom the duty is owed cannot automatically waive its benefit, so one cannot consent to all harms.
- Some duty-based views hold that humans are equal from conception. Interests cannot be aggregated across people.
- Immanuel Kant (Groundwork): treat others never merely as a means but always also as an end.
- John Keown: one must never intentionally kill an innocent human being, even with consent; John Finnis takes a similar line. Both write against abortion and voluntary end-of-life decisions (the "sanctity of life" position).

**(3) Rights-based theories** – value statement: respect individuals' most important interests "unless an individual freely chooses to allow harm to themselves".
- We hold rights (entitlements with *waivable* benefits) that impose duties on others. Because the right-holder can waive the benefit, one can consent to being harmed.
- This is the key contrast with duty-based theories: all duties owed to an individual are subject to that individual's will.
- Not all humans are equal on this view: those able to waive duties (agents) count for more. Interests cannot be aggregated.
- Alan Gewirth: all agents have rights to freedom and well-being, so a range of self-harming actions "ranging from smoking to suicide" must not be interfered with. Other examples given: Shaun Pattinson (who has written on every topic from this perspective) and Mary Anne Warren.

**(4) Virtue ethics** – value statement: "what matters is whether an individual's character is virtuous".
- The other theories are wrong to focus on conduct; what matters is whether character and motive are virtuous.
- Origins in Aristotle (the human good is the soul's activity expressing virtue); revived by Elizabeth Anscombe; adopted by many contemporary feminist writers (Rosalind Hursthouse is the example given).
- Brazier & Cave (ch 4) add that a virtue ethicist may base decisions on death or abortion on compassion rather than autonomy, and link this to feminist critiques and Carol Gilligan's "ethic of care", which stresses relationships and context over abstract principles.

**(5) Mixed theories** – value statement: "none of the above".
- Any position whose criteria go beyond the tenets of a single camp. A miscellaneous group, and not all mixed approaches are coherent.
- Example: Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, with four principles: autonomy (self-determination), non-maleficence (non-harm), beneficence (aid) and justice.
- Brazier & Cave (ch 4) explain these principles further: autonomy has become the dominant principle in legal debate (it underpins the law of consent); beneficence risks shading into paternalism but, properly understood, requires respect for the patient's own view of their good; non-maleficence cannot be absolute because medicine often involves harm (the conjoined twins case is used as an illustration); justice raises resource allocation questions (QALYs, NICE). Critics (e.g. Callaghan) object to the excessive deference given to autonomy, and Onora O'Neill argues autonomy has been distorted into "what I want".

**Human dignity**
- Dignity can mean *comportment* (behaving with dignity).
- It can be an empty rhetorical "debate stopper" (the utilitarian view is that it has no deeper function), or it can name the ground for possessing intrinsic status and being owed duties (as in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights).
- Deryck Beyleveld and Roger Brownsword (*Human Dignity in Bioethics and Biolaw*) distinguish:
  - **dignity as empowerment**: dignity lies in the capacity for autonomous choice, limited only by the rights of others (the rights-based position);
  - **dignity as constraint**: dignity captures our most important interests whatever we choose (a duty-based or mixed position).
- This distinction reappears in the transplantation topic: principled objections to paying organ providers draw on dignity as constraint, while dignity as empowerment points the other way.

## Debate and critique
**How the theories map onto the topics**:
- *Moral status* (end of life, abortion, embryos). Three positions recur: full status, limited status (fixed or proportional) and no status. Finnis and Keown: full status from conception (duty-based). Singer: no status until the fetus can feel pain, then a fixed limited status (utilitarian). Warren: no status (rights-based). Pattinson: proportional status (rights-based). For people with disorders of consciousness end-of-life discussion again uses full / no / limited status.
- *Voluntary end-of-life decisions*. Utilitarianism (e.g. Singer) and rights-based theories are, at first sight, supportive; virtue theory and duty-based theory (e.g. Keown's sanctity of life) are less so. The end-of-life material also names vitalism (life must always be preserved) and the sanctity of life position, which permits withdrawing worthless treatment but never intentional killing.
- *Organ acquisition*. Many theories can support an opt-out system: a utilitarian may favour strict opt-out or even conscription; virtue ethics needs room for virtuous giving, so opposes conscription; for rights and duty theories, the answer turns on the weight given to positive duties to assist.
- *Assisted reproduction*. Why consider the welfare of a future child whose existence depends on treatment? Utilitarians count the child's welfare in the utility balance; virtue theory looks for non-virtuous motives; rights and duty theories ask whether there is an intention to violate the future child's rights, while accepting there can be no right not to be born (Parfit's non-identity problem).
- *Conjoined twins (Re A)*. Analysis is invited of the judges' reasoning in utilitarian, rights-based and duty-based terms.

**Critical points for essays**
- *Relativism as a trap*: an essay arguing "it is all opinion" undercuts any claim that the law ought to change. the line is that disagreement alone does not establish relativism.
- *Aggregation*: the sharpest divide is between utilitarianism (which adds interests together) and duty/rights theories (which do not). This explains, for instance, why a utilitarian may accept conscription of organs but a rights theorist would not without a strong positive duty.
- *Waiver of benefit*: the rights/duty divide turns on whether the person can consent to harm. This underlies debates on assisted dying, the public policy limits on consent (R v Brown), payment for organs and living donation.
- *Dignity's ambiguity*: dignity is invoked on both sides of most debates (e.g. "dignity in dying" and dignity as a reason to forbid killing). Beyleveld and Brownsword's distinction is the tool to expose which sense is being used.
- *Principlism under attack*: Brazier & Cave report criticism of the four principles as imperialist, inconsistent or inadequate, though they remain useful as a framework for "time-pressed clinicians" (Huxtable) and students.
- *Autonomy and relational autonomy*: Brazier & Cave note a shift toward relational models of autonomy and some resurgence of soft and hard paternalism ("nudges").

**Lesson**: keep to the question, state the viewpoint at the start and keep it throughout; cover fewer issues in more depth rather than many superficially.

## Applying it to a problem question
Ethics rarely decides a problem question, which asks for legal advice, but it is central to essays. Checklist:
1. **State a position early.** Decide which theory (or which combination) your argument rests on and say so in the introduction.
2. **Identify the moral issue**: is it about moral status (fetus, embryo, PDOC patient), consent to self-harm, aggregation of interests, or who should decide?
3. **Test the law against the theory**: e.g. does the law's treatment of the embryo match a proportional status view (as is suggested for the HFEA 1990)? Does the ban on assisted suicide reflect a duty-based view that one cannot waive protection of life?
4. **Anticipate the opposing theory** and answer it (e.g. answer a slippery slope argument; answer the utilitarian case for conscription).
5. **Use dignity carefully**: specify whether you mean empowerment or constraint.
6. **Do not confuse GMC guidance with ethics**: guidance shows what the profession expects, not what is right.
7. **Trap**: do not treat "different people disagree" as proof that morality is relative.
