# Re R (A Minor) (Wardship: Consent to Treatment) [1992] Fam 11; [1991] 4 All ER 177 (CA) (case note)

> Written from Lowe and Juss (1993) 56 MLR 865, which discusses Re R alongside Re W. The judgment itself is not held.
> (The article's footnote gives the report as "[1992] Fam Law 11"; the Family Division report is [1992] Fam 11.)

## Overview
Court of Appeal (leading judgment Lord Donaldson MR). A **15-year-old ward of court** refused anti-psychotic medication; the
court authorised it. Source of the "**keyholder**" analogy and of the first (obiter) statements that a competent minor's
**refusal can be overridden**.

## Facts
R, 15, had suffered emotional abuse, became disturbed and suicidal, and was admitted to an adolescent psychiatric unit where
she was given drugs. During a **lucid period** she said she would refuse the treatment. The local authority, which had made
her a ward, sought authority to continue it.

## Held
- R was held **not to be Gillick-competent**: her capacity **fluctuated** with her mental illness, so a lucid interval did
  not make her competent to refuse (Lowe and Juss call this ruling "perhaps controversial").
- In any event, the **wardship court** can override a ward's refusal (and consent) in the ward's best interests.
- **Obiter (Lord Donaldson):** consent is "a key which unlocks the door" and **more than one person** may hold a key (the
  competent child, a parent, the court), so a Gillick-competent child's refusal does not prevent treatment if another key-holder
  consents. He also said, obiter, that FLRA 1969 s 8 does not give 16–17-year-olds exclusive rights to consent.
- No doctor can be required to treat a child against their clinical judgment.

## Debate and critique
- The obiter remarks drew heavy criticism (Bainham; Thornton, 'Multiple Keyholders'; Kennedy; Brazier; Douglas, 'The Retreat
  from Gillick') as inconsistent with Lord Scarman in Gillick. In **Re W** Lord Donaldson dropped "keyholder" ("keys can lock as
  well as unlock") for the "flak jacket", but the Court of Appeal confirmed the substance.
- Lowe and Juss read Re R, Re E and Re W together as two grounds for overriding a child's refusal: (1) **lack of capacity**
  (refusal carries no weight); (2) where capable, the child's **preference is balanced against the harm** of respecting it.

## Applying it to a problem question
Fluctuating capacity: assess competence across the treatment period, not only at a lucid moment. Even a competent minor's
refusal may be overridden by the court (and, on the keyholder or flak-jacket view, by a person with parental responsibility)
where treatment is in their best interests.
