# Transplantation 1 - The Human Tissue Act 2004 Framework and Deceased Donation (Opt-Out and Deemed Consent)

## Overview
Demand for organs far exceeds supply: NHS Blood and Transplant (NHSBT) reports 4,583 solid organ transplants in 2024/25 with 11,979 people on waiting lists. The Human Tissue Act 2004 (HTA 2004) requires "appropriate consent" for removal, storage and use of human material for transplantation. Originally an opt-in system, it had an opt-out ("deemed consent") system grafted on for England by the Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act 2019 (in force May 2020). This note covers the background, the Act's structure and the hierarchy of consent for deceased adults and children.
## The law
**1. Background and context.**
- Reasons for the supply gap: ageing population, improved road safety, and the limited lifespan of transplanted organs (deceased donor kidney about 15-20 years; living donor kidney about 20-25 years); the pandemic also had an impact.
- Alternatives (only temporary or developing): artificial organs (dialysis, ventricular assist devices), tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, xenotransplantation (e.g. a genetically modified pig heart in 2022).
- Deceased donation covers life-saving organs (kidney, liver, heart, lung, pancreas, bowel) and non-vital organs and tissues (hand, face, penis, leg, larynx, womb).
- 3.1 million have recorded a decision on the NHS Organ Donor Register (ODR) (2.7m opt-outs per the outline).
- **Death**: verified by examination; for ventilated patients by brainstem testing. Know the distinction between **donation after brainstem death (DBD)** and **donation after circulatory death (DCD)** (2024-25: 727 DCD, 676 DBD donors). Both are legally recognised; diagnosis according to the Academy of Medical Royal Colleges Code is accepted as legal death, and in cases of conflict a declaration should be sought: *Re M (Declaration of Death of Child)* [2020] EWCA Civ 164.
- **Origins of the Act**: scandals of organ and tissue retention without consent at Bristol Royal Infirmary and Alder Hey; hence a consent-based system.
- Don't forget the general law: transplanting into a recipient and removing from a living donor need consent or another lawful justification to avoid trespass and assault.

**2. Structure of HTA 2004.** Supplemented by secondary legislation, Human Tissue Authority (HTA) Codes of Practice (ss.26, 28, 29; relevant ones: **Code A** (guiding principles and consent), **Code F** (Part 1 living organ donation; Part 2 deceased organ and tissue donation), **Code G** (bone marrow and peripheral blood stem cells)), and the common law and MCA 2005. Four material types matter:
- **"relevant material"** (s.53): material consisting of or including human cells, except gametes and embryos, hair and nail from a living person, and material created outside the body – for s.1 appropriate consent;
- **"permitted material"** (s.3(9)) – for deemed consent;
- **"transplantable material"** (s.33(7) and regs) – for s.33 living donor restrictions;
- **"controlled material"** (s.32(8)-(10)) – for the s.32 commercial dealings ban.

**3. Deceased donors: s.1.** Appropriate consent is required for the **removal, storage and use** of relevant material from a deceased person for transplantation (s.1(1)(a)-(c), Sch 1 Pt 1). Offence: s.5(1) – acting without appropriate consent, unless D reasonably believes there is consent or consent is not required; penalty up to 3 years, a fine or both (s.5(7)).
- **s.43**: where a body lies in a hospital, nursing home or other institution, the person in control may retain it and take the minimum steps necessary to preserve it for transplantation while consent is sought (Code F Pt 2 [205]-[214]).

**4. Acquisition systems (theory).** *Opt-in* (removal only with explicit consent; narrow or wide depending on who may consent); *opt-out* (removal unless an appropriate objection; narrow or wide; formal or informal); *conscription* (removal regardless of consent or refusal).

**5. Appropriate consent for deceased adults: s.3(6)** – a **hierarchy**; a valid decision at one level prevents reliance on a lower one:
- (a) the deceased's own decision (consent or refusal) in force immediately before death;
- (b) if none, consent of a **nominated representative** appointed under s.4;
- (ba) if neither, and s.3(6A) applies, **deemed consent**;
- (c) if neither (a) nor (b), and s.3(6A) does not apply, consent of a person in a **qualifying relationship**.
- *Deceased's consent*: valid consent is established by the common law and capacity legislation (Code A [19]-[20]); the Act does not specify a format [57]; the ODR records wishes [60].
- *Nominated representatives* (s.4): appointment orally or in writing (s.4(3)); oral appointment before two witnesses present together (s.4(4)); written appointment signed by or at the direction of the person, in the presence of at least one witness, or in a valid will (s.4(5)); if several are appointed, one suffices unless the appointment says otherwise (s.4(6)).

**6. Deemed consent.** s.3(6A): applies to removal, storage and use of **permitted material** from a deceased adult for transplantation, unless the body is that of an **excepted adult**. s.3(6B): consent is not deemed if a person in a qualifying relationship provides information that would lead a reasonable person to conclude that the deceased would not have consented.
- *Permitted material* (s.3(9)): relevant material unless excluded by regulations. The Human Tissue (Permitted Material: Exceptions) (England) Regulations 2020/521, reg 2(1) excludes 31 body parts, including arm, face, hand, leg, penis and uterus – so novel/non-vital transplants still need express consent.
- *Excepted adult* (s.3(9)): (a) not ordinarily resident in England for at least 12 months immediately before death; or (b) lacked capacity to understand the effect of s.3(6)(ba) for a "significant period" before death; s.3(10): long enough that a reasonable person would think deeming inappropriate; Code F Pt 2 [34]: as guidance, 12 months at the point of death.
- Code F Pt 2 [92]: where consent could be deemed but there is no family to establish the last known decision, donation should not proceed.

**7. Qualifying relationships (s.27(4))**, ranked: (a) spouse, civil partner or partner; (b) parent or child; (c) brother or sister; (d) grandparent or grandchild; (e) child of a person in (c) (niece/nephew); (f) stepfather or stepmother; (g) half-brother or half-sister; (h) friend of long standing. s.27(5): those in the same paragraph rank equally; s.27(7): consent of one suffices (Code A [30]-[39]).

**8. Family objections.** "The existence of consent permits an activity to proceed, but does not mandate that it must" (Code A [21]). Relatives have no legal right to overrule appropriate consent, but their views should be discussed sensitively and always taken into account (Code A [33]-[34]; Code F Pt 2 [89]-[90], [117]). Where equally ranked relatives disagree, explain that one consent suffices under the Act (Code A [36]).

**9. Deceased children: s.2(7).** (a) the child's own decision in force before death; otherwise (b)(i) consent of a person with parental responsibility immediately before death, or (ii) if none, a person in a qualifying relationship. Hierarchical; **no deemed consent** for children. Code A [91]-[92]: it may be advisable to establish with those with parental responsibility whether the child was competent; discuss fully with relatives and consider the child's age and understanding when the wish was stated.

**10. UK opt-out laws**: Wales – Human Transplantation (Wales) Act 2013 (Dec 2015); England – Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act 2019 (May 2020); Scotland – Human Tissue (Authorisation) (Scotland) Act 2019 (March 2021); Northern Ireland – Organ and Tissue Donation (Deemed Consent) Act (NI) 2022 (June 2023). The Government estimated about 100 extra deceased donors a year; deceased donor numbers: 1,600 (18-19), 1,180 (20-21), 1,600 (21-22), 1,429 (22-23), 1,510 (23-24), 1,403 (24-25).

## Debate and critique
- **Moral theories and opt-out.** a view: the four main camps are prima facie inclined to opt-out over opt-in. A utilitarian may favour strict opt-out or conscription because saving lives outweighs the wishes of the dead or relatives; virtue ethics requires room for virtuous giving, so rejects conscription; for rights- and duty-based theories it turns on the weight of positive duties to assist – a positive right to life may outweigh the rights infringed by posthumous removal.
- **Family veto in practice.** Where the deceased was on the ODR only about 10% of families refused, compared with a general relative refusal rate of about 40%. The system formally prioritises the deceased's decision but relatives are consulted, and any information suggesting objection triggers opt-out – a "soft" opt-out. Is "deemed consent" really consent? (It is imputed consent – a fiction.)
- **Parsons and Moorlock (2020) 'A global pandemic is not a good time to introduce "opt-out" for organ donation' Med Law Int.** Three ethical reasons the change should have been postponed: COVID-19 headlines would prevent awareness of the change, undermining the autonomy of those who did not wish to donate; low transplant activity would make the impact hard to measure; and trust could be damaged by controversy over DNACPR decisions and allocation of intensive care. Note donor numbers fell to 1,180 in 2020-21.
- **Brown (2019) 'Organ donor registration in the UK: the need for informed consent for ante-mortem interventions' Med Law Int.** Argues a specific consent model for ante-mortem donor optimisation procedures is needed, both to serve the best interests of registrants once incapacitated and to meet NHSBT's obligations under the law on informed consent; NHSBT owes a duty of care to ODR registrants.
- **Ethnicity disparities.** NHSBT publishes a separate annual ethnicity report (2023/24) – relevant to equitable access and consent rates.
- **Exclusions.** Excluding non-vital body parts (face, hands, uterus) from deemed consent reflects their symbolic and identity significance.

## Applying it to a problem question
(the materials: Connie, who briefly regained consciousness before death and asked doctors to "do everything to save Ant".)
1. Preservation: s.43 allows minimal steps to preserve the body while consent is clarified.
2. Is there a decision of the deceased (s.3(6)(a))? Did Connie have capacity when she spoke? Does "do everything to save Ant" amount to a consent to donation at all, and is it conditional/directed (see allocation note)? Was there an ODR registration?
3. If no decision: nominated representative (s.4 formalities)?
4. If none: deemed consent (s.3(6)(ba)) – Connie an adult, ordinarily resident in England for 12 months, not lacking capacity for a significant period; permitted material (kidney is not excluded); unless a qualifying relative provides information that she would not have consented (s.3(6B)).
5. If deemed consent does not apply: qualifying relatives in rank order (s.27(4)) – her sons Ant (in a coma) and Bert (fluctuating capacity) are in category (b); one consent suffices (s.27(7)); a relative with capacity must give it.
6. Even with appropriate consent, practice: discuss sensitively with family; consent permits but does not mandate (Code A [21]); no family to consult → do not proceed on deemed consent (Code F [92]).
7. Allocation to Ant: separate question (requested allocation policy).
8. Traps: applying deemed consent to a child; forgetting that removal without appropriate consent is an offence under s.5; ignoring the hierarchy.
