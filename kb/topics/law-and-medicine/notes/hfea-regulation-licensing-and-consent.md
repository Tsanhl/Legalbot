# Reproductive Medicine 1 - The HFE Act Regulatory System, Licensing and Consent to Use of Gametes and Embryos (Evans)

## Overview
This topic concerns assisted reproduction, especially **in vitro fertilisation (IVF)**. Following the Warnock Report (1984), the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 established a licensing regime overseen by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA). The 1990 Act was significantly amended by the HFE Act 2008; except for legal parenthood (now in the 2008 Act itself), the law is in the **1990 Act as amended**. This note covers the context, the regulatory structure, licensing and the consent regime in Schedule 3, including *Evans* (withdrawal of a former partner's consent to the use of embryos). Surrogacy is outside the syllabus.

## The law
**1. Context.**
- At least a quarter of heterosexual couples experience infertility and at least 1 in 6 seek specialist advice. NICE (2017) advises women with unexplained infertility having regular unprotected intercourse to try for at least 1 year before investigations and 2 years before IVF.
- *Involuntary childlessness* (the physical inability to fulfil the desire to have a genetically related child) is wider than infertility and can be as distressing as grief.
- Types of assisted reproduction: medical advice, corrective surgery, fertility drugs, artificial insemination, surrogacy (full/gestational and partial/traditional), IVF, ICSI, GIFT, mitochondrial replacement; possible future techniques (cloning, parthenogenesis, artificial gametes, ectogestation).
- IVF: first birth Louise Brown (July 1978; Steptoe and Edwards). Over 13 million babies worldwide; 20,700 in the UK in 2023; live birth rate per fresh embryo transferred 31% overall (34% aged 18-34; 9% aged 43-44); 27% NHS-funded in 2023.
- Examples discussed: Mandy Allwood's multiple pregnancy after fertility drugs (1996); a couple who spent £80,000 on 15 rounds of IVF.

**2. The HFEA.** Independent regulatory and licensing authority; maintains registers (ss.31, 31A, 31B); can issue mandatory directions (ss.23-24); must maintain a Code of Practice (ss.25-26) – 9th edn, last revised (v9.4) Oct 2023.

**3. Three regulatory responses.**
- **Outright prohibition** – e.g. s.3(2) (placing in a woman an embryo or gametes other than "permitted" ones), and s.3(3): a licence cannot authorise keeping or using an embryo after the appearance of the primitive streak (taken to be no later than 14 days from when creation began, ignoring storage: s.3(4)), placing an embryo in an animal, or uses prohibited by regulations.
- **Prohibition subject to a licence only**: creation of an embryo outside the body (ss.3(1), 1(2)); storage or use of embryos; storage of gametes (s.4(1)(a)); use of gametes in treatment services except "partner-donated sperm which has been neither processed nor stored" (s.4(1)(b)); creating, keeping and using human admixed embryos and mixing human and animal gametes (s.4A(2)).
- **Prohibition subject to a licence or third party agreement**: keeping or processing without storage of an embryo for human application (s.3(1A)(b)); procurement or distribution of embryos for human application (s.3(1B)); procurement, testing, processing or distribution of gametes for human application (s.4(1A)).
- **Criminal offences** (s.41): prosecution requires the **consent of the DPP** (s.42) – compare s.2(4) Suicide Act.

**4. Licences.** Four types: treatment; non-medical fertility services; storage; research. What needs a licence: creation, storage, use, procurement or distribution of embryos outside the body; storage of gametes; use of gametes other than unprocessed, unstored partner-donated sperm. Therefore medical advice, corrective surgery and fertility drugs are **not** licensed.
- *Partner-donated sperm*: where donor and recipient "declare that they have an intimate physical relationship" (s.1(5)). *Processing*: "any operation involved in their preparation, manipulation or packaging" (s.2(1)). The HFEA website says a licence is needed for IUI, ICI and any insemination where processing occurs.

**5. Consent (Schedule 3).**
- Exception for "basic partner treatment services" (s.12(1)(c), s.1(2)).
- Consent must be in writing and signed (para 1(1)) and not withdrawn ("effective consent", para 3 per slides).
- Must specify the purposes for which an embryo may be used (para 2(1)) and, for embryos and gametes, the maximum storage period and what is to be done if the person dies or loses capacity (para 2(2)).
- May be varied or withdrawn by notice (para 4(1)); but consent to use of an embryo cannot be varied or withdrawn once the embryo has been "used" for treatment, training or research (para 4(2)).
- **"Cooling off" period**: 12 months where one gamete provider withdraws consent to storage (Sch 3 para 4A).
- **Counselling**: a "suitable opportunity to receive proper counselling" must be offered before treatment using donor gametes, donor embryos or in vitro embryos (s.13(6), Sch 3ZA Pt 1), and before consenting to parenthood under ss.37 or 44 of the 2008 Act (s.13(6A), Sch 3ZA Pt 2); also for storage of gametes or embryos (Sch 3 para 3).

**6. Evans.**
- *Evans v Amicus Healthcare* [2003] EWHC 2161 (Fam); [2004] EWCA Civ 727, [2005] Fam 1: Natalie Evans, diagnosed with ovarian tumours, had embryos created with her then partner Howard Johnston before her ovaries were removed. After they separated, he withdrew consent to continued storage and use. Held she could not prevent this: the embryos had not been "used" until the "final stage" (transfer to the body) [103]; while frozen they were not used, so withdrawal under Sch 3 was effective.
- *Evans v United Kingdom* (No 6339/05) (2008) 46 EHRR 34 (Grand Chamber, 10 April 2007): Art 8(1) was engaged (undisputed), but by 13:4 there was no violation. A **wide margin of appreciation** applied because there was no European consensus on regulating IVF and IVF raises "sensitive moral and ethical issues against a background of fast-moving medical and scientific developments" [59]. The embryos had no Art 2 right to life, as domestic law did not recognise them as rights holders.
- Discussion question (lecture): the Act implicitly gives the embryo **proportional status**; should that have been given weight in Evans, given the ECtHR said the competing Art 8 rights of the gamete providers were fairly balanced by the legislation?

**7. U v Centre for Reproductive Medicine** [2002] EWCA Civ 565: Mr U's withdrawal of consent to posthumous use of his sperm, made after pressure from a nursing sister applying clinic policy, was effective; no undue influence (see voluntariness note).

**8. Conscientious objection**: the HFE Act s.38 confers a right similar to s.4 Abortion Act to refuse to participate in regulated activities (Brazier & Cave).

## Key authorities
- HFE Act 1990 (as amended) ss.1(2), 1(5), 2(1), 3(1)-(4), 3ZA, 4(1), 4(1A), 4A, 12(1)(c), 13(6), 13(6A), 23-26, 31, 38, 41, 42; Sch 3 paras 1-4A; Sch 3ZA.
- *Evans v Amicus Healthcare* [2004] EWCA Civ 727; *Evans v UK* (2008) 46 EHRR 34.
- *U v Centre for Reproductive Medicine* [2002] EWCA Civ 565.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the 1990 Act fit for purpose?** (the materials Q2.) Example introduction: defend the regulatory *structure* (licensing authority, layered prohibitions), its approach to PGT (prohibiting unethical use, otherwise permissive), but argue reform is needed on specific matters, especially the clinical discretion in s.13(5) (welfare of the child). HFEA, *Modernising Fertility Law* (Nov 2023), proposes amendments (e.g. donor anonymity), suggesting some provisions are dated.
- **Bright-line consent rules (Evans).** Supporters: certainty, equal treatment of gamete providers, the right not to become a genetic parent. Critics: the woman bears greater burdens (surgery, loss of fertility); Karin Webster, 'Whose embryo is it anyway? A critique of Evans v Amicus Healthcare' (2006) 7(3) J Int Women's Studies 71, argues the law rests on a constructed and changeable division between human subject and property, so it is no more coherent or legitimate than accepting the claimants' case would have been, and that a presumption of equal rights over the embryo can consistently prejudice women despite apparent neutrality. The dissenting ECtHR minority (4 judges) found a violation.
- **Margin of appreciation.** Evans shows Strasbourg deference on contested reproductive ethics (compare Karsai on assisted dying).
- **Medicalisation and gatekeeping.** Licensing covers processing and storage but not drugs or advice; DIY insemination with unprocessed partner sperm is outside regulation; lay sperm donation arrangements may fall outside licensed protections.
- **Clinic ethics v patient choice** (U; Pattinson 2002): clinics' ethical policies can effectively override patients' prior decisions.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the activity: creating embryos, storing gametes/embryos, using donor gametes, testing embryos, research – does it need a licence or third party agreement, or is it prohibited outright?
2. Consents under Sch 3: in writing, signed, specifying purposes, storage period and what happens on death/incapacity.
3. Withdrawal: possible by notice until the embryo is "used" (transfer/training/research) – Evans. 12-month cooling-off on withdrawal of storage consent.
4. Posthumous use: requires written consent to posthumous use (and see parenthood rules for registration); voluntariness of any withdrawal (U).
5. Counselling must be offered (s.13(6)).
6. Criminal liability: s.41 offences, DPP consent (s.42).
7. Human rights: Art 8 engaged, wide margin (Evans v UK).
8. Traps: applying the 2008 Act as a standalone code (except parenthood); forgetting that fertility drugs are unregulated by the Act.
