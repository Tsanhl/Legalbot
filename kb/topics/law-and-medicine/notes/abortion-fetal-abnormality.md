# Abortion 3 - Abortion on Grounds of Fetal Abnormality (s.1(1)(d)), Prenatal Diagnosis, Crowter and Reform

## Overview
Section 1(1)(d) of the Abortion Act 1967 permits termination **at any gestation** where two doctors believe in good faith that there is a "substantial risk" that the child, if born, would suffer such physical or mental abnormalities as to be "seriously handicapped". It accounts for about 1% of abortions (3,075 in 2023) but is the most contested ground: it is the only ground tied to the characteristics of the fetus, it has no time limit, and its key terms are undefined. Disability-rights challenges (*Crowter*) and complaints about particular terminations (*Jepson*, cleft lip and palate) have failed, but reform bills and academic critiques continue. This is a set essay topic (Q1: "Critically examine the view that abortion on grounds of fetal abnormality is in need of legislative reform").

## The law
1. **The ground.** s.1(1)(d) [Ground E in statistics]: substantial risk that if born the child would suffer such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped. No 24-week limit (contrast (a)); two RMPs in good faith; s.1(3) place requirements. For multiple pregnancies, s.5(2) allows selective termination of a fetus to which (d) applies.
2. **Interpretation left to doctors.** Neither "substantial risk" nor "seriously handicapped" is defined.
   - **RCOG (1996) factors**: probability of effective treatment; probable degree of self-awareness and ability to communicate; likely suffering; need for support.
   - **RCOG Working Party (2010)**: "sees little reason to change the current law regarding the definition of abnormality"; a definitive list of conditions would be unrealistic/impractical because diagnostic techniques cannot accurately define abnormalities or predict seriousness, and consequences are hard to predict for the fetus, the child and the family (pp 9-10). Brazier & Cave report that RCOG sets a minimum threshold of a disability causing "significant suffering or long term impairment of their ability to function in society", and that specialists take different moral views.
   - RCOG (2010): feticide before medical abortion after 21 weeks 6 days, to avoid live birth.
3. **Prenatal screening and diagnosis.** Pre-conception risk v prenatal screening (maternal blood test and ultrasound: no miscarriage risk; gives risk, not a definite answer) v prenatal diagnosis: amniocentesis (usually 15-20 weeks; RCOG 2021: not before 15+0 weeks) and chorionic villus sampling (usually 11-14 weeks; from 11+0 weeks); additional miscarriage risk likely below 0.5% (RCOG 2021).
4. **Four example conditions.**
   - *Down's syndrome* (chromosome 21; about 1 in 350 births; risk rises with maternal age from about 1 in 1,500 at 20 to 1 in 50 or higher at 45): the most common reason for (d) abortions (19% (618) in one source; 15% (685) in another). Linked to *Crowter*.
   - *Duchenne muscular dystrophy*: X-linked; a carrier mother has a 1 in 2 chance of passing it on, affecting boys; about 30% spontaneous mutations; illustrates sex-linked reasons for abortion (to avoid disease, as opposed to preference for a sex).
   - *Cleft lip and/or palate*: no known cause; sometimes purely cosmetic, sometimes associated with major malformations; about 1.5 per 1,000 births; 1% (40) of (d) abortions. Linked to *Jepson*.
   - *Huntington's disease*: late onset (usually 30-50), progressive neurodegeneration, death 10-20 years later; 1 in 2 risk if a parent has it. Raises the "right not to know" and the effect of future treatment (gene therapy reported in 2025 as slowing progression by 75% over 3 years).
5. ***R (Crowter) v Secretary of State for Health and Social Care*** HC [2021] EWHC 2536 (Admin); CA [2022] EWCA Civ 1559 (esp. [70], [73], [80]; required reading). An adult with Down's syndrome (Heidi Crowter) and a young child with Down's syndrome argued that s.1(1)(d) "perpetuates and reinforces" negative stereotypes about the value of disabled people's lives, contrary to Arts 8 and 14 ECHR. Dismissed. Underhill LJ: s.1(1)(d) is to be interpreted by reference to an objective standard; it is concerned with fetuses, had no "direct impact" on the born appellants, and the "social impact" claim was unconvincing.
6. ***Jepson v Chief Constable of West Mercia*** [2003] EWHC 3318 (Jackson J). An abortion after 24 weeks (Brazier & Cave: at 28 weeks) of a fetus diagnosed by ultrasound with bilateral cleft lip and palate. The claimant, a Church of England curate born with a significant facial abnormality, argued this could not be "serious handicap" and sought judicial review of the police decision not to prosecute (mandatory order to charge the clinicians under OAPA ss.58/59 and ILPA s.1). The RCOG Vice President stated there is no precise definition and the decision is for the practitioner with the parents. Permission was granted, though Jackson J said the claimant faced "substantial evidential hurdles and substantial legal hurdles". The CPS (March 2005) decided not to prosecute.
7. **Reform bills (Lord Shinkwin)**: Abortion (Disability Equality) Bill [HL] 2016-17 and 2017-19, cl.1: insert a 24-week limit into s.1(1)(d), and require that before a (d) termination "the parents of that child" be given full and accurate information on all options including keeping the child, including information from disability family support groups and organisations led by disabled people. Not enacted.

## Debate and critique
- **Sheldon and Wilkinson, 'Termination of Pregnancy for Reason of Foetal Disability: Are there Grounds for a Special Exception in Law?' (2001) 9 Med L Rev 85.** Examine three defences of s.1(1)(d) as an exception to the 24-week limit: (1) the *fetal interests* argument fails because, insofar as it works at all, it covers only a small minority of cases within the "seriously handicapped" test; (2) the *replacement* argument fails, mainly because it justifies too much (a generally permissive policy, not a disability exception); (3) the *parental interests* argument goes a long way, subject to the *disability discrimination objection*: parental interests should count only insofar as they relate to harms caused directly by impairment, not harms caused by social discrimination (including lack of support). They argue for a presumption of equal treatment of women seeking abortion, displaced only by a sound argument for differential treatment.
- **McGuinness, 'Law, Reproduction, and Disability: Fatally "Handicapped"?' (2013) 21 Med L Rev 213.** Criticises and rejects the legitimacy of s.1(1)(d), especially post-24 weeks; uses it as a catalyst for more dramatic changes to abortion law in Great Britain; drawing on disability studies, argues it problematically reifies physical aspects of disability; proposes alternative legislative approaches.
- **Robinson, 'Discrimination in Abortion Law and the Message the Law is Sending: R (Crowter)' (2024) 87(1) MLR 218.** Crowter is significant because rights arguments were framed around disabled people (not the woman or fetus), reflecting the *expressivist* argument. She finds Underhill LJ's conclusion that s.1(1)(d) concerns only fetuses "difficult to accept": a ground based on the fetus being female would hardly be seen as expressing a view only about female fetuses; s.1(1)(d) refers to "the child" the fetus will become. The "message" argument could have far-reaching implications.
- **Two reform directions** (example introduction): (i) give women a broader right to abortion where the fetus has little moral status (critique of the restrictive structure and of reliance on ground (a)); (ii) protect the fetus and those with minor congenital disabilities from unjustified discrimination (critique of (d)'s lack of time limit and vagueness, cf. Jepson). Shinkwin's bills take the second route. A rights-based proportional status view could support both: a wider early right, but greater protection for later fetuses irrespective of disability.
- **Defending the status quo.** RCOG: definition is impractical; decisions belong to clinicians and parents; late diagnoses (e.g. anomalies detected after 20 weeks) would be hard cases under a 24-week limit. Parental interests (Sheldon & Wilkinson) and the woman's bodily autonomy (Thomson) support access.
- **Comparison with PGT.** HFE Act Sch 2 para 1ZA(2) uses "significant risk" of a "serious" condition, cf. "substantial risk" of being "seriously handicapped" in s.1(1)(d); s.13(9) HFEA forbids preferring embryos known to have a serious abnormality (e.g. deaf embryos).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Timing**: after 24 weeks only (b), (c) or (d) are available.
2. **Two doctors, good faith**: is there a genuine opinion of a *substantial* risk of *serious* handicap? Use RCOG 1996 factors (treatability, self-awareness/communication, suffering, support needs) and the 2010 view that seriousness depends on the condition and family context.
3. **Condition-specific analysis**: purely cosmetic cleft lip v cleft with major malformations (Jepson); Down's (Crowter: lawful under (d)); sex-linked disease (DMD: abortion of a male fetus at risk is lawful under (d) if risk substantial; contrast sex preference); late onset (Huntington's – "seriously handicapped" when born? the provision speaks of the child "if born").
4. **Uncertainty of diagnosis**: screening gives risk only; diagnosis via CVS/amnio involves small miscarriage risk.
5. **Liability if outside (d)**: OAPA s.58 and ILPA s.1 (viable fetus) – doctors protected only if the AA is complied with (s.5(1)); prosecution decisions (Jepson) and CPS discretion.
6. **Human rights challenge**: Crowter forecloses Arts 8/14 challenges for now.
7. Essay plan: introduction stating the four grounds, identifying (a) and (d) as relevant to fetal abnormality, and setting a thesis (e.g. reform needed both to grant a right to early abortion and to protect late fetuses from discrimination); sections on the law and practice, the arguments for the exception (Sheldon & Wilkinson), the expressivist/discrimination critique (Crowter, Robinson, McGuinness), reform options (Shinkwin; time limit; decriminalisation), conclusion.
