# Children - Consent, Competence (FLRA s.8 and Gillick), Proxies and Necessity

## Overview
A "child" or "minor" is anyone under 18. Treatment of a child is lawful on one of three bases: **(a) the child's own consent**, **(b) the consent of a proxy** (a person with parental responsibility, or the court), or **(c) another lawful justification without consent** (s.5 MCA for 16-17s; necessity for under-16s). The capacity rules split by age: 16-17-year-olds are presumed capable under s.8(1) Family Law Reform Act 1969 (FLRA), rebuttable only by the adult MCA test; under-16s must be "Gillick competent". Unlike adults, a competent child's *consent* is effective but their *refusal* can be overridden (separate note). This note covers the consent side, proxies and their limits, disputes between proxies, and necessity.

## The law
**1. Overview of lawful treatment (lecture summary).**
- (a) Child's consent: 16-17: FLRA 1969 s.8(1); under 16: if the child has "sufficient understanding and intelligence" (Gillick).
- (b) Proxy consent: someone with parental responsibility, or the court.
- (c) Without consent: 16-17: s.5 MCA 2005 (which applies from 16); under 16: necessity (Gillick).
- Consent of one proxy is usually sufficient: s.2(7) Children Act 1989.

**2. 16-17-year-olds: FLRA s.8(1).** The consent of a minor aged 16 or over to surgical, medical or dental treatment that would otherwise be a trespass is as effective as if they were of full age, and parental consent is then unnecessary. Meaning:
- a 16-17-year-old is **presumed** to have capacity to consent to medical treatment;
- the presumption is rebutted only by failing the adult two-stage MCA test (ss.2-3), not by a more demanding test;
- *A NHS Trust v X* [2021] EWHC 65 (Fam), Sir James Munby [55]: until 16 the inquiry is Gillick competence; from 16, Gillick falls away and the child is assumed to have capacity under s.8 unless incapacitous in the adult sense.
- s.8 covers "treatment" only, not organ donation (transplantation handout); and per Brazier & Cave s.8(3) preserves concurrent parental and court powers (Re W).

**3. Under-16s: Gillick.** *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1986] AC 112: Mrs Gillick challenged guidance allowing doctors to give contraceptive advice to under-16s without parental consent, and failed.
- A child under 16 who is "Gillick competent" can give valid consent without parental involvement. Lord Scarman: the child needs "sufficient understanding and intelligence to enable him or her to understand fully what is proposed". The parental right "yields" to the child's right when the child reaches that level (as quoted in Brazier & Cave).
- *What must be understood?* Lord Fraser referred to understanding the doctor's advice; Lord Scarman included moral and family questions and emotional implications. *An NHS Trust v A, B, C* [2014] EWHC 1445 adopted Lord Fraser's view.
- Competence is **decision-specific**: a child may be competent for some procedures but not others.
- Lord Fraser's five conditions for contraceptive advice without parental knowledge: (1) the girl will understand the advice; (2) she cannot be persuaded to tell her parents or let the doctor tell them; (3) she is very likely to have intercourse with or without contraception; (4) without it her physical or mental health is likely to suffer; (5) her best interests require the advice/treatment without parental consent. He also said he would not expect a jury to find "at all frequently" that a child under 14 had sufficient understanding (as noted in the materials answer).

**4. Bell v Tavistock (who applies Gillick?).**
- Divisional Court [2020] EWHC 3274 (Admin): set out what a child must understand to consent to puberty blockers for gender dysphoria (including progression to cross-sex hormones, possible surgery, fertility loss, poor evidence base). It said it was "highly unlikely" a child of 13 or under would be competent, and it was very doubtful a 14-15-year-old could understand the long-term risks.
- Court of Appeal [2021] EWCA Civ 1363 (Lord Burnett CJ): reversed. The ratio of Gillick was that it is for **doctors, not judges**, to decide the capacity of under-16s. The guidance effectively required court applications where there was no legal obligation, putting patients, parents and clinicians in a very difficult position and in practice denying treatment for want of resources and through delay [86]. Clinicians must exercise judgement; it is not for the court to give them a "manual". Brazier & Cave note policy controls will now determine access.

**5. Proxies.**
- **The court**: wardship, inherent jurisdiction, Children Act 1989 s.8 orders. Welfare is the paramount consideration (CA 1989 s.1(1)).
- **Those with parental responsibility** (CA 1989 ss.3(1), 2(5), 2(7)): the mother (s.2); sometimes the father (ss.2, 4(1)); a legal guardian (s.5(6)); sometimes a local authority (ss.33(3)(b), 44(4)(c)). One person's consent usually suffices (s.2(7)).
- **Limits of parental consent**: must be exercised in the child's interests. Which standard: best interests, "not against the interests" (*S v S* [1972]), or not causing significant harm? The **best interests** test is preferred; the significant harm test was rejected in *Yates and Gard v GOSH* [2017] EWCA Civ 410 and was not enacted as an amendment to the Health and Care Act 2022.
- Parents can consent if the child cannot, provided the child does not object: *AB v CD & Ors* [2021] EWHC 741 (study materials). Consent can be overridden by the court, but not by parents.

**6. Disputes between proxies.** Some decisions are so important that one parent's consent should not suffice and the court should decide:
- *Re J (Specific Issue Orders: Child's Religious Upbringing and Circumcision)* [2000] 1 FLR 571 (esp. 577): ritual circumcision where parents disagreed – dual consent / court.
- *Re C (A Child) (Immunisation: Parental Rights)* [2003] EWCA Civ 1148 (esp. [17]): parental disagreement over immunisation.
- *F v F* [2013] EWHC 2683.
Disputes between proxies and doctors (e.g. *Re B* [1990]; *Re T (A Minor)* [1997]; *R v Portsmouth Hospitals NHS Trust ex p Glass* [1999]) are covered in the end-of-life children note.

**7. Necessity (under-16s).** In an emergency, vital treatment may be given without consent.
- Lord Templeman (Gillick): if there is no time for a court decision, a doctor may safely treat in an emergency if he believes treatment vital to the survival or health of an infant, despite parental opposition or inability to alert the parent.
- Lord Scarman (Gillick): emergency, parental neglect, abandonment, or inability to find the parent are examples justifying treatment without parental knowledge and consent.
- Over 16: use s.5 MCA instead.
- But see E & F [2021] (Brazier & Cave): the Court of Appeal "very much doubt[ed]" an emergency power exists for treatment that has been foreseen and refused by a capacitous patient.

## Key authorities
- FLRA 1969 s.8(1): presumption of capacity for 16-17s.
- *A NHS Trust v X* [2021] EWHC 65 (Fam) [55]: Munby's summary of the age-based tests; he also scorned importing the MCA test for under-16s ([73]-[75], per Brazier & Cave).
- *Gillick* [1986] AC 112: Gillick competence; Lord Fraser's guidelines; Templeman and Scarman on necessity.
- *An NHS Trust v A, B, C* [2014] EWHC 1445: Lord Fraser's narrower view of what must be understood.
- *Bell v Tavistock* [2020] EWHC 3274 (Admin); [2021] EWCA Civ 1363: Gillick is for clinicians; no court "manual".
- Children Act 1989 ss.1(1), 2, 3(1), 4(1), 5(6), 8, 33, 44.
- *S v S* [1972]; *Yates and Gard v GOSH* [2017] EWCA Civ 410: best interests, not significant harm.
- *Re J* [2000] 1 FLR 571; *Re C* [2003] EWCA Civ 1148; *F v F* [2013] EWHC 2683: disputes between parents.
- *AB v CD* [2021] EWHC 741: parents may consent where the child cannot and does not object.
- *Re W* [1993] Fam 64: Lord Donaldson's "flak jacket": a doctor needs only one valid consent (from a 16+ minor, a Gillick-competent child or a person with parental responsibility).

## Debate and critique
- **Cave, 'Goodbye Gillick?' (2014) 34(1) Legal Studies 103.** Gillick promised competent minors autonomy, but the refusal cases (Re R, Re W) and outcome-driven competence findings (Re E, Re L) eroded it. Competence became a flexible label whose threshold rises with the gravity of the outcome. Cave proposes abandoning Gillick for a **single capacity concept for all ages**: extend the MCA (with its presumption and facilitative duties) to all minors, with the inherent jurisdiction developed to cover non-autonomous decisions due to immaturity, inexperience or undue influence; genuinely competent decisions (consent or refusal) could not be vetoed. She also criticises McFarlane LJ's narrower 2011 proposal to apply the MCA test only to otherwise Gillick-competent refusers.
- **Who decides: doctors or courts?** Bell CA returns Gillick to clinicians. Critics of the Divisional Court saw it as raising the bar for one group of children; supporters saw a need for protection given poor evidence. A theme from Topic 1: decisions must be made by someone within the limits of their expertise.
- **Status v competence.** The age rules combine a status test (16+) with a competence test (under 16). Is a bright line at 16 defensible?
- **Best interests v significant harm.** Auckland and Goold argue for a significant harm threshold for state interference with parental decisions; Archard, Cave and Brierley defend best interests (see the end-of-life children note).
- **Parental disagreements.** The "one consent suffices" rule (s.2(7)) is qualified for irreversible, non-therapeutic or contested procedures (circumcision, immunisation), raising the question why some decisions are reserved to the court.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Age** decides the test: 16-17 → s.8(1) presumption + MCA test; under 16 → Gillick (consider the child's maturity, the complexity of the decision, what must be understood: Fraser v Scarman).
2. **Is the child consenting?** If competent and consenting, treatment is lawful without parents (s.8; Gillick). Parents cannot veto a competent child's consent.
3. **Is the child refusing?** Go to the refusal note: a proxy or the court can override (Re R, Re W, E & F).
4. **Proxy consent**: who has parental responsibility (mother; sometimes the father under ss.2 and 4(1); guardian; sometimes a local authority)? One consent normally suffices (s.2(7)) unless the decision is of the special category (Re J; Re C) requiring court approval.
5. **Limits**: the proxy must act in the child's best interests.
6. **Emergency**: necessity (under 16) or s.5 MCA (16-17).
7. **Doubt or dispute** → apply to court (wardship, inherent jurisdiction, CA 1989 s.8 specific issue order); welfare paramount.
8. Example (the materials further question): Salla aged 10 refusing elective limb lengthening. The MCA does not apply. It is highly unlikely she is Gillick competent for such a complex, non-essential procedure; her parents can consent, but her strong objection weighs heavily in a welfare assessment, and a court would be much less likely to find an elective, painful procedure in her best interests than life-saving treatment. If Salla were an older minor needing amputation, her father's consent could provide the "flak jacket" even against her refusal, though in practice the hospital would seek court authority.
9. Traps: applying the MCA to under-16s; treating FLRA s.8 as covering organ donation; assuming parental consent is unlimited.
