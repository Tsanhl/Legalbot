# Children Refusing Treatment - Re R, Re W and Their Affirmation

## Overview
A capacitous adult's refusal is binding. A child's is not. Even a Gillick-competent under-16, or a 16-17-year-old with capacity under s.8 FLRA and the MCA, can have a refusal of (especially life-sustaining) treatment overridden by a person with parental responsibility or by the court in the child's best interests. This asymmetry (consent effective, refusal overridable) was established in *Re R* (1991) and *Re W* (1992) and has been reaffirmed after the Human Rights Act and the MCA, most authoritatively by the Court of Appeal in *E & F* (2021). the materials's conclusion: in child refusals of life-sustaining treatment, competence is **relevant but not determinative**.

## The law
1. **Under 16: Re R (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment)** [1992] Fam 11 / [1991] 4 All ER 177 (CA). R, 15, in care, with fluctuating psychiatric illness, refused anti-psychotic medication in lucid phases. Held she was not Gillick competent (fluctuating understanding; compare s.3(3) MCA, which tolerates brief retention). Treatment authorised under wardship. Lord Donaldson's **obiter**: even a Gillick-competent child cannot veto treatment; consent is a "key" that unlocks the door, and parents remain keyholders until 18.
2. **16-17: Re W (A Minor) (Medical Treatment: Court's Jurisdiction)** [1993] Fam 64 / [1992] 4 All ER 627 (CA). W, 16, critically ill with anorexia nervosa. Held:
   - s.8 FLRA only empowers a 16-17-year-old to give an *effective consent*; it does not confer a right to refuse, and s.8(3) preserves parental and court powers;
   - when exercising the inherent jurisdiction, the child's wishes are a factor but not determinative;
   - Lord Donaldson replaced the "key" with the **"flak jacket"**: consent protects the doctor from legal claims; the doctor needs only one, whether from a 16+ minor, a Gillick-competent child, or a person with parental responsibility; anyone may take theirs back, but as long as the doctor has one, he may proceed;
   - Balcombe and Nolan LJJ restricted overriding to cases involving the risk of "death of the child or ... severe permanent injury" (slides). Balcombe LJ also observed that in logic there is no difference between ability to consent and ability to refuse (quoted by Cave).
3. **Competence findings used to avoid the issue.** *Re E (A Minor)* [1993] 1 FLR 386 / [1992] 2 FCR 219: 15-year-old Jehovah's Witness refusing blood transfusion; *Re L (Medical Treatment: Gillick Competency)* [1998] Fam 19 / 2 FLR 810; *Re M (Medical Treatment: Consent)* [1999] 2 FLR 1097. Slides pose the question whether the test is the *ability* to understand or *actual* understanding (Re L), and how high the threshold is (Re E).
   - *Re M* facts: M, 15, needed an emergency heart transplant and refused, saying she would rather die than have someone else's heart and take medication for life, while also saying she did not want to die. The Official Solicitor considered she was overwhelmed. The court authorised the operation: her wishes carried weight given her maturity, but the risks of surgery and of future resentment were outweighed by the need to preserve her life.
4. **Post-HRA and post-MCA reaffirmation.**
   - *A NHS Trust v X (In the matter of X (A Child) (No 2))* [2021] EWHC 65 (Fam): X, a Gillick-competent 15-year-old Jehovah's Witness with sickle cell disease, refused transfusions. Sir James Munby refused an anticipatory declaration authorising transfusions until her 18th birthday (preferring case-by-case decisions to avoid "medical paternalism"), but **affirmed Re R and Re W**, saying any change is for Parliament, not the courts (per Brazier & Cave).
   - *E & F (Minors: Blood Transfusion)* [2021] EWCA Civ 1888 (also *E v Northern Care Alliance NHS FT*): two young Jehovah's Witnesses with capacity appealed orders permitting transfusion if necessary during surgery. Court of Appeal affirmed Re R/Re W on the inherent jurisdiction: a minor's refusal of life-sustaining treatment can be overridden by the court in their best interests. Welfare assessments for capacitous young persons balance "two transcendent factors: the preservation of life and personal autonomy" [53]; sometimes autonomy will prevail. The court "very much doubt[ed]" any emergency power to treat without a court order where treatment was foreseen and refused by a capacitous patient [24], and warned that undifferentiated lists of authorities do not help [71].
   - *Re J (Blood Transfusion: Older Child: Jehovah's Witness)* [2024] EWHC 1034 (Fam): an application where the refusal was **upheld** (slides).
   - Brazier & Cave also cite *A Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust v DV* [2021] EWHC 1037 (Fam): a 17-year-old Jehovah's Witness previously transfused against his will (leading to PTSD) – an example where best interests may not favour treatment.
5. **Summary rules (slides).** Capacity test: MCA for 16-17; Gillick for under 16. But the power to refuse life-sustaining treatment turns on best interests, not on either test. Refusal can be overridden by the court; parents can consent if the child cannot consent and does not object (*AB v CD* [2021] EWHC 741); consent can be overridden by the court but not by parents; the child's wishes are important but not decisive.

## Key authorities
- *Re R* [1992] Fam 11: keyholder obiter; fluctuating competence.
- *Re W* [1993] Fam 64: flak jacket; s.8 FLRA confers no right of veto; IJ overrides; Balcombe/Nolan LJJ limit to death or severe permanent injury. Formative feedback: Re W "has been expressly followed and supported" by the CA in E v Northern Care Alliance and should be cited for this point.
- *Re E* [1993] 1 FLR 386; *Re L* [1998] Fam 19; *Re M* [1999] 2 FLR 1097: competence thresholds; Re M is a weaker authority because M was not unequivocally competent (formative feedback).
- *A NHS Trust v X (No 2)* [2021] EWHC 65 (Fam): Re R and Re W affirmed; anticipatory declaration refused.
- *E & F / E v Northern Care Alliance* [2021] EWCA Civ 1888: CA confirmation; balance of life and autonomy.
- *Re J* [2024] EWHC 1034 (Fam): refusal upheld.
- *An NHS Foundation Hospital v P* [2014] EWHC 1650 and *University Hospitals Plymouth NHS Trust v B* [2019] EWHC 1670 (Brazier & Cave): refusals by 17- and 16-year-olds overridden; Art 2 positive obligation to protect life outweighing Art 8.

## Debate and critique
- **"Re R and Re W make a nonsense of Gillick"** (Brazier & Cave): adolescent autonomy is "little more than myth" where treatment is life-sustaining. They suggest the cases are explicable because R and W, ill as they were, might well have lacked capacity even as adults. Parents overriding a competent child's refusal is the most controversial part (Lady Hale in *Re D* [2019] UKSC 42 called it a "controversial question"); the court's inherent jurisdiction is easier to justify, with Art 8 interference likely justified under Art 8(2) and Art 2 where life is at stake.
- **Cave (2014)**: competence thresholds are inflated when outcomes are grave (Re E, Re L), so assessors focus on outcome rather than function; she calls for a unified MCA-based capacity test for all ages with the IJ confined to non-autonomous decisions, removing the veto over genuinely competent refusals and the coercive lever of threatened court applications.
- **Asymmetry of consent and refusal.** Logically, the capacity to consent implies capacity to refuse (Balcombe LJ). Defenders invoke the welfare principle and "future-oriented autonomy" (protecting the adult the child will become). Rights-based theorists would point to the agent's right to waive; duty-based theorists to the duty to preserve life.
- **Religious refusals.** Many cases involve Jehovah's Witnesses; the courts balance faith-based autonomy against life (E & F [53]). Re J (2024) and DV show autonomy can prevail.
- **Parliament or courts?** Munby in X (No 2) leaves change to Parliament; this links to the module-wide theme of institutional competence.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Age and competence: 16-17 (s.8 FLRA; MCA test) or under 16 (Gillick). Note the facts on maturity, understanding, fluctuation (Re R), pressure from family (Re T voluntariness).
2. If the child refuses: does anyone with parental responsibility consent? Under Re W, that consent is a legal flak jacket even against a competent child's refusal, but in practice, for serious or contested treatment, advise seeking the court's authority (inherent jurisdiction/wardship).
3. Court test: best interests; welfare paramount; child's wishes and feelings important but not decisive; balance preservation of life and autonomy (E & F); severity (death or severe permanent injury: Balcombe/Nolan LJJ).
4. Emergency: if no time, necessity (under 16) or s.5 MCA (16-17) for a child who cannot express a view; but doubt about emergency power where refusal was foreseen (E & F [24]).
5. Elective/non-life-saving treatment is far less likely to be imposed on an objecting child.
6. Example (the materials further question): if Simon were 15 and pleading to end his life on a ventilator, his refusal would be relevant but not determinative; his mother's consent or the court could authorise continued treatment if in his best interests; withdrawal would turn on best interests (end-of-life children note).
7. Example (the materials further question): Ant aged 15 refusing a kidney transplant – compare *Re M* (heart transplant authorised despite refusal) and consider whether dialysis is a less invasive alternative.
8. Traps: saying a Gillick-competent child has the same right to refuse as an adult; citing Re M as a case on an unequivocally competent child; ignoring E & F.
