# Consent to Treatment - Legal Function, Requirements, Information and Public Policy

## Overview
The starting point is that touching a patient without consent or another lawful justification is both a tort (trespass to the person, mainly battery) and a crime (assault). Valid consent has three components: **capacity, information and voluntariness** ("CIV"). In criminal law there is a fourth hurdle for serious harm: the procedure must be consistent with **public policy**. This note covers the framework, forms of consent, the information component and public policy, plus the two key limits on what consent can achieve. Capacity and voluntariness have their own notes.

Montgomery is mentioned only to contrast negligence with trespass.

## The law
1. **Function of consent.** Lord Goff in *Re F* (1989): as a general rule, performing a medical operation on someone without their consent is unlawful. Consent is "a state of mind personal to the victim of the battery" (Lord Diplock, *Sidaway*).
2. **Regulatory overview.**
   - Civil: trespass to the person (primarily battery) and, outside this topic, negligence.
   - Criminal: common assault and battery (Criminal Justice Act 1988); ABH and GBH (Offences Against the Person Act 1861).
   - Other: GMC disciplinary procedures and NHS complaints.
3. **Declaratory relief.** Courts in this area often make *declarations*: statements, at a party's request, about facts or legal principle. They are available at common law and under **s.15 Mental Capacity Act 2005** (capacity and the lawfulness of acts done or proposed). They usually come *before* the event (e.g. does P have capacity? what is in P's best interests?) rather than as a remedy afterwards.
4. **Forms of consent.** Consent may be written or oral and may be:
   - *express*: explicit words or conduct;
   - *implicit*: suggestive conduct, e.g. rolling up a sleeve for a blood pressure check (cf. *O'Brien v Cunard SS Co* (1891), cited as the classic example);
   - *implied* by an express consent;
   - *imputed*: a fiction, with no real consent (e.g. opt-out or "deemed" consent);
   - *proxy*: given by another, e.g. a parent for a child; proxies may be appointed (e.g. an attorney) or imposed.
5. **Requirements of a legally valid consent or refusal.** The patient must:
   - (a) have **capacity** (the legal authority to consent or refuse);
   - (b) be **broadly aware** of what is proposed (information);
   - (c) act **voluntarily**, not under coercion or undue influence.
   If these are met, no action in trespass can succeed. The conditions are the same for trespass and criminal assault.
6. **Public policy (criminal law).** Consent is no defence to ABH/GBH unless the harm is inflicted for a reason public policy supports: *R v Brown* [1993/1994]. But "proper medical treatment ... is in a category of its own" (Lord Mustill). Therapeutic procedures are proper medical treatment (see also *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland*). Contrast *Corbett v Corbett* [1970/1971] and *R v BM* [2018] EWCA Crim 560 (a tattooist and body piercer who carried out body modifications: contrasted with proper medical treatment).
7. **Information component.** In trespass and criminal assault, consent is valid if the patient is **broadly aware of the nature** of the treatment: *Chatterton v Gerson* [1981] QB 432. Failing to disclose risks does not vitiate consent for battery; the right to be told material risks and reasonable alternatives belongs to **negligence** (*Montgomery v Lanarkshire HB* [2015] UKSC 11).
8. **Two limits of consent law.**
   - **Courts will not compel a doctor to treat.** *Re J (A Minor) (Child in Care: Medical Treatment)* [1993] Fam 15; *R (Burke) v GMC* [2005] EWCA Civ 1003 ("a patient cannot demand that a doctor administer a treatment which the doctor considers is adverse to the patient's clinical needs"); *Aintree University Hospitals NHS FT v James* [2013] UKSC 67. A capacitous patient can choose among treatments offered, or go to another professional who will agree, but no professional can be ordered to provide an intervention they think inappropriate (Burke CA, as summarised in the case file).
   - **No right to be fully informed** for a valid consent: broad awareness suffices.
9. **Hygiene and pain relief.** Commentators note that a patient cannot refuse measures designed to maintain basic hygiene and pain relief (citing *Bland*); the MCA Code of Practice also says basic care cannot be refused by advance decision (see the end-of-life notes).

## Key authorities
- *Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1: sterilisation of a 36-year-old woman with a learning disability; Lord Goff's statement that treatment without consent is generally unlawful; pre-MCA doctrine of necessity (now replaced by s.5 MCA).
- *Sidaway*: Lord Diplock's description of consent as a state of mind personal to the patient.
- *O'Brien v Cunard SS Co* (1891): illustration of implicit consent by conduct.
- *Chatterton v Gerson* [1981] QB 432: broad awareness of the nature of the procedure is enough to defeat battery; information-based complaints lie in negligence.
- *Montgomery v Lanarkshire HB* [2015] UKSC 11: negligence duty to disclose material risks and reasonable alternatives. Noted for contrast. Brazier & Cave (ch 4) present Montgomery as treating patients as right-holders and recognising shared decision-making, while clinicians may decline to offer treatment that is not clinically indicated.
- *R v Brown* [1993] UKHL 19 (sometimes dated [1994]): consent no defence to ABH/GBH absent a public policy reason; Lord Mustill: proper medical treatment is in a category of its own.
- *Corbett v Corbett* [1970] 2 All ER 33 and *R v BM* [2018] EWCA Crim 560: limits of the "medical treatment" category (body modification by a non-medical practitioner).
- *Re J* [1993] Fam 15; *R (Burke) v GMC* [2005] EWCA Civ 1003; *Aintree v James* [2013] UKSC 67: courts will not order doctors to provide treatment they regard as clinically inappropriate.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 s.15: Court of Protection declarations on capacity and lawfulness.

## Debate and critique
- **Battery v negligence divide.** Because trespass needs only broad awareness, the legal "right to informed consent" is protected only by negligence, which requires proof of damage and causation. Cave (2020, J Med Ethics, cited as optional reading) discusses valid consent. A critical essay can ask whether a consent that is "valid" for battery but uninformed respects autonomy in any real sense.
- **What counts as "proper medical treatment"?** Lord Mustill's category is not defined. Cosmetic surgery, gender reassignment and body modification test the line (contrast *Corbett* and *BM*). The issue links to living organ donation, which is non-therapeutic for the donor yet lawful unless the harm is extreme (see the living donation note).
- **Autonomy is negative, not positive.** The law protects a right to *refuse* (even irrationally) but not to *demand*. Burke shows this asymmetry; Pattinson argues that the Court of Appeal missed a chance to recognise positive rights of vulnerable patients.
- **Imputed consent is not consent.** Labelling opt-out organ donation "deemed consent" is a fiction; essays on transplantation can use this classification.
- **Moral issues**: public policy limits express a duty-based or mixed view that some harms cannot be waived; a rights-based view would allow more self-regarding risk.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify every intervention (e.g. amputation, blood transfusion, anaesthetic) and who is touching whom.
2. For each, ask: is there a **valid consent**? Run CIV:
   - *Capacity*: adult (MCA ss.1-3), 16-17 (FLRA s.8(1) + MCA), under 16 (Gillick).
   - *Information*: was the patient broadly aware of the nature of the procedure (Chatterton)? Do not drift into negligence.
   - *Voluntariness*: any undue influence (Re T)? Look for family pressure, religious conversion, pain, fatigue.
3. If no valid consent: is there **another lawful justification**? Advance refusal (blocks treatment), LPA/deputy, s.5 MCA general legal authority (best interests), court declaration, or for under-16s, proxy consent or necessity.
4. For serious, non-therapeutic harm, check **public policy** (Brown) for criminal liability.
5. If the patient is *demanding* treatment, remember the doctor cannot be compelled (Burke, Aintree, Re J).
6. Suggest a **declaration** (common law or s.15 MCA) where capacity or best interests are in doubt.
7. Traps: treating a capacitous adult's refusal as overridable because it is unwise; citing Montgomery as if it determined battery; forgetting the criminal law dimension.
8. Example (Salla): a hospital proposing amputation for a woman who now refuses because of a new faith requires analysis of her capacity (pain, fear of hospitals), the voluntariness of her refusal after talking to her mother, and, if she is a child, the different rules for minors. Advise on the facts, state what further facts are needed, and do not assume.
