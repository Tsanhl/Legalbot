# Re R (A Minor) (Wardship: Consent to Treatment) [1992] Fam 11 (CA) (case note)

> Caution: this note was built from secondary sources and the original judgment was not read. Check all wording and pinpoints against the law report before citing.

## Overview

This is a 1991 Court of Appeal decision (Lord Donaldson MR, Staughton and Farquharson LJJ). It is also reported at [1991] 3 WLR 592 and [1991] 4 All ER 177. It is a leading authority that a court using its wardship jurisdiction may authorise treatment that a minor refuses, whether or not the minor is Gillick competent. The child's welfare is the paramount consideration. The case is also known for Lord Donaldson MR's 'keyholder' remarks about parental consent.

## Facts

- R was a 15-year-old girl who had been living in a children's home. Her mental state swung between normal behaviour and psychosis, and she had been violent.
- She was placed in an adolescent psychiatric unit. There she was sedated from time to time with her consent.
- The unit wanted to give her anti-psychotic medication, and she refused.
- The local authority started wardship proceedings. It asked for leave for the unit to give medication, including anti-psychotic drugs, whether or not R consented. The judge granted the application, and the case went to the Court of Appeal.

## Held

The Court of Appeal upheld the order allowing the unit to medicate R without her consent.

1. **Wardship power.** A wardship court may override a ward's refusal of treatment whether or not the ward is Gillick competent. Gillick competence is not the test the court applies. The court decides on the ward's welfare, which is paramount.
2. **R was not Gillick competent.** Because her condition fluctuated, R was held to lack Gillick competence. Her refusal could therefore not have been decisive anyway. One practitioner note says she was competent, but the other accounts agree that she was not.
3. **Parental consent: the 'keyholder' remarks.** Lord Donaldson MR went further, against Lord Scarman's view in Gillick that parental rights end once the child is competent. He said a competent child's right to consent does not include a right to veto treatment, so someone with parental responsibility can still give consent. In his image, the child and the parent each hold a 'key' that can unlock the door to treatment. R had been found not competent, so this point was not needed to decide the case. The sources checked do not show whether Staughton LJ or Farquharson LJ agreed with it.

## Later treatment

- **Re W (A Minor) (Medical Treatment: Court's Jurisdiction) [1993] Fam 64 (CA):** this case concerned a 16-year-old with anorexia nervosa. The court again held that it could override a minor's refusal. Lord Donaldson MR restated the parental-consent point with a 'flak jacket' analogy instead of keys: a doctor needs only one valid consent to be protected.
- Commentators criticise Re R and Re W for undermining the spirit of Gillick. A competent child's consent counts, but the child's refusal may not.
- **Re X (A Child) (No 2) [2021] EWHC 65 (Fam):** Sir James Munby, sitting as a High Court judge, treated Re R and Re W as the binding starting point for a first-instance court. He held that neither the Mental Capacity Act 2005 nor Convention case law cast doubt on them.
- **E & F (Minors: Blood Transfusion) [2021] EWCA Civ 1888:** the Court of Appeal described the court's power to intervene in a minor's best interests as settled law.

## Applying it to a problem question

- If a child under 18 refuses treatment, first decide whether they are Gillick competent. Then explain that competence is not decisive once the court is involved, because the court decides on welfare and best interests.
- If the child's mental state fluctuates, use Re R to argue that lucid periods may not make them Gillick competent.
- A court is most likely to override a refusal that risks serious harm to health, or death.
- If the facts rely on a parent consenting over a competent child's refusal, cite the keyholder and flak-jacket remarks. Point out that the Re R remark was not needed for the decision and has been criticised. The safer advice is to apply to the court.
- Keep refusal (Re R) separate from a competent child's consent (Gillick).
