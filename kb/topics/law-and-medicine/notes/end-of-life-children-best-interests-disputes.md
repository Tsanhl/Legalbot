# End-of-Life Decisions for Children - Best Interests, Parental Disputes and Conjoined Twins

## Overview
the materials of the end-of-life topic deals with infants and young children who cannot decide for themselves. Disputes arise in two directions: **doctors favour treatment but parents refuse**, and **parents want treatment (often experimental or abroad) but doctors do not**. The court decides on the child's **best interests**, with welfare paramount and a strong (rebuttable) presumption in favour of prolonging life. Parental views carry weight but are not decisive, and the courts have rejected a "significant harm" threshold for overriding parents (Gard; Alfie Evans). Scholarly debate centres on whether parents should have more authority (Auckland & Goold) or whether best interests remains the right yardstick (Archard, Cave & Brierley). *Re A (conjoined twins)* adds criminal law questions of intention, double effect and necessity.

## The law
1. **Intolerability v balancing.**
   - *Re J (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment)* [1991] Fam 33 / [1990] 3 All ER 930 (CA) (not to be confused with Re J [1993] or [2000]). Taylor LJ: judge the quality of life the child would endure if treated and decide whether such a life would be so afflicted as to be "intolerable" to that child. Munby J in *Burke* [2004] (obiter) called intolerability the "touchstone" of best interests.
   - *Portsmouth Hospitals NHS Trust v Wyatt* [2004] EWHC 2247 / [2005] EWCA Civ 1181 (Wall LJ) discouraged that: the judge decides best interests; welfare is paramount; the question is looked at from the assumed point of view of the patient; there is a strong but rebuttable presumption in favour of prolonging life; best interests include medical, emotional and all other welfare issues; a balancing exercise, helpfully done with a balance sheet [86]. Munby J's intolerability touchstone was at best obiter [90].
2. **Doctors favour treatment, parents refuse.**
   - *Re B (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment)* [1981] 1 WLR 1421 (CA): the materials's lead example of doctors favouring treatment against parental refusal; Lord Donaldson later regarded Re B as near-binding authority that a balancing exercise of the child's best interests is required (per Brazier & Cave on Re J). Contrast *Re C (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment)* [1989] (Brazier & Cave): an inevitably dying baby with severe hydrocephalus could be treated palliatively rather than to prolong life.
   - *Re T (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment)* [1997] 1 WLR 242 / [1997] 1 All ER 906 (CA): T had biliary atresia; his parents, both health professionals, opposed a liver transplant and took him abroad. The CA held the parents' view should prevail: on the "unusual facts" the presumption for prolonging life was rebutted; best interests are not purely clinical; the mother's commitment was integral to post-transplant care and "mother and child are one" (Butler-Sloss LJ). Brazier & Cave ask whether Re T is an aberration. (The transplantation lectures contrast Re T (liver) with Re M (heart).)
   - *Re C (A Minor)* [2025] EWHC 413 is listed as a recent example.
3. **Parents favour treatment, doctors do not.**
   - *R v Portsmouth Hospitals NHS Trust ex p Glass* [1999] (CA); *Glass v UK* [2004] ECHR 103: administering diamorphine to David Glass against his mother's wishes, without a court order, breached Art 8. Doctors may not unilaterally override parental objection outside emergencies (per Brazier & Cave).
   - Mandatory relief is not available: courts will not order doctors to treat (*Re J* [1993]).
   - *Portsmouth City Council v King* [2014] EWHC 2964 (Fam) (Ashya King): parents removed Ashya, who had a brain tumour, to seek **proton beam therapy** (which NHS England did not fund for his condition) abroad; wardship proceedings followed. Baker J allowed the family to take him to Prague for proton therapy. Baker J [31]: responsibility for decisions about a child rests with the parents, and the State has no business interfering unless the child is suffering, or likely to suffer, significant harm from care that is not what it would be reasonable to expect a parent to give.
   - *Yates and Gard v GOSH* [2017] EWCA Civ 410 (Charlie Gard): the Court of Appeal held that a parental preference may give an option weight, but ultimately the judge chooses the best course. In King there was nothing to choose between the two forms of radiotherapy, so the court stood back; in Gard the trial judge found that going to America would be futile and simply prolong Charlie's awful existence [96]-[97]. McFarlane LJ held there is no separate "significant harm" threshold where parents propose a viable alternative; if Baker J had meant that, it would have "no foundation as a matter of law" (Cave & Nottingham).
   - *Evans v Alder Hey Children's NHS FT* [2018] EWCA Civ 805 and the UKSC permission determination (Alfie Evans): best interests is the "gold standard"; it is not lawful to give treatment that is not in the child's interests, and allowing ventilation to continue simply because it is not causing significant harm would be inconsistent with that founding rule. A habeas corpus claim failed.
   - *Raqeeb v Barts Health NHS Trust* [2019] EWHC 2531 (Admin); *Barts NHS FT v Raqeeb* [2019] EWHC 2530 (Fam) (Tafida Raqeeb): MacDonald J agreed with the parents that Tafida could be moved to Italy (the Trust did not appeal). Where there is no pain or awareness of suffering, best interests must be found in value-laden factors such as futility, dignity, the meaning of life and sanctity of life, which mean different things in a diverse society; best interests remains the gold standard [191].
   - *Manchester University NHS FT v Verden & McLennan* [2022] EWCOP 9 (William Verden, 17): kidney transplant was not futile; though likely to increase suffering in the short to medium term, it offered a chance of long-term survival, so it was in his best interests [160]-[162].
   - Also listed: *Barts Health NHS Trust v Dance (Archie Battersbee)* [2022] EWFC 80; *Fixsler v Manchester University NHS FT* [2021] EWCA Civ 1018.
4. **Standard for proxies (consent lectures).** Parental responsibility must be exercised in the child's best interests; the significant harm test was rejected in Gard and an amendment to the Health and Care Act 2022 to introduce it was not enacted.
5. **Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation)** [2000] EWCA Civ 254 / [2001] Fam 147. Separating Jodie and Mary would save Jodie but kill Mary; without separation both would die. The parents refused consent; the court authorised separation.
   - **Ward LJ**: separation in Jodie's best interests, not Mary's; faced with a conflict of duties, choose the lesser evil / least detrimental alternative; Mary was "beyond help" so the scales came down heavily for Jodie. Criminal law: doctors appreciate that severing the common aorta will kill Mary; double effect "may be difficult to reconcile with R v Woollin".
   - **Brooke LJ**: double effect cannot apply because surgeons could not be acting in good faith in Mary's best interests; but the defence of **necessity** applies: (i) act needed to avoid inevitable and irreparable evil; (ii) no more done than reasonably necessary; (iii) evil inflicted not disproportionate to the evil avoided. All three satisfied.
   - **Robert Walker LJ**: surgery in both twins' best interests, since remaining conjoined deprived each of bodily integrity and human dignity; Mary's death would be the inevitable consequence, not the purpose, of the operation (no intention to kill).
   - Moral issues: are the twins of equal moral status? Should the parents' refusal have prevailed given the decision was so finely balanced (Raanan Gillon)? Parents as proxy decision-makers v as closely affected persons.

## Key authorities
- *Re J* [1991] Fam 33 (intolerability); *Wyatt* [2005] EWCA Civ 1181 (balance sheet, presumption for life).
- *Re B* [1981] 1 WLR 1421 c/f *Re T* [1997] 1 WLR 242.
- *Glass v UK* [2004] ECHR 103: Art 8 breach.
- *Re King* [2014] EWHC 2964 (Fam): parental choice between reasonable options; Baker J's [31] significant harm dictum.
- *Yates and Gard v GOSH* [2017] EWCA Civ 410: best interests prevails; no significant harm threshold.
- *Evans v Alder Hey* [2018] EWCA Civ 805 / UKSC permission determination: best interests is the founding rule.
- *Raqeeb* [2019] EWHC 2530 (Fam); *Verden* [2022] EWCOP 9: cases where parents' preferred course was upheld or treatment found in best interests.
- *Re A (conjoined twins)* [2000] EWCA Civ 254: three different routes to lawfulness.

## Debate and critique
- **Auckland and Goold, 'Parental Rights, Best Interests and Significant Harms' (2019) CLJ 287.** The current threshold for intervention is too low. Parents should have prima facie authority to choose between the available medical options; this authority should yield only where their decision carries a "serious risk of significant harm" to the child. It echoes the threshold argued in Gard and s.31 Children Act 1989, but their justification is ethical: respect for parents' rights, family life and value pluralism.
- **Archard, Cave and Brierley, 'How should we decide how to treat the child: harm versus best interests in cases of disagreement' (2024) 32 Med L Rev 158.** Defend best interests from ethical, legal and clinical perspectives: a harm threshold is no less indeterminate; doctors' legal duties are to act in the patient's best interests, so allowing sub-optimal care creates conflict; parental rights exist to promote the child's welfare, not as ownership.
- **Cave and Nottingham, 'Who Knows Best (Interests)? The Case of Charlie Gard' (2018) 26(3) Med L Rev 500.** The legal principles were "all but lost" in the global debate (interventions from the Pope, Donald Trump, US Congress), but were compassionately and correctly applied. They analyse McFarlane LJ's two readings of King (best interests applied where options were equal, or Baker J simply wrong) and note the need for better facilitation of mediation.
- **Value pluralism.** Raqeeb shows that when a child has no awareness, the "objective" test relies on contested values; this supports critics who say judges impose their own values (Auckland & Goold) and supporters who say only the court can arbitrate neutrally.
- **Re A and moral theory.** Utilitarian reasoning (save one rather than lose two) aligns with Ward LJ; duty-based (Keown/Finnis-style) objections to intentionally killing an innocent would condemn separation unless death is not intended (Robert Walker LJ's double effect approach); rights-based views ask whether Mary was a right-holder at all. Brazier & Cave use Re A to show "do no harm" to Mary meant not doing good for Jodie.
- **Re A and Woollin.** How can Mary's virtually certain death not be intended? This links to the double effect debate in the life-shortening note.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the dispute: parents v doctors (which direction?), or parent v parent.
2. Who can consent? Those with parental responsibility; but doctors cannot be compelled to treat (Re J [1993]; Burke), and they should not override parents without a court order outside emergencies (Glass).
3. Apply to court (wardship, inherent jurisdiction, CA 1989 s.8): welfare paramount (s.1(1)).
4. Best interests (Wyatt [86]): child's assumed point of view; strong rebuttable presumption for life; medical, emotional and other welfare; balance sheet. Ask: is the proposed treatment futile? burdensome? any realistic benefit? Pain or awareness of suffering?
5. Parental views give weight but are not determinative (Gard); where options are genuinely equal the court may let parents choose (King).
6. Reject the significant harm threshold as current law but discuss it in essays (Auckland & Goold v Archard et al).
7. For older children with capacity, add autonomy (E & F balance) and see the refusal note.
8. Conjoined twins type facts: analyse best interests of each child, then criminal liability (intention/Woollin; double effect; necessity per Brooke LJ).
9. Traps: confusing the three Re J cases; treating King as establishing a significant harm test; ignoring Glass when doctors act without court approval.
