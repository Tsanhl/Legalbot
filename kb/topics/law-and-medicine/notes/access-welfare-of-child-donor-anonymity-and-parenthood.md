# Reproductive Medicine 2 - Access to Treatment (Welfare of the Child), Donor Anonymity and Legal Parenthood

## Overview
This topic deals with who may access assisted reproduction and the status of the resulting child. The central access provision is **s.13(5) HFE Act 1990**: treatment may not be provided unless account has been taken of the welfare of any resulting child (including its need for **supportive parenting**). It makes clinicians gatekeepers and raises the philosophical **non-identity problem**. Challenges to refusals of treatment rarely succeed (Harriott, Seale; prisoners: Mellor, Dickson). Commentary also covers **donor anonymity** (removed from 2005 for identifying information at 18) and **legal parenthood** under the HFE Act **2008** (mother, father, second female parent, parental orders). Parenthood must not be confused with parental responsibility.

## The law
**A. Access: the welfare of the child (s.13(5)).**
1. Text: "A woman shall not be provided with treatment services unless account has been taken of the welfare of any child who may be born as a result of the treatment (including the need of that child for supportive parenting), and of any other child who may be affected by the birth."
2. Before the 2008 amendments, it referred to the child's "need ... for a father" (replaced to recognise same-sex parents).
3. "Woman" is the person in whom embryos or gametes are placed (throughout the Act: e.g. ss.3(2), 3ZA(6), 4(4)). "Treatment services" (s.2(1)): medical, surgical or obstetric services provided to the public or a section of it for the purpose of assisting women to carry children (Code [8.1]).
4. Compare s.1(1)(a) Abortion Act ("any existing children of her family").
5. **HFEA Code of Practice (9th edn, v9.4)**:
   - the assessment must be done in a non-discriminatory way (para 8.6);
   - the centre should consider factors likely to cause a risk of significant harm or neglect to any child who may be born or any existing child (para 8.14);
   - supportive parenting is a commitment to the health, well-being and development of the child; all prospective parents are presumed supportive in the absence of reasonable cause for concern (para 8.15);
   - if treatment is refused, the centre should explain in writing (para 8.20).
6. **Challenging refusals** (public bodies, judicial review): *R v Ethics Committee of St Mary's Hospital, ex p Harriott* [1988] 1 FLR 512; *R v Sheffield HA, ex p Seale* (1994) 25 BMLR 1. Now see NICE (2017): up to 3 IVF cycles for women under 40 with more than 2 years of infertility; 1 cycle at 40-41. For other countries see *Lia v Malta* (No 8709/20, 5 May 2022) (2023) 76 EHRR 14 [36].
7. **Prisoners**: *R (Mellor) v SoS for the Home Department* [2002] QB 13 (Arts 8 and 12); *Dickson v Premier Prisons* [2004] EWCA Civ 1477 (CA: Secretary of State lawfully denied access to artificial insemination); *Dickson v UK* (No 44362/04): Chamber 18 April 2006, (2007) 44 EHRR 21; Grand Chamber 4 December 2007, (2008) 46 EHRR 41.

**B. Donor information.**
1. The HFEA keeps a register of donors since 1991 (s.31). s.31ZA: request for information about genetic parentage.
2. HFEA (Disclosure of Donor Information) Regulations 2004/1511: at **16**, entitled to non-identifying information (reg 2(2)); at **18**, identifying information (reg 2(3)) for donations after 31 March 2005.
3. Effects of removing anonymity on donor profile: median sperm donor age rose from under 30 to 36; donors with children of their own fell (sperm 45% to 18%; eggs 54% to 39%).
4. HFEA, *Modernising Fertility Law* (Nov 2023): recommends amending the 1990 Act to enable removal of donor anonymity from the birth of any donor-conceived child.
5. 10-family limit per donor (Code 11.9, 11.56).

**C. Legal parenthood (HFE Act 2008).** No more than two legal parents: a mother and either a father or another parent.
1. **Mother**: the woman who is carrying or has carried the child (s.33), unless the child is adopted (s.33(2)) or parenthood is transferred by parental order (ss.54/54A). The egg donor is not automatically a parent (s.47).
2. **Father**:
   - the sperm donor is not the father (s.41(1));
   - if the mother is married to, or the civil partner of, a man, he is the father of a child conceived with donor sperm unless he did not consent (s.35) (illustration of the complexities: *Leeds Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust v A* [2003] EWHC 259; no need to read);
   - if neither s.35 nor s.42 applies, a man can be the father of a child created with donor sperm in licensed UK treatment if the **agreed fatherhood conditions** are met (s.36); s.37(1): licensed treatment provided to the woman; the man consents to being treated as father; the woman consents; neither has withdrawn; the woman has not since consented to someone else being the other parent; the two are not within prohibited degrees (s.58(2)); consents in writing and signed (s.37(2));
   - only a father by virtue of s.35 or s.36 is the father (s.38).
3. **Deceased fathers**: a man whose sperm (or an embryo from it) is used after his death is not the father (s.41(2)); but may be registered as father for birth registration only if he consented in writing to the posthumous use and to being treated as father, and the woman elects this within 42 days (s.39); s.40 covers a husband/civil partner (or unmarried partner meeting conditions) who dies before transfer of an embryo created with donor sperm.
4. **Female second parent**: a female civil partner (or wife) is treated as the other parent as a husband would be (s.42); an unmarried female partner via the **agreed female parenthood conditions** (ss.43-44); deceased female partner parallel to s.40 (s.46).
5. **Parental orders** (ss.54, 54A): re-register the birth and make the applicants legal parents with parental responsibility (surrogacy context). Since 2019 regulations, s.54 deals with two applicants and s.54A with one. Conditions (s.54): child conceived by IVF, GIFT or artificial insemination using gametes of at least one applicant; applicants married, civil partners or in an enduring family relationship and not within prohibited degrees; application within 6 months of birth (but after 6 weeks); child living with applicants; applicants at least 18; consent of the surrogate and any other parent; no prohibited payment (beyond reasonable expenses) unless authorised by the court. s.54A mirrors these for a single applicant (child carried by a woman who is not the applicant).
6. **Parenthood ≠ parental responsibility**: parental responsibility (legal authority, e.g. to consent to treatment) was covered under consent (Children Act 1989).

## Debate and critique
- **Why consider the welfare of a child who exists only because of the treatment?** Derek Parfit's **non-identity problem** (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, ch 16): the decision does not change the situation of a particular future person, it changes *which* person exists. Theories answer differently: utilitarians count the future child's welfare in overall utility; virtue theory treats the assessment as revealing non-virtuous motives; rights and duty theories see it as revealing an intention to violate the future child's rights, while accepting there can be no right not to be brought into existence. Theories defining harm as making a particular individual worse off (rights- and duty-based) face the non-identity problem most acutely (Pattinson textbook p 282).
- **Medicalised gatekeeping.** s.13(5) gives clinicians wide discretion, potentially beyond their medical expertise (cf. s.4(2) MCA, requiring consideration of all relevant circumstances). **Lee, Macvarish and Sheldon** ('Accessing child welfare under the HFE Act 2008: a case study in medicalisation') challenge the idea that replacing "a father" with "supportive parenting" was unequivocally progressive, situating it in policy attention to "parenting"; their interview study of clinic staff shows professionals' understandings resonate with wider oversight of parent-child relations; in practice the power is used with "a very light touch".
- **Discrimination and equality.** The old "need for a father" wording disadvantaged single women and same-sex couples; non-discrimination is now required (Code 8.6), yet the assessment still singles out those needing assisted conception, whereas natural reproduction is unscreened.
- **Resource rationing.** NICE limits and local criteria (Seale) restrict NHS access; only 27% of IVF cycles NHS-funded in 2023.
- **Prisoners' reproduction.** Mellor and Dickson (CA) upheld the Secretary of State's policy of restricting access; the challenge then went to Strasbourg (Chamber 2006, Grand Chamber 2007). The sources list but do not summarise the Strasbourg outcome, so check the judgment before relying on it.
- **Donor anonymity.** Children's interest in knowing genetic origins (supported by HFEA 2023 proposals) v donor supply and privacy; the change altered the donor profile.
- **Reform**: the example introduction proposes focusing reform on s.13(5)'s discretion while defending the overall structure.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the service a "treatment service" requiring a licence (s.2(1); s.4)? Then s.13(5) applies.
2. Has account been taken of the welfare of the future child and existing children (supportive parenting; risk of significant harm or neglect, Code 8.14)? Presumption of supportive parenting (8.15); non-discriminatory (8.6); written reasons if refused (8.20).
3. Challenge to refusal: judicial review of a public body (Harriott; Seale); human rights (Art 8, 12; Mellor; Dickson).
4. Counselling (s.13(6)) where donor gametes/embryos used.
5. Donor-conceived child's information rights at 16 and 18.
6. Parenthood: identify the gestational mother (s.33); her marital/civil partnership status; consent of spouse (s.35/s.42); unmarried partner's written agreed-parenthood consents (ss.36-37, 43-44); donor not a parent (s.41); posthumous situations (ss.39-40, 46); surrogacy → parental order conditions (s.54/54A).
7. Traps: confusing parenthood with parental responsibility; applying the 1990 Act parenthood provisions (replaced by the 2008 Act); treating s.13(5) as a bar on single women or same-sex couples.
