# Adult Capacity - the Mental Capacity Act 2005 Test and the Right to Refuse

## Overview
Capacity is the first and largest component of valid consent. If an adult has capacity, their decision (including a refusal of life-saving treatment) must be respected, whatever their reasons. If they lack capacity, others may act in their best interests under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA). So the capacity finding is usually the hinge of a problem question. The MCA (ss.1-3) codified the earlier common law test (Re T, Re C, Re MB), and the Supreme Court in *A Local Authority v JB* [2021] UKSC 52 fixed the order in which the statutory questions are asked.

## The law
**1. Capacity v competence.**
- *Capacity* (legal capacity): the legal power to give a valid consent or refusal, i.e. to exercise legal rights.
- *Competence* (mental capacity): the cognitive ability to make the decision in the situation.

**2. Possible tests for capacity.**
- *Competence-based / cognitive functional* test: capacity depends on the ability to decide.
- *Reasonable outcome* test: capacity only if the decision is regarded as reasonable.
- *Expression of preference* test: anyone able to express a preference has capacity.
- *Status* test: capacity attaches to a status (e.g. age or qualification).
- *Combination* test.
England and Wales use a **competence-based (functional) test** for adults, under both the MCA and the prior common law. For children there is a mix of status and competence elements (see the children notes). Brazier & Cave (ch 7) stress that the functional test asks whether the person has the understanding needed for the *particular* decision.

**3. Consequences of having capacity (common law).**
- An adult with capacity can refuse treatment in almost all circumstances, even if others think the decision irrational. *Re T (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* [1992] EWCA Civ 18, Lord Donaldson MR: such a patient has an "absolute right" to consent or refuse, and this applies whether the reasons are "rational, irrational, unknown or even non-existent". He added that the only possible qualification was where the choice might lead to the death of a viable fetus (later rejected: see the obstetric note).
- *Re B (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment)* [2002] EWHC 429 (Fam) (also called Ms B v An NHS Hospital Trust): a tetraplegic woman with capacity could require her ventilator to be switched off; treating her against her wishes was trespass (nominal damages of £100).
- *Re W (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* [2002] EWHC 901 (Fam) is listed as further required reading on the same point.

**4. MCA 2005 - scope and principles.**
- Applies to those aged **16 or over** who are reasonably believed to lack capacity under ss.2-3 (s.2(5)).
- It codifies and replaces the common law: *ZH v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2012] EWHC 604, [44]. But the High Court keeps an **inherent jurisdiction** for vulnerable adults (*Re L / DL* [2012]; see the voluntariness note).
- **Section 1 principles:**
  - s.1(2) presumption of capacity unless lack of capacity is established;
  - s.1(3) not to be treated as unable to decide unless all practicable steps to help have failed;
  - s.1(4) not to be treated as unable to decide merely because the decision is unwise;
  - s.1(5) acts/decisions for a person lacking capacity must be in their best interests;
  - s.1(6) consider whether the purpose can be achieved in a less restrictive way.

**5. Common law forerunners of the test.**
- *Re T* [1992]: presumption of capacity; capacity may be removed "by reason of temporary factors, such as unconsciousness or confusion or other effects of shock, severe fatigue, pain or drugs".
- *Re C (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment)* [1994] 1 All ER 819 / [1994] 1 WLR 290: patient must be able to (a) comprehend and retain, (b) believe, and (c) weigh the relevant information.
- *Re MB (Caesarean Section)* [1997] EWCA Civ 1361: a person lacks capacity only if "some impairment or disturbance of mental functioning renders the person unable to make a decision", judged by the Re C test. Temporary incapacity through needle phobia.

**6. The statutory two-stage test (ss.2-3).** Under s.2(1), a person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if at the material time they are unable to make a decision for themselves in relation to the matter *because of* an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain. Following *A Local Authority v JB* [2021] UKSC 52, [67]-[79], ask in this order:
- **(1) Functional question:** is the patient unable to make a decision for himself in relation to the matter? (s.3(1))
- **(2) Causal/diagnostic question:** if so, is that inability "because of" an impairment of, or disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain? (s.2(1))
- If yes to both, the patient lacks capacity. Note JB reversed the order in the 2007 Code of Practice and in Pattinson 2020, which put the diagnostic question first.
- Brazier & Cave also note JB [92]: the relevant information includes the "reasonably foreseeable consequences" of deciding or not deciding, and these are not limited to consequences for P.

**7. Section 3(1): inability to decide.** P is unable to decide if unable:
- (a) to understand the information relevant to the decision;
- (b) to retain it;
- (c) to use or weigh it as part of the decision-making process;
- (d) to communicate the decision (by any means).
Supplementary rules: information includes the reasonably foreseeable consequences of deciding one way or the other, or failing to decide (s.3(4); *A v A* [2010] EWHC 1549); retaining information for a short period only does not prevent capacity (s.3(3), relevant to fluctuating capacity). Brazier & Cave note it is enough to understand the salient features (*Mental Health Trust v DD* [2014] EWCOP 13) and that using or weighing has been held to include believing the information (*Local Authority X v MM* [2007]).

**8. Section 2(1): the impairment and causation.** Two aspects: (a) an impairment/disturbance, which may be permanent or temporary, and (b) the inability must be *caused* by it. The Code of Practice para 4.12 gives examples: some forms of mental illness, dementia, significant learning disabilities, long-term effects of brain damage, physical or medical conditions causing confusion, drowsiness or loss of consciousness, delirium, concussion, and the symptoms of alcohol or drug use.

**9. Code of Practice.** Issued Feb 2007 under s.42; under s.45(5) it can be used in evidence in civil and criminal proceedings. A revised draft was issued for consultation in 2022 (also covering Liberty Protection Safeguards under the Mental Capacity (Amendment) Act 2019); commentary notes the DoLS reform has been "paused" and the 2019 Act not commenced, so the 2007 Code still applies though partly out of date.

## Key authorities
- *Re T (Adult: Refusal of Treatment)* [1992] EWCA Civ 18 / [1993] Fam 95: absolute right of a capacitous adult to refuse; temporary factors can remove capacity; also key on undue influence and advance refusals.
- *Re C* [1994] 1 All ER 819: C, with chronic paranoid schizophrenia and delusions (e.g. that he had a distinguished medical career), refused below-knee amputation for gangrene (said to give about a 15% survival chance without it). Thorpe J held his general capacity was impaired but he still understood, retained, believed and weighed the treatment information, so he could refuse; injunction granted. Shows capacity is decision-specific and mental illness is not incapacity.
- *Re MB* [1997] EWCA Civ 1361: impairment must cause inability; needle phobia temporarily removed capacity. Butler-Sloss LJ (as quoted by Brazier & Cave): the graver the consequences, the greater the level of competence required.
- *Re B* [2002] EWHC 429 (Fam): Butler-Sloss P warned against confusing capacity with the nature of the decision; the patient's view may reflect a difference in values rather than lack of competence. Previous ambivalence was irrelevant unless it went to the root of capacity; requiring experience of rehabilitation was unrealistic; £100 nominal damages for trespass.
- *ZH v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2012] EWHC 604, [44]: MCA replaces the common law (including necessity).
- *A Local Authority v JB* [2021] UKSC 52: functional question first, causative link second; [92] reasonably foreseeable consequences include those for others.
- *A v A* [2010] EWHC 1549: s.3(4) information about consequences.
- *Heart of England NHS FT v JB* [2014] EWHC 342 (COP) (from Brazier & Cave): Peter Jackson J warned against letting "the tail of welfare" wag "the dog of capacity".
- MCA 2005 ss.1, 2, 3, 2(5), 42, 45(5); Code of Practice ch 2 and Part 4 (esp para 4.12).

## Debate and critique
- **Outcome creep.** The law says rationality of the decision should not determine capacity, but Brazier & Cave observe capacity is rarely challenged unless others disagree with the outcome, and judges do not wholly ignore outcomes (Re MB: graver decisions need more competence). Is the graduated standard a disguised reasonable-outcome test?
- **"Use or weigh" as a flexible gateway.** A critique of "incapacity" as a label used to override autonomous wishes must pin down exactly which part of the test is misused, distinguishing the diagnostic and functional thresholds as in Re C, Re W [2002] and JB. "Weighing" is the element most open to value-laden judgments (e.g. anorexia cases).
- **Borderline acquiescence.** Brazier & Cave ask whether we should worry more about patients of borderline capacity who simply go along with doctors or family, since their capacity is never tested.
- **Communication and learning disability.** It can be too easy to assume incapacity in dementia or learning disability; s.1(3) requires practicable steps to help.
- **Ordering after JB.** Putting the functional question first is meant to prevent a diagnosis alone driving the finding; commentary flags that older texts (and the 2007 Code) use the reverse order.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Age**: 18+ adult; 16-17 (FLRA s.8(1) presumption, rebutted only by the MCA test); under 16 (Gillick).
2. **Start with the presumption** (s.1(2)). The burden is on those alleging incapacity.
3. **Decision-specific**: identify the precise decision (e.g. refusing an amputation requiring a blood transfusion).
4. **Functional test (s.3(1))**: can P understand, retain, use/weigh, communicate? Use the facts: pain, fear, fatigue, drugs, shock (Re T's temporary factors), phobia (Re MB), delusions (Re C).
5. **Causal test (s.2(1))**: is any inability because of an impairment/disturbance of mind or brain (Code 4.12 examples)?
6. **Practicable steps (s.1(3))** and **unwise decisions (s.1(4))**: has the hospital tried to help? Is the refusal merely unwise or religious?
7. **Who assesses?** The doctor must have a *reasonable belief*; advise what further information the hospital should gather. Do not decide the capacity question yourself where facts are missing; give alternative advice.
8. **Fluctuating capacity**: s.3(3) (brief retention may suffice); consider waiting for a lucid interval.
9. **Outcome**: capacity → follow the decision (Re B; treating = trespass). No capacity → advance refusal? proxy? s.5 GLA and best interests (next note). Doubt → seek a declaration (s.15).
10. Traps: equating mental illness with incapacity (Re C); treating a refusal for religious reasons as evidence of incapacity; using the reverse JB order without comment.
