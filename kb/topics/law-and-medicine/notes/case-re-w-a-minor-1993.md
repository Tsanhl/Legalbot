# Re W (A Minor) (Medical Treatment: Court's Jurisdiction) [1993] Fam 64; [1992] 4 All ER 627 (CA) (case note)

> Written from Lowe and Juss, 'Medical Treatment – Pragmatism and the Search for Principle' (1993) 56 MLR 865, which
> sets out the facts and holdings with All ER page references. The judgment itself is not held.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Lord Donaldson MR, Balcombe and Nolan LJJ). Can the High Court authorise treatment of a **16-year-old**
against her refusal? Yes. The leading authority that **neither FLRA 1969 s 8 nor Gillick competence gives a minor a veto**.

## Facts
W, 16, in local authority care after her parents' deaths and a series of losses, developed **anorexia nervosa** at 14.
Her condition deteriorated; she was treated in a specialist unit (Lord Donaldson stressed she had in fact consented to the
nasogastric tube). The local authority sought, under the High Court's **inherent jurisdiction** (Children Act 1989 s 100(3)
leave), authority to move her to a new unit and to treat her **without her consent**.

## Held (unanimous)
1. **FLRA 1969 s 8(1)** lets a 16–17-year-old give a **valid consent** (no parental consent needed) but gives **no absolute
   power to veto** treatment; s 8(3) preserves other effective consents.
2. **Gillick** decided only that a competent under-16 can **consent**; it did not give a power of veto, and it did not limit
   the **court's** power.
3. Under its **inherent (parens patriae) jurisdiction** the High Court can, after careful regard to the child's views,
   **override even a 16 or 17-year-old's refusal** in the child's best interests. The inherent jurisdiction is co-extensive with
   wardship. Balcombe LJ: the point comes where refusal threatens **death or severe permanent injury**.
- Lord Donaldson replaced his "**keyholder**" analogy from *Re R* ("keys can lock as well as unlock") with the "**flak jacket**":
  the doctor needs only one valid consent (from the child, a person with parental responsibility, or the court).
- Lord Donaldson doubted W was in fact Gillick-competent: anorexia can destroy the ability to make an informed choice. The
  trial judge had found her competent.

## Debate and critique
- Seen as a blow to children's autonomy: consent and refusal are treated asymmetrically ("a yes without a no").
- **Lowe and Juss defend it**: a pragmatic, case-by-case approach; consenting to treatment the doctor recommends is different
  from vetoing treatment for one's benefit; the court "should be very slow to allow an infant to martyr himself"
  (Ward J, *Re E*).
- Contrast the adult position (a competent adult may refuse even life-saving treatment: *Re T*, *Re MB*, *Ms B*), and later
  developments such as *Re X (No 2)* and *Bell v Tavistock* in the children notes.

## Applying it to a problem question
Child 16–17: consent valid under s 8; refusal can be overridden by a person with parental responsibility or, in practice, the
court, if treatment is in the child's best interests, especially where refusal risks death or serious permanent harm.
Under 16: check Gillick competence, then the same override analysis.
