# Voluntariness, Undue Influence and the Inherent Jurisdiction over Vulnerable Adults

## Overview
The third component of a valid consent or refusal is voluntariness: the decision must be the patient's own, not the product of coercion or undue influence. The leading case is *Re T* (1992), where a refusal of blood was influenced by the patient's mother. The threshold for vitiating a decision is high, as *U v Centre for Reproductive Medicine* (2002) shows, and Pattinson argues it is too high. After the MCA 2005, the High Court's **inherent jurisdiction** survives as a "safety net" for adults who have capacity under the MCA but whose decisions are compromised by undue influence or other factors (*Re L / DL* 2012), though its application in medical law is very limited.

## The law
1. **Undue influence (pre-MCA common law).** *Re T (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment)* [1992] EWCA Civ 18:
   - The question is whether the patient "really mean[s] what he says or is he merely saying it for a quiet life" (Lord Donaldson MR).
   - Two aspects are of crucial importance: **(i) the strength of the patient's will** – someone very tired, in pain or depressed is much less able to resist than someone rested, pain-free and cheerful; and **(ii) the relationship of the "persuader"** to the patient.
   - Butler-Sloss LJ: influence may be "subtle, insidious, pervasive" and especially powerful where religious beliefs are involved.
   - Persuasion, however strong, does not vitiate a decision unless it overbears the independence of the patient's decision (Re T as applied in U).
2. **High threshold.** *U v Centre for Reproductive Medicine* [2002] EWCA Civ 565:
   - Facts (from Pattinson's article): Mr U consented in writing to posthumous use of his stored sperm. At a later meeting a specialist nursing sister, applying the clinic's ethical policy against posthumous treatment, asked him to change the form; he did. He then died unexpectedly. Mrs U claimed it was "strongly implied" that treatment depended on the change.
   - Butler-Sloss P at first instance accepted the pressure "must have been considerable" but held that more than pressure is needed: the will must be overborne. It was hard to say that an able, intelligent, educated man of 47, with a responsible job and in good health, had his will overborne. The Court of Appeal (Hale LJ) upheld this, so the withdrawal of consent stood.
   - The burden of proving undue influence lay on Mrs U; this was "not a situation in which undue influence is to be presumed".
3. **Inherent jurisdiction (post-MCA).** *Re L* (also *DL v A Local Authority*) [2012] EWCA Civ 253:
   - Parents who just satisfied the MCA capacity test but were vulnerable to their son's undue influence regarding their residence.
   - Held: the inherent jurisdiction survived the MCA. Absent express provision, where matters fall outside the statutory scheme the jurisdiction continues as the "great safety net" (McFarlane LJ).
   - It applies to individuals who lack capacity for a reason *other than* an impairment or disturbance in the functioning of the mind or brain (e.g. constraint, coercion, undue influence).
4. **Gap cases.** *A NHS Trust v A* [2013] EWHC 2442: Dr A, who had a delusional disorder, went on hunger strike; he lacked capacity under the MCA but fell between the MCA and the Mental Health Act. The inherent jurisdiction could authorise a deprivation of liberty, including feeding under restraint, for treatment not for his mental disorder. Not an undue influence case.
5. **Limited reach in medical law.** Commentators stress the IJ has very limited application to medical decisions: Re L concerned multiple decisions all unduly influenced, and A concerned a gap in the law. Further (extended): *Southend-on-Sea BC v Meyers* [2019] EWHC 399 (Fam) (Hayden J extends the ambit of the IJ); *Norfolk and Suffolk NHS FT v HJ* [2023] EWFC 92; Sir James Munby, *Whither the Inherent Jurisdiction* (2020).

## Key authorities
- *Re T* [1992] EWCA Civ 18 / [1993] Fam 95: undue influence by mother on a pregnant young woman refusing blood; strength of will and relationship of persuader; religious influence especially powerful. Also authority for the absolute right of capacitous refusal and temporary incapacity.
- *U v Centre for Reproductive Medicine* [2002] EWCA Civ 565: undue influence requires the will to be overborne; no presumption; withdrawal of consent to posthumous use of sperm upheld. (Also a HFEA Act Sch 3 consent case.)
- *Re L / DL v A Local Authority* [2012] EWCA Civ 253: IJ survives MCA as a safety net for vulnerable capacitous adults.
- *A NHS Trust v A* [2013] EWHC 2442: IJ used where P fell between the MCA and the MHA.
- *Southend-on-Sea BC v Meyers* [2019] EWHC 399 (Fam): expansion of the IJ (extended reading).

## Debate and critique
- **Pattinson (2002) 'Undue Influence in the Context of Medical Treatment' 5 Med Law Int 305.** Argues *U* was wrong on its facts. In equity (transaction cases such as *Etridge*), evidential presumptions of undue influence protect vulnerable people in relationships of trust. Medical treatment is a context of dependence, and the couple were vulnerable (committed mentally, emotionally and financially to treatment, as the CA itself noted). Pattinson argues vulnerable patients should be protected from overbearing *ethical* advice by an **evidential presumption** that any such pressure rendered the decision involuntary. He also criticises deference to the medical profession's ethical views, noting the clinic had no statutory power to direct patients to accept its ethical stance.
- **Contrasting views.** Morgan and Devenney (2003): "it is difficult to be over-critical of the decision". Grubb (on Re T, 1993): "One can only agree with [the] President's conclusion".
- **Autonomy v protection.** The IJ protects autonomy by freeing decision-making from coercion (commentary frames it as enhancing the autonomy of the vulnerable), but critics fear it allows courts to override capacitous adults outside the MCA's safeguards; Meyers is cited as an expansion.
- **Religion and family.** Re T recognises religious influence as especially powerful. In problem questions involving sudden conversion after talking to a relative (Salla and her mother), this is the core issue, but a conversion is not by itself evidence of undue influence.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Look for signals: a sudden change of mind after private discussion with a relative or adviser; dependence; pain, exhaustion, depression; religious pressure; a clinic's policy pushing a patient.
2. Apply *Re T*: strength of the patient's will at the time (pain, fatigue) and relationship with the persuader (mother, spouse, religious figure). Does the patient really mean it?
3. Remember the threshold (*U*): pressure or persuasion is not enough; the will must be overborne. No presumption – the person alleging undue influence must prove it.
4. If undue influence is established, the refusal is not a valid decision. Doctors must then act on another lawful justification (s.5 MCA if incapacitous; otherwise seek court guidance, possibly the IJ for a capacitous but unduly influenced adult).
5. Practical advice: speak to the patient alone, give time, re-check later; seek a declaration if doubt remains.
6. Distinguish incapacity (MCA) from involuntariness (common law/IJ). A patient may have capacity yet be unduly influenced (Re L).
7. Example: Salla's conversion after talking privately to her mother and her father's claim she is "just saying this to keep her mother happy" – apply Re T; she is also in extreme pain. Advise further assessment; the father's wishes do not authorise treatment of an adult.
