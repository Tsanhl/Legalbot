# End of Life (Adults) 4 - Withholding and Withdrawing Life-Sustaining Treatment from Adults Lacking Capacity (PDOC)

## Overview
This is the "non-voluntary" end-of-life category: the patient cannot decide, and the question is whether life-sustaining treatment, including clinically assisted nutrition and hydration (CANH), may be withheld or withdrawn. *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* (1993) held that CANH is medical treatment, that withdrawing it is an omission, and that there is no duty to continue treatment that is not in the patient's best interests. The MCA 2005 now governs (s.4 best interests; s.4(5) no motivation to cause death). Since *Aintree v James* (2013) best interests are assessed from the patient's point of view, and since *An NHS Trust v Y* (2018) court applications are not routinely required. The law has moved from patients in a vegetative state to those in minimally conscious states.

## The law
1. **Terminology.** Older cases use permanent or persistent vegetative state (PVS/VS: no reproducible responses); later cases recognise the minimally conscious state (MCS: simple responses to the environment). Disorders of consciousness exist on a spectrum, chronic or permanent; "prolonged disorders of consciousness" (PDOC) now covers coma, VS and MCS (RCP guideline 2020 recommended for background).
2. **Moral status of PDOC patients**: full status, no status, limited status. Rights-based and preference utilitarian views: patients in PVS have little or no status. Sanctity of life (Keown): full status; wrong to kill them intentionally, but no moral obligation to give worthless treatment.
3. ***Airedale NHS Trust v Bland*** [1993] AC 789. Anthony Bland was crushed at Hillsborough (1989) aged 17 and left in PVS, his higher brain functions destroyed. Held:
   - omitting life-sustaining treatment is unlawful only where there is a **duty** to provide it;
   - **CANH is medical treatment**;
   - removing CANH is an **omission**;
   - doctors are obliged to treat an incapacitated patient only in his best interests;
   - continued CANH was not in his best interests (no prospect of improvement);
   - therefore it was lawful to withdraw CANH and withhold antibiotics.
   - The question is not whether it is in the patient's best interests to die, but whether it is in his best interests that his life be prolonged by continuing treatment (Lord Goff; Lord Browne-Wilkinson, as quoted in *Aintree* [22]).
   - **Safeguards**: (a) good practice to seek a declaration; (b) 6 months minimum for rehabilitation attempts; (c) 12 months minimum before diagnosis confirmed; (d) diagnosis verified by two independent doctors; (e) "great weight" to the family's views.
   - **Safeguards later weakened or disregarded**: e.g. *Frenchay Healthcare NHS Trust v S* [1994], *Re G* [1995], *Re D* [1998], *Re G* (2002), *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018].
4. **MCA 2005.** PDOC patients will usually lack capacity. s.1(5): decisions must be in best interests. s.4(5): when the determination relates to life-sustaining treatment, the decision-maker must not be motivated by a desire to bring about death. s.4(10): life-sustaining treatment is that which, in the view of the healthcare provider, is necessary to sustain life. s.4(6)-(7): past and present wishes, beliefs and values; consultation.
5. ***Aintree University Hospitals NHS FT v James*** [2013] UKSC 67 (Lady Hale):
   - [35] "the starting point is a strong presumption that it is in a person's best interests to stay alive", but it is not absolute;
   - [39] decision-makers must consider welfare in the widest sense (medical, social, psychological), the nature and prospects of the treatment, its likely outcome, try to put themselves in the patient's place and ask what his attitude would be, and consult carers and others interested in his welfare;
   - [40]-[44] the judge rightly asked whether treatment was **futile** in the sense of ineffective or of no benefit to the patient, rather than whether it would cure the underlying condition; "recovery" means resuming a quality of life the *patient* would regard as worthwhile, not "good health";
   - [45] the purpose of the test is to consider matters **from the patient's point of view** (rejecting an objective "reasonable patient" test), though his wishes need not prevail.
6. **When must the court be involved?** *Bland*: good practice. ***An NHS Trust v Y*** [2018] UKSC 46: CANH is medical treatment that may be withdrawn when no longer in P's best interests without the doctor committing murder; going to court is **not a legal requirement**; seek a declaration if the decision is finely balanced or disputed and life hangs in the balance; neither court nor patient can require a doctor to provide treatment they consider clinically unjustified.
7. **A more patient-centred approach.**
   - *W v M & Ors* [2011] EWHC 2443 (Fam) (Baker J): first application to withdraw ANH from an MCS patient; refused. VS: the balance falls in favour of withdrawal in every case; MCS: balance sheet. Life preserved; importance of P's wishes and feelings.
   - *M v N* [2015] EWCOP 76 (Hayden J): Mrs N's views expressed through her family; she would have found her circumstances "profoundly humiliating" and wished not to be a burden; choice "where rational, informed and un-coerced is the essence of autonomy" so those who would not wish to live in this way must have their views respected; preserving her life would be disrespectful to her.
   - *Briggs v Briggs* [2016] EWCOP 53 (Charles J; also cited: [2016] EWCOP 48 at [28]): clash between the treating consultant's sanctity of life view and Mr Briggs' autonomy, which his family sought to vindicate; per Halliday, Charles J held that if the decision P would have made can be ascertained with sufficient certainty it should generally prevail. (A preliminary scope point was overruled in *Director of Legal Aid Casework v Briggs* [2017] EWCA Civ 1169 – not relevant.)
   - Additional: *Royal Bournemouth and Christchurch Hospitals NHS FT v TG* [2019] EWCOP 21 (impact of faith and evidence of wishes); *Sandwell and West Birmingham NHS Trust v TW and FY* [2021] EWCOP 13.
8. **Doctors cannot be required to treat.** *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018]; *Re AA* [2024] EWCOP 39 (Henke J: dispute with AA's parents; only one available option, so "no best interest decision to make").
9. **Postscript: weight of wishes.** *Re E (Medical Treatment: Anorexia)* [2012] EWHC 1639 (COP): E, 32, with severe anorexia nervosa, had signed an advance decision refusing treatment. Peter Jackson J held she lacked capacity (her fear of weight gain prevented weighing; sedation clouded judgment), that evidence of capacity when making the AD was equivocal so it did not apply, and noted her "Catch-22" (by deciding not to eat she proves she lacks capacity) (Brazier & Cave). In weighing her wishes he said: "We only live once ... E is a special person, whose life is of value. She does not see it that way now, but she may in future."
10. **Summary**: VS – declaration should be sought (now only if disputed/finely balanced), routinely granted (Bland; s.4); MCS – careful balancing (Aintree). Bland was restricted to PVS but later cases apply to MCS; best interests is interpreted broadly from P's point of view.

## Key authorities
- *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] AC 789: CANH treatment; withdrawal an omission; best interests.
- *Aintree v James* [2013] UKSC 67 [35], [39]-[45]: presumption for life; futility; patient's point of view.
- *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018] UKSC 46: no mandatory court involvement.
- *W v M* [2011] EWHC 2443 (Fam); *M v N* [2015] EWCOP 76; *Briggs v Briggs* [2016] EWCOP 53; *TG* [2019] EWCOP 21.
- *Re E (Anorexia)* [2012] EWHC 1639 (COP); *Re AA* [2024] EWCOP 39.
- MCA 2005 ss.1(5), 4(5), 4(6), 4(7), 4(10).

## Debate and critique
- **Acts and omissions.** Bland's characterisation of removing a feeding tube as an omission is the classic target of critique: the intended outcome is death, yet active euthanasia remains murder. John Finnis ('Bland: Crossing the Rubicon' (1993) 109 LQR 329, listed as further reading) criticised the decision from a full status perspective; Keown accepts no duty to give worthless treatment but resists any intention to kill.
- **Is CANH "treatment"?** Treating food and water by tube as medical treatment allows withdrawal; the MCA Code says ANH may be refused by advance decision, while basic care (food by mouth) cannot.
- **Patient-centred best interests.** Aintree, M v N and Briggs move toward substituted judgment, honouring the person's values; supporters see respect for autonomy and dignity; critics (and the note that the MCA prioritises welfare) ask whether this sits with s.4's objective framing and the presumption for life. Halliday contrasts this approach with obstetric cases where women's wishes receive little weight.
- **Reduced court oversight.** Y removes a procedural safeguard; supporters cite cost, delay and distress to families; critics worry about inconsistent practice and protection of disabled people (Wicks and Huxtable are cited by Brazier & Cave).
- **Who decides?** Doctors control which options exist (Re AA 2024: no choice to make), so best interests operates within clinical gatekeeping.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm incapacity (ss.1-3) and check for an advance refusal (ss.24-26, writing/witness/"even if life at risk") and any LPA/deputy.
2. Characterise the act: withdrawal/withholding (omission) v active ending of life (murder).
3. Is there a duty to continue? Only if treatment is in best interests (Bland).
4. Best interests (s.4; Aintree): presumption for life; futility (ineffective/no benefit); burdens; quality of life the patient would regard as worthwhile; patient's point of view; past wishes (s.4(6)); family views (s.4(7)); not motivated by desire to cause death (s.4(5)).
5. VS v MCS: stronger case for withdrawal in VS; MCS requires careful balance (W v M; M v N; Briggs).
6. Court: apply for a declaration if finely balanced or disputed (Y); otherwise follow professional guidance.
7. Doctors cannot be compelled to provide treatment they consider clinically inappropriate (Y; Burke).
8. Example: if Simon lacks capacity and his oral request is not a binding advance decision, his earlier wishes and current pleas feed into best interests; Constance must be consulted; withdrawal of ventilation is lawful only if continued ventilation is not in his best interests. Disputes → declaration.
9. Traps: describing Bland's safeguards as current mandatory law; treating MCS like PVS; forgetting s.4(5).
