# Re E (A Minor) (Wardship: Medical Treatment) [1993] 1 FLR 386 (case note)

> Caution: the original judgment was not read. This note relies on later judgments and secondary sources, so check any wording or pinpoint against the report at [1993] 1 FLR 386.

## Overview

A Family Division decision of Ward J, given on 21 September 1990 and reported in 1993. The court authorised blood transfusions for a Jehovah's Witness boy with leukaemia who was just under 16, overriding both his refusal and his parents'. It is a leading example of a demanding approach to Gillick competence where refusing treatment is likely to mean death.

## Facts

- The boy was 15, a few months short of 16, and was being treated in hospital for leukaemia.
- His treatment needed blood transfusions. He and his parents were all Jehovah's Witnesses and refused consent to blood on religious grounds.
- On the medical evidence, there was a strong risk that he would die without a transfusion.
- The hospital authority applied in wardship for leave to treat him as it considered necessary, including with blood. He was made a ward of court, and the Official Solicitor acted on his behalf.

## Held

Ward J gave the hospital leave to treat him, including by transfusion.

- **Section 8 FLRA 1969 not engaged.** The boy was under 16, so he fell outside s 8 of the Family Law Reform Act 1969. The question was therefore his competence under *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1986] AC 112.
- **Not Gillick competent.** The judge found that the boy did not sufficiently understand the full implications of refusal, in particular what the process of dying would involve. He therefore lacked the understanding needed to make this decision.
- **Welfare paramount.** In wardship, the child's welfare governs. The boy's wishes and beliefs were given weight but were overridden.
- As later quoted by Balcombe LJ in *Re W*, Ward J said: "There is compelling and overwhelming force in the submission of the Official Solicitor that this court, exercising its prerogative of protection, should be very slow to allow an infant to martyr himself."

## Later treatment

- *Re W (A Minor) (Medical Treatment: Court's Jurisdiction)* [1993] Fam 64 (CA): Balcombe LJ cited Re E, then unreported, as an example of a minor refusing treatment where death was a strong risk. He said Ward J was right to authorise the transfusion.
- *Re G (Education: Religious Upbringing)* [2012] EWCA Civ 1233 at [44]: Munby LJ cited Re E for the point that Jehovah's Witness beliefs will not stop the court ordering a blood transfusion for a child, even where both parents and child vehemently object.
- *E & F (Minors: Blood Transfusion)* [2021] EWCA Civ 1888 at [55]: in appeals about a 16-year-old and a 17-year-old Jehovah's Witness, the Court of Appeal quoted Balcombe LJ's passage on Re E, including the "martyr himself" statement.
- Postscript (not a legal development): later accounts report that, as an adult, he refused a further transfusion and died. The accounts differ on his age at the time.

## Applying it to a problem question

- **Under-16 refusing:** s 8 FLRA 1969 does not apply, so ask whether the child is Gillick competent for this particular decision. Re E suggests the child must understand the consequences of refusal, including what dying would involve, and not just the treatment. Sincere belief is not enough.
- **Even if competent:** Re W, approving Re E, shows the court can override a minor's refusal where welfare requires it. E & F takes the same approach for 16- and 17-year-olds. The court will be "very slow" to let a child die for their beliefs.
- **Parents:** a parent's refusal on religious grounds does not bind the court, and the hospital can apply for authority to treat.
- **Weighing wishes:** weigh the child's age and maturity and how sincere the belief is. Then explain why, on Re E, these are unlikely to prevail where refusal probably means death.
- **Evaluation:** contrast the adult position, where a refusal by someone with capacity must be respected. Ask whether a high competence bar that applies only to refusals is principled.
