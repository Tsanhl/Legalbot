# End of Life (Adults) 2 - Life-Shortening Treatment, Euthanasia and the Doctrine of Double Effect

## Overview
English law draws a sharp line between **killing** and **allowing to die**. Actively administering a lethal substance to end a patient's life, even at their request and out of compassion, is murder (or attempted murder): *R v Cox*. Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment is an omission, lawful where there is no duty to continue (*Bland*). Between these lies **palliative treatment that may shorten life**: under the **doctrine of double effect (DDE)**, a doctor may give pain relief intending to relieve suffering while foreseeing it may hasten death (*R v Adams*). The difficulty is reconciling DDE with the criminal law of intention in *R v Woollin*.

## The law
1. **Definitions** (lay definitions from the Concise Oxford Dictionary):
   - *Assisted dying*: a terminally ill or incurably ill person voluntarily ends their life with another's help (typically a doctor providing lethal drugs).
   - *Euthanasia*: a gentle and easy death, or bringing this about, especially for incurable and painful disease.
   - *Palliative care*: care for the terminally ill and their families.
   - *Passive euthanasia*: withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment knowing the person will die.
   the focus: life-shortening treatment designed primarily to end life, distinguished from palliative pain relief.
2. **Active killing is murder.**
   - *R v Cox* [1993] Med LR 232 / (1992) 12 BMLR 38: Dr Cox injected potassium chloride (which has no therapeutic or pain-relieving properties) into a patient with unbearable pain from rheumatoid arthritis who begged him to end her suffering. As her body had been cremated, causation could not be proved, so he was convicted of **attempted murder** (Criminal Attempts Act 1981, s.4(1)) and received a suspended sentence; the GMC let him continue practising under conditions. Ognall J: what he did was criminal and "a total betrayal" of his duty as a physician.
   - *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] (Lord Goff, obiter): "mercy killing" by active means is murder; kind motives make no difference in law (as quoted in Brazier & Cave).
   - Brazier & Cave: sympathy for mercy killers is confined to sentencing (e.g. *R v Inglis* [2010] EWCA Crim 2637: mother convicted of murder for killing her comatose son with heroin; minimum term reduced on appeal). The DPP policy (para 33) also says that ending another's life at their request is murder or manslaughter.
3. **Double effect.**
   - *R v Adams* [1957] Crim LR 365 (also cited as 773): Dr Adams was charged with murdering an elderly stroke patient by large doses of heroin and morphine. Devlin J: shortening life by weeks or months can be murder, and the law knows no special defence; but a doctor is "entitled to do all that is proper and necessary to relieve pain and suffering, even if the measures he takes may incidentally shorten human life". Endorsed in *Bland*.
   - The statement of DDE: a distinction between foreseeing an undesirable outcome and intending it. The doctor foresees death from increasing pain relief (the unintended consequence) but intends only to relieve pain (the good effect).
   - *Dr Moor's case* (1999, unreported): Dr Moor openly admitted to helping terminally ill patients in pain and distress die by fatal doses of diamorphine (BBC 2000, as quoted); commentary lists the case with Adams and Cox.
4. **Woollin problem.** *R v Woollin* [1999] 1 AC 82, Lord Steyn: where a person realises it is for all practical purposes inevitable that his actions will cause death or serious harm, the inference may be irresistible that he intended it, however little he desired it. A doctor who knows a dose will virtually certainly hasten death seems to fall within this. Four responses:
   - (1) **Distinguish**: Woollin does not apply to proper medical treatment (the dominant view: DDE applies in medical cases and not others).
   - (2) **Apply**: the jury has a discretion ("may" infer), so it can decline to find intention.
   - (3) **Apply, but rely on alternative principles** (e.g. necessity or the "proper medical treatment" category).
   - (4) **Overrule** the DDE line (John Keown's view).
   - *Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins)* [2001] Fam 147 refers to DDE, showing post-Woollin judicial acceptance, though Ward LJ said it "may be difficult to reconcile" with Woollin and Brooke LJ relied on necessity instead. *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* [2014] UKSC 38 is also listed in this context.
5. **Withholding and withdrawing** life-sustaining treatment is an omission; it is unlawful only where there is a duty to act. There is no duty to give treatment that is not in the patient's best interests (*Bland*; see the PDOC note), and a capacitous refusal must be respected (*Re B*).
6. **Summary of the law**: lethal treatment = murder (or attempted murder: Cox); life-shortening palliation = lawful if it accords with DDE (Adams; Woollin issue); assisted suicide = crime with DPP discretion.

## Key authorities
- *R v Cox* [1993] Med LR 232 / (1992) 12 BMLR 38: active euthanasia by doctor is (attempted) murder.
- *R v Adams* [1957] Crim LR 365: DDE in English law.
- *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] AC 789: acts/omissions; Lord Goff's obiter on mercy killing; endorsement of Adams.
- *R v Moor* (1999, unreported): diamorphine case.
- *R v Woollin* [1999] 1 AC 82: oblique intention.
- *Re A (Conjoined Twins)* [2000] EWCA Civ 254 / [2001] Fam 147: DDE, Woollin and necessity.
- *R v Inglis* [2010] EWCA Crim 2637 (Brazier & Cave): mercy killing by relative is murder.

## Debate and critique
- **Moral positions**: *preference utilitarians* and rights-based theorists see no intrinsic moral difference between killing and letting die where the patient wants to die; *vitalism* holds life must always be preserved; the *sanctity of life* position (Keown, Finnis) holds intentional killing of the innocent is always wrong but there is no duty to strive officiously to keep alive ("Thou shalt not kill; but needst not strive / Officiously to keep alive"). The ethical map: life-shortening medicine is permissible for voluntary decisions; for some non-voluntary decisions; only where the intention is to relieve pain (DDE); or never (vitalism).
- **Is the act/omission line coherent?** Withdrawing a ventilator is treated as an omission though it involves a positive act; the patient dies either way. Critics see the distinction as formalism protecting doctors; defenders (sanctity of life) see it as tracking intention and causation.
- **DDE and intention.** Keown would overrule the attempt to exempt doctors from Woollin, holding instead that the doctor genuinely does not intend death; others treat DDE as a policy exception for "proper medical treatment". Brazier & Cave note Devlin J's ruling is not crystal clear (hours, or months?), and the Gosport Independent Panel (2018), finding over 450 lives unnecessarily shortened by high-dose medication, is "a stark lesson" on DDE's limits.
- **Continuous deep sedation** (Brazier & Cave): increasingly used at the end of life; may be accommodated by DDE if death is imminent, but raises tension with assisted dying debates.
- **Motive irrelevant.** The law ignores compassionate motive in liability (Cox, Inglis) but allows it at sentencing and in prosecutorial discretion for assisted suicide. Is this consistent?
- **Reform link.** The Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill would permit *self-administration* only, preserving the ban on doctors administering lethal treatment.

## Applying it to a problem question
(Simon pleads with his wife Constance and doctor Felix "to do everything they can to end his life".)
1. List the options: continuation or withdrawal of the ventilator; lethal intervention; life-shortening pain relief; assisted suicide.
2. **Lethal intervention**: by Constance or Felix = murder (Cox); consent and motive are no defence (Bland per Lord Goff).
3. **Life-shortening pain relief**: Constance (not a doctor, no "proper medical treatment") = murder if she intends or (Woollin) foresees death as virtually certain. Felix: lawful under DDE if his intention is to relieve pain and the treatment is proper (Adams); discuss whether this survives Woollin and the four reconciliation options; Nicklinson noted.
4. **Withdrawal**: lawful omission if Simon has capacity and refuses (Re B) or if continued ventilation is not in his best interests (Bland; s.4 MCA); otherwise withdrawal in breach of duty could be murder (Bland).
5. **If Simon survives withdrawal**: any further act to end life returns to points 2-3; basic care (comfort, food and water by mouth) continues (CoP 9.28).
6. **Assisted suicide**: see the next note (Suicide Act s.2(1), (4); DPP policy – compassion; healthcare professional caring for victim).
7. Traps: treating compassion or consent as a defence; equating palliative care with euthanasia; ignoring Woollin.
