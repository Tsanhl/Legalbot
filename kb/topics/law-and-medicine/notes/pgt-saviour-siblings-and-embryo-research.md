# Reproductive Medicine 3 - Preimplantation Genetic Testing, Sex Selection, Saviour Siblings and Embryo Research

## Overview
IVF makes it possible to test embryos before transfer (preimplantation genetic testing, PGT) and to use surplus or specially created embryos in research. Both involve the **destructive use of embryos**, so the moral status of the embryo is central. Since the 2008 amendments, **Schedule 2 para 1ZA** of the HFE Act 1990 lists four purposes for which a treatment licence may authorise embryo testing; non-medical sex selection is prohibited (para 1ZB); and s.13(9) prevents preferring embryos known to carry a serious abnormality. Saviour sibling tissue typing, controversially permitted in *R (Quintavalle) v HFEA* (2005), now has an express basis. Embryo research is "permissive but strict": limited to 14 days or the primitive streak and to listed purposes. Commentary argues the Act implicitly adopts a **proportional status** view of the embryo.

## The law
**1. Moral status of the embryo.**
- *Full status* from conception (e.g. Finnis, Keown): implies opposition to IVF itself, because its present and future development depends on embryo research and IVF produces surplus embryos.
- *No status* until birth or beyond (e.g. Mary Anne Warren, rights-based).
- *Limited status*: *fixed* (Peter Singer, utilitarian: counts only once able to feel pain; he originally put this at 18 weeks, but RCOG (2022) says pain perception before 28 weeks is unlikely) or *proportional* (Pattinson, rights-based).

**2. PGT before and after 2008.** Technique: e.g. blastomere biopsy at the 6-8 cell stage. Before 2008 the 1990 Act was silent on whether a treatment licence could authorise embryo testing: *R (Josephine Quintavalle) v HFEA* [2002] EWHC 2785; [2003] EWCA Civ 667; [2005] UKHL 28. Now Sch 2 para 1(1)(b) permits licensing of "testing" embryos.

**3. The four permitted purposes – Sch 2 para 1ZA(1)** (one or more of):
- (a) establishing whether the embryo has a gene, chromosome or mitochondrion abnormality that may affect its capacity to result in a live birth;
- (b) where there is a **particular risk** that the embryo may have a gene, chromosome or mitochondrion abnormality, establishing whether it has that or any other such abnormality – but only if the HFEA is satisfied there is a **significant risk** that a person with the abnormality will have or develop a **serious** mental or physical disability, serious illness or other serious medical condition (para 1ZA(2)) – compare "substantial risk" of being "seriously handicapped" in s.1(1)(d) Abortion Act;
- (c) where there is a particular risk that a resulting child will have or develop a **gender-related** serious disability, illness or condition, establishing the sex of the embryo;
- (d) **tissue typing**: where a sibling (a child of the persons whose gametes are used, or of either) suffers from a serious medical condition that could be treated by umbilical cord blood stem cells, bone marrow or other tissue of the resulting child, establishing whether the resulting child's tissue would be compatible with the sibling's; "other tissue" excludes any whole organ (para 1ZA(4)).
- **Licence conditions** (Sch 2 para 1(2)): only genetic tests "expressly authorised" may be used (Code of Practice Parts 9 and 10); the HFEA website lists permitted conditions.

**4. Five example conditions for which PGT is licensed**: beta thalassaemia (serious, potentially fatal blood disorder); Down's syndrome; sensorineural (autosomal recessive non-syndromic) deafness; BRCA1 (increased susceptibility to breast cancer – note the Angelina Jolie story); Huntington's disease (late onset). The inclusion of late-onset and susceptibility conditions shows how far "serious medical condition" extends.

**5. Deaf embryos – s.13(9).** Embryos known to have an abnormality involving a significant risk of a serious medical condition "must not be preferred" to those not known to have one. This prevents a deaf couple choosing a deaf embryo (commentary refers to a 2002 news report of a deaf couple who sought to have a deaf child).

**6. Sex selection.**
- Sch 2 para 1ZB(1): a licence cannot authorise any practice designed to secure that a child will be of one sex rather than the other, except within para 1ZA(1)(c); "gender-related" means affecting only one sex, or one sex significantly more than the other (para 1ZB(4)).
- 2006 White Paper (leading to the 2008 Act, paras 2.45-2.47): two reasons to prohibit non-medical sex selection – strength of public opinion and cultures with a preference for boys. An alternative argument: social sex selection displays and encourages unacceptable gender expectations. Commentary also refers to an earlier (2000) news story about Alan and Louise Masterton.
- Sperm sorting: because of concerns about reliability, sperm sorted for sex selection by gradient methods should not be used for medical reasons (Code of Practice para 10.22).
- Contrast abortion law, which has no explicit sex ground (sex selection notes in the Abortion Act note).

**7. Saviour siblings.**
- *Hashmi family*: Zain Hashmi had beta thalassaemia. HFEA granted permission for PGT with tissue typing subject to conditions: the condition must be severe or life-threatening; only for a sibling; case-by-case; only for cord blood use; embryos must be at hereditary risk of the condition.
- *Whitaker family*: Charlie Whitaker had Diamond-Blackfan anaemia; HFEA refused because there was no hereditary risk (testing would be solely for tissue typing).
- *R (Quintavalle) v HFEA* [2005] UKHL 28: PGT with tissue typing was within the HFEA's licensing power because the 1990 Act, read purposively, allowed licensing of activities for the purpose of "assisting women to carry children", which covered anything relevant to the woman's decision whether to carry the child.
- Now express: para 1ZA(1)(d), excluding whole organs (para 1ZA(4)) – and no requirement of hereditary risk to the embryo.

**8. Embryo research.**
- Creation of embryos for research is permitted (Sch 2 para 3(1)(a)).
- Only up to the appearance of the primitive streak or 14 days after creation began, whichever is earlier (s.3(3)(a), (4)).
- Embryos used in research cannot be used for any other purpose (s.15(4)).
- Consent of gamete donors under Sch 3.
- Research must be "necessary or desirable" for a listed purpose (Sch 2 para 3A): (a) increasing knowledge about serious disease or other serious medical conditions; (b) developing treatments for them; (c) increasing knowledge about the causes of congenital disease or conditions not within (a); (d) promoting advances in infertility treatment; (e) increasing knowledge about the causes of miscarriage; (f) developing more effective contraception; (g) developing methods for detecting gene, chromosome or mitochondrion abnormalities in embryos before implantation; (h) increasing knowledge about the development of embryos.

**9. The embryo's status under the Act.** Warnock: the embryo of the human species "ought to have a special status". Long title: to make provision in connection with human embryos and their subsequent development. Commentary reads the law as **implicitly proportional**, with four legally relevant stages (the 1990 Act also amended the Abortion Act): pre-implantation (14-day limit, research permitted); up to 24 weeks (s.1(1)(a) AA); after 24 weeks (only grounds (b)-(d)); and birth (legal personality). Question: should this proportional status have counted in *Evans*?

## Debate and critique
- **Question**: "The HFE Act 2008 represented a missed opportunity to prohibit the use of genetic diagnosis and tissue-typing on in vitro embryos. Discuss." The example introduction instead defends the Act's approach to PGT: prohibiting unethical uses while being otherwise permissive.
- **Full status critique**: PGT and research destroy embryos with full moral status; tissue typing treats the future child as a means to an end (Kantian objection). Response: proportional status makes early embryo interests weak; the saviour sibling will also be wanted and loved; the restriction to cord blood/bone marrow and exclusion of whole organs (para 1ZA(4)) limit harm to the child.
- **Disability critique (expressivism)**: selecting against embryos with Down's syndrome or deafness may send a negative message about disabled people (compare Crowter and Robinson in the abortion notes). s.13(9) is criticised from the other direction for forbidding deaf parents from choosing a deaf child.
- **Line-drawing**: inclusion of BRCA1 and Huntington's (late onset; possibly treatable in future) shows "serious" is elastic; licensing by condition list gives the HFEA a gatekeeping role.
- **Sex selection**: the ban rests on public opinion and gender-preference concerns; liberals argue it restricts procreative liberty without clear harm; others (alternative argument) say it entrenches gender expectations. Contrast the legal position in abortion (Greasley).
- **Non-identity**: selecting among embryos does not harm the resulting child (that child would not otherwise exist), which complicates welfare-based objections (Parfit; see access note).
- **14-day rule** reflects proportional status and pragmatic line-drawing at the primitive streak.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the clinic licensed for the activity (testing embryos, research)? Testing must fit a para 1ZA purpose and an expressly authorised test.
2. Purpose (b): particular risk + HFEA satisfied of significant risk of a serious condition (e.g. family history of beta thalassaemia, Huntington's, BRCA1).
3. Purpose (c): sex determination only for a gender-related serious condition (e.g. Duchenne muscular dystrophy); otherwise para 1ZB bars sex selection; sperm sorting not for medical use (Code 10.22).
4. Purpose (d): saviour sibling – sibling's serious condition treatable by cord blood, bone marrow or other tissue (not whole organs); no hereditary risk now needed (contrast Whitaker refusal under old policy; Quintavalle).
5. Choosing an affected embryo (e.g. deafness): prohibited preference (s.13(9)).
6. Research: 14 days/primitive streak; listed purposes; consent; no later use for treatment.
7. Also check s.13(5) welfare of the child and Sch 3 consents for embryos created.
8. Traps: citing pre-2008 Quintavalle reasoning as the current basis; forgetting the "whole organ" exclusion; applying Abortion Act terms to PGT.
