# Transplantation 2 - Allocation of Deceased Donor Organs, Conditional and Directed Donation

## Overview
Once an organ is lawfully obtained from a deceased donor, it is allocated through a national system run by NHS Blood and Transplant (NHSBT) according to agreed clinical criteria. Can the donor or family control who receives it? Policy distinguishes **conditional donation** (limited to recipients in, or not in, a specified group – prohibited) from **directed donation / requested allocation** to a named relative or long-standing friend (permitted only in tightly defined circumstances under NHSBT POL322/1 (2023)). The HTA 2004 itself says nothing explicit on this, so the debate is whether the policy reflects the law (Cronin & Douglas; Pattinson say it overreaches). Also remember the recipient side: transplanting into a patient needs consent or another lawful justification.

## The law
1. **Definitions.** A donation is *conditional* where it is limited to the recipient being, or not being, in a specified group (e.g. anyone living in a particular city). A *directed* donation is to a specified individual.
2. **History.**
   - Scandal at Northern General Hospital, Sheffield (July 1998): a deceased donor's organs were accepted on condition that they went only to white recipients and were transplanted into white people (Pattinson 2011; Brazier & Cave). The DH report (2000) rejected not only racist conditions but **all** conditions.
   - Media interest in the 2008 "Ashworth affair": the post-2000 policy was relied on to deny Rachel Leake a transplant of a kidney from her deceased 21-year-old daughter, Laura Ashworth (Pattinson 2011). This led to DH (2010) *Requested Allocation of a Deceased Donor Organ*, which continued to reject directed donation but supported "requested allocation".
   - Current policy: **NHSBT (2023) POL322/1 Exceptional Donation Requests Policy**, referred to by Code F Pt 2 ([45]), marginally rewording the 2010 policy.
3. **Rules under POL322/1.**
   - All conditional donation, including directed donation as a condition, is not permitted: "It is ... not acceptable to attach any conditions to the donation of organs, other than by specifying the organ/s for which consent/authorisation has been given." If a deceased donation is conditional, the organ will not be allocated to anyone.
   - But a **preference** may be expressed. Allocation to a specified individual is permitted where (lecture's four conditions):
     1. appropriate consent is **not conditional** on allocation to a specific group or person (unconditional consent);
     2. **no one is in more urgent clinical need** – priority goes to patients on the NHSBT Super-Urgent/Urgent Heart or Lung lists or Super-Urgent Liver list, if the organ is clinically suitable;
     3. the preferred recipient is a **relative or friend of long standing**;
     4. the preferred recipient is **on, or eligible to be on, the transplant waiting list** and a transplant is clinically indicated.
   - The policy text adds that the deceased in life indicated a decision to donate to the named person, or, absent that, the family expresses such a decision; and that it applies across the UK (in Scotland, conditions are prohibited by s.49 Human Tissue (Scotland) Act 2006). Cases are expected to be very few.
   - **Justification** (2010 Guidance and 2023 policy [10]-[11]): two key principles underpin the programme – **absence of conditionality** ("conditionality offends against the fundamental principle that organs are donated" freely/altruistically) and **equitable treatment** (organs allocated according to agreed criteria; the 2010 version said "in a fair and unbiased way").
4. **Recipients.** A lawful justification is needed to transplant into a patient: consent, or s.5 MCA (adults lacking capacity, best interests), or for children, proxy consent/court/necessity. Compare *Re T (A Minor)* [1997] 1 WLR 242 (parents' refusal of a liver transplant for their baby upheld) and *Re M (A Child) (Medical Treatment: Consent)* [1999] 2 FLR 1097 (15-year-old's refusal of heart transplant overridden).

## Key authorities
- NHSBT (2023) POL322/1; DH (2010) Requested Allocation; DH (2000) report.
- HTA Code F Pt 2 [45].
- HTA 2004 (silent on allocation; s.1 appropriate consent).
- *Re T* [1997] 1 WLR 242; *Re M* [1999] 2 FLR 1097 (recipient consent).

## Debate and critique
- **Cronin and Douglas, 'Directed and Conditional Deceased Donor Organ Donations: Laws and Misconceptions' (2010) 18(3) Med L Rev 275.** Treating the "central principle" of unconditional donation as legally binding, rather than as operational policy, is a misinterpretation. Directed and conditional donations are **not illegal per se**; overruling an individual's request or condition may conflict with "appropriate consent", the "golden thread" of the 2004 Act; and allocating organs as if donated without restriction could even risk criminal liability for transplant teams (citing s.33). Regulators may misinterpret the law by applying their own ethics beyond what the law allows.
- **Pattinson, 'Directed Donation and Ownership of Human Organs' (2011) 31(3) Legal Studies 392** (ignore the property discussion for this module). Argues that attempts to prohibit or stringently restrict directed donation are challenged by a proper understanding of the principles underlying the HTA 2004 – in particular its consent-based structure.
- **Equity v autonomy.** Utilitarians favour allocation by clinical need (maximising lives saved), though they might accept directed donation if it increases supply. Rights-based views stress the donor's (or family's) authority over posthumous use. Duty-based views may focus on fairness to all on the waiting list. The policy's insistence on unconditionality protects against discriminatory conditions (Sheffield), but treats a mother's wish to help her daughter like a racist condition unless framed as a "preference".
- **Living v deceased asymmetry.** Virtually all living donation is directed to a specific recipient, yet deceased directed donation is exceptional. Is this consistent?
- **Tutorial further question (b)**: should donors or families be permitted to attach conditions or direct donation? Use the above arguments; distinguish discriminatory conditions from directed donations to loved ones.

## Applying it to a problem question
(the materials: Connie's dying words "do everything to save Ant".)
1. First establish appropriate consent for removal (deceased donation note).
2. Is the consent **conditional** on Ant receiving the kidney? If yes, policy says the organ will not be allocated at all. If it is a general consent plus a **preference**, consider requested allocation.
3. Apply the four conditions: unconditional consent; nobody more urgent on super-urgent/urgent lists (for a kidney these lists do not apply in the same way, but check urgency principles); Ant is a relative (son); Ant is on or eligible for the waiting list and transplant is clinically indicated (kidney damage).
4. Consider **lawfulness of treating Ant** (he is in a coma): s.5 MCA and best interests (s.4(3), (6), (7); s.1(6) – dialysis as less invasive; Aintree presumption for life).
5. Note the policy's status: guidance, not statute; consider the Cronin & Douglas argument in an essay or "all options" question.
6. Traps: treating requested allocation as a right; ignoring the recipient's consent; confusing conditional with directed donation.
