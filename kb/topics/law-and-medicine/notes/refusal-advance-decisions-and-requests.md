# End of Life (Adults) 1 - Contemporaneous Refusals, Advance Decisions and Requests for Treatment

## Overview
End-of-life law distinguishes the doctor's involvement (administering lethal treatment; withholding/withdrawing life support; assisting self-administration) and the patient's involvement (voluntary, involuntary, non-voluntary). This note covers **voluntary** decisions by adults about life-sustaining treatment: (1) **contemporaneous refusals**, which bind if the patient has capacity; (2) **advance decisions to refuse treatment (ADRTs / ARs)** under ss.24-26 MCA 2005, which bind if they *exist*, are *valid* and are *applicable*; and (3) **requests** for treatment, contemporaneous or in advance, which do *not* bind doctors (*R (Burke) v GMC*). The asymmetry between binding refusals and non-binding requests is a major essay theme.

## The law
**1. Contemporaneous refusal.** An adult with capacity can lawfully refuse treatment even if death results: *Re B* [2002] EWHC 429 (Fam); *Re T* [1992] EWCA Civ 18 (Lord Donaldson MR: an absolute right to consent, refuse, or choose one of the treatments offered). In Re B, the ventilator had to be switched off at Ms B's request; continuing it was trespass (nominal damages). Brazier & Cave also cite *Re AK* [2001] (a man with motor neurone disease communicating by blinking could validly instruct that his ventilator be switched off).

**2. Advance refusals at common law (superseded).** *Re T*: an anticipatory choice binds if "clearly established and applicable in the circumstances" (two major "ifs"); in case of doubt, resolve in favour of preserving life.

**3. MCA 2005 ss.24-26.** The Act does not give binding force to advance directives generally, but recognises binding advance refusals.
- **s.24(1) definition / existence**: a decision made by P, **after reaching 18** and **when he has capacity**, that if at a later time and in specified circumstances a specified treatment is proposed and P then lacks capacity, the treatment is not to be carried out or continued. s.24(2): treatment and circumstances may be specified in layman's terms. s.24(3): may be withdrawn or altered while capacitous; s.24(4): withdrawal need not be in writing; s.24(5): alteration need not be in writing unless s.25(5) applies.
- **s.25(2) validity**: not valid if P (a) withdrew it with capacity, (b) later created an LPA giving the donee authority over the same treatment, or (c) did anything else "clearly inconsistent" with it remaining his fixed decision.
- **s.25(3)-(4) applicability**: not applicable if P has capacity at the material time; or (a) the treatment is not the one specified, (b) specified circumstances are absent, or (c) there are reasonable grounds to believe circumstances exist which P did not anticipate and which would have affected the decision. (Code of Practice para 9.44 gives an example.)
- **s.25(5)-(6) life-sustaining treatment** (defined in s.4(10)): only applicable if verified by P's statement that it applies "even if life is at risk", and the decision and statement are in writing, signed by P (or by another in P's presence at P's direction), signed or acknowledged in the presence of a witness, who signs or acknowledges in P's presence.
- **s.25(7)**: other LPAs do not affect validity.
- **s.26(1) effect**: a valid and applicable AR has effect as if P had made it with capacity when the treatment question arises.
- **s.26(2)**: no liability for treating unless the person is **satisfied** that a valid and applicable AR exists.
- **s.26(3)**: no liability for withholding/withdrawing if the person **reasonably believes** that a valid and applicable AR exists.
- **s.26(4)**: court may declare whether an AR exists, is valid and is applicable.
- **s.26(5)**: nothing stops life-sustaining treatment, or acts reasonably believed necessary to prevent serious deterioration, while a court decision is sought (see also CoP para 9.56 on emergencies).
- **s.5(4)**: the general legal authority does not apply where there is a valid AR.
- **Basic care**: an AR cannot refuse actions needed to keep a person comfortable (warmth, shelter, cleanliness, the offer of food and water by mouth): Code of Practice para 9.28. It *can* refuse artificial nutrition and hydration (paras 9.26, 9.28).
- *Re AB (ADRT: Validity and Applicability)* [2025] EWCOP 20 (T3), Poole J [53]: a signed, written ADRT that is valid and applicable is legally binding; no best interests discussion is needed because the patient has made the decision; neither family wishes nor clinicians' views of its wisdom can override it.

**4. Requests for treatment.** *R (Burke) v GMC*:
- **Munby J** [2004] EWHC 1879 (Admin): GMC guidance on withholding/withdrawing life-prolonging treatment was unlawful; withdrawing ANH contrary to a capacitous patient's wishes or a valid advance request would breach Arts 2, 3 and 8; a court declaration could be required (applying *Glass v UK*); obiter that intolerability is the "touchstone" of best interests.
- **Court of Appeal** [2005] EWCA Civ 1003: set the declarations aside. No practical need for them; the guidance contained nothing unlawful. A capacitous patient can ask for treatment, or go elsewhere, but no doctor can be ordered to provide treatment they consider inappropriate; "a patient cannot demand that a doctor administer a treatment which the doctor considers is adverse to the patient's clinical needs". There is no absolute duty to keep people alive. But a doctor who withdrew ANH from a capacitous patient who wanted to be kept alive by it would be guilty of murder. Best interests is an objective test; declarations were not required by *Glass*.
- ECtHR declared Burke's application manifestly ill-founded (Pattinson).
- **Rule**: advance requests are not binding; they are a factor in best interests (s.4(6)). Courts will not compel doctors: *Re J* [1993]; *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018] UKSC 46; *Aintree* [2013]; *Re AA* [2024] EWCOP 39 (Henke J: only one option was available, so there was no best interests decision to make).

## Key authorities
- *Re B* [2002] EWHC 429 (Fam): binding contemporaneous refusal of ventilation.
- *Re T* [1992] EWCA Civ 18: common law ARs; doubt resolved for life.
- *X Primary Care Trust v XB* [2012] EWHC 1390 (Fam) (Theis J): XB had motor neurone disease, on invasive ventilation since 2003, communicating by eye movements. His AR (on an internet pro forma) asked for ventilation to be removed in defined circumstances but described it as "non-invasive ventilation" and contained a "valid until" date. Theis J was satisfied XB had capacity when making it and that it complied with the formalities; the "valid until" date had not been discussed with or agreed by XB and was there only in the context of review, so the AR was not time-limited. Wider guidance: investigate AR disputes urgently; there is no set form (Code paras 9.10-9.23, 9.19 lists helpful contents); pro forma providers should reconsider "valid until" boxes.
- *Newcastle upon Tyne Hospitals FT v LM* [2014] EWCOP 454: a Jehovah's Witness patient's long-standing statements of never accepting blood were in effect treated as an advance decision although not meeting the exact MCA requirements (Brazier & Cave, who also cite *Nottinghamshire Healthcare NHS Trust v RC* and *Barnsley Hospital NHS FT v MSP*, where a procedurally deficient AD was still strong evidence of wishes in the best interests assessment).
- *Re PW (Jehovah's Witness: Validity of Advance Decision)* [2021] EWCOP 52; *HE v A Hospital NHS Trust* [2003] EWHC 1017: validity of advance refusals (listed).
- *Re AB* [2025] EWCOP 20 (T3) [53]: binding effect; no best interests overlay.
- *R (Burke) v GMC* [2004] EWHC 1879; [2005] EWCA Civ 1003: no right to demand treatment.
- *An NHS Trust v Y* [2018] UKSC 46; *Re AA* [2024] EWCOP 39: doctors cannot be required to treat.
- MCA 2005 ss.4(6), 4(10), 5(4), 24-26; Code of Practice paras 9.19, 9.24-9.28, 9.44, 9.56.

## Debate and critique
- **Halliday, 'Legislating to give effect to precedent autonomy' (2011) 11 Med Law Int 127.** Comparing Austria, Germany and England, she concludes each legislature erected significant hurdles to binding anticipatory decisions, preserving medical discretion and the presumption for life in most cases and so undermining precedent autonomy. A key practical failing: there is no central register; a patient who lacks capacity bears the burden of getting the AR to the doctor, and copies risk conflicting versions. She argues for a central electronic register with a duty on doctors to consult it.
- **Formalities v autonomy.** XB shows formal defects (pro forma wording, "valid until") can threaten a clear wish; courts may read ARs purposively. But s.25(4)(c) (unanticipated circumstances) and s.26(2) (no liability unless "satisfied") give doctors room to treat. Halliday (2023, obstetric note) urges extreme specificity and enhanced formalities.
- **Precedent autonomy v the present person.** Should the earlier capacitous self bind the later incapacitated self (e.g. someone with dementia who now seems content)? Duty-based views stress present interests; rights-based views give weight to the prior agent's choice.
- **Refusals bind but requests do not.** Pattinson ('Contemporaneous and advance requests: the fight for rights at the end of life' in Herring & Wall (eds), *Landmark Cases in Medical Law* (Hart, 2015)) argues Munby J was read "uncharitably" and the Court of Appeal missed the chance to recognise the rights of potentially vulnerable patients (e.g. a right not to die in distress from starvation); later case law gives more weight to an incapacitated patient's previously autonomous wishes than Burke allowed. Many commentators supported overturning Munby J.
- **Clinical judgment as gatekeeper.** The no-compulsion rule protects professional integrity and resource allocation but makes autonomy "negative" only.
- **Basic care exception.** Is it coherent that ANH can be refused but spoon-feeding offers cannot?

## Applying it to a problem question
(the materials: Simon, now brain-injured on a ventilator, who previously asked that "nothing be done" to keep him alive and now pleads to end his life.)
1. **Capacity now?** Apply ss.1-3 (JB order). If Simon has capacity, his contemporaneous refusal of ventilation binds (Re B); continuing is trespass. His requests for active killing cannot be met lawfully (see life-shortening and assisted suicide notes).
2. **If he lacks capacity, is there an AR?**
   - Existence (s.24(1)): made at 18+ (he was 21) with capacity (was he depressed at the time? Depression does not automatically remove capacity).
   - Specificity: "nothing be done to keep him alive" – does it specify treatment in lay terms (s.24(2))?
   - Validity (s.25(2)): any later inconsistent conduct?
   - Applicability (s.25(4)): circumstances anticipated? A new, unexplained brain insult may be unanticipated (s.25(4)(c)).
   - Life-sustaining treatment (s.25(5)-(6)): oral request fails the writing, signature, witness and "even if life is at risk" requirements – likely **not applicable** to ventilation.
3. **If no applicable AR**: is there an LPA or deputy? Otherwise s.5 GLA and s.4 best interests: his previous wishes and current pleas are relevant under s.4(6) (consider *Newcastle v LM* on long-standing views); consult Constance (s.4(7)); presumption in favour of life (Aintree); s.4(5) not motivated by desire to cause death.
4. **Protection for Felix**: s.26(2)/(3) – treats unless *satisfied* AR is valid and applicable; may withdraw if *reasonably believes* it is. Seek a declaration (s.26(4); s.15) in doubt; s.26(5) permits life support meanwhile.
5. **Withdrawal is an omission**, lawful if treatment is no longer in best interests (Bland); murder risk only arises if a duty to treat continues.
6. **Requests**: Simon cannot compel any particular treatment; his wishes are a factor (Burke).
7. Traps: treating an oral wish as a binding AR for life-sustaining treatment; forgetting the basic care exception; confusing advance requests with refusals.
