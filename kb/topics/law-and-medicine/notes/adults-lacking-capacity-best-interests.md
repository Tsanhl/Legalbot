# Treating Adults Who Lack Capacity - Proxies, the s.5 General Legal Authority and Best Interests

## Overview
Once it is established that an adult (16+) lacks capacity under ss.2-3 MCA 2005, two questions follow: **who** may decide, and **on what criteria**? Before the MCA, no one could consent for an incapacitated adult; treatment was justified by the common law doctrine of necessity and a best interests test (*Re F*). The MCA now provides a structured scheme: a valid advance refusal binds; otherwise an attorney (LPA) or court-appointed deputy may decide, or, most commonly, the person treating relies on the **s.5 general legal authority (GLA)**. Every decision must be in P's **best interests (s.4)** and consider the least restrictive option (s.1(6)). The s.4 test was designed to include an element of substituted judgment.

## The law
**A. Who decides? Theoretical options.** (1) The patient, by a prior decision; (2) a proxy: appointed (e.g. attorney), imposed (e.g. deputy) or the court; (3) no one, treatment being justified on other grounds.

**B. Old law (for comparison).** *Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1: no proxies; treatment lawful under the doctrine of necessity if in the patient's best interests, with best interests tied to the Bolam standard (*Bolam v Friern HMC* [1957] 1 WLR 582). *Re Y (Mental Patient: Bone Marrow Donation)* [1997] Fam 110 applied a broad best interests test. Necessity no longer applies to those within the MCA: *ZH v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2012] EWHC 604, [44].

**C. The MCA order of questions** (lecture's "four simple questions"):
1. Is there a valid and applicable **advance refusal** (ss.24-26)? If so it binds (covered in the end-of-life note).
2. If not, is there a **lasting power of attorney** (ss.9-11, 13) or **court-appointed deputy** (ss.16-20)?
   - LPA: donor 18 or over; LPA registered with the Public Guardian.
   - Deputy: appointed where there is no attorney and a one-off court decision will not suffice.
3. If not, can the treater rely on the **s.5 GLA**?
4. If capacity or best interests are in doubt, apply to the Court of Protection for **declaratory relief** (s.15: declarations on capacity and on the lawfulness of acts done or proposed).

**D. Section 5 general legal authority.** Where D does an act in connection with P's care or treatment, D is protected if:
- (a) before acting, D took **reasonable steps** to establish whether P lacks capacity in relation to the matter; and
- (b) when acting, D **reasonably believes** (i) P lacks capacity and (ii) the act is in P's best interests.
- Effect (s.5(2)): D incurs no liability that D would not have incurred had P had capacity and consented. (Code of Practice para 4.53.)
- **Limits:** s.5 does not apply where there is a legally effective advance refusal (s.5(4)), or where it would conflict with a decision properly made by a donee or deputy (s.6(6)), except that action may be taken to save life or prevent serious deterioration while the Court of Protection resolves the issue (s.6(7)).

**E. On what criteria? Options**: P's interests (prior values / substituted judgment, or best interests); overall interests of all affected; or no criteria. The MCA chose best interests, modified to include P's own perspective.

**F. Best interests checklist (s.4).**
- s.4(1) **non-discrimination**: not merely on the basis of age or appearance, or a condition or behaviour that might lead to unjustified assumptions.
- s.4(2) consider **all relevant circumstances** (defined in s.4(11): those the decision-maker is aware of and that it would be reasonable to regard as relevant) and take the listed steps.
- s.4(3) consider whether P is likely to **regain capacity** and when.
- s.4(4) so far as reasonably practicable, permit and encourage P to participate.
- s.4(5) for **life-sustaining treatment**, must not be motivated by a desire to bring about death (s.4(10) defines life-sustaining treatment as treatment that, in the view of the person providing care, is necessary to sustain life).
- s.4(6) consider, so far as reasonably ascertainable, P's **past and present wishes and feelings** (especially any relevant written statement made with capacity), the **beliefs and values** likely to influence P's decision if capacitous, and other factors P would be likely to consider.
- s.4(7) **consult**, if practicable and appropriate, anyone named by P, carers and those interested in P's welfare, any donee and any deputy, as to P's best interests and the s.4(6) matters.
- s.4(8) duties apply also to attorneys and those acting on a reasonable belief of incapacity; s.4(9) a non-court decision-maker complies if, having followed s.4(1)-(7), they reasonably believe the act is in P's best interests.
- Plus s.1(5) (must be in best interests) and s.1(6) (less restrictive alternative).

**G. Case law gloss on best interests** (developed in the end-of-life topic): *Aintree University Hospitals NHS FT v James* [2013] UKSC 67 – strong (not absolute) presumption in favour of life; best interests are wider than medical; the purpose is to consider matters from the patient's point of view. Courts will not order doctors to give treatment they consider clinically inappropriate (Aintree, Burke).

## Key authorities
- *Re F* [1990] 2 AC 1: pre-MCA necessity; sterilisation of a 36-year-old with learning disability; today s.5 would apply.
- *Bolam v Friern HMC* [1957] 1 WLR 582: the professional standard formerly used to judge best interests.
- *Re Y (Mental Incapacity: Bone Marrow Transplant)* [1997] Fam 110 (also reported [1997] 2 FCR 172): bone marrow donation by a severely learning-disabled woman (25) to her sister (36) dying of leukaemia held to be in Y's best interests because the transplant would preserve and enhance Y's relationship with her mother (who would otherwise be burdened by the sister's death and caring for a grandchild); risk and discomfort minimal. Connell J stressed the case was unusual and not a precedent for more intrusive surgery (e.g. kidney donation). Brazier & Cave question whether the benefit to Y was rather remote.
- *ZH v Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [2012] EWHC 604, [44]: MCA codifies and replaces the common law; necessity no longer applies.
- *AN NHS Foundation Trust v MC* [2020] EWCOP 33: stem cell/bone marrow donation by an 18-year-old held in her best interests given the emotional, social and psychological benefit of her mother's life being extended (see living donation note).
- *Aintree v James* [2013] UKSC 67: best interests from the patient's point of view.
- MCA 2005: ss.1(5)-(6), 4, 5, 6(6)-(7), 9-11, 13, 15, 16-20.

## Debate and critique
- **Objective v subjective.** The statute "prioritises the patient's welfare over what they want" (EoL outline) but cases since Aintree show a "shift towards patient-centred decision-making". Is best interests now in substance a substituted judgment test? Formative feedback recommended contrasting substituted judgment with best interests by asking how far *past wishes* can be given effect where no formal advance decision was made.
- **Weight of past wishes.** s.4(6) requires wishes to be *considered*, not followed. Critics argue this undervalues precedent autonomy (Halliday, end-of-life note); supporters say it protects the person as they now are.
- **Who protects the donor?** Re Y and MC extend "best interests" to benefits flowing through family relationships. This risks using incapacitated people as means to others' ends (Kantian objection). Cohen J in MC (per Brazier & Cave) warned of a "vacuum" in protecting donors under 18, since the HTA focuses on consent and inducements rather than the donor's best interests.
- **Reasonable belief protection.** s.5 protects practitioners who act on reasonable beliefs about capacity and best interests. This is pragmatic but may let mistaken assessments go unchallenged.
- **Doctors as gatekeepers.** Even under s.4, the options available are those clinicians are willing to offer; the court cannot compel treatment.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm incapacity (ss.1-3; JB order) and that P is 16+.
2. **Advance refusal?** Exists (18+, capacity), valid, applicable (ss.24-26). Life-sustaining treatment needs writing, signature, witness and an "even if life is at risk" statement.
3. **LPA / deputy?** Is the LPA registered and does it cover health decisions? A later LPA covering the treatment can invalidate an earlier advance decision (s.25(2)(b)).
4. **s.5 GLA**: reasonable steps to assess capacity; reasonable belief in incapacity and best interests.
5. **Best interests (s.4)**: run the checklist on the facts: regaining capacity soon (s.4(3) – can treatment wait? e.g. a comatose patient expected to wake, or a fluctuating patient who may have a lucid period); past wishes, beliefs (e.g. religious beliefs) (s.4(6)); consultation with family (s.4(7)); less restrictive option such as dialysis rather than transplant (s.1(6)); presumption in favour of life (Aintree).
6. **Disputes or doubts** → seek a s.15 declaration from the Court of Protection; life-saving treatment can be given meanwhile (s.6(7); s.26(5) for advance decisions).
7. **Relatives have no power to consent** unless attorney/deputy: their role is consultation (s.4(7)).
8. Traps: treating relatives' wishes as decisive; forgetting s.1(6); confusing an advance *request* (a factor) with an advance *refusal* (binding).
9. Example (the materials): Ant is in a coma; treating him (dialysis or transplant) relies on s.5 and best interests, with s.4(3) (likely to regain consciousness), s.4(6), s.4(7) and the Aintree presumption; his mother's dying wish to "do everything to save Ant" is relevant as consultation/views, not consent.
