# Transplantation 3 - Living Donation (Three Hurdles, s.33 HTA) and the s.32 Ban on Commercial Dealings

## Overview
Living donation ranges from minor (blood, bone marrow) to major and risky (kidney, liver lobe, lung segment, womb). In 2024-25 there were 944 living solid organ donors, mostly of kidneys, with notable gender disparities (more male recipients than male donors). Lawful living donation must clear **three hurdles**: (1) the **removal** must be lawful at common law (or under the MCA); (2) **storage and use** require **appropriate consent** under s.1 HTA 2004 (offence under s.5); and (3) **removal and use of "transplantable material"** must satisfy **s.33** and the 2006 Regulations (HTA approval). Separately, **s.32** prohibits commercial dealings in "controlled material", raising the ethical debate about paying organ providers.

## The law
**Risk scale (handout).** Blood: minor. Bone marrow: minor surgery, replenishes in 4-6 weeks. Kidney: major surgery, about 0.03% death and 1% complications (Kiberd & Tennankore 2017 estimate donation may reduce life expectancy by 6 months to a year for most donors). Liver lobe: major surgery, regenerates in about 12 weeks, notable risk (0.5-1% death). Lung segment: major, does not regenerate. Womb: major surgery. Also domino transplants.

**Hurdle 1: lawfulness of removal (common law / MCA).**
- *Adults with capacity*: valid consent makes removal lawful, unless contrary to public policy (*R v Brown*). Living donation is contrary to public policy only where removal is likely to cause irreversible harm or death.
- *Adults lacking capacity*: must be in their **best interests** under the MCA (s.5 and s.4; s.1-3 capacity, noting s.3(3) and s.1(3) for fluctuating capacity).
  - *Strunk v Strunk* (1969) (US): kidney donation from an incapacitated brother authorised.
  - *Re Y (Mental Incapacity: Bone Marrow Transplant)* [1997] Fam 110: bone marrow from a severely learning-disabled woman to her dying sister held in Y's best interests (preserving her relationship with her mother). Connell J: an unusual case and a particularly close family; doubtful precedent for more intrusive surgery.
  - *AN NHS Foundation Trust v MC* [2020] EWCOP 33 (Cohen J): donation by 18-year-old MC to her mother was in MC's best interests – "clear benefits, emotional, social and psychological, to MC of her mother's life being extended". Cohen J also warned (per Brazier & Cave) of a "vacuum" in protecting donors under 18, since the HTA focuses on consent and inducements rather than the donor's best interests.
  - Cf. European Convention on Human Rights and Biomedicine, Art 20 (lecture comparison).
- *Children*: FLRA 1969 s.8(1) applies to "treatment" only, not donation, so a 16-17-year-old consenting to donation is not treated as an adult. *Re W* [1993] Fam 64, Lord Donaldson (obiter): consent must come from a proxy or a Gillick-competent child; it is "highly improbable" that a child would be Gillick competent to donate; "inconceivable" that a doctor would proceed without both parents' and child's consent; the Gillick-competent child's right to consent "extends to the donation of blood or organs"; doctors should apply to court for guidance. Compare *Bell v Tavistock* [2021] (Lord Burnett): it is for clinicians to judge competence, not for the court to give them a "manual".

**Hurdle 2: appropriate consent for storage and use (s.1, s.5 HTA).** Appropriate consent is required for storage and use of relevant material from a living person for transplantation (s.1(1)(d), (f), Sch 1 Pt 1).
- *Adults*: the donor's consent (s.3(2)).
- *Adults lacking capacity*: s.6 allows regulations to deem consent; reg 3(2)(a) of the 2006 Regulations deems consent where the person acts in what he reasonably believes to be the donor's best interests (Code A [114]ff).
- *Children* (under 18: s.54(1)): the child's consent (s.2(2)); or, if the child has not made a valid decision and is not competent or fails to decide, consent of a person with parental responsibility (s.2(3)). Code F Pt 1 [44]: children can be living organ donors only in extremely rare circumstances; Code A [131]: Gillick is the benchmark for competence.

**Hurdle 3: s.33 restrictions.**
- s.33(1)/(2): offence to remove or use **transplantable material** from a living person for transplantation; s.33(3): unless regulations permit, where (a) the HTA is satisfied there is no reward in contravention of s.32 and the regulations' conditions are satisfied, and (b) there is compliance with those conditions. Defence of reasonable belief: s.33(5); penalty s.33(6); definition by regulations: s.33(7).
- **Human Tissue Act 2004 (Persons who Lack Capacity to Consent and Transplants) Regulations 2006/1659:**
  - *Reg 10 – transplantable material*: (a) an organ, or part of an organ if to be used for the same purpose as the entire organ; (b) bone marrow; (c) peripheral blood stem cells – removed from a living person with the intention of transplanting into another. Reg 10(2): not transplantable where the primary purpose of removal is treating the donor. Reg 10(3): (b) and (c) apply only where the donor is an adult or child lacking capacity to consent. Simplified: capacitous donor → organs only; incapacitated donor → organs, bone marrow and PBSC. Reg 2: "organ" means a differentiated and vital part of the body, formed by different tissues, maintaining structure, vascularisation and capacity to develop physiological functions with an important level of autonomy.
  - *Reg 11 – conditions*: (2) the doctor with clinical responsibility for the donor has referred the matter to the HTA; (3) the HTA is satisfied about s.32 and that removal is consensual or otherwise lawful; (6)/(7) unless authorised by court order, qualified persons have conducted separate interviews with the donor (or person giving consent) and the recipient (Code F Pt 1 [80]ff).
  - *Reg 12 – three-member panel* where: the donor is an adult lacking capacity or a child and the material is an organ or part (reg 12(2)/(3)); or the donor is a capacitous adult and the case involves paired, pooled or non-directed altruistic donation (reg 12(4)).
- **Court approval** before HTA approval for: an organ or bone marrow from an incapacitated adult; an organ from a child; bone marrow from a child who is not competent where there is dispute or doubt about best interests (Code F Pt 1 [45]-[46], [56]; Code G [37], [48]). Code F Pt 1 [45]: court approval should be obtained before removing a solid organ or part organ from a child; [46]: HTA panel approval only after court approval.
- **Types of living donation**: almost all is directed to a specific recipient; most donors are genetically and emotionally related (panel needed only if the donor lacks capacity or is a child). Paired (2-way) and pooled (3-way) exchanges: 125 paired/pooled kidney transplants in 2024-25; 60 non-directed altruistic kidney donations.

**s.32 prohibition of commercial dealings.**
- s.32(1): offences to (a) give or receive a **reward** for the supply of, or an offer to supply, **controlled material**; (b) seek a person willing to supply it for reward; (c) offer to supply it for reward; (d) initiate or negotiate such an arrangement; (e) take part in managing a body that does so. s.32(2): publishing or distributing advertisements inviting or offering supply for reward. Penalties s.32(4)-(5).
- s.32(7): "reward" excludes reimbursement of expenses and loss of earnings.
- **Controlled material** (s.32(8)/(10)): material that consists of or includes human cells and is removed for transplantation, except (s.32(9), s.54(7)) gametes, embryos, "material which has become property by reason of the application of human skill", and cell lines created outside the body. It is narrower than "relevant material" and wider than "transplantable material".
- s.32(3): HTA may designate persons who may lawfully trade.
- The handout asks whether GMC powers alone were insufficient (cf. the Raymond Crockett case: a doctor struck off in 1990 for procuring organs, noted in Cronin & Douglas).

## Debate and critique
- **"Best interests" of incapacitated donors.** Re Y and MC find benefit to the donor through relationships. Brazier & Cave ask whether Re Y amounted to "enforced donation", the benefit being "somewhat remote". Critics see a Kantian use of a person merely as a means; defenders see a relational understanding of welfare. Venter's commentary on MC ("Revisiting best interests and 'altruistic' incapacitous stem cell donation") discusses this extension.
- **Child donors.** Re W's caution and the requirement for court and panel approval show heightened protection; Cohen J's "vacuum" concern for under-18 bone marrow donors.
- **Uterus transplantation (Alghrani 2018, J Law & Biosciences).** Explores whether there is a positive "right" to gestate grounded in procreative liberty, and whether UTx should be publicly funded and extended beyond cisgender women – raising questions about non-life-saving living (and deceased) donation and resource allocation.
- **Payment for organs.** Some theories oppose commercialisation in principle (dignity as constraint, and some utilitarians on consequences), whereas dignity as empowerment points the other way. Three analogies offering prima facie support for paying living providers (do they work?):
  1. **Risky employment** – we pay boxers, fire-fighters, soldiers, racing drivers, oil rig workers to take risks;
  2. **Paid participation in first-in-human trials**;
  3. **The unpaid analogy** – we already permit unpaid risky donation (related and non-directed altruistic donation), so the risk itself is accepted; why is payment the problem?
- **Harris and Erin, 'An Ethical Market in Human Organs' (2003) 29 JME 137.** Propose a regulated **monopsony**: a single purchaser (an agency like the NHS) buying organs and allocating them by a fair conception of medical priority; no direct sales, no exploitation of low-income countries; testing and provenance controls; vendors and their families equally eligible to receive organs. They cite a view that trade in organs should be regulated rather than banned. Objections: exploitation of the poor, undermining altruism, coercive inducement; respond using the analogies and the monopsony safeguards.
- **Formative feedback**: arguments about commercialisation are best focused on s.32 HTA 2004.

## Applying it to a problem question
(the materials: Bert, with fluctuating capacity, in a lucid period wants to donate a kidney to his estranged brother Ant.)
1. **Removal (common law/MCA)**: Does Bert have capacity (ss.1-3)? Fluctuating – s.3(3) (short retention may suffice), s.1(3) practicable steps (wait for and support a lucid interval). If capacitous and consenting → lawful (public policy not breached for kidney donation). If not → s.5 and s.4 best interests: s.4(3) likely regain capacity – wait; s.4(6) his expressed wish; s.4(7) consult; relationship benefit is weak (brothers estranged; mother dead) – contrast Re Y and MC; Strunk.
2. **Storage and use (s.1 HTA)**: Bert's consent (s.3(2)); if he lacks capacity, deemed consent via s.6 and reg 3(2)(a) best interests.
3. **s.33**: a kidney is transplantable material; referral to HTA (reg 11(2)); HTA satisfied no reward and consensual/otherwise lawful (reg 11(3)); interviews (reg 11(6)-(7)); if Bert lacks capacity, a 3-member panel (reg 12) and prior court approval (Code F Pt 1).
4. **Recipient**: Ant (coma) – s.5 MCA best interests; consider dialysis as less invasive (s.1(6)).
5. **s.32**: any payment or reward beyond expenses/lost earnings is an offence.
6. Further question (a): Ant aged 15 refusing transplant – Gillick/Re W/Re M; court could override in best interests.
7. Traps: assuming FLRA s.8 covers child donors; forgetting the three separate hurdles; applying reg 10(1)(b)-(c) to capacitous donors.
