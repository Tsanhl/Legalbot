# Re P (Medical Treatment: Best Interests) [2003] EWHC 2327 (Fam) (case note)

> Caution: this note is built from secondary sources. The original judgment was not read. Check the facts, the short quotation and any pinpoint against the judgment or law report before citing.

## Overview

Re P is a first-instance decision of Johnson J in the Family Division. An NHS Trust asked the court to authorise blood transfusion for a 16-year-old Jehovah's Witness who refused it, with his parents' support. The judge reluctantly made the order, holding that the young person's best interests, judged broadly, required it. The case is cited to show that a 16- or 17-year-old's power to consent to treatment does not carry a matching right to refuse life-saving treatment.

## Facts

- The patient was a boy aged 16.
- He had a chronic medical condition that meant he might need a blood transfusion.
- He was a Jehovah's Witness and refused consent to any transfusion on religious grounds. His parents supported his refusal.
- The NHS Trust caring for him applied for an order allowing doctors to give him blood, against his and his parents' wishes, if that became necessary to save his life.

## Held

- Johnson J made the order in the terms the Trust sought.
- He did so reluctantly. The young person's clearly stated, religiously based refusal and his parents' views were weighty but not decisive.
- Best interests were assessed "in the widest possible sense": medical, religious and social factors, not medical factors alone. On that assessment, allowing a transfusion served the young person's best interests.
- The judge accepted that treatment imposed against a patient's wishes should be avoided where possible. He also recognised that in some cases the refusal of a young person nearing 18 might be respected, especially where treatment would only postpone an inevitable death. He did not treat this as such a case.

## Applying it to a problem question

1. **Age.** A 16- or 17-year-old can consent under s 8 of the Family Law Reform Act 1969, but the court can override a refusal where welfare requires it (Re W [1993] Fam 64). Re P applies this to a Jehovah's Witness refusing blood.
2. **Risk.** Ask whether treatment is needed to prevent death or serious harm. Re P concerned a risk to life. A lower-risk or elective setting may change the balance.
3. **Weighing.** Take a broad view of best interests. Weigh the young person's age, maturity, settled beliefs and wishes, and the parents' views against the risk to life.
4. **Limits.** Flag the judge's caveats: a refusal by someone close to 18, or a case where treatment only postpones an inevitable death, may deserve respect.
5. **Framing.** Consider limiting any authority to situations where blood is needed to save life, so the young person's wishes are respected as far as possible.
6. **Current law.** Treat Re P as an illustration. Apply the Court of Appeal framework in Re E & F (Minors: Blood Transfusion) [2021] EWCA Civ 1888, which also concerned 16- and 17-year-old Jehovah's Witnesses. Contrast Re J (Blood Transfusion: Older Child: Jehovah's Witnesses) [2024] EWHC 1034 (Fam), where Cobb J declined to authorise blood products for a 17-year-old Jehovah's Witness facing surgery. The outcome depends on the facts.
