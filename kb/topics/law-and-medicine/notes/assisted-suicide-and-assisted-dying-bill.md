# End of Life (Adults) 3 - Assisted Suicide, the DPP Policy and the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill

## Overview
Suicide has not been a crime since the Suicide Act 1961, but **encouraging or assisting suicide** remains an offence (s.2(1)), punishable by up to 14 years. Prosecution requires the DPP's consent (s.2(4)), and since *Purdy* (2009) the DPP has published a policy listing public interest factors for and against prosecution. Repeated human rights challenges (Pretty, Nicklinson, Conway) have failed, the courts holding that Parliament is the proper forum. Parliament is now considering the **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill** ("TIA Bill"), a private member's bill that would allow terminally ill, capacitous adults to be provided with assistance to end their own lives, subject to safeguards. This is a major essay area combining law, ethics and institutional competence.

## The law
1. **Suicide Act 1961, s.2** (as amended in 2009):
   - s.2(1): D commits an offence if (a) D does an act capable of encouraging or assisting the suicide or attempted suicide of another, and (b) D's act was intended to encourage or assist suicide or an attempt.
   - s.2(1C): triable on indictment; maximum 14 years' imprisonment.
   - s.2(4): no proceedings except by or with the consent of the DPP.
   - Distinguish: doing the act that ends another's life is murder or manslaughter, even if requested (DPP policy para 33, per Brazier & Cave).
2. **Human rights litigation.**
   - *R (Pretty) v DPP* [2001] UKHL 61; *Pretty v UK* (2002) 35 EHRR 1: Mrs Pretty, dying of motor neurone disease, sought an undertaking that her husband would not be prosecuted for helping her die. The HL rejected arguments under Arts 2, 3, 8, 9 and 14: Art 2 protects the sanctity of life, not a right to die (Lord Bingham); Art 3 imposes no obligation to help someone end their life. Lord Steyn said Art 8 does not relate to the manner of dying. The ECtHR was "not prepared to exclude" that the ban interfered with Art 8(1) but held it justified under Art 8(2) to protect the vulnerable. Lord Bingham: an elderly person might opt for an early death to avoid being a burden.
   - *R (Purdy) v DPP* [2009] UKHL 45: Ms Purdy (multiple sclerosis) feared her husband would be prosecuted for helping her travel to a Swiss clinic. Lower courts held Art 8 not engaged and the policy sufficiently clear; the House of Lords held **Art 8 was engaged** and the absence of an offence-specific policy meant the DPP's discretion did not satisfy Art 8(2) ("in accordance with the law"). The DPP was required to publish guidance.
   - *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice; R (AM) v DPP* [2014] UKSC 38: Appeal 1 (Nicklinson, Lamb) sought a declaration that s.2 is incompatible with Art 8. A 5-4 majority held the court *could* in principle consider compatibility, but only Lady Hale and Lord Kerr would have granted a declaration; Lord Neuberger thought Parliament should first have the chance to consider the issue; Lords Clarke, Sumption, Reed and Hughes said it was for Parliament. Dismissed 7-2. Appeal 2: the DPP overturned the CA's declaration that the 2010 policy lacked sufficient clarity (Martin's claim). Lord Sumption [255] gives a summary of the current law; Burnett LJ in *Conway* [2017] EWHC 640 [7]-[17] summarises the nine justices' positions (both recommended by the materials). The 2015 ECtHR application was inadmissible.
   - **DPP policy update (Oct 2014)** after Nicklinson: the suspect being a healthcare professional is a factor in favour of prosecution only where the victim was "in his or her care".
   - *R (Conway) v Secretary of State for Justice* [2018] EWCA Civ 1431: Mr Conway (motor neurone disease) argued the blanket ban breached Art 8 and proposed a scheme with safeguards including High Court approval. The ban was held proportionate; "Parliament is a far better body" to decide given contested ethical views and the risks of change. Supreme Court refused permission to appeal.
   - *Karsai v Hungary* [2024] ECHR 516: states have a "considerable" margin of appreciation, both on whether to intervene and on the detailed rules, though not unlimited [144]; Art 2 does not prevent states from allowing physician-assisted dying provided appropriate and sufficient safeguards prevent abuse [145].
3. **DPP Policy for Prosecutors in Respect of Cases of Encouraging or Assisting Suicide** (Feb 2010, updated Oct 2014). Two stages: evidential and public interest.
   - **Para 43: 16 factors in favour of prosecution**, including: victim under 18; victim lacked capacity (MCA) to reach an informed decision; victim had not reached a voluntary, clear, settled and informed decision; decision not clearly communicated to the suspect; victim did not personally seek the suspect's help; suspect not wholly motivated by compassion (e.g. stood to gain); suspect pressured the victim or did not take reasonable steps to ensure no one else did; history of violence or abuse; victim physically able to do the act; suspect unknown to the victim and helped via websites/publications; suspect helped more than one unconnected victim; suspect was paid; **suspect acted as a doctor, nurse, other healthcare professional, professional carer or person in authority and the victim was in his or her care** (43.14); suicide in a public place; suspect involved in an organisation providing a physical environment for suicide.
   - **Para 45: 6 factors against prosecution**: victim's voluntary, clear, settled and informed decision; suspect wholly motivated by compassion; only minor encouragement or assistance; suspect sought to dissuade; reluctant assistance in face of a determined wish; suspect reported the suicide and assisted police.
   - Para 44: gain is not decisive if compassion was the only driving force. Para 47: lists not exhaustive; each case on its facts.
   - Brazier & Cave: the policy shifts emphasis from victim characteristics to suspect motives; it has produced a permissive approach (e.g. CPS figures 2009-2021: most referrals not proceeded with; few prosecutions). Critics (e.g. Laing) say it changes criminal law without Parliament.
4. **Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill (2024-25 / 2025).** Status per the sources (autumn 2025): a private member's bill sponsored by Kim Leadbeater MP (Commons) and Lord Falconer (Lords); Government neutral. Commons Third Reading on 13 June 2025 (MPs voted 314 to 219 for it to progress); Lords Second Reading on 12 and 19 September 2025; a Lords Select Committee took evidence; Lords Committee stage due to start 14 November 2025. the materials warns the Bill's status and content are subject to change.
   - **Purpose**: allow terminally ill adults, subject to safeguards, to request and be provided with assistance to end their own life.
   - **Eligibility** (slides): 18+; terminal illness with life expectancy of 6 months or less; capacity; clear, settled and voluntary request; self-administration.
   - **Process** (impact assessment summary, as at end of Commons committee stage): preliminary discussion with a registered medical practitioner (recorded); first declaration; assessments by a co-ordinating doctor and an independent doctor (diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, palliative/hospice care); referral to an Assisted Dying Review Panel (law, psychiatry, social care); second declaration; reflection period (slides: 14 days); provision of an approved substance which the person must self-administer (cl.23(8)).
   - **Safeguards**: capacity; a clear, settled and informed wish; voluntariness without coercion. **Offences** of inducing a declaration or self-administration (cl.31) and falsifying or destroying documents (cl.32); providing assistance in accordance with the Bill is not an offence (cl.29); codes of practice (cl.36); oversight by a Voluntary Assisted Dying Commissioner.
   - The Lords Delegated Powers and Constitution Committees criticised heavy reliance on delegated powers (TIA resources page).

## Debate and critique
- **Slippery slope.** John Keown (*Euthanasia, Ethics and Public Policy*, 2002) argues a *logical* slippery slope: if voluntary euthanasia is justified by autonomy plus a doctor's judgment that life is not worth living, consistency requires accepting non-voluntary euthanasia for those who cannot ask. Stephen Smith ('Fallacies of the Logical Slippery Slope in the Debate on Physician-Assisted Suicide and Euthanasia' (2005) 13(2) Med L Rev 224) responds that this is fallacious: autonomy and medical judgment are both *necessary* conditions, so the request requirement blocks the slide; his earlier work found insufficient evidence for an *empirical* slippery slope.
- **Courts v Parliament.** Nicklinson's majority and Conway defer to Parliament on a contested moral question; Lady Hale and Lord Kerr thought a declaration of incompatibility appropriate. The TIA Bill tests whether Parliament will now act.
- **The DPP policy as de facto law.** It brings clarity (Purdy) but arguably legalises assistance by compassionate relatives while deterring doctors (43.14), the reverse of what regulated schemes like the Bill propose.
- **CLADD critique of the Bill** (Owen, Ruck Keene and Sleeman, Complex Life and Death Decisions Group, Aug 2025 briefing; neutral on principle): "not good law as drafted" because it does not properly delineate the cohort (e.g. tension between tight "terminal illness" scope and the right to refuse treatment that could reverse the disease); its capacity test is not fit for purpose; it is unclear whether assistance is "treatment"; it inadequately addresses palliative care; multi-disciplinary consideration comes too late; advocacy is unclear; the Panel is both over- and under-protective; complications after assistance are not addressed; monitoring is inadequate.
- **Moral theories.** Utilitarian and rights-based theories are prima facie supportive of voluntary assisted dying (Gewirth: self-harming acts up to suicide must not be interfered with); duty-based (sanctity of life: Keown, Finnis) and some virtue theories oppose. Pretty's emphasis on protecting the vulnerable reflects a duty-based or mixed concern.
- **Discrimination against the disabled.** Those physically unable to end their own lives (Nicklinson, Lamb) are worst affected by the ban; but the Bill would still exclude those not terminally ill, and require self-administration.

## Applying it to a problem question
(the materials: Simon asks Constance and Felix to help end his life.)
1. Is the proposed conduct assistance (Simon performs the final act) or killing (D performs it)? Killing = murder (Cox).
2. **s.2(1)**: does D's act have the capacity to assist, and does D intend to assist? Providing drugs, arranging travel abroad may qualify.
3. **Prosecution discretion (s.2(4)) and the DPP policy**: for Constance (wife): factors against – Simon's capacity and voluntary, clear, settled and informed decision; compassion; reluctance; reporting. Watch for gain (inheritance) and pressure. For Felix: factor in favour – healthcare professional with the victim in his care (43.14).
4. Simon's **capacity** matters both for the policy (43.2) and for his ability to refuse ventilation (which is lawful and may achieve his aim without assisted suicide).
5. **Human rights**: no right to assistance under Arts 2, 3, 8 (Pretty, Nicklinson, Conway); Art 8 engaged but ban justified.
6. **Reform**: if the TIA Bill were enacted in its current form, Simon would need a terminal illness with ≤6 months' prognosis and capacity, would have to self-administer, and would go through the two-doctor/Panel process; check the current status before relying on it.
7. Traps: saying assisted suicide is lawful because prosecutions are rare; ignoring that suicide abroad may still involve acts in England; describing the Bill as law.
