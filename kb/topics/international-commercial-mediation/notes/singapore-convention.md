# The Singapore Convention on Mediation

## Overview
The **United Nations Convention on International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation** (the "Singapore Convention") is the centrepiece of international mediation law. It aims to do for mediated settlement agreements what the **New York Convention 1958** did for arbitral awards: provide a uniform, efficient cross-border framework for enforcing them, and for relying on them as a defence. Before it, an international mediated settlement agreement (MSA) was a contract that had to be enforced through litigation under the enforcing state's private international law, or converted into a judgment or consent award. Feehily says the Convention elevates "an otherwise mere contract to a sui generis status akin to an arbitral award or a judgment". The Convention works alongside the revised **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Mediation and International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation** (2018), whose section 3 (arts 16–20) mirrors arts 1–6 of the Convention.

## The law

### 1. History and status
- Adopted by the UN General Assembly on **20 December 2018** after almost three years of work in UNCITRAL Working Group II.
- Opened for signature in Singapore on **7 August 2019**: **46 states** signed that day, including China and the USA. Feehily says this was the largest number of states to sign a new convention; the Overview piece calls it one of the highest numbers of first-day signatories for any UN trade convention.
- Entered into force on **12 September 2020**, six months after the third ratification (art 14). Each new party is bound six months after it ratifies, accepts, approves or accedes (arts 11, 14).
- Status (treaty-status extract in the raw folder): 58 signatories and 20 parties at the time recorded. Singapore ratified on 25 February 2020; the **United Kingdom signed on 3 May 2023** (no ratification is recorded there); the USA and China signed on 7 August 2019 without ratification recorded.
- Comparison: the New York Convention began with 10 signatories and now has over 160 contracting states.
- **UK implementation**: after a consultation (2 February to 1 April 2022), the Government's response of 2 March 2023 confirmed an intention to sign and ratify; the UK signed on 3 May 2023. A later consultation on implementation proposed Regulations under the **Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020**, extending UK-wide (with devolved consent), plus court-rule changes. Proposals include: a **registration** procedure in a designated court (closer to the Civil Jurisdiction and Judgments Act 1982 model than to the "leave to enforce" model in Arbitration Act 1996 s 101 / CPR 62.18); the court may decide registration **ex parte** or direct service on the respondent; the respondent can then challenge registration on art 5 grounds; and parties can invoke a settlement as a defence. On reservations, most consultation respondents opposed both art 8 reservations (one warned that under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties 1969 arts 20–21, other states could invoke the same reservations reciprocally against the UK); a minority favoured opt-in on party-autonomy grounds.
- Examples of reservations made: Belarus, Saudi Arabia (art 8(1)(a): no application to settlements involving the state or its agencies); Pakistan (both art 8(1)(a) and (b)).

### 2. Rationale
- Parties who choose mediation should not have to use another ADR process (arbitration) just to get equivalent enforcement status. It may also be awkward in an amicable negotiation to raise enhanced enforcement.
- The parties agreed to both process and outcome, which justifies more than "mere" contract status. It is ironic that arbitral awards (process agreed, outcome not) had a better enforcement status (Schnabel).
- Lack of a cross-border mechanism was seen as a significant barrier to commercial use of mediation. Most surveys found enhanced enforcement would encourage users (Strong; Köhler).
- Feehily: the Convention's success will be shown by its being **rarely invoked**, because parties comply voluntarily. Its biggest impact may be legitimacy, credibility and visibility for mediation, and a push for domestic mediation laws and mediation hubs.

### 3. Five key compromises (McCormick and Ong, cited by Feehily)
1. "Recognition" was not used because of its varying meanings; a **functional description** was used instead.
2. MSAs already enforceable as **judgments or consent awards are excluded** (they have other regimes).
3. **Opt-in and opt-out** approaches were both accommodated (the art 8(1)(b) reservation).
4. **Mediator-misconduct defences** were included but narrowly framed with a high burden of proof.
5. Instrument form: a **convention plus a revised Model Law**, working together.

### 4. Scope (art 1) and definitions (art 2)
- Applies to an agreement **resulting from mediation**, **concluded in writing**, by parties to resolve a **commercial dispute**, which **at the time it was concluded** was **international** because:
  - (a) at least two parties have their places of business in different states; or
  - (b) the state where the parties have their places of business differs from (i) the state where a substantial part of the obligations is performed, or (ii) the state most closely connected with the subject matter (art 1(1)).
- Unlike the Model Law, parties cannot simply agree that a settlement is international.
- Art 2(1): with more than one place of business, the relevant one is the one most closely related to the dispute resolved; with none, the party's habitual residence.
- It does not matter whether any of the states involved is a party, or where the mediation took place (UK consultation). A purely domestic UK settlement is outside scope.
- **No "seat"**: the Convention deliberately has no seat or state of origin, so no state can set the settlement aside with effect elsewhere and there is no reciprocity requirement. The MSA is effectively a **"stateless instrument"** reviewed only where relief is sought (Schnabel; Chong and Steffek).
- **"Mediation"** (art 2(3)): a process, "irrespective of the expression used or the basis upon which the process is carried out", in which parties try to reach an amicable settlement "with the assistance of a third person or persons ('the mediator') lacking the authority to impose a solution". This is broad: the parties need not call it mediation. Med-arb is not excluded if the neutral had no power to impose an award while mediating. A judge who will decide the case cannot be the "mediator". There is no minimum time for the mediator's involvement.
- **"In writing"** (art 2(2)): content recorded in any form, including accessible electronic communications. This can therefore cover online dispute resolution, which Alexander and Chong suggest could widen access for small and medium businesses.

### 5. Exclusions (art 1(2)–(3))
- Settlements arising from **consumer** transactions (personal, family or household purposes) and those relating to **family, inheritance or employment law**. The rationale is the likelihood of unequal bargaining power and the complexity of building protections in; "commercial" should otherwise be read broadly.
- Settlements **approved by a court or concluded in court proceedings and enforceable as a judgment** in that state (avoiding overlap with the Hague Choice of Court Convention 2005 and Judgments Convention 2019).
- Settlements **recorded and enforceable as an arbitral award**. To avoid both a gap and an overlap with the New York Convention, this is judged from the enforcing state's perspective.
- The Convention does **not** cover **agreements to mediate** (unlike the Model Law), because they are not exclusive like arbitration agreements; the scope of any agreement to mediate is irrelevant. Butlien criticises this omission and argues for an amendment.

### 6. Obligations of states (art 3)
- **Art 3(1) "sword"**: each party "shall enforce a settlement agreement in accordance with its rules of procedure and under the conditions laid down in this Convention". No particular procedure is required, but enforcement must happen, without prior litigation on the merits or conversion into a judgment, notarial act or award.
- **Art 3(2) "shield"**: if a dispute arises about a matter a party says was already resolved by a settlement agreement, the state must allow that party to invoke the agreement to prove the matter is resolved. This gives a complete defence. Because "recognition" is avoided, the Convention uses the word "relief" elsewhere.
- Art 3 is the only provision imposing positive obligations on states.

### 7. Requirements for reliance (art 4) – exhaustive
- The party must supply: (a) the settlement agreement **signed by the parties**; (b) **evidence it resulted from mediation**, such as (i) the mediator's signature on it, (ii) a document signed by the mediator confirming the mediation was carried out, (iii) an attestation by the administering institution, or (iv) other evidence acceptable to the competent authority.
- Electronic signatures suffice if a method identifies the person and indicates intention and is appropriately reliable or proven in fact (art 4(2)).
- Translation may be requested (art 4(3)); the authority may require documents needed to verify compliance (art 4(4)); it "shall act expeditiously" (art 4(5)).
- The "resulted from mediation" evidence responds to fears of misuse for fraud or money laundering. The list cannot be extended by domestic law, though public-register rules (e.g. land transfers) still apply.

### 8. Grounds for refusing relief (art 5) – exhaustive and permissive
At the request of the resisting party, and only if it **proves** the ground (art 5(1)):
- (a) a party was under some **incapacity**;
- (b) the agreement (i) is **null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed** under the law the parties validly chose or, failing that, the law deemed applicable by the enforcing authority; (ii) is **not binding or not final** according to its terms; or (iii) has been **subsequently modified**;
- (c) the obligations (i) **have been performed**, or (ii) are **not clear or comprehensible**;
- (d) granting relief would be **contrary to the terms** of the agreement;
- (e) a **serious breach by the mediator** of applicable standards, without which the party would not have entered the agreement;
- (f) the mediator's **failure to disclose** circumstances raising **justifiable doubts** about impartiality or independence, with a material impact or undue influence, without which the party would not have entered the agreement.

The authority may also refuse of its own motion (art 5(2)) if: (a) relief would be contrary to the **public policy** of that state; or (b) the subject matter is **not capable of settlement by mediation** under its law.

Points to note:
- There are **nine** grounds. Several echo the New York Convention (incapacity; null and void; public policy; non-arbitrability equivalent).
- The **burden** is on the resisting party (except for art 5(2)), reversing the old position where the enforcing party had to prove an enforceable contract.
- Art 5(1)(b)(i) imports ordinary contract vitiating factors (duress, unconscionability, undue influence, misrepresentation, mistake, fraud). With art 5(1)(c)(ii), there is a concern that these could turn expedited enforcement back into protracted litigation (Abramson). Feehily hopes courts will construe them narrowly, guided by arbitral case law favouring a narrow, international view of public policy.
- "Not final or binding" is judged from the agreement itself, not external evidence. "Subsequently modified" ensures the final version is enforced. "Performed" prevents double recovery. "Contrary to the terms" preserves autonomy (e.g. parties can exclude enforcement in certain states: Köhler).
- Grounds based on **domestic formalities** (mediator licensing, institutional rules, notarisation) are barred.
- The grounds are **permissive** ("may refuse"), and states implementing by legislation need not make all grounds available.
- Overlap between grounds was discussed but a guidance paragraph was not adopted (UNCITRAL A/CN.9/934).
- Misconduct grounds (e) and (f): see the separate note.

### 9. Parallel applications (art 6) and more favourable law (art 7)
- Art 6: if a related application has been made to a court, tribunal or other authority that may affect the relief, the enforcing authority may **adjourn** and, on request, **order security**. Chong and Steffek suggest this may create an implied ground for refusal where another court's refusal is recognised.
- Art 7: the Convention does not deprive a party of rights to rely on a settlement under more favourable domestic law or treaties (a "more favourable right" provision).

### 10. Reservations (art 8)
- Only two are permitted: (1)(a) not applying the Convention to settlements to which the state, its governmental agencies or persons acting for them are a party; (1)(b) applying it **only if the parties have agreed** to its application (**opt-in**).
- No other reservations; they can be made or withdrawn at any time, taking effect six months after deposit if made later (to prevent strategic use); they apply only in the reserving state's courts. Feehily says reservations have no reciprocal effect; a UK consultation respondent disagreed, pointing to the Vienna Convention rules.
- Art 9: the Convention and reservations apply only to settlements concluded after entry into force for that state.
- The default (opt-out) approach plus minimal formalities assumes parties want enforceability. The opt-in reservation would greatly weaken it in practice.
- Art 12: regional economic integration organisations (e.g. the EU) may join; art 13: territorial units.

### 11. Relationship with the Mediation Model Law
- Model Law art 15: settlement agreements are binding and enforceable. Section 3 (arts 16–20) mirrors Convention arts 1–6 but covers **all** settlement agreements, mediated or not. It is the domestic vehicle for implementing the Convention and an alternative for states not ready to ratify.

## Debate and critique
- **Is it needed?** Compliance with mediated settlements is already high; Clark and Sourdin ask whether it is "a solution in search of a problem". Feehily's answer: its main value is legitimacy, credibility and encouraging mediation where it would not otherwise be tried, with success measured by rare use.
- **Arbitration-shaped?** Critics (e.g. a panellist quoted by Kallipetis) say it is "redolent of arbitration" and imports arbitral concepts (bias, misconduct) into a consensual process. Defenders say the wording was chosen carefully to reflect mediation's differences (materiality, causation, burden).
- **Defences as a back door to protracted litigation**: art 5(1)(b)(i) and (c)(ii) may let defendants reopen everything. Narrow construction is needed. The empirical US data (Coben) show most defences fail.
- **No seat**: it promotes stateless enforcement and avoids artificial localisation, but leaves uncertainty about which law and standards apply (especially for art 5(1)(e): Köhler).
- **Silence on confidentiality**: the Convention does not mention confidentiality or privilege, so proving defences depends on the forum's evidence law (Köhler). This produces inconsistency between states.
- **Opt-in reservation**: if widely used it would undercut the Convention. The UK consultation majority opposed it.
- **Agreements to mediate excluded**: Butlien argues mediation cannot reach full effectiveness without enforcing them.
- **Exclusions**: consumer, employment and family matters protect weaker parties but narrow the reach.
- **Future**: depends on ratifications (major economies have signed but, in the status extract, not ratified), use of reservations, and courts adopting a pro-enforcement approach like the one in arbitration (Feehily; Alexander and Chong).
- Butlien's thesis: the Convention is "extremely effective, though short of perfect", and mediation may and should increasingly be used, perhaps primarily, for international commercial disputes.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is the forum a party?** Has the Convention entered into force there (six months after ratification), and was the settlement concluded after that date (art 9)? Any art 8 reservations (state party; opt-in, and if so did the parties opt in)?
2. **Scope**: does the agreement result from "mediation" (art 2(3), a neutral without power to impose)? Is it in writing (including emails)? Is the dispute commercial? Is it international at conclusion (art 1(1)(a) or (b); art 2(1) for places of business)?
3. **Exclusions**: consumer/family/inheritance/employment; court-approved and enforceable as a judgment; recorded as an arbitral award.
4. **Art 4 requirements**: signed by the parties; evidence of mediation (mediator signature, mediator document, institutional attestation, other evidence); translation.
5. **Art 5 defences**: go through each, remembering the resisting party carries the burden, the grounds are exhaustive and permissive, and courts should read them narrowly. Check the governing law for 5(1)(b)(i). Check whether obligations are clear (5(1)(c)(ii)). Consider misconduct (5(1)(e), (f)) with causation. Public policy and non-mediability are for the court of its own motion.
6. **Parallel proceedings**: adjourn and security (art 6).
7. **Alternatives**: more favourable domestic law (art 7); converting to a consent award; ordinary contract claim.
8. **UK-specific**: the UK has signed, not (per the sources) ratified. Explain the proposed registration procedure if asked.

Traps: applying the Convention to domestic settlements; forgetting the judgment/award exclusions; assuming it enforces mediation clauses; treating art 5 as mandatory.
