# Mediator Liability, Immunity, the Practice of Law and Judges as Mediators

## Overview
The second half of Chapter 5 / the materials asks when a mediator can be held legally responsible, how mediators protect themselves (conduct, disclaimers, immunity), when mediating turns into practising law, and whether judges should mediate. There are very few claims against mediators. Feehily explains why: confidentiality, the facilitative style, substantial compliance with obligations, and above all the **causation problem**, since the parties, not the mediator, decide. A small number of cases (*Sejane*, *Kasipersad*, *Clay v Lenkiewicz Foundation*, *Tapoohi v Lewenberg*) show where risk lies: evaluating, advising, acting like an agent of one side, and drafting settlement terms. The jurisdiction is mainly comparative: England, Australia, South Africa and the US.

## The law

### 1. Why claims are rare
- Mediation is confidential and usually facilitative; the dearth of cases reflects substantial compliance and a low-risk activity (Feehily).
- It is hard to show a causal link between the mediator's conduct and loss, because the mediator neither advises nor decides (Koo, "Exploring Mediator Liability in Negligence" (2016)).
- Two possible bases: **substantive** (a party could have settled on better terms or should not have settled) and **procedural** (unfair process regardless of outcome). Procedural claims are notionally easier, especially where lawyers are present and the mediator keeps a clear process/content distinction.
- Coben's US data (2013–17): mediator misconduct was rarely raised and never succeeded as a defence to enforcement.

### 2. Liability in contract
- Private commercial mediation is a **creature of contract**. The mediator has no implied authority; power is conferred by the parties for as long as they wish. Duties come from the **agreement to mediate** (often a provider's standard form, which will usually limit liability).
- An implied term to act with **reasonable care, skill and diligence** is likely. The standard is hard to fix given the flexible process and differing styles. Mediators hold themselves out as skilled facilitators, so codes of conduct and industry practice help define reasonableness (in England, compliance with Law Society codes is evidence of good practice for solicitor-mediators).
- The claimant must prove breach, **actual loss caused by the breach**, and that loss is not too remote. The hurdles are: defining reasonable mediator behaviour; showing causation when parties make the decisions; quantifying damages ("largely theoretical"); mitigation; and **exclusion/immunity clauses**. Breach of confidence or conflict of interest would be easier to prove than negligence.

### 3. Liability in tort
- A negligence claim (act or omission, or **negligent misstatement** causing economic loss) requires: duty of care (a relationship of **proximity**), breach, actual damage, causation, and no contributory negligence. Other possible torts include defamation, breach of confidence, misleading conduct, undue influence, unconscionability and statutory torts.
- **Standard of care**: "reasonable in the circumstances", informed by standards and the agreement to mediate. Commercial parties are usually sophisticated and represented.
- **Proximity is hard to establish** if the mediator does not give advice (especially in their own professional field), recommends independent advice to unadvised parties, and stays out of drafting the settlement.
- **Causation**: one cannot know what the party (or the other side) would otherwise have done, and legal representation or a recommendation to get advice breaks the link. *Lange v Marshall* 622 SW 2d 237 (Mo Ct App 1981): a negligence claim for failing to negotiate a better settlement failed for lack of causation.
- **Negligent misstatement** needs reliance that the mediator knew or ought to have known of. The mediator can **disclaim responsibility** for the accuracy of statements and recommend independent advice. A knowingly false statement intended to be relied on would be fraudulent misrepresentation.
- For liability to become realistic, the mediator's role would have to be redefined to give **greater control over process and outcome**.

### 4. Fiduciary obligations
- Fiduciary duties are strict and do not need a contract or a predefined standard. Mediators owe "fiduciary-like" duties of trustworthiness, diligence and absence of bias. These could be breached by withholding information influential to settlement, creating coercion, secret communications with one party, or dishonesty about qualifications.
- But a mediator **cannot act for two beneficiaries** with conflicting interests and is appointed to be neutral, not to act in either party's best interests. This is incompatible with fiduciary status. An **equitable duty of confidence** is more likely.

### 5. Other liability and accountability
- **Consumer and fair-trading legislation**: false advertising, misleading statements about services, unconscionable conduct; implied warranties of due care and skill.
- **Criminal liability**: being party to fraud or an offence, or colluding in a crime such as tax evasion.
- **Third parties**: e.g. subcontractors relying on advice given to a head contractor. Duty and causation are hard to prove, but this shows why the mediator should be **non-advisory and facilitative**.
- **Professional regulation**: lawyer-mediators remain bound by legal professional standards; straying into other professions risks unauthorised practice. *Werle v Rhode Island Bar Association* 755 F 2d 195 (1st Cir 1985) as described in the textbook: a mediator was successfully sued by the Rhode Island Bar Association on the basis that his mediation practice amounted to the unauthorised practice of law.
- **Provider discipline** (accreditation revoked, removal from panel) and **the market** (blacklisting by law firms).

### 6. Mediator behaviour and limiting exposure
- **It is not the mediator's responsibility to ensure a fair or just settlement.** The obligation is to facilitate an agreement the parties freely and willingly sign; the mediator does not inquire into why parties agree terms, provided the agreement is enforceable. The European Code part 3.2 allows the mediator to end a mediation if the settlement appears unenforceable or illegal.
- Practical steps: suggest parties bring advisers; avoid giving advice; recommend independent advice; avoid drafting; disclaim the accuracy of statements; ensure disclosure of documents is authorised and make no assertions about their provenance; record material representations in the settlement; use immunity clauses, "no reliance on the mediator" recitals and cooling-off clauses.
- **Cases**:
  - ***Sejane v Commission for Conciliation Mediation and Arbitration*** [2001] ZALC 156 (South African Labour Court): a settlement was set aside because an illiterate party was unrepresented. The CCMA Commissioner, whose role resembles a facilitative mediator's, was "derelict in her duties" for not properly considering the party's interests regarding representation.
  - ***TH Kasipersad v CCMA*** [2002] ZALC 89, (2003) 24 ILJ 178 (LC): an applicant withdrew after the Commissioner said he had a 50/50 chance, that a hearing would take days, and that he might have to pay for representation and further costs if he lost, without explaining the outcome if he won. The settlement was set aside: by sketching only some outcomes the Commissioner showed **bias**. A neutral who scenario-sketches must "present fully and dispassionately all the consequences". The court also held that a rule barring evidence about conciliation proceedings was unconstitutional, and suggested procedural advice should be given only if both parties ask.
  - ***Clay v Lenkiewicz Foundation*** (Plymouth County Court): the mediator conveyed the defendants' offer including a painting "professionally valued at £80,000, if sold at auction", using the defendants' valuation. It was in fact an **insurance** (replacement) valuation, higher than market value. The claimant accepted in reliance and later alleged misrepresentation, arguing the mediator had actual or apparent authority as the defendants' **agent**. The case settled. Lesson: the mediator is an independent actor, not an agent; authorise disclosures and make no claims about documents; recite relied-on facts in the settlement.
  - ***Tapoohi v Lewenberg (No 2)*** [2003] VSC 410 (Habersberger J): two sisters settled an estate dispute; the mediator dictated the settlement terms with little lawyer involvement and did not make them subject to tax advice despite the lawyers raising it. Capital gains tax reduced the settlement's value. When the former solicitors sought contribution from the mediator, the court held it "not beyond argument" that the mediator could be in breach of contractual and tortious duties, and refused summary judgment. The case then settled. No agreement to mediate had been signed, so there was no contractual immunity; the mediator bears the burden of proving immunity ([89]).
- Moffitt, "Ten Ways to Get Sued: A Guide for Mediators" (2003) is cited.

### 7. Mediator immunity
- **US and Australia**: immunity is mostly linked to **court-annexed** programmes. **England**: no immunity, because mediation is mostly private (court schemes are opt-in).
- Policy reasons for immunity: protection from defamation claims; finality of settlements; protecting confidentiality and integrity; avoiding risk-averse, legalistic mediators; ensuring a supply of mediators for court programmes. In the US the key justification is the link to the judicial process (Brooker).
- Mediators do not perform judicial or quasi-judicial functions, so there is **no common law immunity**. The sources are **statute** (unqualified, or qualified e.g. by good faith; there are very few provisions for private mediators in the US or Australia) and **contract**.
- **Contractual immunity**: providers' agreements usually exclude liability of mediator and provider (their effectiveness is unclear) and may include an indemnity. Exclusion clauses must be clear; doubts are construed against the mediator; they are ineffective for acts outside the agreement, where they conflict with statute, or for fraud or deliberate breach. In practice they are clear and a major impediment to claims.
- Feehily: immunity should be subject to **good faith** and the relevant code, and should fall away for bias, coercion or defective professional advice.
- Professional indemnity insurance is often required (e.g. CEDR Code). Premiums are low because there is no recorded claim against mediators in the UK.
- *Morgan Phillips Inc v JAMS/Endispute LLC* 44 Cal Rptr 3d 782 (2006): an arbitrator who withdrew to mediate lost arbitral immunity, as there was no sufficient nexus between the roles.

### 8. Mediation and the practice of law
- Lawyers' ethics codes did not originally address mediation (confidentiality, conflicts, unauthorised practice).
- **"Pure" facilitative mediation is not the practice of law.** US case law defines the practice of law as applying legal principles to concrete facts. *Dauphin County Bar Association v Macaro* 456 Pa 545 (1976) included evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of a client's case against an adversary's. The absence of a lawyer–client relationship has been used as the test, but reliance-based liability makes that less decisive.
- When mediators **predict or apply legal standards to facts**, especially when **drafting settlement agreements**, that looks like lawyers' work whether or not there is a lawyer–client relationship. Mediators are more likely to "deserve" immunity when all parties have independent advice.
- A lawyer-mediator must **separate the roles**; the optimal approach is the **facilitative model** (no advice, emphasis on impartiality).
- Bar associations and law societies often treat mediation as a specialised form of legal practice (e.g. Queensland Law Society Standards of Conduct for Solicitor Mediators 1996; Law Society of England and Wales Civil and Commercial Mediation Accreditation Scheme 2011). Lawyer duties of confidentiality, disclosure and reporting may conflict with mediator codes.
- **ABA Section of Dispute Resolution resolution (2002)**: "mediation is not the practice of law". Discussing legal issues does not create an attorney–client relationship or amount to legal advice. Drafting a memorandum or settlement recording the parties' terms is not practising law, but drafting beyond their terms may be. It is not practising law if all parties have counsel and the mediator says proposals are informational and not to be relied on as legal advice. Mediators should explain their role and its limits and the right to independent advice, especially before signing.
- The UMA's optional impartiality allows an advisory model that might amount to unauthorised practice (Alexander). Mediation Model Law **art 7(4)** (the mediator may make settlement proposals) should not be read as permitting advice.

### 9. Judges as mediators
- **Referral** may come from statute, CPR, practice directions, precedent or informally; it may be voluntary or mandatory. Two approaches: the **market approach** (parties choose the mediator; the court manages but does not conduct) and the **justice approach** (the court appoints from judges, registrars or court staff).
- **Four judicial dispute resolution models**:
  1. **Judicial settlement**: the **trial judge** facilitates settlement and hears the case if it fails. Longer established in civil law; directive, legalistic, short, usually no private sessions.
  2. **Judicial mediation**: more legal-authority-based with a settlement focus; the mediating judge **cannot be the trial judge**; the outcome is usually a consent judgment or order.
  3. **Judicial moderation**: a wider range of techniques (investigative, directive, advisory, managerial, settlement, facilitative); in some systems the moderator may then hear the case unless a party objects.
  4. **Facilitative judging / mediative adjudication**: communication and facilitation skills integrated into judging; no separation of roles.
- **Objections**: judicial mediation seems a "contradiction in terms" (judges judge, apply law, evaluate, order, decide). Courts were instituted to decide, not mediate (Fuller). Concerns include constitutional and human rights principles, procedural fairness, independence and impartiality; public confidence; perceived **judicial coercion** and weakened autonomy (Resnik, "Managerial Judges"); lack of oversight and judicial immunity shielding allegations of duress. Judges as "**fiduciary custodians of the justice system**" (Street) should not be seconded to settlement work. Private caucuses would breach natural justice, and there is no appeal from a confidential process.
- **Responses**: avoid private sessions, or use a different judge or non-judicial official (Germany, Australia, the US); full-time separate judicial mediators; prohibit the mediating judge from trying the case. **EU Mediation Directive art 3(a)** (and recital 12) excludes mediation by the judge responsible for the proceedings; **UMA s 3(b)(3)** excludes mediation by a judge who might rule on the case; Poland bars active judges from mediating. Contrast *Zhu v Countrywide Realty Co* 66 F App'x 840 (10th Cir 2003): no impropriety where the judge who facilitated settlement heard the enforcement application.
- In England (e.g. judicial mediation in the Technology and Construction Court) and South Africa, retired senior judges have become successful commercial mediators after training and accreditation.

## Debate and critique
- **Accountability gap**: the combination of confidentiality, immunity clauses and the causation problem makes mediators almost unaccountable at law. Is that justified by the facilitative role and party control, or does it risk abuse? Feehily: immunity should yield to bad faith, bias, coercion and defective advice. Brooker asks whether England needs statutory immunity.
- **The more evaluative, the more exposed**: *Kasipersad*, *Tapoohi* and the practice-of-law rules all show that liability grows as the mediator moves from facilitating to advising and drafting. This links to the facilitative/evaluative debate (mediation-models-and-process.md).
- **Fair outcome vs free agreement**: Feehily says the mediator is not responsible for a fair settlement, only a freely agreed one. Critics of mediation (power imbalance, see process-controversies.md) would say this leaves weaker parties exposed. *Sejane* shows courts will intervene where a vulnerable unrepresented party is involved (in a labour context).
- **Lawyer-mediators**: role confusion and conflicting codes ("attorney-mediators" in Texas; see the lawyers note). The ABA resolution offers a workable line.
- **Judicial mediation**: efficiency and authority versus impartiality and public confidence. The orthodox solution is strict role separation.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the claim**: contract (agreement to mediate, implied term of care and skill), tort (negligence, negligent misstatement), fiduciary or confidence, statute (consumer law), or professional discipline.
2. **What did the mediator do?** Advise or predict outcomes? Present one-sided scenarios (*Kasipersad*)? Convey documents with assertions (*Clay*)? Draft or dictate the settlement (*Tapoohi*)? Ignore an unrepresented or vulnerable party (*Sejane*)?
3. **Duty and proximity**: were the parties represented? Did the mediator recommend independent advice or disclaim statements?
4. **Causation and loss**: would the party (and the other side) have acted differently? Is the loss quantifiable? Was there mitigation?
5. **Defences**: an immunity or exclusion clause (signed? clear? covers this conduct? not fraud or deliberate breach?); statutory immunity (court-annexed scheme?); burden on the mediator to prove immunity.
6. **Alternative remedy**: set aside the settlement (contract defences; Singapore Convention art 5(1)(e)/(f)) rather than sue the mediator; sue one's own lawyer.
7. **Practice of law**: if a lawyer-mediator advised or drafted beyond the parties' terms, consider professional rules and the ABA resolution.
8. **Judicial mediator**: was the judge the trial judge? Private caucus? Consider natural justice, the EU Directive art 3(a) and the UMA s 3(b)(3).
