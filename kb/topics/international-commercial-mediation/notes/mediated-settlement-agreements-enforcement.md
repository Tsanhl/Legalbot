# Mediated Settlement Agreements – Review, Evasion and Enforcement

## Overview
A successful mediation ends with a **mediated settlement agreement (MSA)**. At domestic level it is usually just a contract, so it can be attacked with ordinary contract defences (no binding contract, duress, undue influence, fraud, mistake, incapacity, lack of authority) and enforced only by suing on it. This is the materials / Chapter 8. The chapter covers: (1) how courts review MSAs and the grounds parties use to escape them; (2) drafting steps that make MSAs harder to attack; (3) converting MSAs into judgments or arbitral **consent awards** so they can travel under the New York Convention; (4) enforcement under the **UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law** (arts 15–20); and (5) the **Singapore Convention** (covered in its own note). Feehily's key point: in practice compliance with commercial settlements is high and enforcement is not a big problem, but legal certainty of enforcement matters for mediation's credibility.

## The law

### 1. General approach to review
- The signed-writing requirement is meant to avoid confusion about content.
- MSAs are subject to **normal contract principles** and must accurately record what was agreed. Where it is unclear what was agreed, the court must carry out a complex fact-finding exercise.
- Courts usually "look behind" an MSA between willing parties only in exceptional circumstances.
- In the **US**, courts usually consider evidence on whether a binding contract was formed and review defences as in any contract dispute. Most reported litigation about mediation comes from the US.

### 2. The without-prejudice bar
- Mediation communications are without prejudice, but **once agreement is reached the settlement is no longer protected**.
- *State Bank of New South Wales v Freeman* (NSW SC): an MSA comes into existence after, not in the course of or pursuant to, the mediation.
- *Dow v Bombardier* (Canadian SC) (the textbook discusses the same principle under *Union Carbide Canada Inc v Bombardier Inc* [2014] 1 SCR 800): a confidentiality clause in an agreement to mediate does not stop a party proving the settlement terms with mediation communications unless the agreement clearly says so.

### 3. Is there a binding contract?
- Agreements to mediate usually provide that any settlement binds only when **written and signed**.
- An MSA must meet the requirements of a valid contract: intention to create legal relations, certainty of terms, any required formalities.
- **The absence of a binding contract has been the most successful ground for resisting MSAs** in the US and in international disputes, because sophisticated, represented parties rarely succeed with duress, incapacity or lack of authority.
- US courts recognise that complex cases may not produce a final document at the mediation. They have enforced settlements where all **material terms** were agreed, even though a fuller document was contemplated. **Wording matters**: a deal "subject to" a formal agreement was unenforceable, whereas one to "be followed by" a formal agreement can be enforced. A few unresolved ancillary points will not defeat enforcement.
- Second thoughts do not undo a settlement. But a release that was "to be mutually agreeable" left no meeting of minds on a material term. Courts sometimes delete later-added terms (e.g. expanded release language) rather than void the whole deal.
- **Oral settlements**: enforced in the US if there was a meeting of minds on all material terms and an intention to be bound; not enforced if the parties intended to be bound only by a signed document, or if law or court rules require writing. Under the Uniform Mediation Act and the international trend, oral settlements are **not exempt** from mediation privilege, so evidence of them may be inadmissible.
- English comparison: *Universal Satspace v Kenya* (QB 2013): an oral agreement to sign was enforced as a collateral contract despite a signed-writing clause (see the agreements-to-mediate note; probably different after *MWB v Rock Advertising* [2018] UKSC 24).

### 4. Rescission for an "unjust factor"
Grounds: fraud, undue influence, unconscionability, duress, incapacity, lack of authority, illegality; also mistake.
- **Undue influence**: *Studer v Konig* (NSW SC): claim (including against the party's own solicitor) dismissed. There was no evidence that Konig knew of the allegations against Studer's solicitor, and Studer had executed the mortgage that implemented the settlement. Lesson: informed consent; mediators should stress the value of independent advisers; consider a **cooling-off clause**.
- **Duress and coercion**: *Abriel v Westpac Banking Corp* (FCA): a strike-out of a duress claim was declined. Relevant questions: did the party protest at the time, have an alternative open, or get independent advice? Commercial pressure to settle is unlikely to be duress. In the US duress makes an agreement unenforceable but rarely succeeds. *Olam v Congress Mortgage Co* 68 F Supp 2d 1110 (ND Cal 1999): the claimant said she did not take part or understand, but the mediator's testimony contradicted her and the settlement was enforced. *Olam* lists signs of excessive pressure: discussions at an unusual time or place; insistent demands for quick completion; heavy emphasis on the costs of delay; many influences used by a dominant party against a weaker one; no advisers for the weaker party; statements that there is no time to consult advisers. Most claims are dismissed as "merely the garden variety exercise in bargaining power".
- **Unconscionability**: *Pittornio v Meynert*: failed, as there was no acceptable evidence the defendants knew of the claimant's difficulties.
- **Incompetence or incapacity**: the burden is on the person claiming it, and claims rarely succeed. US courts rejected claims based on medication side-effects such as severe depression, physical pain or memory loss. Lawyers should advise against proceeding if their client may lack capacity; without lawyers, a vigilant mediator should propose a break.
- **Lack of authority**: in England and the US, legal representatives have **ostensible authority** to bind clients (but lawyers acting beyond express instructions may be liable to their clients: see lawyers note).
- **Fraud**: *Crystal Decisions (UK) Ltd v Vedatech Corp* [2007] EWHC 1062 (Ch): nothing in the settlement supported the claim, and innocent or negligent misrepresentation was not enough for rescission (the final judgment upholding the MSA came almost five years after it was signed). *Gillford Pty Ltd v Burdon Pty Ltd*: alleged fraudulent misrepresentations about assets and liabilities not made out. US courts require a knowing, material misrepresentation intended to induce reliance, with justifiable reliance; mere non-disclosure is insufficient without a duty to disclose.
- **Mistake**: claims of mutual and unilateral mistake are usually rejected (e.g. *Feldman v Kritch* 824 So 2d 274 (Fla 2002); *Stewart v Preston Pipeline Inc* 36 Cal Rptr 3d 901 (2005)). Exception: *Brandsmart USA v DR Lakes Inc* 901 So 2d 1004 (Fla 2005) (a US$600,000 clerical error corrected on clear and convincing evidence).

### 5. Providing certainty to enhance enforcement (drafting checklist)
Background safeguards: trained and accredited mediators, observance of standards, and legal advisers who understand mediation. Practical steps from the case law:
1. **Writing and signature**: do not leave with only an oral deal and a promise to draft. The MSA should be signed by the parties or authorised signatories, state that it is admissible in enforcement proceedings, and consider waiving confidentiality for enforcement disputes. Keep a running record of agreed points; some mediators start from a draft settlement.
2. **Material terms and clear language**: include all material terms; state the intention to be bound; avoid "subject to"/"effective only upon" further documents unless intended; a "full and final settlement" must resolve all issues and define the dispute; expressly reserve other claims. Provide certified translations if more than one language is used.
3. **Material representations and ancillary documents**: write the representations relied on into the MSA (avoiding later breaches of confidentiality to prove them); finalise apologies and releases at the mediation. Hong Kong's Apology Ordinance (cap 631, 2017) provides that an apology is not an admission of liability.
4. **Competence and independent judgement**: recitals that the parties understand the terms and know they are binding and enforceable; that representations are incorporated; that there was no duty on the mediator or the other side to provide information; that there was no impairment; that they had the chance to consult lawyers; that they acted voluntarily; and that they have authority.
5. **Litigation matters**: say whether the agreement itself or its performance settles the litigation (usually the agreement); define the disputes settled; withdraw claims and counterclaims; deal with costs (some jurisdictions do not assume they are included), payment amounts, who pays, interest, confidentiality, governing law and jurisdiction, and any ongoing relationship terms.
6. **Converting the agreement**: incorporate it into a judgment, have the court retain jurisdiction, or have the mediator act as arbitrator to issue an award (not allowed everywhere: *Minkowitz v Israeli* 77 A 3d 1189 (NJ 2013)).

### 6. Making the MSA a judgment or arbitral award
- Enforcing an MSA as a contract leaves the party "exactly where it started": with a contract to enforce, now subject to the private international law rules of the enforcing state.
- **Judgment / consent order**: where proceedings are on foot, many systems let the court record the MSA as a consent order or decree. Elsewhere there may be registration, deposit or notarisation. But judgments travel badly across borders compared with arbitral awards, which is the paradox that a consensual outcome was harder to enforce internationally than an adjudicated one.
- **EU Mediation Directive art 6**: Member States (except Denmark) must allow parties to request that a written mediated agreement be made enforceable, unless it is contrary to the law of that state or that law does not provide for its enforceability. These exceptions are broad, so diversity continues.
- **Consent awards under the New York Convention 1958**: Arbitration Model Law **art 30** lets a tribunal record a settlement as an "award on agreed terms" (unless it objects), with "the same status and effect as any award on the merits" (art 30(2)); also UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules art 36 and Arbitration Act 1996 s 51. The NYC does not mention consent awards, but the **predominant view** is that they fall within it. Contrary argument: mediation lacks the adjudicative character the NYC presupposes (Steele).
- **Timing problem**: if settlement is reached **before** any arbitration, there is no "present or future dispute" (Arbitration Act 1996 s 6(1); NY CPLR s 7501) to submit, so an arbitrator appointed afterwards lacks authority, and appointment may resemble an abuse of process (Newmark and Hill). NYC art 1.1 ("differences between persons") does not say when the differences must exist, so the NYC itself may not prohibit recognition, but domestic law and art V(2) (arbitrability; public policy) create risk.
- **Consent award considerations**: appoint the arbitrator **before** mediation ("arb-med-arb"); keep the issues consistent with the arbitration agreement; consent awards cannot bind third parties (e.g. group companies); conditional or revocable settlements raise finality problems; tribunals can refuse to record settlements that are unlawful or affect third parties (*Société Viva Chemical (Europe) NV v APTD* (Paris CA 2009): a settlement that was part of a fraudulent asset-stripping scheme breached international public policy). Parties may be taken to have waived most challenges; France does not enforce domestic consent awards (*MYS c Société BC*, Cass 2012).

### 7. Enforcement under the UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law
- **Art 15**: "If the parties conclude an agreement settling a dispute, that settlement agreement is binding and enforceable."
- **Section 3 (arts 16–20)** covers international settlement agreements and mirrors arts 1–6 of the Singapore Convention: art 16 scope and definitions; art 17 general principles; art 18 requirements for reliance; art 19 grounds for refusing relief; art 20 parallel applications (a court may adjourn and order security).
- Section 3 is more technical, rigid and detailed than the rest of the Model Law, so that standards are uniform. Adopting states should make only the changes essential to fit their systems. States could in theory adopt the Model Law without section 3.
- **Difference from the Convention**: the Model Law covers enforcement of **all** settlement agreements, whether or not they result from mediation; the Singapore Convention covers only mediated ones.
- UNCITRAL Working Group II prepared both instruments together: the Model Law provides the framework to implement the Convention and is an alternative for states not ready to ratify.

## Key authorities
- *State Bank of NSW v Freeman*: MSA arises after the mediation; not covered by without-prejudice protection.
- *Dow v Bombardier* (slides) / *Union Carbide Canada v Bombardier* [2014] 1 SCR 800 (textbook): confidentiality clauses do not bar proof of terms unless clearly drafted.
- *Studer v Konig* (NSW SC): undue influence claim failed.
- *Abriel v Westpac* (FCA): duress claim not struck out; factors.
- *Olam v Congress Mortgage Co* 68 F Supp 2d 1110 (ND Cal 1999): restrictive approach to duress; list of pressure factors; mediator testimony used.
- *Pittornio v Meynert*: unconscionability failed.
- *Crystal Decisions v Vedatech* [2007] EWHC 1062 (Ch): fraud claim failed; enforcement delay.
- *Gillford v Burdon*: fraudulent misrepresentation not established.
- *Universal Satspace v Kenya* (QB 2013); *MWB v Rock Advertising* [2018] UKSC 24.
- New York Convention arts I, IV, V; Arbitration Model Law arts 30, 31, 32, 34; Arbitration Act 1996 ss 6, 51.
- EU Mediation Directive art 6; Mediation Model Law arts 15–20.

## Debate and critique
- **Contract or something more?** Feehily argues MSAs deserve a more privileged status than a "mere" contract because parties have given up rights and agreed both process and outcome. That is the rationale for the Singapore Convention. The counter-concern is that elevated status reduces the protection of contract defences, which matters for weaker parties (hence the exclusions for consumer, family and employment matters).
- **Paradox of enforcement**: arbitral awards (parties agreed the process but not the outcome) were easier to enforce than MSAs (parties agreed both).
- **Consent awards as a workaround** are imperfect: there are timing problems, arbitrability and public policy risk, the "adjudicative character" objection, and the awkwardness of raising enforcement protection in an amicable negotiation.
- **Empirical picture (Coben, cited in ch 8)**: enforcement disputes made up 47% of mediation-related cases in 1999–2003 and 39% in 2013–17; the overall enforcement rate was 69%; mediator-misconduct defences failed 100% of the time; unconscionability claims were rejected 93%, duress 88%, incapacity 87%, and fraud resulted in 86% enforcement; procedural/jurisdictional arguments were the most successful (53% enforcement).
- **Confidentiality tension**: nearly every defence depends on what happened in the mediation, so courts must choose between finality and protecting the process (see confidentiality note).
- **Uniformity**: the Model Law's rigid section 3 and the Convention aim to replace the patchwork of domestic regimes (EU art 6 exceptions show the limits of regional harmonisation).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Was there a concluded agreement?** Signed writing? Required by the agreement to mediate? All material terms agreed? "Subject to" vs "to be followed by" wording? Oral deal: is there intention to be bound, any writing requirement, and is evidence admissible given privilege?
2. **Defences raised**: test each against the case law. For duress, look at protest, alternatives, advice and the *Olam* factors; commercial pressure is not enough. For undue influence, was the other party aware? For fraud, a knowing material misrepresentation plus reliance; mere non-disclosure fails. Incapacity: burden on the claimant. Authority: ostensible authority of lawyers. Mistake: usually fails.
3. **Evidence**: can the court hear what happened? Without-prejudice protection ends once agreement is reached (*Freeman*); a confidentiality clause must be clear (*Bombardier*); a mediator may be a witness (*Olam*).
4. **Enforcement route**: domestic contract claim; consent order/judgment; consent award (was an arbitrator appointed before settlement?); EU art 6 procedure; Model Law art 15 / section 3 if adopted; Singapore Convention if the settlement is international, commercial and the state is a party (see that note).
5. **Drafting advice**: apply the checklist in section 5 above.

Traps: assuming an MSA is automatically enforceable like an award; overlooking that the Model Law section 3 applies to all settlements, the Convention only to mediated ones; forgetting the timing problem for consent awards.
