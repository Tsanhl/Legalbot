# The Singapore Convention and China's Commercial Mediation System

## Overview
China signed the Singapore Convention on its opening day (7 August 2019) but, according to the sources, has not ratified it. This note uses one journal article on the topic: "Research on the Integration of the Singapore Mediation Convention with China's Commercial Mediation System" (2025) 2(2) *Journal of International Economics and Global Governance* 65–83. **Authorship caution**: the raw file is labelled as Xinyu Zhang and Yanying Li, but the article's own byline names **Kaiheng Hu and Qifan Jiang** (East China University of Political Science and Law). Cite it by title, and check the authorship before relying on either name. The article explains why the Convention fits awkwardly with China's system: different terminology, the treatment of non-institutional mediation, judicial review, fraud risk, third-party protection and judicial burden. It proposes a phased move from a **"dual-track"** to a **"unified"** implementation model. It is a useful comparative case study of how a civil-law, mediation-friendly state with strong judicial oversight would absorb the Convention, and of the general tension between expedited enforcement and protection.

## The law

### 1. Background
- Mediation has deep roots in China. the materials traces its origins to the Chou Dynasty (1100–256 BC), and the article refers to China's traditional preference for resolving disputes outside court (*Wusong*, avoiding litigation). It also links ratification to improving the business environment and to the Belt and Road Initiative (many signatories are BRI partners).
- The article says the Convention had 58 signatories and only 15 ratifications when written, with China among the non-ratifying signatories. The treaty-status extract also shows China signing on 7 August 2019 with no ratification recorded.
- Reasons for hesitation (as the article puts it): the Convention requires enforcement of all qualifying settlements with no reciprocity reservation; it gives them full enforceability and, in effect, some res judicata effect; and it requires domestic legal changes.

### 2. China's current system (per the article)
Mediation is grouped into three types by enforceability:
1. **Litigation (or arbitration) mediation**: within court or arbitral proceedings; the outcome has the force of a judgment or award.
2. **Institutional mediation**: by legally established mediation organisations outside litigation; the settlement can be **judicially confirmed** under the Civil Procedure Law and then compulsorily enforced.
3. **Non-institutional mediation**: by other entities; the settlement is an **ordinary civil contract**, needing a court judgment to become enforceable. It is not recognised as formal mediation.

Terminology: the Civil Procedure Law speaks of "mediation decisions" and the People's Mediation Law of "mediation agreements". In Chinese usage "settlement agreement" (the word in the official Chinese text of the Convention) means an agreement the parties reach **without** a third party. This is the opposite of the Convention's meaning.

Judicial confirmation under the People's Mediation Law and Civil Procedure Law requires a **joint application by both parties**. Under the Convention, enforcement can be started by **one party**: art 5 lets the other party ask for refusal, which only makes sense if one party can start enforcement.

### 3. Points of conflict identified
1. **Terminology mismatch** could confuse courts and parties.
2. **Direct enforcement weakens judicial review.** Art 3 requires enforcement without a prior judgment. Most jurisdictions (the article names China, Germany, the US and the UK) treat settlements as contracts or as documents needing conversion; it says only a few (Russia, Spain) treat them as directly enforceable. Chinese courts would be limited to a formal review (did it result from mediation?) instead of a substantive review.
3. **No mediator qualifications in the Convention.** The Convention lets anyone mediate and gives all qualifying settlements equal force, so foreign-related non-institutional mediation would gain the same status as institutional or court mediation. Yet China lacks unified rules on mediator qualifications and ethics, which matter because the art 5(1)(e) misconduct ground depends on "applicable standards". (The article attributes the misconduct grounds to "Article 2"; in the Convention text they are art 5(1)(e) and (f).)
4. **No reciprocity / "free-riding".** Because internationality turns on places of business and there is no seat, settlements concluded anywhere are enforceable in China, while a counterparty's assets in a non-party state stay protected by that state's broader review. Before ratification, enforcement in China required either a Chinese institution plus court confirmation, or a Chinese judgment, both giving substantive review.
5. **Fraudulent mediation and third parties.** Colluding parties could fabricate disputes to harm third parties. The pressure to "act expeditiously" (art 4(5)), the international facts and confidentiality make detection hard, and settlements may be enforced before third parties know. Neither Chinese law (third-party objection to enforcement; third-party revocation suits) nor the Convention gives third parties a remedy against settlement agreements.
6. **Incompatible enforcement mechanism**: joint application and a separate confirmation stage in China versus review inside unilateral enforcement under the Convention. Parties used to settlements not being automatically enforceable could be surprised.
7. **Judicial burden**: China has a history of "difficulties in enforcement", and many foreign-owned assets in China could become targets. Courts would have to apply foreign law to settlements with no connection to China.

### 4. Reform proposals
1. **Terminology**: issue judicial interpretations explaining the Convention's term; run awareness campaigns; or consider the art 8(1)(b) "party consent" (opt-in) reservation so businesses are not misled.
2. **Legal nature**: amend the Civil Procedure Law to make **foreign-related** settlements directly enforceable; leave domestic settlements as they are for now.
3. **Validity defects**: classify settlements that would be **revocable** under Chinese contract law (material misunderstanding, significant unfairness, fraud, coercion, exploitation of a party in distress) as defective agreements under the Convention (i.e. within art 5(1)(b)(i)). Enforcement should then be refused even without a revocation claim, because such defects show the mediation did not add legitimacy.
4. **Mediation quality**: create a training and certification system (modelled on lawyers and notaries); a unified national **Mediator Code of Conduct** covering at least independence, impartiality, disclosure and confidentiality (tracking art 5); and, once the system is mature, direct enforceability for all settlements from qualified mediators in China.
5. **Third-party safeguards**: rigorous review within enforcement while keeping speed; a **public notice** period before enforcement; legislated third-party objection and revocation remedies; require applicants to give **security** in China, extending the notice period if they do not.
6. **Implementation model**: the article treats the Convention as **non-self-executing** (it needs domestic procedure; art 3(1) refers to each state's "rules of procedure"). Options:
   - **Dual-track**: a special regime for international settlements alongside domestic rules. This mirrors China's approach to the New York Convention, adopted because the NYC required recognising foreign **ad hoc** arbitral awards while Chinese law did not accept ad hoc arbitration domestically. The article says Singapore and other early adopters also used dual-track legislation.
   - **Unified**: one procedure for international and domestic settlements.
   - **Recommendation**: start dual-track (to manage fraud risk, an "enforcement explosion" and immature standards), then move to unified. Dual-track is only transitional: it favours foreign-related settlements, encourages parties to invent foreign elements, and undermines legal authority. Movement toward recognising non-institutional mediation is already visible (the 2017 pilot on lawyer-led mediation, 司发通〔2017〕105号).
7. **Strategic aim**: China should not just adapt but help shape global mediation standards (currently led by bodies such as the ICC and JAMS) and build China-led mediation centres.

## Key authorities
- Singapore Convention arts 1, 2(3), 3, 4(5), 5(1)(b), (e), (f), 8(1)(b).
- Chinese law (as described in the article): Civil Procedure Law (judicial confirmation; "mediation decisions"); People's Mediation Law ("mediation agreements"; joint application); contract-law revocation grounds; 2017 lawyer-led mediation pilot.
- New York Convention and China's dual-track implementation (ad hoc arbitration).
- Feehily ch 8: the Convention's "no seat" design and the "stateless" settlement; art 4(5) "act expeditiously"; art 6 security; concerns about fraud and money laundering behind the art 4 evidence requirement; arbitration laws in China allowing consent awards (Arbitration Law of the PRC art 51, cited as an example).

## Debate and critique
- **Efficiency vs protection.** The article is a clear example of the core policy trade-off: expedited, delocalised enforcement against judicial control, third-party protection and fraud prevention. Compare Feehily and Köhler: the Convention's legitimacy rests on the neutral's involvement, so states worry when mediator standards are weak.
- **Free-riding and reciprocity.** Feehily says reservations have no reciprocal effect and the Convention has no seat. The article treats the lack of reciprocity as a reason for caution. A UK consultation respondent made a related point about reciprocity of reservations under the Vienna Convention (see singapore-convention.md).
- **Opt-in reservation.** The article floats it as a terminology safeguard; Feehily and most UK consultees see opt-in as seriously weakening the Convention.
- **Is direct enforceability radical?** The article stresses that direct enforceability is rare in national law. Feehily sees the Convention as justified by the parties' agreement to both process and outcome. Compare EU Directive art 6, which requires a route to enforceability but with broad exceptions.
- **Cultural fit.** the materials emphasises the "traditional vs modern" divide and the role of culture. China's mediation tradition (harmony, social control, state-linked institutions) differs from the Western facilitative, private model the Convention assumes, which helps explain the institutional-vs-non-institutional problem.
- **Quality of the source.** It is a recent, policy-oriented article. Some statements should be checked against the Convention text (e.g. its reference to "Article 2" for misconduct grounds), and the authorship label in the raw file conflicts with the byline.

## Applying it to a problem question
Useful in essays on "Will the Singapore Convention achieve New York Convention-like success?" or on state implementation:
1. Identify the domestic features that clash with the Convention: the legal status of settlements; whether non-institutional mediation is recognised; joint versus unilateral applications; whether review is substantive or formal.
2. Identify the risks the state will worry about: fraud and collusion, third parties, judicial workload, applying foreign law, lack of mediator standards.
3. Match risks to Convention tools: art 5 grounds (null and void, misconduct, public policy), art 6 (adjournment and security), art 8 reservations, and implementing legislation (designated courts, notice procedures).
4. Choose an implementation model: dual-track versus unified, with the New York Convention precedent.
5. Compare with UK proposals (registration, ex parte decisions with a route to challenge).
