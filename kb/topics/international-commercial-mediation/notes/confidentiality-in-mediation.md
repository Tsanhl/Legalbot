# Confidentiality in Commercial Mediation

## Overview
Confidentiality is often described as the **cornerstone** of mediation. It can cover private caucus sessions, the whole process, and mediation "evidence" (what was said, offered or produced). But mediation is "not always as confidential as it is often claimed to be" (the materials / Chapter 9). Many disputes about mediated settlements (duress, incapacity, authority, mistake, fraud) can only be decided by looking at what happened in the room. Courts and legislators therefore have to balance **two competing public policies**: encouraging settlement by protecting the process, and giving courts access to relevant evidence. The chapter surveys the tools that protect mediation evidence (common law privileges, contract, equity, statute) and their limits, comparing England, Australia, the US, the EU and UNCITRAL.

## The law

### 1. The two competing policies
- **Policy 1**: promote settlement and protect processes such as mediation that support it.
- **Policy 2**: courts and tribunals should have access to all relevant evidence.
- Freedom of contract supports confidentiality, but if protection is **too wide** it "sterilises" too much evidence and undermines litigation and arbitration. If it is **too narrow** it discourages parties from mediating or trying their best.
- Courts must support mediation without freezing litigation or protecting illegality. US decisions cover the whole spectrum of attitudes.
- Prigoff (Seton Hall Legislative Journal, 1988) gives reasons for protection: effective mediation needs candour; fairness, since parties speak without the safeguards of formal proceedings and mediation could otherwise be used as a discovery device; the mediator's neutrality in fact and appearance (testimony will always appear to favour one side); privacy as a reason parties choose mediation; and protecting programmes and mediators from distraction and harassment by subpoenas. He notes that the common law exceptions "almost swallow the rules", that Federal Rule of Evidence 408 gives only limited protection, and he argues for a statutory privilege. (Contrast Eric Green's "heretical" view of the mediation privilege, which he cites.)

### 2. Common law privilege

**(a) "Without prejudice" privilege**
- If no settlement is reached, oral or written offers or admissions made in good faith to settle are inadmissible in later litigation about the dispute. Rationale: *Cutts v Head* [1984] Ch 290, 306 (Oliver LJ): parties should be encouraged to settle and not fear that what they say will be used against them.
- It applies whether or not proceedings have begun and whether or not the label is used: *Rush & Tompkins Ltd v Greater London Council* [1989] 1 AC 1280.
- Australian courts have confirmed it applies to mediation (*AWA Ltd v Daniels*), even when mediation only narrows issues (*Lukies v Ripley (No 2)* (1994) 35 NSWLR 283).
- The **parties** can waive it, expressly or impliedly, but only **jointly**; the mediator has no common law veto. Disclosure to the mediator is not a waiver.
- **Once settlement is reached**, what was agreed is admissible to prove and enforce it (*Biala Pty Ltd v Mallina Holdings Ltd* [1989] 15 ACLR 208).
- **Exceptions** (*Unilever plc v Procter & Gamble Co* [1999] EWCA Civ 3027, Walker LJ: seven exceptions, three more added since): to decide whether an agreement was reached (*Brown v Rice* [2007] EWHC 625 (Ch)); misrepresentation, fraud, undue influence, duress; estoppel; **"unambiguous impropriety"** (applied restrictively: in *Unilever* a threat to enforce a patent was justifiable; in *Ferster v Ferster* [2016] EWCA Civ 717 an email amounting to blackmail was admitted); explaining delay; "without prejudice save as to costs" communications on costs; no existing dispute; showing that statements were made (*Ofulue v Bossert* [2009] UKHL 16, Lord Hope and Lord Neuberger: a restrictive approach); background facts to interpret a settlement (*Oceanbulk Shipping & Trading SA v TMT Asia Ltd* [2010] UKSC 44; *Excelsior Group Productions v Yorkshire Television* [2009] EWHC 1751 (Comm): the line is "so fine it almost vanishes").
- **English courts are reluctant to look inside mediation**: *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 [14] (the court "should not know, and therefore should not investigate, why the process did not result in agreement"); *Swampillai v Joseph* [2015] EWCA Civ 261 (a "mediator's note" was not admitted).
- **Pre-mediation**: negotiations about whether to mediate may not be protected (*ARP Capital London Market Services Ltd v Ross & Co* [2004] EWHC 1181) unless marked without prejudice (*Reed Executive v Reed Business Information* [2004] EWCA Civ 887). Contrast NSW Civil Procedure Act 2005 s 30(1), which covers arrangements and follow-up.
- **Consensual waiver cases on costs**: *Chantrey Vellacott v Convergence Group plc* [2007] EWHC 1774 (Ch); *Malmesbury v Strutt & Parker* [2008] EWHC 424 (QB) (unreasonable conduct in mediation treated like unreasonable refusal); *SITA v Watson Wyatt* [2002] EWHC 2025 (Ch). These show that **confidentiality belongs to the parties**, not the mediator or the process.
- *Aird v Prime Meridian Ltd* [2006] EWCA Civ 1866: an experts' joint statement prepared under a court order with mediation in mind was not privileged.
- *Brown v Rice*: communications were admitted to decide whether a settlement had been reached, despite objections from a party and the mediation provider. Parties may vary, waive or be estopped from relying on a signed-writing clause, or a collateral contract may arise. Lesson: mediation confidentiality is only as strong as without-prejudice protection, with the same limits. The judge said a distinct mediation privilege might in future need consideration by the legislature or courts.
- *Cumbria Waste Management Ltd v Baines Wilson* [2008] EWHC 786 (QB) (Kirkham J): the court refused to order disclosure of mediation documents against one party's wishes. The right to waive is joint, not several. Courts should "support the mediation process by refusing, in normal circumstances, to order disclosure". This suggests a special mediation privilege, but it is only a first-instance decision.

**(b) Other participants and interested parties**
- Anyone present can be called as a witness once privilege is waived. Experts and other third parties are often not parties to the agreement to mediate, so they should sign confidentiality agreements. The agreement's confidentiality clause can, but does not always, bind lawyers.
- Defensive use against third parties: *Rush & Tompkins*: subcontractors could not obtain information about the main settlement; privilege was extended beyond settlement in the public interest. Also *Mercantile Mutual Custodians v Village/Nine Network* [1999] QCA 276. Narrower approaches: *Muller v Linsley & Mortimer* [1996] PNLR 74; *Cattley v Pollard* [2006] EWHC 3130 (a third party was allowed position papers to check double counting).

**(c) Legal professional privilege**
- It covers communications for the dominant purpose of legal advice or litigation. It does not cover communications for an illegal purpose, crime or fraud. It covers lawyers' work for clients going into mediation.
- Sharing privileged documents with the mediator is **not a waiver**: *Farm Assist Ltd (in liq) v Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (No 2)* (Ramsey J; cited by Feehily as [2009] EWHC 1102 (TCC) at [44], and by Kallipetis as [2007] EWHC 2870 (TCC)). So the client can stop unauthorised use by the mediator.
- US: attorney–client privilege and the work product doctrine (Federal Rule of Evidence 502). Civil law: "professional secret" in France; "duty of silence" in Germany and Austria.

**(d) A privilege for mediation or mediators?**
- Without-prejudice privilege covers only the **parties**. There is no common law mediator privilege and no authority extending without-prejudice privilege to mediators, so without statute or contract **mediators are compellable**. Feehily argues existing categories could be extended to mediators; waiver would then need both mediator and parties.
- English courts have hinted at a distinct mediation privilege (*Brown v Rice* [20]; *Cumbria Waste*).

**(e) Testimony by the mediator**
- Courts may see the mediator as the best independent witness (intention to be bound, misrepresentation, coercion, capacity).
- US: some courts refuse (*Princeton Insurance Co v Vergano* 883 A 2d 44 (Del Ch 2005): hard to imagine a "more poisonous means" of weakening confidentiality than using the mediator as an opinion witness). Many admit and rely on it (*White v Fleet Bank of Maine* 875 A 2d 680 (Me 2005); *Standard Steel LLC v Buckeye Energy Inc* (WD Pa 2005); *Olam v Congress Mortgage Co*).
- Australia: *National Australia Bank v Freeman* [2000] QSC 295 ("considerable weight" on the mediator's evidence); *Knight v Truss-Michaelis* (Qld DC 1993) (mediator's notes disclosed despite his assurance).
- England: *AB v CD* [2013] EWHC 1376 (TCC): the first known English case of mediator evidence, with significant limits on scope. *Farm Assist (No 2)*: witness summons against the mediator upheld in the interests of justice (economic duress alleged).

**(f) A "mediator secrets privilege"** (proposed by Briggs J, "Mediation Privilege?" (2009) NLJ)
- This would protect information a party gives the mediator privately in **caucus**. Once shared (directly or by authorised disclosure) it loses that privilege and gets only without-prejudice protection.
- Justification: the mediator's unique role in holding one party's confidences, analogous to legal professional privilege. Exceptions would cover mediator misconduct and illegality (fraud, money laundering, tax evasion). It would not interfere with without-prejudice exceptions. Feehily prefers development through the common law.

### 3. Contract
- Without-prejudice privilege is a **rule of evidence**: it stops use at trial but does not make information confidential. Real confidentiality, absent statute, must be **contracted for**.
- This is the oldest method: parties, mediator and other participants agree to keep information confidential and not testify. Public policy favours enforcement, with exceptions. The parties' intentions must be expressed clearly.
- **Contracting out of judicial access** (i.e. excluding the exceptions to without-prejudice privilege): *Unilever* (English CA: possible "in principle", no clear authority); *Union Carbide Canada Inc v Bombardier Inc* [2014] 1 SCR 800 (Canadian SC: possible but only on very clearly drafted terms, which were absent). This is counterintuitive, since a party could then not prove undue influence, but some parties value confidentiality more.
- **Mediation rules and guidelines** incorporated by reference can create duties (breach may lead to expulsion of the mediator). They are organic and change often. Most oblige parties and mediator not to disclose unless agreed or required by law.

### 4. Equitable remedy for breach of confidence
- If contract gives no effective remedy, equity can protect information that (1) has the necessary **quality of confidence** (e.g. a trade secret), (2) was imparted in circumstances importing an **obligation of confidence**, and (3) is **used without authorisation** (*Coco v A N Clark (Engineers) Ltd* [1969] RPC 41). Remedies: injunction, restitution, damages. Equity is unlikely to step in where other remedies exist.

### 5. Confidentiality and conflicts of interest
- A lawyer acting for one party may learn information usable against the other later. In England a solicitor may act against a former client with consent, or without it if the solicitor can still honour confidentiality and fiduciary duties (*Prince Jefri Bolkiah v KPMG* [1999] 2 AC 222).
- Agreements to mediate usually bar a mediator from acting for or against a party in the dispute where they received confidential information.

### 6. Fraud and criminality
- Allegations of fraud or crime during a mediation can justify setting confidentiality aside.
- Australia: in NSW it is an offence simply to conceal a serious crime; in Victoria only if the person benefits from not disclosing. These rules may excuse lawyers acting professionally but not mediators.
- England: **Proceeds of Crime Act 2002** – it is an offence to become concerned in an arrangement known or suspected to facilitate acquisition or control of criminal property, unless an authorised disclosure is made. But a mediator who makes a disclosure that was not required may be sued for breach of confidence. Conviction can mean up to 14 years' imprisonment (s 328; authorised disclosure under s 338). The Civil Mediation Council's Guidance Note No 2 says a mediator may need to withdraw or report. *Bowman v Fels* [2005] EWCA Civ 226 (reversing the approach in *P v P* [2003] EWHC 2260) held that s 328 does not apply to the ordinary conduct of litigation, including settlement, and does not override legal professional privilege; it is unclear whether this extends to mediators. NSW: Crimes Act 1900 s 316; Victoria: Crimes Act 1958 s 326.

### 7. Statute
- Two reasons for statutory rules: (1) parties cannot create some protections by contract (rules of admissibility of evidence are not open to contract); (2) where waiver in advance is rare, e.g. the mediator's duty of confidentiality. Comprehensive statutory protection would make contractual protection unnecessary.
- Statutes often reinforce and extend common law protection, typically making the process confidential and statements and documents inadmissible, **with exceptions**. There is no international consensus on extent.
- **Australia**: no comprehensive federal law but many statutory privileges. NSW: privilege applies before any "tribunal or body", and mediation includes arranging and follow-up steps. Queensland: extends to Dispute Resolution Centre documents.
- **USA**: rules of evidence, privileges, confidentiality and disclosure statutes. There is no general federal mediation privilege, but almost every state has one.
- **EU Mediation Directive art 7**: Member States must ensure that, unless the parties agree otherwise, **mediators and those administering the process** are not compelled to give evidence in civil and commercial proceedings or arbitration about information "arising out of or in connection with a mediation process" (very broad). Exceptions: overriding reasons of public policy (e.g. child protection or prevention of personal harm) or where needed to implement or enforce the settlement. It is silent on without-prejudice rules and on parties and lawyers. National laws vary significantly (Denmark opted out of the Directive).
- **UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law**: **art 10**: unless otherwise agreed, all information relating to the mediation proceedings is confidential, except where disclosure is required by law or for implementing or enforcing a settlement. **Art 9**: information given to the mediator in caucus may be disclosed to the other party unless the giver asks that it be kept confidential. This is an "open communication" rather than an "in-confidence" approach. **Art 11**: certain information is not to be relied on or disclosed in arbitral, judicial or similar proceedings, or in pre-trial discovery (broad). Parties may vary protection by agreement.
- **Uniform Mediation Act**: statutory privilege adopted by many states. s 7(b)(1): mediators may report only whether the mediation occurred or was terminated, whether a settlement was reached, and attendance.
- **California** goes further than the UMA: the mediator must keep caucus information confidential unless the giver expressly consents, and there is a wider duty covering the whole process. *Rojas v Superior Court* 33 Cal 4th 407 (2004): confidentiality of material prepared for mediation is "absolute", with very limited statutory exceptions. *Cassel v Superior Court* 119 Cal Rptr 3d 437 (2009) (via Kallipetis): clients in effect give up malpractice claims against their own lawyers based on mediation communications; Chin J doubted the legislature intended to shield attorneys.
- Prigoff surveyed early US statutes: New York Judiciary Law s 849-b (all mediator files confidential; but "relating to the subject matter" leaves gaps; child abuse reporting tension); Colorado (mediation treated as settlement negotiations); Oklahoma (signed agreement required; privilege waived if the mediator is sued); Florida (research access).

### 8. Confidentiality, costs and the Singapore Convention
- Costs sanctions push courts to examine conduct inside mediation (see conduct-costs-and-compulsion.md). England refuses without consent (*Reed*, *Halsey*, *Cumbria Waste*); some Australian courts admit evidence on costs (*Silver Fox*).
- The **Singapore Convention** is silent on confidentiality. Proof of art 5 defences depends on the forum's evidence and privilege law (lex fori via art 3(1)) (Köhler). Kallipetis concludes that, given *Farm Assist* and *Ferster*, English law would present no confidentiality obstacle to art 5 defences, while California-style absolute confidentiality could block them.

## Key authorities
- *Cutts v Head* [1984] Ch 290; *Rush & Tompkins v GLC* [1989] 1 AC 1280: without-prejudice foundations.
- *Unilever v Procter & Gamble* [1999] EWCA Civ 3027: exceptions; contracting out "in principle".
- *Halsey* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 [14]: do not investigate why mediation failed.
- *Brown v Rice* [2007] EWHC 625 (Ch): exception to decide whether a settlement was reached; possible mediation privilege.
- *Cumbria Waste Management v Baines Wilson* [2008] EWHC 786 (QB): joint waiver; court supports mediation.
- *Farm Assist (No 2)* (Ramsey J): LPP not waived by showing the mediator; mediator compelled to testify.
- *Ferster v Ferster* [2016] EWCA Civ 717: unambiguous impropriety (blackmail).
- *Ofulue v Bossert* [2009] UKHL 16; *Oceanbulk v TMT* [2010] UKSC 44.
- *Union Carbide Canada v Bombardier* [2014] 1 SCR 800: contractual confidentiality must be clear to exclude exceptions.
- *Rojas v Superior Court* (2004); *Cassel v Superior Court* (2009): California absolute confidentiality.
- *Coco v A N Clark* [1969] RPC 41: breach of confidence.
- EU Mediation Directive art 7; Mediation Model Law arts 9–11; UMA s 7; POCA 2002.

## Debate and critique
- **Candour vs truth-finding** (Prigoff's "candor or chaos"): strong protection encourages openness, but may shield duress, fraud or lawyer negligence (Chin J in *Cassel*). The answer is usually privilege with narrow exceptions.
- **Is confidentiality overstated?** the materials: mediation is "not always as confidential as it is often claimed". *Brown v Rice* showed mediators had overpromised. Parties should be told the limits.
- **England's uncertainty**: most authority is first instance (*Cumbria Waste*). There is no statute, only the EU Directive's influence for cross-border cases (pre-Brexit context in the textbook). A distinct mediation privilege or mediator-secrets privilege (Briggs) has been suggested. Feehily prefers common law development, refined by courts, over legislation in an emerging field.
- **Who owns confidentiality?** The English cases treat it as the parties'. Statutes such as the UMA give mediators an independent privilege; the EU Directive protects mediators from being compelled.
- **Mediator as witness**: helpful to courts, but damages neutrality (Prigoff; *Princeton v Vergano*).
- **Open vs in-confidence caucus** (Model Law art 9 default vs California rule): different cultural expectations about the mediator's role.
- **Diversity** among EU states and US states, and the Singapore Convention's silence, mean cross-border users face uncertainty. Choice of forum and clear contract drafting matter.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **What information is sought, by whom, and for what purpose?** (Enforcing or setting aside a settlement; costs; a third party's claim; a negligence claim against a lawyer or mediator; criminal investigation.)
2. **Without-prejudice privilege**: was there a dispute and a genuine attempt to settle? Has a settlement been reached (if so, terms are provable)? Does an exception apply (whether agreed; fraud, duress, misrepresentation; unambiguous impropriety; estoppel; costs; background facts)? Have **all** parties waived it?
3. **Contract**: what does the agreement to mediate say? Does it bind lawyers, experts, the mediator? Does it clearly exclude the exceptions (*Bombardier*)? Are institutional rules incorporated?
4. **LPP**: were privileged documents shown to the mediator (no waiver: *Farm Assist*)?
5. **Mediator testimony**: is the mediator compellable (yes at common law, absent statute or contract)? What does the jurisdiction do (England: *Farm Assist*, *AB v CD*; US: split; California: no)?
6. **Statute**: EU Directive art 7 (mediators not compelled; exceptions); Model Law arts 9–11 if adopted; UMA or state law; Australian statutes.
7. **Crime/fraud**: POCA 2002 duties and risks for the mediator.
8. **Equity**: breach of confidence if unauthorised use and no contractual remedy.
9. **Costs context**: will the court look inside mediation? (England: no, without consent.)

Traps: assuming the mediator can veto disclosure; forgetting that without-prejudice protection ends on settlement; thinking without-prejudice privilege creates confidentiality (it is only an evidence rule); overlooking third parties not bound by the agreement.
