# Mediators – Role, Education, Professionalisation, Training, Accreditation and Standards

## Overview
Chapter 5 asks who mediators are and how they should be regulated. The first half (this note) covers the mediator's qualities and role; mediation education; whether mediation is or should be a "profession" and which regulatory model fits; training and accreditation (using CEDR as the example); and **standards of conduct** (the US Model Standards and the European Code), especially self-determination, neutrality vs impartiality, conflicts of interest and competence, plus their link to the Singapore Convention. The second half of the chapter (mediator liability, the practice of law, judges as mediators) is in a separate note. Commentary opens with "The Blind Men and the Elephant": the **perspective problem**, i.e. each party sees only part of the dispute, and the mediator's job is to help them see the whole.

## The law

### 1. Qualities, skills and role
- Qualities associated with effective mediators: honesty, integrity, impartiality, patience, communication and process skills.
- "To mediate" means "to go between" or "to be in the middle". The mediator is **neither judge, legal adviser, counsellor nor therapist**. The sole function is to bring the parties together to find a solution **of their own making**.
- The mediator should **motivate without manipulating, persuade without coercing**, and use **reality testing** to encourage settlement; fashion the process to suit the parties and the dispute; and marshal the skills and personal qualities needed to help resolution.

### 2. Education
- **USA**: mediation is integrated at all levels of education: peer mediation in elementary and secondary schools; student-run campus programmes; advanced degrees and training for lawyers and judges. Studies of school-level conflict resolution education report better attendance and achievement and fewer suspensions (Crawford and Bodine, cited).
- **UK and Australia**: tertiary courses and qualifications across law, humanities, social science and business; postgraduate diplomas and master's degrees. Programmes are both academic (theory) and practical (skills, competency-based). Similar courses exist in countries as diverse as China, Lebanon and Uganda (Moore).

### 3. Efforts to professionalise
- Wilensky, "The Professionalisation of Everyone" (1964): an occupation must establish a technical basis, claim exclusive jurisdiction, link skill and jurisdiction to training standards, and convince the public its services are uniquely trustworthy. Moore lists milestones of a profession (codified terminology, a body of knowledge, training, university courses, practitioners, associations, codes, qualifications, regulation of entry).
- Professionalisation requires high, transparent minimum practice and ethical standards observed internationally and perceived as effective.
- **Three regulatory models** (Steffek; Hopt and Steffek):
  1. **Market approach** (e.g. **England**): the state does not regulate education or admission; mediators, associations, parties and academia develop training, accreditation and standards, relying on supply and demand.
  2. **Incentive approach** (e.g. **Austria**): the market is open, but the state offers incentives to meet qualification criteria (registration or certification), with favourable rules (e.g. on confidentiality) applying only when a registered mediator is used.
  3. **Authorisation/admission approach** (e.g. **Hungary**): the state controls admission to practise.
- In **commercial** mediation, where parties control entry, the choice of mediator and the outcome, intensive regulation of neutrality and qualifications is unnecessary, so a **market or incentive approach is advisable** (Steffek). A party may even prefer a mediator with links to the other side if that builds trust and momentum (Brower, cited).
- England's development was demand-led and began with practice. What civil lawyers see as a lack of certainty, common lawyers see as preserving autonomy and flexibility (Tirado and Vicente Maravall).

### 4. Training
- Requirements vary and many countries have no national accreditation system, but commercial providers require panel mediators to be trained and accredited.
- **CEDR Mediator Skills Training** (UK and international): five days taught by practising mediators; course materials, case studies and role-plays sent in advance; participants play mediator, adviser and party; a "practice day" with coaching; each participant mediates a simulated case assessed against competencies; three assignments: a written settlement agreement, a self-assessment, and an action plan.
- The value of training: dealing in context with the **grey areas** in standards (e.g. an imminent but apparently inequitable settlement). Simulation is more practical than "abstract and woolly" codes (Boulle and Field).
- The UK **Civil Mediation Council** (private but state-funded) registers providers, sets training and CPD standards, issues guidance, keeps a list of accredited institutions and runs a complaints service. CEDR is registered with it.

### 5. Accreditation
- Accreditation is not an absolute indication of competence, nor a guarantee of a career, but it ensures certain standards are observed (Mistelis).
- CEDR Accreditation is based on assessment plus the three assignments (within two weeks). CPD keeps mediators up to date.
- Accreditation varies considerably. Europe leads in specialist (commercial, intercultural) accreditation, while generalist accreditation is common elsewhere (Alexander).

### 6. Standards
**Purpose and types**
- Standards guide mediators on their commitments, set parties' expectations, and create norms for **discipline and liability**.
- **Three types**: (1) **binding** standards, where deviation may be professional misconduct (e.g. ethical obligations whose breach may lead to a settlement being set aside, liability, or removal from a panel), identified by "**shall**"; (2) **guiding principles** (non-binding standards of care); (3) **hybrid** systems.
- US Model Standards note on construction: "**shall**" is mandatory; "**should**" is highly desirable and departed from only for very strong reasons.
- Australia's National Mediator Accreditation Standards (2015) allow accreditation to be suspended for "significant non-compliance".

**The two key codes**
- **US Model Standards of Conduct for Mediators**: drafted 1994, revised 2005 by the American Arbitration Association, American Bar Association and Association for Conflict Resolution. They may be treated as a **standard of care** and are the most widely adopted in the US.
- **European Code of Conduct for Mediators** (2004): developed by providers, lawyers and specialists with European Commission support. It is shorter and less detailed and the only code seeking Europe-wide effect. **EU Mediation Directive art 4** requires states to encourage voluntary codes. Some states (France, Finland) have national codes; others (Spain, Germany) rely on institutional codes, mostly following the European Code.
- The **International Mediation Institute (IMI)** develops global standards and seeks to harmonise certification (industry-led harmonisation).

**Core standards**
1. **Party self-determination** (Model Standards I): uncoerced, free and informed choices on process and outcome at every stage (selection, design, participation, withdrawal, outcome). It must be balanced against the duty to run a quality process. If capacity for informed choice is doubtful, the mediator should point parties to other professionals. Mediators must not abuse their powers of persuasion (e.g. to boost settlement rates). Chern suggests a written waiver of representation from unrepresented parties.
2. **Neutrality and impartiality**: often used interchangeably, but:
   - **Neutrality** concerns the mediator's prior knowledge of or **interest in the outcome**. It is less absolute and may be qualified by context.
   - **Impartiality** concerns **how the mediator conducts the process**: fair, even-handed, objective and unbiased. It is effectively the rule against bias and **should not be qualified**. So a mediator may not always be neutral but **must** be impartial.
   - Impartiality rules (Model Standards II; European Code 2.2): avoid the appearance of partiality; no gifts or favours (de minimis or culturally appropriate items allowed); withdraw if unable to act impartially. EU Directive art 3(b) defines a mediator by reference to impartiality. Mediation Model Law **art 7(3)**: the mediator "shall seek to maintain fair treatment of the parties". **UMA s 9(g)**: impartiality is optional, so fully informed parties may accept a less impartial mediator.
   - Mayer, *Beyond Neutrality*: neutrality gives credibility but limits mediators; he suggests "conflict engagement specialists".
   - **Conflicts of interest** (Model Standards III; European Code 2.1): disclose prior relationships and actual or potential conflicts; the Model Standards require **reasonable inquiry**; disclosure is a **continuing** duty and its extent is unclear, so a broad approach is advisable. The European Code lists personal or business relationships with a party, financial or other interest in the outcome, and the mediator or their firm having acted for a party in another capacity. After disclosure, consent is needed. Model Standards III(E): withdraw if the conflict might reasonably undermine the mediation's integrity, **even if the parties consent**. UMA s 9(a)(1): reasonable inquiry; s 9(d): failure to disclose means the mediator loses the mediation privilege. Mediation Model Law **art 6(5)**: disclose circumstances likely to give rise to "justifiable doubts". Feehily calls this vague and suggests such matters are better left to codes. NSW Law Society guidelines bar lawyers from mediating where their firm acted for a party.
3. **Competence** (Model Standards IV; European Code 1.1–1.2): only mediate with the necessary process competence: training, experience, continuing education, cultural understanding. In commercial disputes parties often also expect **subject-matter expertise**. The more evaluative the parties want the mediator to be, the more legal experience helps (Kallipetis and Ruttle). The obligation is continuing; withdraw if competence is lost. Law Council of Australia guidelines: look first to mediation skill and experience, then other qualifications.
4. **Confidentiality** (Model Standards V; European Code 4): caucus and process confidentiality with exceptions (consent, legal or public policy). Mediators should explain its extent. The duty is under pressure from compulsion to report to courts.
5. **Quality of the process** (Model Standards VI; European Code 3.1–3.3): diligence, timeliness, appropriate participants, participation, procedural fairness, party competency, mutual respect; caution about mixing roles (e.g. lawyer); avoid furthering crime; end the process where appropriate.
6. **Advertising, solicitation, fees** (Model Standards VII–VIII; European Code 1.3, 3.4): truthful and transparent.
7. **Advancement of mediation practice** (Model Standards IX): e.g. mentoring new mediators.

**Implications of standardisation**
- The Model Standards' three goals: guide mediators, inform parties, promote public confidence.
- There is a tension: confidentiality restricts scrutiny of mediator conduct. Codes reduce uncertainty but leave issues unresolved, which training can address. Standards will evolve through practice and judicial definition, so they are **organic instruments**. Flexible regulation suits changing practice, but national rules are needed for confidentiality, limitation and enforcement (Hopt and Steffek).

### 7. Mediator standards and the Singapore Convention
- Standards may come from ethical codes, codes of conduct, practice standards, agreements to mediate, legislation, case law and practice directions. The Convention reflects this diversity by leaving the "applicable standards" to the enforcing state's authority (Alexander and Chong). The Convention has given professional standards an international visibility they lacked before.
- **Art 5(1)(e)** (serious breach of applicable standards, with causation) and **art 5(1)(f)** (failure to disclose circumstances raising justifiable doubts, with material impact or undue influence and causation): high thresholds and objective tests. Art 5(1)(f) is autonomous of domestic standards. *Lehrer v Zwernemann* 14 SW 3d 775 (Tex 2000): constructive knowledge of the undisclosed relationship defeated the claim. See singapore-convention-mediator-misconduct.md.
- Köhler suggests the Convention may push the IMI or IBA to develop mediation-specific conflict guidelines rather than borrowing arbitration rules.

## Debate and critique
- **Should mediation be a profession?** Professionalisation promises quality and public confidence. But mediation draws on many disciplines and "does not belong to the legal profession". Admission-style regulation risks rigidity and closing the market. Feehily prefers market or incentive approaches for commercial mediation because the parties control the process.
- **Neutrality myth**: should mediators be neutral, or only impartial? Feehily's distinction lets parties choose a mediator with relevant connections or knowledge if they are informed, as the UMA allows even for impartiality. The Model Standards III(E) override of party consent is more paternalistic.
- **Codes vs training**: codes are abstract and "woolly"; training deals with grey areas better.
- **Uniformity vs diversity**: national codes, institutional codes and global IMI standards. The Singapore Convention's reference to "applicable standards" without a uniform code produces uncertainty (Köhler) but respects diversity.
- **Model Law art 6(5)** "justifiable doubts" is vague (Feehily), yet the same phrase is the test in the Singapore Convention art 5(1)(f).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Was the mediator properly appointed and qualified?** What accreditation or panel membership? What code applied (named in the agreement to mediate, the provider's rules, national law)?
2. **Disclosure**: did the mediator disclose relationships or interests? Did the mediator make reasonable inquiries? Did the parties consent after disclosure? Should the mediator have withdrawn regardless (Model Standards III(E))?
3. **Impartiality in conduct**: unequal caucus time, gifts, pressure on one side, evaluative comments. Distinguish neutrality (interest) from impartiality (conduct).
4. **Self-determination**: was there pressure or manipulation? Were unrepresented parties advised to get independent advice?
5. **Competence**: did the mediator have process (and if expected, subject-matter) competence?
6. **Consequences**: provider discipline or removal from panel; settlement challenged (contract defences; Singapore Convention art 5(1)(e)/(f)); liability (see mediator-liability note); UMA loss of privilege for non-disclosure.
