# Introduction to Commercial Mediation and ADR

## Overview
Chapter 1 of Feehily, $1 sets the foundations for the subject. It covers why conventional litigation and arbitration often fail commercial parties, how ADR emerged and is classified, how to choose the right process, and how mediation should be defined. The chapter argues that mediation is not an "alternative" to the justice system so much as an **appropriate**, complementary process operating "in the shadow of the law". These ideas recur in later topics: the definition problem in process controversies, the voluntariness debate in compulsion, and the Singapore Convention's definition of "mediation".

## The law

### 1. Mediation and conventional dispute resolution
- Litigation assumes there is a correct way to decide every issue, and its cost has become onerous.
- Arbitration began as a real alternative to the courts but has become "legalised", more formal and costly.
- Chief Justice Warren Burger, "Our Vicious Legal Spiral" (1977) 16 Judges Journal 23: an early warning about the escalating cost and complexity of litigation.

### 2. Commercial interests and legal rights
- Unforeseen events turn into contractual "breach", and litigation or arbitration usually follows. That rarely serves the parties' commercial interests.
- Mediation is consensual rather than adversarial. **Legal rights are not affected unless a new agreement is reached**. Parties empower themselves to find their own solution.
- The legal system "is not designed to solve people's problems".

### 3. The legal system
- Most commercial cases are about money, and there is systemic pressure to reframe disputes in purely economic terms.
- Remedies such as apologies or changes in policy or practice are rarely ordered. The law/equity division survives in name only.
- The system has limits, and so does the lawyer's role within it.

### 4. The limits of adjudication
- Adjudication (judicial or arbitral) sees black and white; it cannot handle shades of grey or **polycentric** elements (Fuller and Winston, "The Forms and Limits of Adjudication" (1978) 92 Harv L Rev 353, cited).
- Conventional processes reflect an **ethic of conscience** (a "right" answer is attainable). Commercial mediation reflects an **ethic of responsibility** (consequences, moral ambiguity, choosing the lesser wrong).
- Conflict is an opportunity to improve understanding. Since most commercial disputes settle "at the doors of the court", it is regrettable that resolution efforts are left so late.

### 5. The context of ADR
- Modern ADR is usually traced to the **1976 Roscoe Pound Conference**, where **Frank Sander** argued disputes could be processed other than by adversarial litigation. This inspired the "**multi-door courthouse**" and "fitting the forum to the fuss" (Sander and Goldberg). Three groups were drawn in: efficiency-minded judges (Burger); an empowerment-oriented social movement; and "process pluralism" scholars (Menkel-Meadow).
- Opposition: Owen Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984) 93 Yale LJ 1073.
- "ADR" has varied meanings: in the **US** all processes other than litigation; **outside the US** it usually excludes arbitration (though the *Jackson ADR Handbook* includes it). The "A" may mean alternative, **appropriate**, additional or amicable (the ICC ADR Rules 2001 used "amicable"). Stipanowich calls the term outmoded but convenient.
- All dispute resolution operates in the "**shadow of the law**" (Mnookin and Kornhauser (1979) 88 Yale LJ 950): legal rights are a frame of reference for evaluating proposals, and the legal process remains available for duress, breach of confidentiality and enforcement.
- ADR fits well within the court system as part of an integrated dispute resolution system. It should **supplement, not supplant**, adjudication (Pretorius; Hopt and Steffek recommend ADR options at all stages, incentives to choose early, and easy switching).

### 6. ADR and litigation
- Litigation follows familiar, rule-based procedures; in ADR the parties agree the rules. The "pathology" of litigation is a reason for ADR.
- **Integrative** disputes (interdependent goals) suit mediation; some purely **distributive** disputes may not.
- "Any commercial case appropriate for arbitration is likely to be appropriate for mediation".

### 7. Methods of ADR (three categories)
**I. Non-adjudicative**
- **Negotiation**: a voluntary bargaining relationship; give and take; managing "tangibles" (price, terms) and "intangibles" (psychological motivations, values, emotions).
- **Mediation**: an extension of structured negotiation with a third party who only **assists** and does not decide. All decision-making stays with the parties and the process is voluntary. It may be court-provided or private. "Commercial" covers commerce, trade, tax, insolvency, takeovers and similar; family business or property disputes can fall within it, but separation and divorce do not.
- **Conciliation**: also a structured negotiation, but the third party usually makes a formal settlement recommendation. Use of the terms is inconsistent: UNCITRAL renamed its 2002 Model Law on International Commercial **Conciliation** in 2018 as the Model Law on International Commercial **Mediation** and International Settlement Agreements Resulting from Mediation, having treated the terms as interchangeable. In practice the difference is one of degree: mediation is more facilitative and flexible, conciliation more directive and interventionist.
- **Early neutral and/or expert evaluation**: an expert or independent third party gives a non-binding view of the issues, helping narrow them (useful e.g. in software copyright disputes where litigation would outlast the product). A related option is a neutral fact-finding expert. *Lomax v Lomax* [2019] EWCA Civ 1467 (cited in ch 7) held that a court-ordered ENE under CPR 3.1(2)(m) does not breach art 6 ECHR.
- **Mini-trial**: summaries are presented to senior officials with settlement authority, usually chaired by a neutral who may give an advisory opinion or mediate.

**II. Adjudicative**
- **Arbitration**: a binding (usually) determination by a neutral after arguments and evidence; voluntary or compulsory; usually adversarial; often governed by statute.
- **Adjudication**: like arbitration but with less power and strict timelines; e.g. statutory construction adjudication (Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 ss 108, 114(4)); dispute boards.
- **Expert determination**: a specialist decides (e.g. valuations), enforceable in contract.

**III. Hybrid**
- **Med-arb** (mediate, then arbitrate unresolved issues, often the same neutral) and **arb-med**. They need careful thought and **informed consent at a minimum and at each stage**. Fuller warned that combining the roles risks "dangerous confusion" (cited).

### 8. Process selection and design
- Choose the process most appropriate for the dispute. Options vary in applicability, participation and formality.
- **Four criteria** for assessing effectiveness (ch 1 s 1.9): **transaction costs; satisfaction with outcomes; effect on the relationship; recurrence**.
- The choice depends on the circumstances of the dispute and the parties' needs and interests. Parties should know what they want to achieve. An effective justice system offers **multiple processes**, including litigation and mediation, and each dispute should go to the appropriate one.
- Almost all cases are suitable for mediation, but not all parties are. A dispute referred inappropriately will have low settlement prospects whatever the timing (Alexander).

### 9. Defining commercial mediation
- It is hard to differentiate mediation from other ADR because there is no accepted definition, the procedure is flexible, terms like "voluntary" and "neutrality" are open-ended, and practitioners use the word differently.
- **Three approaches** (Boulle):
  1. **Conceptualist**: defines the ideal (values, principles, objectives). Strength: highlights the higher goals that distinguish mediation. Weakness: passes off prescription as description, making it ideological rather than empirical. It predominates in Australia.
  2. **Descriptive**: what happens in practice. Strength: realism. Weakness: superficial given the diversity of practice.
  3. **Market**: leaves definition to practitioners. Strength: flexibility. Weakness: increasingly untenable with accreditation, codes and regulation.
- Comparative consensus on core elements: a dispute; voluntariness; communication; consensual resolution (Hopt and Steffek).

**Key definitions**
- **EU Mediation Directive** (2008/52/EC) art 3(a): "a structured process, however named or referred to, whereby two or more parties to a dispute attempt by themselves, on a voluntary basis, to reach an agreement on the settlement of their dispute with the assistance of a mediator". It may be initiated by the parties or suggested or ordered by a court or prescribed by national law. It **excludes the trial judge** attempting settlement in the proceedings, but includes judges not responsible for the case.
- **Australia**: no national mediation Act and no uniform statutory definition; widely varying practice. The National Mediator Accreditation Standards (2015) definition emphasises self-determination and says the mediator does not evaluate, advise on the merits or determine the outcome.
- **USA – Uniform Mediation Act (2001)**: "a process in which a mediator facilitates communication and negotiation between parties to assist them in reaching a voluntary agreement regarding their dispute". The words "negotiation" and "assist" exclude adjudication; Stulberg criticises it for not capturing the mediator's active role.
- **UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law (2018) art 1(3)**: a process, "whether referred to by the expression mediation, conciliation or an expression of similar import", whereby parties ask a third person to help them reach an amicable settlement of a dispute "arising out of or relating to a contractual or other legal relationship"; the mediator cannot impose a solution.
- **Singapore Convention art 2(3)**: "irrespective of the expression used or the basis upon which the process is carried out", with assistance of a mediator "lacking the authority to impose a solution". It is wider than the Model Law: it is binding (whereas the Model Law can be modified), and it omits the "legal relationship" limitation so interpersonal breakdowns are covered. Feehily suggests a single definition would have been simpler.
- **Irish Mediation Act 2017 s 2**: "a confidential, facilitative and voluntary process...". Allen says definitions that do not confine "voluntary" to continued participation and the decision to settle (as the EU Directive does) may be conceptually inaccurate globally.
- **Description rather than definition** (Sir Laurence Street): (1) an independent mediator structures the process with the parties and meets them privately in confidence; (2) no authority to impose a decision; (3) the course and outcome are voluntary, permeated by consensus.
- **CEDR definition**: "a flexible process conducted confidentially in which a neutral person actively assists parties in working towards a negotiated agreement of a dispute or difference, with the parties in ultimate control of the decision to settle and of the terms of resolution". CEDR dropped "voluntary" from earlier versions.

### 10. The process continuum
- Court (no party control over design or outcome) → arbitration → hybrids and mini-trial → mediation (parties control outcome) → negotiation (parties control everything).
- Commercial clauses now sequence processes: preventative before corrective, amicable before contentious (negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration or litigation). It is easier to focus on interests before rights; once the focus shifts to rights, an interest-based settlement is less likely.
- Litigation and arbitration aim to **win**; mediation aims to **resolve**. Mediation brings forward the time at which cases would settle anyway, saving costs and management time.

## Key authorities
- Burger, "Our Vicious Legal Spiral" (1977); Sander, Pound Conference address (1976); Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984); Mnookin and Kornhauser, "Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law" (1979); Fuller and Winston (1978).
- EU Mediation Directive art 3(a); UMA definition; Mediation Model Law art 1(3); Singapore Convention art 2(3); Irish Mediation Act 2017 s 2; Australian NMAS 2015; CEDR Model Mediation Procedure definition.
- UNCITRAL Notes on Mediation (2021): a practical guide to organising a mediation (commencement, appointment, preparation, conduct, settlement, termination).

## Debate and critique
- **Alternative, appropriate or additional?** The terminology debate reflects whether mediation is seen as a rival to courts or as part of an integrated justice system. Feehily favours complementarity, which is developed in process-controversies.md ("symbiosis").
- **Against settlement**: Fiss's critique (settlement sacrifices public values and justice to efficiency, and power imbalances distort outcomes) is the main counterpoint to the pro-mediation narrative. It is taken up later.
- **Definition**: should mediation be defined at all? Defining it may confine it (Street); not defining it allows anything to be called mediation, which fuels criticism. The Singapore Convention's broad functional definition shows the international preference for inclusiveness, with "lacking authority to impose a solution" as the core.
- **Voluntariness**: if definitions require voluntariness at entry, mandatory schemes would not be "mediation". The EU approach (voluntary participation and decision) accommodates court-ordered mediation.
- **Hybrids**: efficiency against the risk to due process and candour when the mediator later decides.

## Applying it to a problem question
- If asked to **advise on process choice**: identify the parties' interests (relationship, confidentiality, speed, precedent, enforceability, cross-border issues); apply the four criteria (transaction costs, satisfaction, relationship, recurrence); consider whether the dispute is integrative or distributive; whether a precedent or injunction is needed (favouring litigation); whether technical issues suggest ENE or expert determination; whether enforceability abroad matters (arbitration and the New York Convention; mediation and the Singapore Convention).
- If asked **whether a process is "mediation"** (e.g. for the Singapore Convention or EU Directive): does a third party assist without power to impose a solution? Is participation voluntary (at least continuing participation and the decision to settle)? Is the "mediator" the trial judge (excluded by the EU Directive and inconsistent with the Convention)? The label does not matter.
- For **hybrids**: check informed consent at each stage and the risk of challenge to any resulting award.
