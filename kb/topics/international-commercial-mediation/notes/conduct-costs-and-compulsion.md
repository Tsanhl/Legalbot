# Conduct, Costs and Compulsion

## Overview
In England and Wales, the main lever pushing parties into mediation has been **costs**. Since the Civil Procedure Rules 1998 (CPR), a party that unreasonably refuses to mediate may be penalised in costs even if it wins. Chapter 7 / commentary follows the case law from *Dunnett v Railtrack* through *Halsey*, *PGF II* and later cases, and then asks whether pressure to mediate, or outright compulsion, is compatible with art 6 ECHR. It also asks how far courts can look inside a confidential mediation to judge a party's conduct. Commentators add the most recent step: *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council*, which commentary describes as overruling *Halsey* on compulsion and confirming that courts can stay proceedings for, or order, ADR.

This is a core essay topic: the "journey" from encouragement, to quasi-compulsion, to court-ordered ADR.

## The law

### 1. The CPR framework
- The CPR brought a "cultural change"; litigation is to be the last resort.
- **Overriding objective** (CPR 1.1): dealing with cases justly and at proportionate cost (amended after the Jackson Report to add proportionate cost and enforcing compliance). The court must give effect to it and the parties must help (CPR 1.2, 1.3).
- **Active case management** includes "encouraging the parties to use an alternative dispute resolution procedure if the court considers that appropriate[,] and facilitating the use of such procedure" (CPR 1.4).
- **Part 36** (offers to settle) encourages realistic offers; CPR 36.17 imposes extra penalties where a defendant fails to beat a claimant's offer (10% of an award up to £500,000, 5% above, capped at £75,000).
- **Costs follow the event** (CPR 44.2) in England, unlike the US rule that each party bears its own costs. Conduct, before and during proceedings, including compliance with pre-action protocols, is relevant to costs (CPR 44.2(5), 44.4(3)(a)).
- **Standard vs indemnity basis** (CPR 44.3): on the standard basis doubts are resolved for the paying party and disproportionate costs can be cut; on the indemnity basis doubts are resolved for the receiving party and proportionality is ignored. Standard-basis recovery may leave a party bearing 20–30% of its costs; indemnity is close to 100%. A failure to mediate can lead to **indemnity costs**: *Reid v Buckinghamshire Healthcare NHS Trust* [2015] EWHC B21 (Master O'Hare): if the loser was the one unwilling to mediate, the normal sanction is indemnity costs, showing the court's disapproval; *Virani v Manuel Revert y Cia SA* [2003] EWCA Civ 1651.
- Elsewhere: Singapore Rules of Court O 59 r 5(1)(c) and Hong Kong Practice Direction 31 para 4 also make mediation conduct relevant to costs; *iRiver Hong Kong Ltd v Thakral Corp* (HK CA 2008) drew on the English cases.
- Convention in commercial mediations: the parties share the mediator's fees equally and bear their own preparation costs, unless the agreement to mediate makes them costs in the case.

### 2. Early encouragement
- *Dyson v Leeds City Council* [2000] CP Rep 42 (Ward LJ): recommended mediation before a retrial and warned of costs.
- *Cowl v Plymouth City Council* [2001] EWCA Civ 1935 (Lord Woolf): enough is known about ADR to make failure to use it indefensible, especially where public money is involved.

### 3. *Dunnett v Railtrack plc* [2002] 2 All ER 850; [2002] ADR.L.R. 02/22 – the landmark
- Schiemann LJ, granting permission to appeal, strongly suggested ADR. Railtrack refused, even when a free Court of Appeal mediation scheme was available.
- Brooke LJ: skilled mediators can achieve results beyond the court's powers; lawyers who turn down ADR out of hand "may have to face uncomfortable costs consequences".
- Railtrack won the appeal but was denied its costs. Railtrack's reason for refusing (it would have had to offer more than it wanted) was rejected.
- Significance: the first English case in which a **successful** party, who had assessed its legal risk correctly, was sanctioned for refusing to mediate. Feehily calls it the point where encouragement became **"quasi compulsion"**. CEDR reported mediation use rising about 25% a year for two years afterwards.
- Limit: the refusal was of a **court** recommendation, so it is not itself authority for sanctions after an **inter-party** refusal.

### 4. Developing criteria
- *Hurst v Leeming* [2002] EWHC 1051 (Ch) (Lightman J): the critical factor is whether, objectively, mediation had any **real prospect of success**. Leeming's refusal was justified on the facts (the claimant was obsessive and the claim hopeless), but Lightman J warned of the high risk of refusing and said that what appears incapable of mediation beforehand often proves capable of resolution.
- *SITA v Wyatt Co (UK) Ltd* [2002] EWHC 2401 (Ch) (Park J): no sanction where the party proposing mediation had tried to coerce the other into it ("clean hands").
- *Valentine v Allen* [2003] EWCA Civ 915: no sanction where real efforts to settle had been made (reasonable and generous offers). Also *Corenso v Burnden Group* [2003] EWHC 1805 (QB); *McCook v Lobo* (ignored letter excused, but the court warned of a "Dunnett type order").
- *Leicester Circuits Ltd v Coates Bros plc* [2003] EWCA Civ 333: a successful appellant was penalised for withdrawing from an agreed mediation the day before without explanation. *Neal v Jones* [2002] EWCA Civ 1730: costs reduced for ignoring judicial advice to mediate.

### 5. *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 (joined with *Steel v Joy*)
- Both appeals were dismissed; successful parties who had refused to mediate kept their costs.
- **Burden**: the **unsuccessful** party must show that the successful party acted **unreasonably** in refusing ADR, justifying departure from costs following the event. The burden is not on the refusing party to show mediation had no prospect of success.
- **The Halsey factors** (non-exhaustive):
  1. the nature of the dispute and whether it was suitable for mediation;
  2. the merits, and whether the winning side's pre-trial belief that its case was watertight was reasonable;
  3. the extent to which other settlement methods were tried;
  4. whether the costs of mediation would have been disproportionately high;
  5. whether delay in setting up and attending mediation would have been prejudicial;
  6. whether the mediation had a reasonable prospect of success.
- Dyson LJ rejected Lightman J's purely objective test in *Hurst* as "unduly narrow", because it left out the parties' willingness to compromise and the reasonableness of their attitudes.
- The more robust the court's encouragement, the more likely refusal will be found unreasonable. The court's role is "to encourage, not to compel", but encouragement may be robust.
- **Art 6 dictum** [9]: to oblige "truly unwilling parties" to mediate "would be to impose an unacceptable obstruction on their right of access to the courts", in breach of art 6 ECHR.
- **Confidentiality** [14]: parties may take any position in ADR, and the court "should not know, and therefore should not investigate", why ADR failed.
- Duty to advise [11]: lawyers should routinely consider ADR with clients.
- Significance: authority that sanctions can follow an **inter-party** refusal.

### 6. Applying the criteria
- *Reed Executive plc v Reed Business Information Ltd* [2004] EWCA Civ 887: RBI's refusal of the Court of Appeal scheme was not unreasonable. RE proposed it late, from a position of strength; RBI reasonably and rightly believed in its case; there were disputes in other jurisdictions; the parties were far apart; and there were novel points of law. Also: the court would not look at without-prejudice material without consent.
- *Re Midland Linen Service Ltd, Chaudhry v Yap* [2004] EWHC 3380 (Ch): the proposer's attitude to mediation was "inconsistent and uncertain"; a party seeking a sanction must have been unequivocal about mediating.
- *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 (Ward LJ): a small building dispute is "par excellence" suitable for ADR; the claim that it was too complex was "plain nonsense"; ADR costs would be "a drop in the ocean"; the defendants "cannot rely on their own obstinacy" to say mediation would fail. Because the offer predated *Dunnett* and *Halsey*, no extra sanction, but costs of about £185,000 over a £5,025.63 net judgment were "an horrific picture". Also see *Rolf v De Guerin* [2011] EWCA Civ 78 and *Egan v Motor Services (Bath) Ltd* [2007] EWCA Civ 1002 ("a case that cries out for mediation").

### 7. Timing, information and justified refusal
- **Delay**: *Costain Ltd v Charles Haswell & Partners Ltd* [2009] EWHC B25 (TCC): seven months from protocol meeting to mediation was "considerably too long"; costs were reduced.
- **Too early**: *Nigel Witham Ltd v Smith* [2008] EWHC 12 (TCC) and *Wethered Estate Ltd v Davis* [2005] EWHC 1903 (Ch): refusals justified where there was not yet enough information for mediation to work.
- **Untrue case**: *Beattie PN v Canham Consulting* [2021] EWHC 1414 (TCC) (Fraser J): refusal not unreasonable while the claimants were advancing a factually untruthful case.
- **Too late**: *ADS Aerospace Ltd v EMS Global Tracking* [2012] EWHC 2904 (TCC): fewer than 20 working days before trial.
- ***PGF II SA v OMFS Company 1 Ltd* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288**: a dilapidations claim; the claimant twice invited mediation and the defendant never replied. The trial judge deprived the defendant of the Part 36 costs it would otherwise have received (about £250,000 per side at stake). The Court of Appeal (Briggs LJ) upheld this as a "modest extension" of *Halsey*: "silence in the face of an invitation to participate in ADR is, as a general rule, of itself unreasonable", even if a refusal might have been justified. A party with real obstacles (e.g. lack of information) must raise them at the time. The guidance calls for "constructive engagement in ADR rather than flat rejection, or silence".
- *Thakkar v Patel* [2017] EWCA Civ 117 (Jackson LJ): where mediation is obviously appropriate, both parties must "get on with it"; foot-dragging merits a sanction.
- *Garritt-Critchley v Ronnan* [2014] EWHC 1774 (Ch) (HHJ Waksman): rejected "too far apart" ("parties don't know whether in truth they are too far apart unless they sit down and explore settlement"), belief in a watertight case, and acrimony as justifications. Prompt but misguided replies did not help. Contrast *Swain Mason v Mills & Reeve* [2012] EWCA Civ 498 (parties "a hundred miles apart").
- *Northrop Grumman Mission Systems Europe Ltd v BAE Systems (Al Dariyah C41) Ltd* [2014] EWHC 3148 (TCC) (Ramsey J): BAE's reasonable belief in its case did not excuse refusal, but costs still followed the event because of BAE's without-prejudice-save-as-to-costs offer. Allen suggests this weakens *Halsey*.
- *McMillen Williams v Range* [2004] EWCA Civ 294: both sides ignored a recommendation to mediate; "a plague on both your houses", no order as to costs.

### 8. The CPR journey
- *Dunnett*: quasi-compulsion. *Halsey*: sanctions for inter-party refusal plus the factors. The most important factors in later cases are **merits/watertight belief** and **reasonable prospect of success**.
- The sixth factor is criticised: what does "success" mean (Nolan-Haley)? Feehily: it should mean a reasonable prospect of settlement, with failure caused by the winner's intransigence.
- **Most recent addition**: *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council* "overruled the Halsey decision": the court can stay proceedings for, or order, the parties to engage in ADR. Background: in July 2021 the Civil Justice Council report *Compulsory ADR* concluded that mandatory ADR is compatible with art 6 ECHR, since parties compelled to engage in ADR are not compelled to settle and can return to litigation, so access to court is preserved. The Master of the Rolls, Sir Geoffrey Vos, gave the leading judgment and praised the report. (The sources give no citation or further detail; the textbook (2022) predates the decision.)

### 9. Compulsion and art 6 ECHR
- **Consent** has two parts: "front-end, participation consent" and "back-end, outcome consent". Outcome consent is always required; views differ on whether participation consent can be dispensed with (Nolan-Haley: meaningful mediation requires consent).
- Lord Dyson later: "it is one thing to compel parties to consider mediation; it is another to frog march them to the mediation table and deny them access to the courtroom". He conceded that compulsory mediation does not "in and of itself" breach art 6, relying on *Alassini v Telecom Italia SpA* (Joined Cases C-317–320/08) [2010] 3 CMLR 17. In *Alassini* the CJEU upheld Italian rules requiring an out-of-court settlement attempt (30 days, telecoms consumer disputes) before suing, because: the outcome was not binding; the delay was short and limitation was suspended; costs were minimal; and the scheme was in the general interest and proportionate. But it concerned another ADR process, not mediation as such.
- Lightman J ("clearly wrong"), Lord Phillips (courts should be able to require mediation once litigation starts) and Clarke MR (no successful art 6 challenges in Germany, Belgium, Greece) all criticised the *Halsey* dictum. Jackson LJ's Final Report (2009) rejected compulsion but supported sanctions. Sir Alan Ward in *Wright v Michael Wright (Supplies) Ltd* [2013] EWCA Civ 234: perhaps it is time to review *Halsey*. *Lomax v Lomax* [2019] EWCA Civ 1467: ordering early neutral evaluation over objection (CPR 3.1(2)(m)) does not breach art 6.
- **EU**: Mediation Directive art 5(2) allows national laws making mediation compulsory "provided that such legislation does not interfere with the right of access to justice". Italy's Legislative Decree 28 art 5 (mandatory mediation with opt-out) greatly increased the number of mediations. The European Parliament's study (De Palo and others, 2014, "Rebooting" the Mediation Directive) proposed a "mitigated" form: mandatory information sessions plus mediation with an opt-out, and a Balanced Relationship Target Number.
- **US**: the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act 1998 lets federal district courts compel mediation; *Re Atlantic Pipe Corp* 304 F 3d 135 (1st Cir 2002) (inherent power); contrast *Jeld-Wen v Superior Court* 146 Cal App 4th 536 (2007) (cannot order parties to fund private mediation).
- **Key condition**: mediation must be a **condition precedent**, not the only route to resolution. Then costs sanctions are hard to see as breaching art 6.
- **Three possible art 6(1) challenges**: (1) a settlement bars a later court action; (2) money spent on failed mediation could have funded litigation; (3) a winning party suffers an adverse costs order for refusing to mediate and argues access to court is "theoretical and illusory". Answer: participation is voluntary after entry, parties can leave, and settlement is never compelled.
- **Waiver of art 6 rights**: the ECtHR's four conditions: (a) unequivocal (may be implied: *Suovaniemi v Finland* (1999), arbitration); (b) minimum safeguards appropriate to the right's importance (*Poitrimol v France*; legal representation likely suffices); (c) not contrary to an important public interest (*Hakansson v Sweden*); (d) not tainted by constraint: *Deweer v Belgium* [1980] 2 EHRR 429 (a butcher who faced closure within 48 hours paid a fine under constraint). It is arguable that costs threats plus authoritative encouragement amount to constraint (Shipman). The later margin of appreciation and proportionality analysis (*Ashingdane v UK*) applies; legitimate aims include efficient use of court resources.

### 10. Conduct, costs and confidentiality
- Problem: a party may attend only to avoid sanctions, and obstructive conduct is hidden by privilege.
- **Australia (mixed)**: *Silver Fox Co Pty Ltd v Lenard's Pty Ltd (No 3)* [2004] FCA 1570 (mediation offers admitted on costs, Evidence Act 1995 (Cth) s 131(2)(h)); *Western Areas Exploration Pty Ltd v Streeter (No 2)* [2009] WASCA 15 (affidavits about the mediation inadmissible; the mediator can only report failure to cooperate); *Tracy v Bifield* (WASC 1998) (confidentiality upheld despite a statutory reporting power).
- **England**: will not breach confidentiality to assess reasonableness. *Reed* (no disclosure of without-prejudice material without consent; no adverse inference; use "without prejudice save as to costs" offers instead); *Halsey* [14]; *Cumbria Waste Management Ltd v Baines Wilson* [2008] EWHC 786 (QB) (refused disclosure of mediation documents against a party's wishes). Contrast *Malmesbury v Strutt & Parker* [2008] EWHC 424 (QB), where both parties waived confidentiality and the winner's costs were cut for unreasonable conduct in the mediation.
- **Good-faith rules**: no agreed definition. **Narrow rules** (attend, attend for a set time, position paper, make an offer, have settlement authority) are easy to check but easy to game; **broad rules** (meaningful engagement, motivation) require intrusion into confidentiality. ABA policy statement (2004): sanctions only for narrow rules. Uniform Mediation Act s 7(b)(1): mediators may report only whether the mediation occurred, whether it settled, and attendance. The "excluder" theory (Summers) defines good faith by what it excludes.

## Key authorities
- *Dunnett v Railtrack* [2002] 2 All ER 850: first sanction of a winner for refusing a court-suggested ADR.
- *Hurst v Leeming* [2002] EWHC 1051 (Ch): objective real-prospect test (later rejected as too narrow).
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes* [2004] EWCA Civ 576: burden on the loser; six factors; art 6 dictum; encourage, not compel.
- *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358: small building disputes; profession put on notice.
- *Reed Executive v Reed Business Information* [2004] EWCA Civ 887: justified refusal; without-prejudice material.
- *PGF II v OMFS* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288: silence is unreasonable.
- *Thakkar v Patel* [2017] EWCA Civ 117; *Garritt-Critchley v Ronnan* [2014] EWHC 1774 (Ch); *Northrop Grumman v BAE* [2014] EWHC 3148 (TCC); *Costain v Charles Haswell* [2009] EWHC B25 (TCC).
- *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil CBC*: courts may order or stay for ADR; *Halsey* overruled on this point.
- *Alassini v Telecom Italia* [2010] 3 CMLR 17: mandatory pre-action ADR compatible with effective judicial protection.
- *Deweer v Belgium* [1980] 2 EHRR 429: waiver tainted by constraint.
- EU Mediation Directive art 5(2); US ADR Act 1998; Irish Mediation Act 2017 s 21.

## Debate and critique
- **Encouragement or disguised compulsion?** Ahmed ("Implied Compulsory Mediation") and Mulcahy argue English courts already compel mediation in practice through costs. Feehily ("Creeping Compulsion to Mediate", NILQ 2018) explores the constitutional and Convention implications. *Churchill* now makes compulsion explicit.
- **Halsey criticised.** The art 6 dictum was attacked by senior judges. The factors are judged with hindsight; it is unclear how to weigh them; the "non-exhaustive" list is potentially "infinite", which is bad for the rule of law (Koo). Some thought *Halsey* too weak (Lightman J).
- **Sixth factor**: unclear meaning of "success" (Nolan-Haley).
- **Genn's scepticism**: "The outcome of mediation is not about just settlement, it is just about settlement"; she saw a judicial "campaign" to undermine *Halsey*. Allen counters.
- **Consent**: Is mediation still mediation if entry is compelled? Distinguish front-end and back-end consent; Feehily argues compulsion limited to entry, with free exit and legal advice, is compatible with art 6.
- **Confidentiality vs accountability**: sanctions push courts to look inside mediation, eroding confidentiality. The English answer is to look only at external conduct; the US answer is narrow rules.
- **Effectiveness**: costs sanctions function as targeted incentives with effects similar to mandatory rules (Steffek), a compromise between voluntariness, regulation and access to justice. Fewer reported sanction cases recently may show the policy works.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Who refused, when, and in response to what?** A court recommendation (*Dunnett*) or a party's offer (*Halsey*)? Pre-action (*Burchell*: still counts)? Silence (*PGF II*)? Foot-dragging (*Thakkar*)? Withdrawal at the last minute (*Leicester Circuits*)?
2. **Burden**: on the party seeking the sanction to show unreasonable refusal (*Halsey*).
3. **Run the six Halsey factors** against the facts: suitability (small building or commercial disputes are suitable; precedent needed or injunction needed may not be); watertight belief (*Garritt-Critchley*, *Northrop*); other settlement efforts (Part 36 offers: *Valentine*, *Northrop*); cost proportionality (*Burchell*); delay/timing (*Costain*, *ADS*, *Nigel Witham*); prospect of success (do not accept "too far apart" or acrimony).
4. **Proposer's own conduct**: was the offer genuine and unequivocal (*Midland Linen*)? Coercive (*SITA*)? Was the case put honestly (*Beattie*)?
5. **Did the refusing party give reasons at the time and ask for needed information?** (*PGF II*.)
6. **Sanction options**: deprive the winner of costs; partial reduction; indemnity costs against the loser (*Reid*); Part 36 interplay.
7. **Evidence**: the court will not examine what happened inside the mediation without consent (*Reed*, *Halsey* [14], *Cumbria*), so advise "without prejudice save as to costs" offers.
8. **If the court can order ADR**: consider *Churchill* and art 6 compatibility (CJC 2021; *Alassini*; condition precedent; free exit).
9. **Other jurisdictions**: Ireland s 21; EU Directive art 5(2) and national compulsory schemes (Italy); US ADR Act; Hong Kong PD 31.

Traps: saying *Dunnett* covers inter-party refusals; forgetting the burden in *Halsey*; assuming a winner always gets costs; treating *Halsey*'s art 6 dictum as still good law without mentioning the later developments.
