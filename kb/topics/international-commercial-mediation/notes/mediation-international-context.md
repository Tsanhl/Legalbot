# Commercial Mediation in the International Context

## Overview
Chapter 2 places commercial mediation in its global setting. It covers: the long history of mediation across civilisations; how **culture** shapes conflict and mediation; the drive towards **harmonisation** through UNCITRAL instruments, the EU Mediation Directive and the US Uniform Mediation Act; theories of **mediation regulation** (public/private, enabling/guiding, diversity vs harmonisation); and **private international law** seen through a global-governance lens. It provides the institutional map for later topics on clauses, enforcement and the Singapore Convention.

## The law

### 1. Historical background
- Mediation has been used for millennia: in **China** from the Chou Dynasty (1100–256 BC); it was the primary method in ancient Greece and Rome; traditional mediation in African and Arab states; in Europe, the Preamble to the **Peace of Westphalia 1648**; and in Judaism, Islam, Christianity, Hinduism and Buddhism.
- **Traditional vs modern** practice differ in: the role and standing of the mediator; mediation as an institution of social control; the communal interest in restoring harmony; ownership of the process; social harmony vs individual rights; outcome-focused vs process-focused.
- Commercial mediation is traced to **Babylonian commerce (2300 BC)**. Business people have long preferred non-legal settlement: to keep control, save cost and time, focus on commercial solutions and keep long-term relationships.
- The US experience inspired developments in Australia, the UK and elsewhere.

### 2. The role of culture
- Culture is collective identity and behaviour (conscious and unconscious language, values, habits, norms). It is learned, dynamic and overlapping, and hard to confine within borders; it arises within multicultural societies too.
- People from different cultures process the same experiences differently. Ignoring this produces ethnocentrism. Mediating across cultures requires **cultural relativism**: knowing, understanding and accepting difference.
- **High-context vs low-context** communication: Eastern (e.g. Asian) parties tend to be high-context and indirect; Western (e.g. US) parties low-context and direct. Abramson's contrasts: short- vs long-term orientation; individual vs collective decisions; competitive vs co-operative; punctuality vs relaxed time; contract focus vs relationship focus.
- Example: formal apologies are common in Japan and can avoid litigation; in the US an apology may be seen as demeaning and harder to include than money.
- Cultural axes listed: civil vs common law; "qualitative" vs "quantitative" motivations; variation in the desire for regulation; Western vs non-Western orientation; **traditional vs modern** as the two main mediation cultures.
- **Civil vs common law**: uptake has been higher in common law states because litigation is costlier and more disruptive; cheaper litigation slowed civil law uptake. There has since been a big shift in Austria, Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Switzerland (tax and fee incentives). Civil law states tend to locate mediation centrally within the justice system with dense regulation; common law states take a decentralised, market-based approach developed through case law.
- **Qualitative vs quantitative** rationales: qualitative means better communication and problem-solving and a different form of "justice" (Nordic mediation drawing on Nils Christie, "Conflicts as Property" (1977)); quantitative means reducing court workload and improving efficiency. These motivations drive different attitudes to incentives, controls and transparency (concern about the "privatisation of justice": Menkel-Meadow).
- **East Asia and Arab states**: mediation is central to the legal tradition; privacy matters less because conflict is handled within the community; harmonious relationship-preserving outcomes. Chinese and Arab/Muslim mediators traditionally practise an extreme evaluative form ("semi-arbitration") where parties feel morally bound to the proposed solution. Western practice sees conflict as destructive; many non-Western traditions see it as potentially constitutive. Imposing a Western model may reduce uptake.
- **Mediator implications**: mediators should be culturally trained and culturally suitable; recognise culturally shaped interests, impediments and resolutions; cultural issues may be central or an impediment; **co-mediation** or team mediation can work well. Cultural difference may not cause conflict itself, but cultural knowledge improves settlement chances by building trust and identifying values for proposals.

### 3. Increasing harmonisation
- National legal systems are limited and not responsive to parties from different systems. Mediation can transcend systemic differences and offers a potential **"lingua franca"** for transnational disputants. Globalisation drives harmonisation of laws, practices and dispute systems.
- **Global (UNCITRAL and the New York Convention)**:
  - **New York Convention 1958** (adopted 1958, in force 1959): courts must enforce arbitration agreements and recognise foreign awards; a "universal constitutional charter". It is relevant to mediation where settlements are converted into consent awards.
  - **UNCITRAL**: created by the UN General Assembly in 1966 to harmonise and modernise international trade law; 60 elected member states. **Working Group II** (originally arbitration, now dispute settlement) produced the revised Mediation Model Law and the Singapore Convention.
  - **UNCITRAL Mediation Rules** (adopted 23 July 1980, formerly Conciliation Rules; GA Res 35/52): comprehensive procedural rules. They influenced the LCIA, WIPO and ICC rules and the Model Law. Renamed for consistency.
  - **UNCITRAL Arbitration Model Law** (1985, revised 2006): relevant to consent awards (art 30).
  - **UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law** (2002 as the Model Law on International Commercial **Conciliation**, consensus of 90 nations; amended **2018** and renamed). Three sections: (1) general principles (scope, definitions, interpretation); (2) procedure (appointment, commencement, termination, conduct, communications, disclosure, confidentiality, admissibility of evidence, post-mediation); (3) settlement agreements (2018, consistent with the Singapore Convention but covering **all** settlement agreements). **Art 2(1)**: interpretation must have regard to its international origin and the need for uniformity and good faith; **art 2(2)**: gaps are filled by its general principles. These two, plus **art 7(3)** (the mediator must treat parties fairly), are the mandatory provisions. Case Law on UNCITRAL Texts (**CLOUT**) helps harmonisation. Criticism (Alexander): many procedural provisions derive from the 1980 Rules and are "archaic" (e.g. art 9 caucus disclosure; art 7(4) allowing mediator settlement proposals); a Model Law might better have confined itself to rights needing legislative backing (confidentiality, limitation, enforceability).
  - **UNCITRAL Notes on Mediation (2021)**: soft-law guidance on organising mediations.
  - **Singapore Convention** (2018, in force 2020): see singapore-convention.md.
- **Regional**:
  - **EU Mediation Directive** (Directive 2008/52/EC): applies to civil and commercial **cross-border** disputes (not administrative or criminal); all Member States except **Denmark** (opted out); transposition deadline 20 May 2011. It does not harmonise mediation law generally. It covers quality and codes of conduct; courts inviting mediation and information sessions; enforceability of settlements (art 6); confidentiality (art 7); suspension of limitation (art 8); public information. It is silent on mediator liability. It allows compulsory mediation, incentives or sanctions if access to court is preserved (art 5(2)). It is permissive and sets a **floor, not a ceiling**. France, Germany and Ireland went further; some states now have separate domestic and cross-border regimes.
  - **US Uniform Mediation Act (2001)**: main purposes are confidentiality and uniformity; a baseline that does not displace stronger state rules; many states have not adopted it because their own laws (especially on confidentiality) are stronger.

### 4. Mediation regulation
- **Public** regulation (laws) vs **private** regulation (model contracts, self-regulating associations, codes). There is great diversity: Austria is highly regulated; England's CPR deal mainly with costs and leave mediation design and mediator regulation to private bodies and the market.
- The trend is away from civil procedure codes and towards **comprehensive dispute resolution laws** supporting party choice, with incentives to choose the appropriate process.
- **Enabling rules**: frameworks stakeholders can use, e.g. the effect of agreements to mediate, limitation periods, confidentiality, enforcement of settlements, court-annexed schemes.
- **Guiding rules**: incentives or mandatory duties responding to information and decision deficits, e.g. duties of parties and lawyers, mandatory pre-action mediation, court orders, **costs incentives and sanctions**.
- **Balance**: voluntariness and flexibility versus promoting mediation and quality. Comparative empirical research (Hopt and Steffek) shows success depends not on extensive or sparse regulation but on a **targeted approach** suited to the stage of development, the regulatory environment and the conflict culture. Regulation must consider the people who operate it (parties, lawyers, judges).
- **"The core of the method of mediation should be free of regulation."** Regulation must manage the tension between **diversity and harmonisation**, both of which are important (Alexander). Legislation struggles with mediation's non-legal, innovative character, so it often deals only with specific aspects (e.g. the UMA on confidentiality). The World Bank Group recommends a mix of public and private regulation.

### 5. Private international law
- "Conflict of laws": how a country's domestic law handles disputes with a foreign element. There is no single global system. It deals with jurisdiction, choice of law and forum, and recognition and enforcement of foreign judgments. Forum and choice-of-law clauses are standard in commercial contracts.
- Traditional view: a transnational extension of domestic law. **Alternative view: a global governance lens**, providing solutions to regulatory problems. This is seen in the increasing use of **soft law** (rules and model laws that bind only when adopted by statute or agreement), which adds flexibility.
- Applied to mediation:
  - **Jurisdiction/competence** includes enforcing agreements to mediate;
  - **Choice of law** includes determining the law of the contract where unclear;
  - **Soft law** (mediator standards, procedural rules) will be relevant to interpreting the Singapore Convention (e.g. "applicable standards" in art 5(1)(e));
  - **Recognition and enforcement** of international mediated settlements under the Singapore Convention.
- Through a governance lens, private international law is a regulatory system aiming at the best, most consistent and just approach to jurisdiction, choice of law, recognition and enforcement, and cooperation between authorities. **Hard and soft law play complementary, not conflicting, roles.** What matters is whether a new instrument is effective and benefits those in cross-border relationships.
- Before the Singapore Convention, cross-border MSAs depended on the private international law of the enforcing state, which was costly and gave variable results (Chong and Steffek; Alexander and Chong, cited in ch 8).

## Key authorities
- Instruments: New York Convention 1958; UNCITRAL Mediation (Conciliation) Rules 1980; Arbitration Model Law 1985/2006; Mediation Model Law 2002/2018 (arts 2, 7(3)); Singapore Convention 2018; EU Mediation Directive 2008/52/EC (arts 5(2), 6, 7, 8); Uniform Mediation Act 2001; UNCITRAL Notes on Mediation 2021.
- Commentators: Hopt and Steffek (comparative regulation); Alexander ("Harmonisation and Diversity in the Private International Law of Mediation"); Menkel-Meadow (privatisation of justice; cultural variation); Abramson (cultural contrasts); Christie ("Conflicts as Property").

## Debate and critique
- **Harmonisation vs diversity**: is a single global model desirable when culture shapes conflict so deeply? Alexander and Feehily: both values matter, and the tension between them is productive. A Western facilitative model may not suit high-context or harmony-oriented cultures, which is relevant to the Singapore Convention's legitimacy and to China's integration debate.
- **How much regulation?** Feehily, following Hopt and Steffek: targeted regulation; keep the core of the method free; regulate enabling matters (confidentiality, limitation, enforcement) but not the process itself.
- **Soft law's role**: flexibility and adaptability versus uncertainty and lack of binding force; the governance perspective sees soft and hard law as complementary.
- **Qualitative vs quantitative motives**: efficiency-driven (court-docket) mediation risks distorting the process (see process-controversies.md on court-connected mediation resembling settlement conferences).
- **EU Directive's limited impact**: a floor, cross-border only, many exceptions (see the "Rebooting" study in the costs note), with continuing diversity among Member States.
- **The Model Law's archaic procedural content** (Alexander) versus its acceptability to states because it is consistent with the familiar Rules.

## Applying it to a problem question
- In **cross-border scenarios**, identify: the cultural backgrounds of the parties (communication style, attitude to apology, relationship vs contract focus); whether the mediator is culturally suitable or co-mediation would help; which instruments apply (EU Directive for cross-border EU disputes; Model Law if the forum has adopted it; Singapore Convention for enforcement; New York Convention if converting to a consent award); and private international law questions (which law governs the mediation clause, agreement to mediate and settlement; which court has jurisdiction).
- In **policy essays**: use the enabling/guiding distinction to classify reforms (e.g. costs sanctions are guiding; confidentiality statutes are enabling) and argue for targeted regulation.
