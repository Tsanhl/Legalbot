# The Obligation to Advise on Mediation and Agreements to Mediate

## Overview
Chapter 3 ends with two linked topics. First, is a lawyer under a duty to advise a client about mediation? In England this comes indirectly from the CPR and costs case law (*Halsey*, *Burchell*); some jurisdictions (Ireland) have made it statutory, and a South African court has sanctioned lawyers personally. Second, the **agreement to mediate**: the contract signed once a dispute exists and the parties (and mediator) commit to a particular mediation. It differs from the mediation clause, which is agreed in advance in the commercial contract. It governs the practical and legal framework of the mediation, and cases such as *Universal Satspace v Kenya* show that its terms do not always work as intended.

## The law

### A. The obligation to advise on the mediation option
1. **England – CPR and costs.** The CPR encourage mediation, including through costs sanctions on parties who win at trial but unreasonably refused an offer to mediate (CPR 1.1(1) overriding objective; CPR 1.4 active case management, including encouraging ADR; CPR 44 conduct and costs).
2. **Halsey.** Dyson LJ in *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 [11]: "All members of the legal profession who conduct litigation should now routinely consider with their clients whether their disputes are suitable for ADR."
3. **Burchell.** *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 [43] (Ward LJ): the profession "can no longer with impunity shrug aside reasonable requests to mediate"; a request cannot be ignored just because it was made before the claim was issued; defendants who "blithely battle on" can expect little sympathy. Rix LJ at [50]: an unreasonable refusal before formal proceedings can be taken into account (CPR 44.3(5)(a)).
4. **Later reinforcement.** *Rolf v De Guerin* [2011] EWCA Civ 78 (Rix LJ): parties should respond reasonably to offers to mediate or settle, and their conduct may affect costs. *PGF II SA v OMFS Company 1 Ltd* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288: silence in the face of an invitation to ADR is, as a general rule, itself unreasonable.
5. **Negligence risk.** It is conceivable that lawyers could be sued in negligence for failing to advise on mediation. In England, failing to advise on or explore mediation could amount to professional negligence (the materials).
6. **Statutory duty – Ireland.** Under the Mediation Act 2017 s 14 (solicitors) and s 15 (barristers acting directly for clients), before issuing proceedings the lawyer must advise the client to consider mediation and give information about mediation services. If the client still sues, the solicitor must file a statutory declaration confirming the advice was given. Section 21: where the court has invited the parties to consider mediation, it may take into account an unreasonable refusal or failure to consider or attend mediation when awarding costs.
7. **US.** Colorado (bar association), Arkansas (statute) and Ohio, New Jersey and Massachusetts (court rules) require lawyers to advise clients about ADR; many federal and state courts have similar rules.
8. **South Africa – sanctioning the lawyers.** *Brownlee v Brownlee* (High Court, Brassey AJ, 25 August 2009): the lawyers effectively agreed not to recommend mediation in a case that would have benefited from it. They had "positively rejected the use of the [mediation] process" and had to bear responsibility for failing to serve their clients. Each party bore its own costs, and the lawyers' fees against their own clients were capped at party-and-party rates.
9. **Self-protection**: keep evidence of clear advice on mediation, other ADR options and costs, and of the client's instructions in response.

### B. Agreements to mediate
1. **Purpose.** Mediation providers usually require an agreement before the process begins. It deals with practicalities.
2. **Typical contents** (Feehily): appointment, role and functions of the mediator; parties' rights and duties, including that representatives **with authority to settle** attend; how the process will work (conduct and procedure, or incorporated institutional rules and code of conduct); scope; **confidentiality**; conflicts of interest; mediator's fee and charges; liability and immunity; effect on concurrent processes such as litigation; governing law and forum for later disputes; and the requirement that any settlement be **in writing and signed**. It may also provide that the mediator will not be called as a witness.
3. **Signatories**: the parties, the mediator, and possibly lawyers and other representatives. All signatories can be sued for breach. A party may sue a mediator who breaches it, though that means litigation, which is what the parties wanted to avoid. Because there is usually no statutory route to remove a mediator (unlike arbitrators, e.g. Commercial Arbitration Act 2010 (NSW) s 14; Arbitration Act 2010 (Ireland) s 9; Arbitration Act 1965 (SA) s 13), grounds and procedure for removal can be written into the agreement.
4. **Consistency**: its terms should be consistent with the mediation clause. If the parties want to modify the clause, they should say so expressly.
5. **Form**: an oral agreement to mediate is possible, but writing is preferable for clarity and evidence (Hopt and Steffek). In the USA and Australia, statutes supply some default terms, though parties often tailor their own.
6. **Policy favours enforcement in England.** *Beauty Star Ltd v Janmohamed* [2014] EWCA Civ 451: accountants were appointed under an agreement to mediate (following a court order) to report. Because the appointment was contractual, not by the court, the Court of Appeal would not re-examine their approach; even with mistakes, the report bound the parties because that is what they had agreed.
7. **Uncertainty in practice – *Universal Satspace (North America) LLC v Republic of Kenya*** (High Court (QB), Teare J, 20 December 2013). The agreement to mediate said no settlement would bind unless written and signed by all parties. The terms were agreed; Kenya asked for a delay in signing for "administrative" reasons; the parties orally agreed to sign within 21 days; only Satspace signed. Satspace applied to strike out Kenya's defence and counterclaim as an abuse of process. Held: the oral agreement to sign created a **collateral contract** which the agreement to mediate did not govern, and effect was given to the settlement.
8. **Two readings of *Satspace***. (a) Supportive: courts will not let a settled claim continue just because formalities were not completed, which supports mediation outcomes. (b) Critical (Feehily): the court enforced a settlement against an express term of the agreement to mediate. It is hard to see how a collateral contract could exist without the main contract it is supposedly collateral to, when a required formality was never met. Feehily notes the decision was surprising given 14 years of the CPR culture change, and that similar cases would likely be decided differently after *MWB Business Exchange Centres v Rock Advertising* [2018] UKSC 24 (full effect given to "No Oral Modification" clauses).
9. **Drafting lesson**: treat agreements to mediate as **organic** instruments, to be revised as case law develops. If oral agreements should not bind, widen the signed-writing clause so that signed writing is also needed for any collateral contract or waiver of formalities.

## Key authorities
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 [11]: duty to routinely consider ADR with clients.
- *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 [43], [50]: profession cannot shrug aside reasonable requests; pre-action conduct counts.
- *Rolf v De Guerin* [2011] EWCA Civ 78: respond reasonably to offers to mediate.
- *PGF II v OMFS* [2013] EWCA Civ 1288: silence is unreasonable.
- *Brownlee v Brownlee* (SA HC 2009): lawyers sanctioned for failing to advise mediation.
- Irish Mediation Act 2017 ss 14, 15, 21: statutory duty to advise; costs consequences.
- *Beauty Star v Janmohamed* [2014] EWCA Civ 451: agreements to mediate enforced according to their terms.
- *Universal Satspace v Kenya* (QB 2013): oral collateral contract to sign; settlement enforced despite signed-writing clause.
- *MWB v Rock Advertising* [2018] UKSC 24: NOM clauses effective; likely changes the *Satspace* result.

## Debate and critique
- **Indirect versus direct duty.** England uses costs pressure and professional expectation; Ireland legislates a duty and a declaration; South Africa punishes lawyers personally. Points to discuss: whether costs pressure on clients properly reaches lawyers; whether a statutory duty becomes a box-ticking exercise; and whether *Brownlee*-style sanctions risk chilling legitimate litigation advice.
- **Lawyers as gatekeepers.** the materials stresses that lawyers decide whether clients ever hear of mediation. A duty to advise reflects the shift from "rights warrior" to dispute-resolution adviser.
- **Universal Satspace.** It is a good essay example of the tension between formality (certainty, protection against pressure to settle) and substance (giving effect to what was actually agreed). Feehily's critique uses collateral-contract doctrine; the counter-view emphasises abuse of process and finality. *MWB* tips the balance back to formality.
- **Organic drafting.** Like clauses, agreements to mediate need regular revision; reliance on institutional templates is not enough.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Did the lawyer advise on mediation?** Consider the jurisdiction: England (Halsey/Burchell expectation; costs risk; possible negligence), Ireland (statutory ss 14–15 and declaration), US state rules, South Africa (*Brownlee*).
2. **Consequences**: costs sanction on the client (*Halsey* factors; see the costs note); possible negligence claim by the client against the lawyer (duty, breach, causation, loss: was mediation likely to have produced a better result?); professional discipline.
3. **Agreement to mediate**: check who signed (mediator? lawyers? experts?); authority-to-settle term; confidentiality clause and who it binds; signed-writing clause; mediator immunity/limitation; fees.
4. **Was there a binding settlement?** If terms were agreed but not signed, apply *Universal Satspace* (collateral contract, abuse of process) but note *MWB* and the possibility of a better-drafted clause.
5. **Inconsistency between the clause and the agreement to mediate**: did the agreement expressly modify the clause?
6. **Removing a mediator**: no statutory mechanism; look to the agreement's terms.

Trap: confusing the agreement to mediate (post-dispute, process-specific) with the mediation clause (pre-dispute, in the main contract).
