# Singapore Convention – Refusal of Relief for Mediator Misconduct (Art 5(1)(e) and (f))

## Overview
Two of the nine grounds for refusing relief under the Singapore Convention concern the **mediator** rather than the settlement itself: art 5(1)(e) (serious breach of applicable standards) and art 5(1)(f) (failure to disclose circumstances raising justifiable doubts about impartiality or independence). They were the most controversial provisions in the negotiations and are often called the Convention's "Achilles heel". On one side, critics say they import arbitration thinking into a consensual process and invite "settlor's remorse" litigation. On the other, Ben Köhler ("Blaming the middleman?", Journal of Private International Law 2023) argues they are indispensable to the **legitimacy** that justifies expedited enforcement. The reading list includes both Köhler and Michel Kallipetis's insider account (Cardozo J Conflict Resol 2019), so this is likely essay territory.

## The law

### 1. The text
- **Art 5(1)(e)**: relief may be refused, at the request and on proof of the resisting party, where "There was a serious breach by the mediator of standards applicable to the mediator or the mediation without which breach that party would not have entered into the settlement agreement".
- **Art 5(1)(f)**: "There was a failure by the mediator to disclose to the parties circumstances that raise justifiable doubts as to the mediator's impartiality or independence and such failure to disclose had a material impact or undue influence on a party without which failure that party would not have entered into the settlement agreement".
- Both are permissive ("may refuse"), and the **burden is on the party resisting enforcement**.

### 2. Drafting history (Kallipetis; Feehily)
- At Working Group II (Vienna, September 2016) there was robust debate about whether any mediator-related defences belonged in the Convention. Experienced mediators argued they were unnecessary: international commercial parties are represented by legal teams and experts, and the mediator decides nothing. Some delegates, however, had instructions to protect parties harmed by an unfair, biased or misbehaving mediator.
- Kallipetis observes that many delegates seemed unfamiliar with mediation practice and treated mediators like arbitrators. The wording was settled in New York in February 2017 (during a snowstorm, in an informal session that he describes as turning a negotiation into a mediation).
- The agreed principles: (1) the breach or failure must be **material**; (2) materiality is judged **objectively**; (3) the party seeking to resile bears the burden of showing the breach **vitiated its consent**.
- Feehily lists inclusion of narrowly framed misconduct defences with a high burden of proof as one of the Convention's five key compromises.

### 3. Art 5(1)(e): three cumulative elements
**(a) Applicable standards**
- The Convention sets **no uniform standard**. An early draft referred to a substantive standard of fair treatment, later replaced by "applicable standards" (Köhler). This was a compromise between states wanting an autonomous standard and those opposing any mediator ground.
- "Standards" covers both **laws** and **codes of conduct** or professional ethics rules. **Non-binding** best practice is not "applicable" (Schnabel, cited by Köhler).
- Sources may include ethical codes, codes of conduct, agreements to mediate, legislation, case law and practice directions. The drafters intended each state's competent authority to determine the applicable standards (Feehily, citing Alexander and Chong). The provision gave professional mediator standards an international visibility they lacked before.
- Kallipetis: until there is a universal code, mediators should **identify the applicable code in the mediation agreement**.
- **Choice-of-law gap** (Köhler): unlike New York Convention art V(1)(a), (d), (e), which point to the law of the arbitral seat, the Singapore Convention has **no seat** and no conflict rule. Where the parties chose no law, the enforcing state's conflict rules decide. Candidates include the place of mediation, the lex causae, or the state most closely connected (art 1(1)(b)(ii)). The same conduct might therefore be judged by different standards in different states.

**(b) Seriousness**
- "Serious" was chosen after debate over "any", "egregious", "material" and "unacceptable" breach (Kallipetis). It introduces an **objective** assessment of gravity to exclude fanciful, immaterial or subjective claims.
- Köhler: seriousness is an **autonomous, uniform** Convention concept, read in light of the purpose of the breached rule, and interpreters should **err on the side of enforcement**. Only the most egregious misconduct qualifies: conduct that removes the mediation's added legitimacy in the case. Examples he gives: revealing caucus information to the other side in breach of confidentiality ("internal confidentiality"); disclosing confidential information to third parties; unduly inflicting significant emotional distress; misrepresenting material facts to induce settlement. (Alternative tests: Tan, failure of party self-determination; Lung, conduct that deters active participation.)

**(c) Causation**
- "Without which breach that party would not have entered into the settlement agreement". The resisting party must prove causation; **no presumption** is allowed.
- Kallipetis: the party must show that, despite being represented and advised, the mediator's behaviour caused it to consent **against its will**, which he expects to be "extremely rare" in international commercial disputes. In his view the proper remedy for a party pressured into settlement is usually against its own lawyer.
- Köhler offers a wider literal reading: "the settlement agreement" means *this* agreement, so it is enough that the breach affected the decision to conclude these specific terms (the party might otherwise have settled differently), not only that it would never have settled. If the breach happened after terms were agreed, there is no causation. A caucus confidentiality breach during the mediation will usually have affected bargaining position in some way.
- Feehily suggests analogies with misrepresentation in contract law may help define causation (citing Alexander and Chong; Schnabel).

### 4. Art 5(1)(f): three elements
**(a) Failure to disclose circumstances raising justifiable doubts**
- This is an **autonomous** duty under the Convention: it applies whether or not domestic law or codes require disclosure (Feehily; Köhler). So a settlement may be unenforceable even if the mediator complied with all national rules.
- Timing is not specified, but disclosure is expected before the mediation or without undue delay when facts emerge (cf. Model Law 2002 art 5(5)). Disclosure before settlement makes the defence hard to establish.
- "Justifiable doubts" is an objective test mirroring Arbitration Model Law art 12(2). Kallipetis: "justifiable" is a "clever adjective" that imports judicial determination and excludes subjective complaints (rejected alternatives: "any", "serious", "significant", "unequivocal" doubt).
- Impartiality is subjective absence of bias; independence is the objective absence of relationships (e.g. financial dependence).
- Köhler: courts should not simply transplant arbitration standards (e.g. IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest) but develop mediation-specific standards, perhaps through the IMI or IBA. Tan suggests the IBA Non-Waivable Red List could be transposed.
- Parties remain free to choose a conflicted mediator knowingly: the duty is only to disclose (Köhler).

**(b) Material impact or undue influence, and (c) causation**
- The resisting party must show the non-disclosure had a material impact or undue influence, and that it would not otherwise have entered the agreement.
- Kallipetis: "material" lifts the effect above the trivial; "undue influence" is an established legal concept ensuring objectivity.
- Feehily: the test is objective: would the non-disclosure have led a **reasonable person** not to consent? Material impact includes misrepresentation or unconscionability by the mediator. *Lehrer v Zwernemann* 14 SW 3d 775 (Tex 2000): a misconduct claim was summarily dismissed because the claimant had constructive knowledge of the mediator's relationship with the other side's lawyers and could show no damage.
- Interpretive options (Köhler): (i) ask whether the party would have acted significantly differently had disclosure been made; (ii) a stricter view (Alexander, Chong and Giorgadze; Chong and Steffek, citing *Olam* and *BOM v BOK* [2019] 1 SLR 349) that partiality must actually have shown itself in the conduct of the mediation. Köhler rejects (ii), arguing that the duty to disclose exists independently of actual bias and that other misconduct belongs under (e). He also cautions against importing domestic "undue influence" doctrine (not uniform even within common law) or leaving it to the governing law. Some (Yokomizo and Mankowski) treat the words as adding nothing beyond causation.
- Köhler's criticism: the qualifications go too far. It is hard to see why settlements facilitated by a mediator who failed to disclose should enjoy such strong enforceability. The drafters underestimated the subtle influence mediators exert through caucuses and shuttle diplomacy. Courts should give little weight to "undue influence" and "material impact" and refuse enforcement where relevant circumstances were not disclosed.

### 5. Confidentiality and proof
- Proving misconduct requires evidence of what happened inside the mediation, but the Convention **never mentions confidentiality or privilege**. The drafters deliberately left this to domestic law (Köhler, citing the travaux).
- The contractual or statutory confidentiality obligations are governed by the law applicable to the mediation agreement or mediator contract. **Admissibility of evidence and mediator privilege are governed by the lex fori**, as "rules of procedure" under art 3(1).
- Result: considerable uncertainty. A settlement could be enforced in a state with strict privilege (e.g. Massachusetts, Germany's Mediation Act s 4; California's absolute approach in *Cassel v Superior Court* 119 Cal Rptr 3d 437 (2009)) and refused in a state with weak privilege (e.g. Denmark).
- **England** (Kallipetis): courts have admitted mediation evidence in exceptional cases: *Farm Assist Ltd (in liquidation) v Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (No 2)* [2007] EWHC 2870 (TCC) (Ramsey J upheld a witness summons against the mediator, in the interests of justice, where economic duress was alleged); *Ferster v Ferster* [2016] EWCA Civ 717 (an email sent via the mediator containing a blackmail threat was admitted under the "unambiguous impropriety" exception). He concludes there would be no confidentiality obstacle to art 5 in the UK.
- Courts may protect external confidentiality by excluding the public; mediators abroad may refuse to testify.

### 6. Party autonomy: limiting the defences (Köhler)
- There is no CISG art 6-style general power to derogate from provisions. But: parties can choose the law for the mediation agreement and the settlement (reducing uncertainty about standards); restrict enforcement to particular states (respected under art 5(1)(d)); define what counts as a serious breach (the court must take this into account, though it cannot change the Convention meaning); and include a statement that their decision to settle was not affected by the mediator's conduct (raising the evidential burden).
- **Waiver**: an advance waiver in the mediation agreement is far-reaching and doubtful, since the breach is not yet known; a waiver in the settlement should not cover breaches the party was unaware of. Where the party already knew of the breach, a waiver should in principle be possible, and causation would anyway be lacking.
- Confidentiality clauses may limit a party's right to submit evidence, but the court is bound by them only if the forum's procedural law says so.

## Key authorities
- Singapore Convention art 5(1)(e), (f); art 3(1) (rules of procedure); art 5(1)(d).
- Arbitration Model Law art 12(2) (the "justifiable doubts" source); New York Convention art V.
- *Lehrer v Zwernemann* 14 SW 3d 775 (Tex 2000): constructive knowledge defeats non-disclosure claim.
- *Olam v Congress Mortgage Co* 68 F Supp 2d 1110 (ND Cal 1999): mediator testimony; settlement upheld despite distress allegations.
- *BOM v BOK* [2019] 1 SLR 349 (cited by Chong and Steffek, via Köhler).
- *Farm Assist v DEFRA (No 2)* [2007] EWHC 2870 (TCC); *Ferster v Ferster* [2016] EWCA Civ 717: English exceptions to mediation privilege.
- *Cassel v Superior Court* 119 Cal Rptr 3d 437 (2009): strict California confidentiality.
- Empirical: Coben's US data (2013–17) show mediator misconduct rarely raised and never successful as a defence to enforcement.

## Debate and critique
- **For the grounds (Köhler)**: Enforcement of mediated settlements is "a political choice that requires a reasoned justification". That justification is the added legitimacy a neutral third party brings compared with unmediated settlements. Where the mediator was secretly conflicted or seriously breached basic procedural standards, the justification disappears, so refusing relief protects the Convention's legitimacy. He rejects two criticisms: (1) parties can walk away at any time (this undersells a skilled mediator's influence); (2) mediators do not adjudicate (expedited enforcement rests on procedural legitimacy, not only party autonomy). The disclosure duty merely codifies existing international practice. Against the backlash against arbitration, avoiding any appearance of bias matters.
- **Against or sceptical**: Anderson, Beaumont and Verbist; Takahashi (who suggested states omit the grounds, and argues for opt-in); Kallipetis initially critical (a panellist's view that the provisions are "redolent of arbitration"). Concerns: ill-suited to a voluntary process; risk of ancillary disputes; a "quagmire" (Maia, Mason and Masucci); settlor's remorse.
- **Middle view (Kallipetis, Feehily)**: the careful wording (serious, justifiable, material, causation, burden) makes the grounds hard to use. Feehily: "largely symbolic" if US experience is indicative, especially with represented commercial parties. Kallipetis: lawyers' role in ensuring informed, genuine consent becomes even more important.
- **Design flaws (Köhler)**: no choice-of-law rule for applicable standards (he would have borrowed the seat concept); silence on confidentiality; over-qualified art 5(1)(f).
- **Professionalisation link**: the grounds give mediator standards international visibility and may push towards uniform codes (see the mediators note), but uniformity is difficult given cultural and stylistic diversity.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the alleged misconduct** and choose the right ground: conduct during the process is (e); undisclosed relationship or interest is (f). Köhler: keep other misconduct out of (f).
2. **For (e)**: What standards applied (chosen law, institutional rules, code named in the mediation agreement, mandatory domestic law)? Is the breach serious, judged objectively and narrowly (e.g. a caucus confidentiality breach, misrepresenting facts, threats)? Did it cause the party to enter this settlement? Consider legal representation and advice, and timing (after agreement, no causation).
3. **For (f)**: Was there non-disclosure? Would the circumstances raise justifiable doubts objectively? Did the party already know (*Lehrer*)? Material impact or undue influence plus causation: would a reasonable party have refused to settle?
4. **Evidence**: what are the forum's privilege rules? Can the mediator be compelled (*Farm Assist*)? Does an exception apply (*Ferster*)? Do confidentiality clauses bind the court?
5. **Party drafting**: choice of law; jurisdiction restrictions; definition of serious breach; recital of unaffected consent; waivers (validity depends on knowledge).
6. **Outcome**: the court has discretion even if a ground is made out (permissive). Also consider whether public policy (art 5(2)(a)) would apply to egregious cases anyway.

Traps: assuming the Convention sets its own mediator code; forgetting the causation and burden requirements; ignoring confidentiality and privilege; treating art 5(1)(f) as dependent on domestic disclosure rules.
