# Process Controversies – Fairness, Public Interest, Definition and the Symbiotic Justice System

## Overview
Chapter 10 (the final taught topic) deals with the main critiques of mediation, which makes it the most essay-friendly topic. The private, informal nature that makes mediation attractive also removes due process safeguards. Critics say settlements lack the "legitimacy" of judgments, can reproduce power imbalances, deprive the law of precedent, and shift justice into private hands. Feehily groups the controversies into **substantive fairness**, **procedural fairness** and **public interest concerns**. He argues that much of the criticism comes from **definitional confusion** (many processes are wrongly called mediation), and he concludes that mediation and adjudication are **symbiotic** parts of an integrated justice system. The rule-of-law frame is Lord Bingham's: all persons and authorities "should be bound by and entitled to the benefit of laws publicly made... and publicly administered in the courts" ("The Rule of Law" (2007) 66 CLJ 67, 69).

## The law (the arguments)

### 1. Substantive fairness: power imbalance
**The critique**
- Differences in resources can produce real or perceived power disparities. With imbalance, mediation may produce substantively unfair outcomes: weaker parties pressured into offers, so that mediation **reflects and legitimises existing power structures** (Cappelletti). Some speak of "**second-class justice**", though Feehily says that literature mostly concerns low-value, mass-processed individual claims and is not directly relevant to commercial mediation.
- Owen Fiss, "Against Settlement" (1984) 93 Yale LJ 1073: settlement "is a capitulation to the conditions of mass society and should be neither encouraged nor praised".
- Parties may feel pressure even when they entered voluntarily. The hardest problem is a wide gap in means of influence. Mediators, bound to neutrality and impartiality, cannot advocate for the weaker party but must help the parties reach agreement.

**The responses (Feehily)**
- Power is **dynamic and fluid**, changing with the issue; **contextual and situational**; **relational rather than possessory**. Perceptions of power matter as much as reality.
- Powerful parties may be unable or unwilling to use their power (ongoing relationship, reputation, confidentiality). Apparently weak parties may have other sources of power.
- A party seen as weak may make an informed choice to mediate, knowing no process removes all power differences.
- **Voluntariness** is the characteristic principle: whether to settle rests on each party's private autonomy. Mediation is the "**procedural counterpart of the contract**". Voluntariness brings an expectation of an outcome that benefits both, or at least leaves neither worse off. Parties choose the mediator; experts join only with consent; a mediator whose neutrality is questioned may act if the parties want; the parties decide whether and on what terms to settle.
- **Initial vs continued engagement**: even if entry is compelled, continued participation is voluntary. Parties can leave at any stage, no one is forced to compromise, and no adverse consequences follow non-agreement. (Compare the front-end/back-end consent distinction in ch 7.)
- Most commercial mediations involve lawyers. Where they do not, a party can make the settlement subject to its lawyer's approval.
- The same power issues arise in unmediated negotiation. Mediation is better, because the mediator can **perceive, understand and respond** to imbalance (e.g. suggesting independent advice).

### 2. Procedural fairness
**The critique**
- Mediation lacks process safeguards: it can be used **tactically** to delay or to gather information; statements cannot be tested and information may be concealed; there is no interlocutory relief or compulsory disclosure or attendance; no guaranteed preparation time; no formal equality to present and rebut evidence.
- Natural justice obligations of judges and arbitrators do not bind mediators, who may meet parties separately and spend more time with one side.
- Confidentiality means mediator behaviour is not systematically reviewed: no official record, no accountability, no procedural safeguards or judicial review.
- Courts in some jurisdictions can order parties into **private** mediation run by private providers, which impedes public scrutiny and raises costs, with a privately chosen and paid mediator replacing a publicly funded judge.

**The responses**
- Mediation rests on an **alternative set of values**: needs and interests rather than rights; informality rather than formalism; future-focused direct participation rather than fact-finding and liability.
- It offers "**co-existential justice**" or "**mending justice**" (Cappelletti: for two centuries Western societies "glorified the ideal of fighting for one's rights", missing that co-existential justice might better assure access to justice). It is directly accessible and responsive to interests such as preserving relationships.
- It does not promise substantive justice by court rules, but its **procedural justice** benefits can make settlements more acceptable, legitimate and qualitatively better even if they are less than a court might have given.
- **Checks**: mediators can terminate if the process is abused; parties have advisers; cooling-off clauses; mediation operates in the **shadow of the law**, so settlements produced by duress or unconscionability can be set aside. Remaining concerns reflect risks present in any negotiation, which commercial parties take into account.
- **Procedural justice psychology** (Tyler; Hollander-Blumoff and Tyler, "Procedural Justice and the Rule of Law" (2011)): procedural justice means parties' subjective assessment of fairness. Empirical evidence shows it drives **satisfaction** with outcomes and is an independent driver of satisfaction. Parties are more likely to **comply** with outcomes they think were reached fairly. Their sense of justice is tied more to the process and to resolving underlying interests than to the terms.
- So when parties see mediation as legitimate, it is consistent with the **objectives** of the rule of law, if not its specific elements. Parties still want rule-of-law values. Voice, trust and respect compensate for losing a neutral decision-maker. As a party-empowering process, mediation can appear consistent with the rule of law even when it produces extra-legal solutions.

### 3. Public interest concerns
**The critique**
- Courts are "**trustees**" of the public interest: they vindicate rights, ensure accountability, develop the law, articulate standards and create **precedent**, a public good providing consistency and certainty. Some matters (authoritative public-interest decisions, social policy, allocation of public resources) should not be mediated.
- Effects on the profession: fewer trials mean less experience for young lawyers; clients are represented by litigators who fear trial; a shrinking framework for measuring settlements; mediators lack experience for risk assessment; advocacy quality declines.
- Judicial neutrality may be compromised by promoting settlement; the settlement movement may erode public values in adjudication; court-mandated ADR has reshaped how judges see their role. Trial has become "a residual solution of last resort, with judges... ceremonial figures who legitimate other people's decisions".
- Mulcahy ("The Collective Interest in Private Dispute Resolution" (2012)) and Genn (*Judging Civil Justice* (2010)) are the main sceptical voices cited.

**The responses**
- Mediation would only undermine precedent in **indeterminate** disputes with uncertain outcomes, not where precedent is clear. The relationship is **complementary**: courts set precedent; mediation resolves cases where the parties do not need one.
- Mediation reduces cost and delay and frees court time, so cases that need adjudication get better access. Mediation may **enhance** precedent by freeing judicial resources for important cases (both for the courts and for the "shadow of the law" mediation depends on). **Fewer cases need not mean fewer precedents.**
- More mediation need not mean fewer trials: court-sanctioned mediation may attract cases that would have settled anyway.
- **Empirical evidence**:
  - US federal civil trial rate fell from **11.5% in 1962 to 1.8% in 2002**, while filings rose fivefold. So federal courts tried fewer cases in 2002 than in 1962 (ABA Litigation Section, *The Vanishing Trial*; Galanter). Causes identified include the technical complexity and expense of litigation, media-driven misperceptions making defendants risk-averse, "**managerial judging**" (Resnik), and increased ADR.
  - There are no studies of mediation's own impact on adjudication rates. The **Resolution Systems Institute** (Chicago) summarised **62 studies** of over **100 court mediation programmes**: findings were mixed, but in most studies **mediation had no impact on the trial rate**. Other benefits included higher settlement rates, satisfaction, perceived fairness, cost savings, speed, better relationships and higher compliance.
  - **2011 survey** of Fortune 1,000 corporate counsel (Cornell Scheinman Institute, Pepperdine Straus Institute, International Institute for Conflict Prevention and Resolution; Stipanowich and Lamare): mediation's growth has reduced the use of **arbitration**.

### 4. Definitional issues
- Much criticism arises because **many different processes are called "mediation"**: users with different backgrounds, skills and purposes use the term differently.
- US experience: "mediation" has often looked like **non-binding arbitration**, where a third party hears evidence and gives an advisory opinion without discussion.
- Court-connected mediation in the US can be less voice-driven and self-determining, resembling litigation with the mediator steering the outcome. Empirical studies (Welsh, among others) found parties were largely not involved, rarely participated, and mediators rarely encouraged integrative negotiation. It resembled a **privatised judicial settlement conference**.
- Feehily: this is a **varied and misrepresentative use of the term**; the criticisms really concern other forms of ADR, not mediation properly practised. **Mediator neutrality and party consensuality** are cornerstones that legitimise the profession. Some legal systems have not grasped the theory, hence the many inconsistent court approaches and standards.
- When mediation is understood as the CEDR-type process (a mediator actively assists parties towards a negotiated agreement, with the parties in ultimate control of the decision to settle and the terms), "much of the above criticisms are moot".

### 5. The symbiotic character of the modern justice system
- Civil litigation is a funnel: many claimants at entry, few judgments. Most parties neither expect nor want trial or appeal. Many trials raise little law. **Precedent-setting cases are a very small proportion** of justiciable disputes.
- The debate should move from the relative merits of adjudication, mediation and settlement to **how the common law needs to be developed**.
- Many settlements already happen under time, cost or lawyer pressure without public scrutiny. Fairness and public-interest concerns can look academic where settlement is the dominant way disputes end worldwide.
- The claimed negative effect of mediation on trial rates and precedent is **not factually substantiated**. The bigger threats to access to justice are **high legal costs and court fees** and, in some places, delay. Settlement was inevitable long before mediation became widespread.
- A civil justice system should ensure mediation is **considered at appropriate intervals** to save expense and court resources: hence CPR costs sanctions in England, mandatory pre-trial mediation in the US and Australia, and EU developments stimulated by the Mediation Directive.
- **Justice according to law** (transparent rules, an independent judiciary, equal and impartial application, final and enforceable outcomes) is idealised. It overlooks ambiguous rules, discretion, conflicting norms, procedural hurdles, the human dimension, and the assumption that people can afford, understand and feel comfortable with it. So **equality before the law is "more a façade than a reality"**, which is why law is supplemented by mediation.
- Avoid extremes: neither the "**adjudication romanticist**" nor the mediation "**zealot**". Mediation and adjudication are integrated parts of the system, each with a distinct, complementary and essential function. Some disputes need adjudication (clarifying and developing law, precedent); others need mediation (e.g. complex multi-jurisdictional commercial disputes needing flexible remedies the bilateral adversarial process cannot give). They are **interdependent**: judges promote mediation in appropriate cases, and mediation supports the justice system.
- The rule of law now legitimises a different process as courts sponsor settlement. Lord Woolf, the architect of the CPR, said case management aims "to encourage settlement of disputes at the earliest appropriate stage" (*Access to Justice: Interim Report* (1995)), meaning justice may be achieved through settlement.
- For procedural justice, set expectations at the outset about the **type** of mediation. Mediated settlements are not products of the rule of law but can **co-exist harmoniously** with it if the process is procedurally just: parties have a voice, are treated with respect and believe the process is fair.
- Future judicial focus: admission of mediation evidence, the contours of mediator liability, enforcement of agreements to mediate and settlement agreements. "**Justice cannot be equated with one system.**"

## Debate and critique
This topic is itself the debate. Ways to structure an essay:
- **Thesis options**: (a) Feehily's symbiosis thesis: mediation complements adjudication, and the critiques are overstated or misdirected at pseudo-mediation; (b) the sceptical thesis (Fiss, Genn, Mulcahy): settlement erodes public adjudication, power distorts outcomes, and courts should not push parties out; (c) a middle position: support mediation but insist on safeguards (voluntary continued participation, legal advice, mediator standards, narrow good-faith rules, enforcement defences).
- **Link to compulsion**: if courts can now order ADR (*Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil CBC*), procedural-fairness and public-interest critiques become more pressing. Feehily's answer is the distinction between compelled entry and voluntary continuation and outcome.
- **Link to the Singapore Convention**: elevating mediated settlements internationally heightens fairness concerns. The art 5 defences (vitiating factors, mediator misconduct, public policy) and the consumer, family and employment exclusions are the system's safeguards.
- **Genn's line**: "The outcome of mediation is not about just settlement, it is just about settlement" (quoted in ch 7) versus Woolf's view that justice can be achieved through settlement.
- **Empirical rebuttal**: vanishing trials have many causes; mediation programmes mostly do not affect trial rates. The real access-to-justice problem is cost.
- **Definitional discipline**: critics and supporters may be talking about different processes. Precise definition (EU Directive, Singapore Convention, CEDR) is essential to evaluating critiques.

## Applying it to a problem question
- Essay question ("Mediation privatises justice and undermines the rule of law. Discuss."):
  1. Define the rule of law (Bingham) and mediation (CEDR; Singapore Convention art 2(3)).
  2. Set out the substantive fairness critique (Fiss, Cappelletti) and the responses (fluid power, voluntariness, lawyers, mediator responses).
  3. Set out the procedural fairness critique and the procedural justice evidence (Tyler).
  4. Set out the public interest and precedent critique and the empirical evidence (vanishing trial; RSI 62 studies; Fortune 1,000).
  5. Address definitional confusion (court-connected programmes resembling settlement conferences).
  6. Conclude on symbiosis or another view, with reference to recent compulsion developments and enforcement safeguards.
- Problem question with a weaker party: identify imbalance indicators (no lawyer, resource disparity, time pressure); mediator responses (suggest advice, break, cooling-off, termination); later remedies (duress, unconscionability, undue influence; Singapore Convention art 5(1)(b), (e)).
