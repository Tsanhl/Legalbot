# Approaches to Mediation – Models, Process and Mediated Solutions

## Overview
Chapter 4 explains **how** mediation works. It covers the four models (settlement, facilitative, transformative, evaluative) and the facilitative/evaluative debate; Riskin's influential "grid"; the stages and phases of a mediation, including the timing problems of when a mediation starts and ends; how negotiation theory (interests, BATNA/WATNA, value creation) operates; what makes mediated solutions different from judgments; and mediation's use beyond disputes, for conflict avoidance and management. This is the practical core needed to judge later legal questions: whether a mediator's evaluation risks liability or the practice of law, what informed consent requires, and whether a settlement was reached during the mediation.

## The law

### 1. The four models
Four "models" have been identified to conceptualise tendencies in practice (Boulle; Kovach):
1. **Settlement**: the aim is a compromise.
2. **Facilitative**: promote negotiation in terms of underlying **needs and interests** rather than legal rights and obligations.
3. **Transformative (or therapeutic)**: underlying causes of behaviour may be considered; the process may change how parties respond to conflict (Bush and Folger, *The Promise of Mediation*).
4. **Evaluative**: legal rights and entitlements and the likely range of court outcomes guide the settlement. Also called "directive".

These are **not discrete forms** but ways of describing tendencies. A mediation may start in one mode and shift (e.g. facilitative, then evaluative).

### 2. Facilitative vs evaluative
- These are the most prominent differences in style and the most relevant to commercial mediation. They are also described as **process-oriented** (facilitative: the parties produce the solution) and **substance-oriented** (evaluative: the mediator assesses the case from experience and makes recommendations).
- **For facilitative as "true" mediation**: the philosophy of mediation requires an empowering process; evaluation may breach impartiality ("you can't facilitate when you evaluate"); the mediator's task is to facilitate the parties' own evaluation and decision-making. Lela Love, "The Top Ten Reasons Why Mediators Should Not Evaluate" (1997); Kovach and Love, "Mapping Mediation: The Risks of Riskin's Grid" (1998): evaluative mediation is an **oxymoron**, and the grid unfortunately legitimised it.
- **For evaluation**: some evaluation is unavoidable. The debate led to more facilitative training and more joint sessions, with less time in caucus.
- **Feehily's view**: successful mediations often come from a **"mixed process"**. A purely facilitative approach is unlikely to exist in practice. Mediators evaluate on many levels and react to opportunities, and many successful mediators are not consistently one or the other. Strategy depends on the mediator's assessment of the participants and the dispute, and the mediator should fashion the process to suit them. Evaluation should **not be a first resort**: begin facilitatively and evaluate where helpful and necessary. **The parties must give informed consent** to the approach and interventions. The test is whether the mediator and style help the parties settle. Mediators' personal qualities also have significant influence.

### 3. Riskin's grid (Riskin, "Understanding Mediators' Orientations, Strategies, and Techniques: A Grid for the Perplexed" (1996) 1 Harv Negot L Rev 7)
Two continua:
- **Problem definition (horizontal: narrow to broad)**, illustrated with the "Computec" hypothetical (a disputed $30,000 expenses bill between a bank and its IT contractor in a 10-year contract):
  - **Level I – litigation issues**: settle on terms approximating the likely trial result; focus on each side's strengths and weaknesses "in the shadow of the law".
  - **Level II – business interests**: issues a court would not reach (fee structure, quality of performance, the ongoing relationship, profits, reputation).
  - **Level III – personal, professional and relational issues**.
  - **Level IV – community interests**: interests of communities or entities not party to the dispute.
- **Role of the mediator (vertical: facilitative to evaluative)**: strategies and techniques that facilitate the parties' negotiation versus those that evaluate.
- **Four quadrants**: evaluative-narrow, facilitative-narrow, evaluative-broad, facilitative-broad.
  - **Evaluative-narrow**: helps parties understand strengths, weaknesses and likely outcomes, but stresses the mediator's own education. The mediator studies documents, hears positions in joint session, then caucuses using techniques (from least to most evaluative): assess strengths and weaknesses; predict court outcomes; propose position-based compromises (from a gentle figure to "I think $12,000 would be a good offer"); urge or push the parties to accept, including "head-banging".
  - **Facilitative-narrow**: the same educational goal but without the mediator's own assessments, predictions or proposals, and without pressure; the burden of decision stays with the parties.
  - **Broad** mediators emphasise underlying interests over positions and look for needs not shown in documents.
- Riskin himself favoured the facilitative-broad approach. The grid is descriptive: mediators move along the continua and among quadrants. Kressel and Pruitt and Silbey and Merry (bargaining vs therapeutic styles) are discussed as comparisons. The grid is meant to help parties **select mediators**.

### 4. The mediation process
**Pre-mediation practicalities**
- Agreement to mediate and confidentiality agreements; pre-mediation meeting(s) (practicalities, fees, attendees, documents); confidentiality agreements for non-parties before the session starts.
- UNCITRAL Notes on Mediation (2021) list matters to consider: commencement; selection and appointment; preparatory steps; conduct; settlement agreement; termination.

**Temporal contours (when does mediation begin and end?)**
- This matters because: confidentiality is likely to cover only that period; limitation periods may be suspended; the clause may set a time frame; there are cost implications; and it can matter whether an offer was made during or after the mediation.
- *Brown v Rice* [2007] EWHC 625 (Ch): an offer left open after the mediation day was still an offer made in the mediation. So an oral acceptance did not create a binding agreement when one party reneged before the agreement was written and signed, as the agreement to mediate required.
- Possible start points: appointment of the mediator; pre-mediation meeting; signing the agreement to mediate; the substantive session. **Mediation Model Law art 5(1)**: proceedings "commence on the day on which the parties to that dispute agree to engage in mediation proceedings" (what counts as agreement is left to other law, so practice varies). Record start and end in the agreement to mediate or correspondence.
- Practice tip: leave final offers open for, say, 28 days with a possible further session (Kallipetis and Ruttle).

**Four stages** (may not be sequential; not every case uses all; complex cases may be at different stages for different issues):
1. **Opening**: introductions, mediator's and parties' opening statements, usually in plenary.
2. **Exploration/information**: joint and/or caucus; "reality-testing"; probing underlying issues; generating options.
3. **Negotiation/bargaining**: the mediator acts as broker, devising strategies to break deadlocks.
4. **Settlement/closing**: drafting the settlement agreement.

**Three phases** (overlapping; emphasis shaped by culture):
1. **Opening channels of communication**: critical for integrative solutions; distinguishing **needs and interests from positions**; may need formal structure or initially indirect communication through the mediator.
2. **Building bridges of understanding**: **trust** should run through all phases (commentators list "two levels, two dilemmas, two elements to assist").
3. **Facilitating informed negotiations**: interdependence of goals (**distributive vs integrative**); a single issue means only "claiming value", while multiple issues allow "creating and claiming value" (Lax and Sebenius). Mutual exploration can uncover gains for all. The mediator helps parties pursue self-interest by developing options **better than the no-agreement alternative**, testing each side's case against its **BATNA** (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) and **WATNA** (worst alternative), with full risk and cost analysis including irrecoverable costs. The **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement) lies between the parties' BATNAs. Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*: interest-based negotiation. Parties do best with "healthy, active self-interest" in a collaborative, problem-solving relationship. **"Win-win"** refers to the **fact** of settlement (both prefer it to the alternative), not necessarily to the **terms**, which usually involve compromise.

### 5. Mediated solutions
- A trial or arbitration ends with a judgment or award: enforcement of established rights, based on past events, with a limited range of remedies (mostly damages).
- In mediation the only limits are the parties' **imagination and resourcefulness**. Parties can decide their future and emerge "stronger – personally and contractually".
- Mediated solutions can reflect needs and interests better than a win/lose outcome. They are negotiated "in the shadow of the law" (case law is the frame of reference). They can deal with human and emotional concerns. Parties may settle for more than they would get if they lost and less than if they won, and mediation can give a sense of equality. There is a "fundamental symbiosis" with the civil justice system.
- Undisclosed motives to settle: reputation, publicity, limited means, risk and litigation aversion, loss of face, commercial realities, unrelated matters (e.g. a planned merger).
- Mediation lets parties exchange information and views, reappraise risk and test their case privately, without anyone being forced to settle and without endangering their case if they go on to court (the manufacturer and technical expert example: a new type of manufacturing plant).
- Fuller ("Mediation: Its Form and Functions" (1971)): reorienting the parties towards each other is the central quality of mediation. There is scope for a transformative dimension even in commercial mediation.

### 6. Conflict avoidance, management and resolution
- Mediation is not limited to disputes. There are three closely related, sequential approaches:
  - **Contractual negotiation stage – conflict avoidance**: "transactional mediation", "deal mediation" or "assisted deal-making" for complex, non-contentious transactions. The mediator manages the process to help create a mutually favourable contract, often at the start of a relationship, anticipating differences. It is preventative or pre-emptive.
  - **Contractual performance stage – conflict management**: "project mediation" or "strategic mediation" for problems in long-term or multi-party contracts (e.g. contractors and subcontractors on large construction projects) so the project is not delayed.
  - **Conflict resolution**: dispute mediation.

## Debate and critique
- **Evaluative mediation**: oxymoron (Love, Kovach) or inevitable (Feehily's "mixed process")? Risks of evaluation: loss of impartiality and party self-determination; drift towards the "practice of law" and liability (see mediator-liability note); in court-connected US programmes, mediation began to resemble judicial settlement conferences (process-controversies note). Benefits: reality-testing for represented commercial parties who want it; culturally expected in some traditions (Chinese and Arab "semi-arbitration").
- **Informed consent** to style is Feehily's answer: if parties know and accept the approach, much of the debate is defused. It also matters for "front-end" consent in the compulsion debate (ch 7).
- **Riskin's grid**: useful for choosing mediators and describing practice; critics say it legitimises evaluation and oversimplifies. Riskin later revised his terminology ("Retiring and Replacing the Grid", cited as "directive").
- **Win-win rhetoric**: Feehily clarifies that the "win" lies in the fact of settlement compared with alternatives, not in equal terms. This counters the critique that mediation simply produces compromise.
- **Breadth of problem definition**: broad mediations create value but take time and may strain confidentiality and the scope of authority; narrow ones are efficient but may miss interests.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **What model or style is being used?** Did the mediator give opinions on the merits or propose figures? Did the parties consent to that? (Relevant to complaints about mediator conduct, impartiality and the Singapore Convention art 5(1)(e).)
2. **Process timing**: when did the mediation start and end? Was an offer made "in" the mediation (*Brown v Rice*)? Is confidentiality engaged? Has limitation been suspended (Model Law art 5 footnote; EU Directive art 8)?
3. **Was there a settlement?** Check the writing and signature requirement and the stage reached (closing stage drafting).
4. **Negotiation analysis**: identify interests vs positions, the BATNA/WATNA of each party, and the possibility of creating value (non-monetary terms, future business, apologies).
5. **Non-dispute contexts**: should deal or project mediation be recommended for a long-term contract?
