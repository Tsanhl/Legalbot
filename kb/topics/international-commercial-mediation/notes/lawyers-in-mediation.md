# Lawyers and Other Professional Mediation Supporters

## Overview
Chapter 6 / the materials slides (taught with Chapter 5) focuses on the people who advise parties in mediation. Lawyers decide whether clients ever hear about mediation, and how they behave in it shapes whether it works. The chapter covers: the **role transition** from adversarial advocate to adviser and counsellor; lawyers' **duties** before, during and after mediation (including the *Halsey* criteria they must weigh); **ethics** (ABA Model Rules, Law Council of Australia guidelines); **legal liability** (failing to advise on mediation; negligence in settlement); the overlap and conflict between the legal and mediation professions; lawyers as **gatekeepers**; **other professionals** (experts, interpreters, tax advisers); and a jurisprudential discussion of mediation's link to **justice** ("the umbilical link") and "professional transcendence". the materials opens with the "solitary lawyerly approach" versus "reimagining the approach" (quoting Henry Ford: customers would have asked for "a faster horse").

## The law

### 1. The lawyer's role transition
- Disputes start in a contentious atmosphere, and commercial parties tend to hire adversarial lawyers. Over time, costs (legal fees, emotional and commercial costs, management time) build up and attitudes may soften. Parties are often unprepared, and most settle.
- Lawyers can move from **legal advocate and representative** to **legal adviser and counsellor**, advising on a voluntary settlement that meets needs and interests and defines future behaviour, through a confidential, collaborative problem-solving process.
- Ideally, advice on mediation should be given at the **contract-drafting** stage and at every later stage where mediation could help. Once mediation is chosen, the lawyer helps as much as needed: preparation, opening statements, plenary and caucus sessions, drafting.
- **Different audience**: in mediation the main audience is the **other party**, not a judge or arbitrator, so representation must change.
- **Difficulty**: lawyers' core values and methods match those of courts: linear thinking, rules, ethical codes, adversarial orientation, predictable procedures, compartmentalised legal issues. Legal education stresses issue-spotting and "scientific" application of law to facts, ignoring culture, politics and values, and implying emotions have no place in law. Rules of evidence make the lawyer the sole voice of the client. Ethics codes were designed for adjudication, so many lawyers emphasise advocacy over advice. This mismatch can fundamentally affect mediation practice.

### 2. The lawyer's duties
**(a) Criteria for advising on mediation (the *Halsey* factors)**
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 gives the non-exhaustive list a lawyer must weigh: (1) nature of the dispute and suitability; (2) merits and whether a belief that the case is watertight is reasonable; (3) other settlement methods tried; (4) whether mediation costs would be disproportionate; (5) whether delay would be prejudicial; (6) reasonable prospect of success.
- *Halsey* produced a large body of case law (see conduct-costs-and-compulsion.md). It gives English lawyers objective criteria, but if refusal is based on the wrong criteria, or a court weighs them differently, **costs consequences** follow.
- *Brownlee v Brownlee* (South African High Court, 2009): the court went further and sanctioned the lawyers who "positively rejected the use of the [mediation] process. For this they are to blame and they must, I believe shoulder the responsibility that comes from failing properly to serve the interests of their clients".

**(b) Preparing for mediation**
- Explain the nature of mediation, how it will work and the client's role.
- Consider legal, practical and commercial aspects of the dispute in developing solutions.
- Help the client identify interests, carry out a **risk analysis**, link risks to interests, and develop strategies and settlement options.
- **Pre-mediation meeting** (usually lawyers and mediator): establish the relationship and practicalities (date, venue, fees, attendees, agreement to mediate including confidentiality, documents). A second pre-mediation meeting may involve the parties and the mediator.

**(c) Legal advisers at the mediation**
- The level of involvement depends on the size and nature of the dispute and the parties' resources. It is **client-led**: be as involved as the client wants.
- Lawyers are present as **legal advisers, not legal representatives**. Failing to see the difference can directly obstruct the process.
- Threefold role: (1) advise and help the client on strategy, risk and options; (2) discuss legal, evidential and practical matters with all parties; (3) **prepare the mediated settlement agreement and ensure it is enforceable**.

**(d) Post-mediation**
- Advice may be needed if the settlement contains an error needing rectification, or a party breaches and enforcement is needed.
- If there is no settlement or only a partial one, the lawyer returns to the role of advocate, advising on litigation or arbitration.
- Lawyers may be involved where a party claims the settlement should be set aside for fraud, undue influence, unconscionability, duress, incapacity, lack of authority or illegality (see mediated-settlement-agreements-enforcement.md).

**(e) Ethical considerations**
- General professional duties apply: conflicts of interest and legal professional privilege; no use of mediation for **ulterior motives** (e.g. information gathering or delay); confidentiality, competence and other fiduciary duties.
- Lawyers must advise and educate clients during mediation and about settlement options and their implications.
- Lawyers usually have **ostensible (possibly implied) authority** to bind clients to a settlement, but some have been held liable for exceeding express instructions. Breach of ethics can also lead to professional sanctions.
- **Law Council of Australia Guidelines**: a general obligation that lawyers and clients act in **good faith** to try to settle; lawyers should explain what good faith means; suspected bad faith by others may be raised privately with the mediator.
- **ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct**: the Preamble describes the negotiator's duty to seek a result advantageous to the client consistent with honest dealing. **Rule 1.2**: the lawyer must abide by the client's decision whether to settle (self-determination). **Rule 1.4**: explain matters so the client can make informed decisions. **Rule 2.1**: candid advice, which may include moral, economic, social and political factors. **Rule 4.1**: no knowingly false statement of material fact or law to a third person, and no failure to disclose a material fact where disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a client's crime or fraud. "Puffs" (obvious exaggeration) are not statements of fact. ABA Formal Opinion 06-439 (2006): the same truthfulness standard applies in caucused mediation as in direct negotiation; withholding information from the mediator rarely breaches r 4.1 but in extreme cases may breach the duty of competence (r 1.1).

### 3. Legal liability of lawyers in mediation
**(a) Failing to advise on mediation**
- It is conceivable that lawyers could be sued in negligence for failing to advise on mediation, and public policy is moving that way. NSW's "reasonable prospects" rule (Legal Profession Act 2004 (NSW) s 349) bars solicitors from acting unless the claim or defence has reasonable prospects. *Caboolture Park Shopping Centre Pty Ltd v White Industries (Qld) Pty Ltd* (1994) 45 FCR 224: a firm could be ordered to pay the other side's costs for advising litigation as a delaying tactic.
- In **England**, failing to advise on or explore mediation could amount to **professional negligence** (*Halsey* [11]; *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 [43]: the profession "can no longer with impunity shrug aside reasonable requests to mediate"). Liability might follow where mediation was in the client's interests, or failure to mediate caused a worse outcome, irrecoverable costs or a costs sanction.
- **US**: Colorado (bar association), Arkansas (statute), and Ohio, New Jersey and Massachusetts (court rules) require lawyers to advise clients about ADR, and many federal and state courts have similar rules. Ireland: Mediation Act 2017 ss 14–15 (statutory duty and declaration; see duty-to-advise-and-agreements-to-mediate.md).
- **Self-protection**: keep evidence of clear advice on mediation, other ADR and costs, and of the client's instructions.

**(b) Negligence in a settlement context**
- Possible bases: insufficient advice on the merits, leading the client to accept a clearly worse outcome than reasonable; failure to get clear instructions or acting beyond authority with a clearly worse result; inadequate advice on proposed terms or enforcement.
- *Secombs v Sadler Design Pty Ltd* [1999] VSC 79: after the clients left the mediation, the lawyers signed settlement terms without their consent and did not tell them or explain the consequences of non-compliance. The solicitors were negligent in not communicating with their clients on the day or the next business day.
- *Studer v Boettcher* [2000] NSWCA 263 (FitzGerald JA): claims that mediation was oppressive and coercive failed. Advice to compromise is not negligent just because litigation might have produced more, given litigation's unpredictability and financial and personal costs. (Related: *Studer v Konig* (NSW SC 1993); *Studer v Boettcher* [1998] NSWSC 524; *Abriel v Australian Guarantee Corp* [1999] FCA 1198.)
- Principle from the Australian cases: **settlement decisions are for clients**; lawyers ensure decisions are informed and **may persuade but not coerce**. Protect yourself by making settlement terms **subject to client approval** (also *Louis v Galbally & O'Bryan* [2008] VCAT 2186, cited).

**(c) Lawyering and mediation: conflict and confluence of professions**
- In the US and elsewhere, more mediators come from law than from any other profession. There is tension when a mediator keeps an active bar licence. In Texas some call themselves "**attorney-mediators**", which is confusing and harms both professions.
- Whether a bar treated mediating as practising law affected discipline: if it is the practice of law, rules on client confidences and reporting may clash with mediation rules. Some states say mediation is not the practice of law; others use nuanced tests.
- Where rules conflict, the specific regime for mediators is likely to prevail; where mediation law is silent, the law governing lawyers may apply.
- Each profession can learn from the other (a "symbiosis of knowledge"), e.g. in responding to difficult behaviour. But **mediation does not belong to the legal profession**: it is not reserved to any single professional group.

### 4. Lawyers as gatekeepers
- Mediator skills training and experience of mediation have turned many sceptics into proponents. Legal professional culture has changed so that non-adversarial advice is now central.
- A main challenge is ensuring enough competent lawyers who understand and are willing to engage in mediation. Education and training help: law curricula and lawyer training now include communication, negotiation and mediation skills, reflecting that the human and commercial dynamics behind disputes matter as much as the legal issues. Training also includes periods in non-contentious work before conflict work, and specialised training for lawyers and other professionals.

### 5. Other professionals
- In litigation, party-appointed experts often become "**duelling experts**", and the judge must choose. In mediation, expert reports can be shared in advance and experts used non-adversarially; **independent or neutral experts** can be consulted to build consensus.
- Other advisers (e.g. tax advisers) may attend and are bound by their own professional duties.
- **Interpreters** may be needed in international or intercultural mediation; their primary duty is to the **process**, not to one party.
- Experts and advisers should sign **confidentiality agreements** before taking part (as they are usually not parties to the agreement to mediate).

### 6. Mediation and the practitioner: the umbilical link to justice
- Mediation bridges the gap between what the legal system offers and what commercial parties need. It helps parties use personal power and autonomy to settle with their lawyers' help and the mediator's skills.
- The relationship between the mediated settlement and **justice**: favouring "equality, reciprocity or equivalence between individuals", justice as the virtue of order and exchange (equitable order and honest exchange). Equality is about the **subjects** of the exchange, not the objects: a transaction is just only if equals in power, knowledge and rights might have agreed to it. The "**golden rule of justice**" is a useful test of fairness in context. (The chapter draws on Aristotle's idea of equity as justice going beyond the letter of the law.)
- **The Lost Lawyer** (Kronman): a "spiritual crisis" in the legal profession; the collapse of the "lawyer-statesman" ideal, which put good judgment over technical competence and encouraged public spirit. Over 50 years, allegiance to professional excellence has failed, eroding lawyers' identity. Causes: a scholarly counter-ideal that downplays wisdom and character; the growth of large firms; bureaucratised courts. A multidisciplinary approach can look like a dilettante's understanding, but the lawyer's strength remains imaginative probing of specific cases.

### 7. Professional transcendence
- Law is supplemented with ADR to make justice fair and accessible. Lawyers have an added responsibility: their role goes beyond finding and mechanically applying law. The "**beauty of responsible freedom**" requires them to make law and remedies reflect parties' real needs and interests.
- Mediation has reshaped court rules, practice directions and legal processes. Experience of mediation has made some lawyers less legalistic and more client-centred, collaborative and self-reflective.
- Because most cases settle, lawyers are **dispute-resolvers rather than litigators**. Settlement practices in the US and Canada have shaped the "**new lawyer**" (Macfarlane, *The New Lawyer*): from "**rights warrior**" to dispute-resolution advocate.

## Key authorities
- *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576: factors; duty to consider ADR.
- *Burchell v Bullard* [2005] EWCA Civ 358 [43].
- *Brownlee v Brownlee* (SA HC 2009): lawyers sanctioned.
- *Secombs v Sadler Design* [1999] VSC 79; *Studer v Boettcher* [2000] NSWCA 263; *Caboolture Park v White Industries* (1994) 45 FCR 224; *Louis v Galbally & O'Bryan* [2008] VCAT 2186.
- ABA Model Rules 1.1, 1.2, 1.4, 2.1, 4.1, 8.4(c); ABA Formal Opinion 06-439; Law Council of Australia Guidelines for Lawyers in Mediations; Legal Profession Act 2004 (NSW) s 349; Irish Mediation Act 2017 ss 14–15.
- Kronman, *The Lost Lawyer*; Macfarlane, *The New Lawyer*.

## Debate and critique
- **Lawyers as obstacle or engine?** Lawyers' adversarial training and fee incentives can obstruct mediation (role conflict; treating mediation as a litigation tactic). But lawyers are also its gatekeepers and, once trained, its promoters. Costs sanctions (*Halsey*, *Burchell*, *PGF II*) and duties to advise have pushed the profession.
- **Adviser vs representative**: the distinction is central to effective mediation advocacy (the audience is the other side) and to the practice-of-law line for lawyer-mediators.
- **Good faith duty for lawyers** (Australia) versus *Walford v Miles* scepticism about good-faith negotiation in English law.
- **Truthfulness** in caucus: ABA Opinion 06-439 does not raise the standard for mediation, which may be criticised given mediation's reliance on candour.
- **Justice through settlement**: Feehily's "umbilical link" and the golden rule argue that mediated settlements can be just if made between parties with roughly equal power, knowledge and rights. Critics (Fiss, Genn) say settlement is not justice. See process-controversies.md.
- **Professional identity**: Kronman's lost-lawyer thesis versus Macfarlane's "new lawyer": mediation as a route back to judgment and public-spiritedness.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Did the lawyer advise on mediation** (at contract stage, pre-action, after an offer)? Apply the *Halsey* factors to whether refusing was reasonable. Consider costs exposure for the client and negligence exposure for the lawyer (England; US state rules; Irish statute; *Brownlee*).
2. **Preparation**: was there a risk and interest analysis, client education, a pre-mediation meeting?
3. **Conduct at mediation**: did the lawyer act as adviser rather than advocate? Was any statement a misrepresentation (r 4.1; puffery)? Was mediation used for information gathering or delay?
4. **Authority and instructions**: was the settlement within instructions? Was it subject to client approval? (*Secombs*.) Did the lawyer pressure the client (persuade, not coerce: *Studer v Boettcher*)?
5. **Drafting**: is the settlement enforceable and complete (see the MSA checklist)?
6. **Experts and interpreters**: have they signed confidentiality agreements? Is the interpreter's duty to the process?
7. **Lawyer-mediator**: were the roles kept separate? Practice-of-law concerns (ABA resolution).
