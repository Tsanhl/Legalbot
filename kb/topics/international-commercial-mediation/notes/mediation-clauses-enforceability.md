# Mediation Clauses and their Enforceability

## Overview
A mediation clause is a term in a commercial contract that obliges the parties to mediate (or to consider mediating) disputes that later arise. It may be a stand-alone clause or one tier of a stepped ("escalation") clause that moves from negotiation to mediation and then to arbitration or litigation. The legal question is whether, and how, a court will hold a party to such a promise when that party goes straight to court or arbitration. This is Chapter 3 (Feehily, *International Commercial Mediation*, CUP 2022). The theme running through the chapter: courts must balance the **certainty** that contract law demands against the **flexible uncertainty** that mediation practice needs, and policy across England, Australia and the US has moved towards enforcing clauses where possible.

In practice compliance is rarely a big issue (most parties comply), but the case law is important for drafting and for problem questions.

## The law

### 1. Starting point
- Without specific legislation, **general contract principles** decide enforceability. Courts often reason by analogy with arbitration clauses.
- The tension is between party autonomy (freedom to agree a process) and the constitutional right of access to court.
- The main issues commentary lists: severability; certainty; completeness; attempts to oust the court's jurisdiction; other policy considerations (e.g. procedural fairness); providing the required certainty; certainty of compliance; waiver; remedies for breach.

### 2. Severability
- Question: does a valid mediation clause survive if the main ("matrix") contract is terminated or is void ab initio?
- In arbitration this is well settled in England: *Harbour Assurance Co (UK) Ltd v Kansa General International Insurance Co Ltd* [1993] QB 701 (CA), now Arbitration Act 1996 s 7. The parties are presumed to want disputes resolved by the chosen process, and the dispute clause is collateral to the main contract.
- Feehily argues the principle **could be extended** to mediation clauses because they also derive authority from the parties' agreement. France and Germany appear to take this approach (citing Alexander).
- Limit: where the mediation step sits inside a **multi-tiered** clause and other parts of the clause are invalid, the whole clause is likely to fail. *New South Wales v Banabelle Electrical Pty Ltd* (2002) 54 NSWLR 503 (Einstein J): severing the offending part was inappropriate because what was left would not reflect the parties' intention (nomination procedure for an expert was uncertain, so the clause failed entirely).

### 3. Certainty: the central issue
- Contract terms are void if the parties' rights and obligations cannot be assessed, or if they leave matters to be agreed in future.
- If whether mediation happens depends on the future wishes of a party, the clause is an **"agreement to agree"** and fails.
- Traditional English hostility to agreements to negotiate: *Courtney & Fairbairn Ltd v Tolaini Bros (Hotels) Ltd* [1975] 1 WLR 297; *Paul Smith Ltd v H & S International Holding Inc* [1991] 2 Lloyd's Rep 127; *Walford v Miles* [1992] 2 AC 128 (HL).
- Feehily's key distinction: an agreement to **mediate** differs from an agreement to **agree**. The latter depends on agreeing an essential term; the former is an agreement to use a recognised process with a neutral third party.

**Australia (the leading line):**
- *Hooper Bailie Associated Ltd v Natcon Group Pty Ltd* (1992) 28 NSWLR 194 (Giles J) is the "watershed". A stay of arbitration was granted because the conciliation agreement was sufficiently certain about the conduct required. An agreement to conciliate/mediate is more than an agreement to negotiate in good faith: it is a commitment to **take part in a process**, which is different from a commitment to reach agreement. Giles J declined to follow "uncompromising" English authority. The required certainty came from a defined procedure (appointment of the conciliator, procedural matters, legal representation, information exchange); a solicitor's letter setting out the procedure gave a "clear structure".
- *Elizabeth Bay Developments Pty Ltd v Boral Building Services Pty Ltd* (1995) 36 NSWLR 709 (Giles J again): clause failed. It referred to mediation "administered by" an ADR organisation but neither set out a procedure nor clearly incorporated that body's rules, nor identified the agreement to be signed.
- *Aiton Australia Pty Ltd v Transfield Pty Ltd* [1999] NSWSC 996 (Einstein J): an agreement to mediate is enforceable if framed as a **condition precedent** to litigation/arbitration (so, like *Scott v Avery* (1856) 10 ER 1121, it does not oust the court). A good-faith requirement did not of itself make it uncertain, but the clause failed because of uncertainty over **who pays the mediator**.
- *Computershare Ltd v Perpetual Registrars Ltd (No 2)* [2000] VSC 233 (Warren J): a more liberal, ADR-friendly approach. A stay was granted even though the ADR procedure was left to be agreed when a dispute arose. Warren J said, in effect, that parties cannot logically fix in advance the principles by which mediation must produce an outcome; they have to negotiate their own solution.
- Other Australian cases cited: *Heart Research Institute Ltd v Psiron Ltd* [2002] NSWSC 646; *Con Kallergis v Calshonie* [1998] 14 BCL 201 (an agreement to negotiate may be enforceable if the process has an identifiable end).

**England:**
- *Channel Tunnel Group Ltd v Balfour Beatty Construction Ltd* [1993] AC 334 (HL) weakened the view that agreements to negotiate are unenforceable. A panel-then-arbitration clause can work if well defined and with reasonable time limits; a stay was granted.
- *Cable & Wireless plc v IBM United Kingdom Ltd* [2002] 2 All ER (Comm) 1041 (Colman J): the key post-CPR case. The clause referred disputes to an ADR procedure recommended by CEDR. The claimant argued it was just an agreement to negotiate. Held: enforceable; stay granted while the parties complied. Colman J's main points: a reference to ADR without an identifiable procedure would not necessarily fail; what matters is whether the obligation is in **unqualified and mandatory** terms; where it is, a "sufficiently certain and definable minimum duty of participation should not be hard to find"; the ADR reference is analogous to an arbitration agreement, a free-standing ancillary agreement enforceable by stay or injunction. Refusing to enforce such clauses for uncertainty would go against the public policy in the CPR.
- *Sulamérica Cia Nacional de Seguros SA v Enesa Engenharia SA* [2012] EWCA Civ 638: the mediation clause in an insurance policy did **not** create a binding obligation. Unlike *Cable & Wireless*, it set out no defined mediation process and named no provider's procedure. At most it might oblige a party to invite the other to an ad hoc mediation, and even that was too uncertain to enforce. Moore-Bick LJ declined to "define the minimum ingredients" of an enforceable clause; each case depends on its own terms.
- *Holloway v Chancery Mead Ltd* [2007] EWHC 2495 (TCC) (Ramsay J, following Einstein J in *Aiton*): three elements needed: (1) a sufficiently certain process, so no further agreement is needed before matters can proceed; (2) a defined administrative process for selecting and paying the mediator; (3) sufficiently certain details of the process (e.g. the model to be followed).
- *Halifax Financial Services Ltd v Intuitive Systems Ltd* [1999] 1 All ER 303: refused to enforce a "meet in good faith" structured-negotiation clause. Feehily calls such decisions anachronistic, pre-CPR, and likely to be decided differently now.

**USA (selected):**
- Uncertainty can be fatal: *Cumberland & York Distributors v Coors Brewing Co* (D Me 2002): mediation was a condition precedent to arbitration but had no time limit, so no stay.
- *Fluor Enterprises Inc v Solutia Inc* 147 F Supp 2d 648 (SD Tex 2001): a pre-litigation mediation requirement was satisfied simply by selecting a mediator.
- Supportive approaches: *Brosnan v Dry Cleaning Station Inc* (ND Cal 2008) (dismissal without prejudice); *Tattoo Art Inc v TAT International LLC* 711 F Supp 2d 645 (ED Va 2010) (dismissal even though mediation was requested after filing); *DeValk Lincoln Mercury Inc v Ford Motor Co* 811 F 2d 326 (7th Cir 1987) (summary judgment; letters and months of negotiation were not "mediation"); *CB Richard Ellis Inc v American Environmental Waste Management* (stay and compelled mediation); *N-Tron Corp v Rockwell Automation Inc* (SD Ala 2010) (condition precedent, but dismissal would be unfair because of limitation, so a stay was used instead).
- A certain process: *Ex parte Mountain Heating & Cooling Inc* 867 So 2d 1112 (Ala 2003) (clause mixing arbitration with "Mediation Rules" unenforceable); *Forte v Ameriplan Corp* (Tex App 2002).

### 4. Completeness
- This is closely linked to certainty and matters less in practice. A contract is void if it omits an important part of the transaction.
- *Triarno Pty Ltd v Triden Contractors Ltd* (1992) 10 BCL 305: the court could not create procedures for a binding expert determination clause that set none.
- Courts are traditionally reluctant to imply procedural terms, but growing familiarity with mediation and pro-enforcement policy may soften this. The more control the parties have over process, mediator and outcome, the less need to police validity (Steffek and others).

### 5. Ouster of the court's jurisdiction
- Parties cannot contract out of access to the courts. A clause making mediation the **exclusive** alternative to litigation would be unenforceable as contrary to public policy.
- The answer is to draft mediation as a **condition precedent** to, not a substitute for, litigation or arbitration (*Scott v Avery*).
- Linked concern about creeping compulsion: Dyson LJ in *Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust* [2004] EWCA Civ 576 [9] said forcing truly unwilling parties to mediate would breach art 6 ECHR. He later accepted that compulsory mediation does not in itself breach art 6, relying on *Alassini v Telecom Italia SpA* (Joined Cases C-317–320/08) [2010] 3 CMLR 17. See the costs note for the later position, including *Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil CBC*.

### 6. Other policy considerations
- A clause may be attacked for failing **procedural fairness**, e.g. a party cannot get essential information, or the nominated mediator has a conflict. The risk is greater with med-arb, where the same person mediates and then arbitrates.
- *Garrett v Hooters-Toledo* 295 F Supp 2d 774 (ND Ohio 2003): restrictive employment mediation terms (10-day request window, out-of-state venue) were unconscionable.
- Overall, policy favours enforcement: courts balance legal certainty against flexibility (*Computershare*).

### 7. Certainty of compliance
- There is no firm authority on what a party must do to comply. Possible criteria: attendance, disclosure, following the mediator's directions, constructive negotiation, good faith.
- *Elizabeth Bay*: Giles J saw a tension between self-interested negotiation and good faith. Feehily criticises this for ignoring the difference between unassisted negotiation and mediator-assisted interest-based bargaining. Einstein J in *Aiton*: keeping good faith is not inconsistent with self-interest (also *United Group Rail Services Ltd v Rail Corporation (NSW)* [2009] NSWCA 177).
- Bad faith (e.g. sitting silent throughout) is easier to identify than good faith. The English experience is that unreasonable parties rarely keep their unreasonableness inside the confidential mediation room, so evidence is usually available. US experience: only the **narrowest** good-faith requirements (e.g. compulsory attendance) work in practice.

### 8. Waiver and remedies
1. **Waiver**: parties may agree to bypass the clause (e.g. a precedent is needed, or the relationship has broken down). This should be documented in writing; once waived the clause cannot be enforced. In France waiver may be by clear conduct.
2. **Dismissal / injunction / summary judgment**: the US is divided between dismissal and stay; the prudent course is to seek dismissal and, in the alternative, a stay. An injunction may be available where no proceedings are pending (*Cable & Wireless*).
3. **Specific performance**: traditionally difficult, because of supervision problems and futility; equity will not order it even if the clause is valid (*Hooper Bailie*; *Banabelle*). Some US courts have granted it (*Fisher v GE Medical Systems* 276 F Supp 2d 891 (MD Tenn 2003)), but the trend is against.
4. **Damages**: hard to quantify, because no one knows whether mediation would have succeeded or on what terms. Recovery may be limited to wasted expenditure. A liquidated damages clause risks being an unenforceable penalty.
5. **Stay of proceedings**: the most effective and feasible remedy. The court enforces the clause "by default" (Einstein J in *Banabelle*). Unlike arbitration, there is usually no statutory stay for mediation, so it rests on the court's inherent powers. Factors: validity of the clause, practical effect of the order, likelihood that mediation would resolve the dispute (stays refused in *Hyslop v Liverpool Hospital* (1998) 25 IR 280 and *Allco Steel (Queensland) Pty v Torres Strait Gold Pty Ltd*).
6. **Denial of lawyers' fees**: a contractual sanction. *Frei v Davey* 124 Cal App 4th 1506 (2004): a party that did not first mediate lost its right to recover fees.

### 9. Regional and international instruments
- The **EU Mediation Directive** (Directive 2008/52/EC) and the **Singapore Convention** contain **no** provisions on enforcing mediation clauses. Nor does the US Uniform Mediation Act.
- The **UNCITRAL Mediation Model Law** does not address enforceability directly but recognises the validity of mediation undertakings in **art 14**: where parties have expressly undertaken not to start arbitral or judicial proceedings for a period or until an event, the tribunal or court must give effect to that undertaking, except so far as a party needs to preserve its rights. Starting proceedings is not in itself a waiver.
- **Art 5(2)** Model Law: if an invitation to mediate is not accepted within 30 days (or another stated period), the inviting party may treat it as rejected. Feehily warns that this could be abused to empty a mediation clause of meaning and should be revised where the Model Law is used as a template.

## Key authorities
- *Walford v Miles* [1992] 2 AC 128 (HL): lock-out/negotiation agreement case. Lord Ackner said a duty to negotiate in good faith is "inherently repugnant to the adversarial position of the parties" and "unworkable in practice"; a bare agreement to negotiate has no legal content. It is the traditional English obstacle that mediation clauses must get past.
- *Hooper Bailie v Natcon* (1992) 28 NSWLR 194: Australian watershed. A process-based agreement to conciliate is enforceable if the procedure is certain.
- *Elizabeth Bay v Boral* (1995) 36 NSWLR 709: failed for lack of procedure or incorporated rules; good-faith difficulties.
- *Aiton Australia v Transfield* [1999] NSWSC 996: condition precedent framing; failed on the mediator's costs.
- *Computershare v Perpetual Registrars (No 2)* [2000] VSC 233: liberal approach, stay granted despite open procedure.
- *Channel Tunnel v Balfour Beatty* [1993] AC 334: stay for a well-defined multi-step clause.
- *Cable & Wireless v IBM* [2002] 2 All ER (Comm) 1041: ADR clause enforceable; minimum duty of participation; stay.
- *Sulamérica v Enesa* [2012] EWCA Civ 638: clause with no defined process or provider is not binding.
- *Holloway v Chancery Mead* [2007] EWHC 2495 (TCC): three drafting elements.
- *Banabelle Electrical* (2002) 54 NSWLR 503: severance and stays.
- *Scott v Avery* (1856) 10 ER 1121: condition precedent does not oust the court.
- Mediation Model Law arts 5(2), 14; EU Mediation Directive; Singapore Convention (no clause enforcement).

## Debate and critique
- **Certainty vs flexibility.** Feehily frames the core problem as balancing legal certainty against the "flexible uncertainty" mediation needs. *Computershare* shows courts may relax certainty to support party autonomy and freedom of contract. *Sulamérica* shows English courts still require a defined process.
- **Agreements to mediate are not agreements to agree.** The process-based distinction (Giles J in *Hooper Bailie*; Allen's view that mediation is a structure partly independent of the parties) is the intellectual move that gets mediation clauses past *Walford v Miles*. Critics might reply that the outcome still depends on future negotiation, but the obligation is only to take part, not to agree.
- **Good faith.** *Walford* treats good-faith negotiation as unworkable; Einstein J (*Aiton*) and Feehily argue good faith and self-interest can co-exist, especially with a mediator. The "excluder" theory (Summers), discussed in ch 7, defines good faith by the bad faith it excludes. The practical consensus is that only narrow, objective requirements are enforceable.
- **Remedies gap.** Damages and specific performance are weak, so the stay is the only real tool. Boulle and Alexander suggest courts should be readier to order specific performance given growing support for mandatory mediation.
- **Model Law art 5(2)** could undermine contractual commitments; Feehily recommends amending it on adoption.
- **Compulsion concerns.** The more courts enforce mediation, the more it looks compulsory, raising art 6 ECHR arguments. Condition-precedent drafting is the answer at clause level.
- Even before Australian courts enforced such clauses, unenforceable clauses were used in hundreds of disputes because they carried moral and commercial force (Alexander). So enforceability is not the only measure of a clause's value.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the clause** and quote it. Is mediation mandatory ("shall") or permissive ("may", "consider")? Unqualified mandatory wording points to enforceability (*Cable & Wireless*).
2. **Is there a defined process?** Look for: named provider or incorporated rules (CEDR etc.), mediator appointment mechanism with a fallback, payment of the mediator, timetable or time limit, venue and language. Run the *Holloway* three-element test. If it says only "seek to resolve amicably by mediation", think *Sulamérica*.
3. **Agreement to agree?** Does anything still need to be agreed before the process can start (e.g. choosing the mediator with no default)? If yes, the clause is likely void (*Banabelle*; *Elizabeth Bay*), unless a court takes the *Computershare* line.
4. **Good-faith wording**: note *Walford* but contrast *Aiton*; a good-faith requirement alone is unlikely to be fatal if there is a framework.
5. **Condition precedent or ouster?** If mediation is the exclusive route, it is void as an ouster; if it is a precondition, it is fine (*Scott v Avery*).
6. **Multi-tier clause**: if another tier is invalid, the whole clause may fall (severability limits).
7. **Has the clause been waived** (expressly or by conduct)?
8. **Remedy**: advise seeking a stay (best) or, in the US, dismissal; explain why damages and specific performance are weak. Consider limitation-period risk (EU Directive art 8(1); Model Law footnote on suspension).
9. **Cross-border**: note that the Singapore Convention and the EU Directive do not enforce clauses; check whether the forum has adopted Model Law art 14.
10. **Drafting advice** (common in questions): avoid agreements to agree; annex or precisely reference external rules; specify costs, venue, timetable, procedures, law, language; condition-precedent wording; carve-out for emergency relief; deal with limitation; a comprehensive signed-writing clause (see *Universal Satspace*); consider a penalty such as forfeiting costs.

Typical traps: treating *Walford v Miles* as conclusive against mediation clauses; forgetting that *Sulamérica* turned on the absence of any defined process; overlooking the mediator's fees (fatal in *Aiton*).
