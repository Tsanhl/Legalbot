# Article 101 TFEU / Chapter I – Agreements, Decisions and Concerted Practices

## Overview
Art 101 TFEU (EU) and s 2 CA 1998, the Chapter I prohibition (UK), prohibit collusion: agreements between undertakings, decisions by associations of undertakings and concerted practices that may affect trade and have as their **object or effect** the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition. Prohibited agreements are void (Art 101(2); s 2(4)) unless exempt (Art 101(3); s 9). The US equivalent is Sherman Act s 1. the materials opens with **game theory**, which explains why competitors are tempted to collude and why cartels are unstable. It then sets out the **legal requirements** of the prohibition. This note covers the structure of the prohibition and the forms of collusion. The object/effect distinction, exemptions, cartels and verticals have their own notes.

## The law

### 1. Game theory: why collusion happens (handout pp 1–3)
- Game theory models firms' strategic interaction: each firm chooses price or output in light of assumptions about rivals' strategies. With perfect information, players know all prior moves. In a zero-sum game one player's gain is another's loss.
- **Prisoners' dilemma (pricing):** firms A and B each choose a high or low price. Both high: 10 each. One low, one high: 30 for the low-pricer, 0 for the other. Both low: 4 each. The **Nash equilibrium** is both low, even though both prefer both high, because each has an incentive to undercut.
- **Thelma and Louise:** confession dominates for each prisoner, so both confess (15 years each) although both silent would mean 5 years each.
- Where each firm has an incentive to cheat but both are worse off if both cheat, this is a prisoners' dilemma. In **repeated games** (tit-for-tat), strategic behaviour may lead to successful collusion. This links to leniency, which deliberately recreates the dilemma for cartelists.

### 2. The texts
- **CA 1998 s 2(1):** subject to s 3, agreements between undertakings, decisions by associations of undertakings or concerted practices which (a) may affect trade within the UK and (b) have as their object or effect the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition within the UK, are prohibited unless exempt. Section 2(2) gives examples (a)–(e): price or trading-condition fixing; limiting production, markets, technical development or investment; sharing markets or supply sources; discrimination; contractual tying. Section 2(3): applies only if the agreement is, or is intended to be, implemented in the UK. Section 2(4): prohibited agreements are void. Section 2(5): references to agreements apply equally to decisions and concerted practices. Section 2(7): for an agreement operating only in a part of the UK, "the UK" means that part. Section 2(8): "the Chapter I prohibition".
- **Art 101(1) TFEU:** same structure for agreements that "may affect trade between Member States" and restrict competition "within the internal market", with examples (a)–(e). **Art 101(2):** "automatically void". **Art 101(3):** exemption (see exemptions-article-101-3-and-section-9.md).
- **Sherman Act s 1 (US):** every contract, combination or conspiracy in restraint of inter-State or foreign trade is illegal. Fines are up to $100m for a corporation and $1m for others, or imprisonment up to ten years.

### 3. Horizontal versus vertical
- **Horizontal** agreements are between competitors at the same level. Examples: fixing prices or trading conditions; exchanging current or future price information; sharing markets or supply sources; limiting output, sales, technical development or investment. Cartels are the paradigm.
- **Vertical** agreements are between firms at different levels (supplier and distributor). They involve cooperation and specialisation that may be mutually beneficial. Examples of restraints: minimum resale price maintenance, export bans, exclusive dealing or distribution. The module treats them as generally less harmful (handbook section C).
- Other categories in the handout: discrimination and contractual tying (Art 101(1)(d)–(e)).

### 4. Legal requirements (handout p 5)
1. An **agreement between undertakings**, a **decision by an association of undertakings**, or a **concerted practice**.
2. Its **object or effect** is the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition in the relevant market.
3. It **may affect trade** between Member States (EU) or within the UK. Under CA 1998 it must also be implemented in the UK.
4. The restriction must be **appreciable** (de minimis).
5. It is not exempted under Art 101(3) / s 9 or a block exemption.

### 5. Undertakings
Every entity engaged in economic activity, regardless of legal status and financing (*Höfner and Elser*). Intra-group arrangements within a single economic unit are outside Art 101 (*Viho*). See undertakings-and-economic-actors.md.

### 6. Agreement
- An agreement requires a **"concurrence of wills between at least two parties"** (*Bayer*, T-41/96, para 69). *Bayer* concerned Adalat, whose prices were fixed by national health authorities and were about 40% lower in Spain and France than in the UK, so Spanish and French wholesalers exported to the UK.
- **Form is irrelevant.** Examples include contracts, gentlemen's agreements or simple understandings, a protocol reflecting genuine concurrence of will, standard terms and conditions, a trade association's constitution, guidelines issued by one party and adhered to by another, and exchanges of correspondence (handout p 6).

### 7. Decisions by associations of undertakings
- Rules and regulations of a trade association accepted by its members. Non-binding recommendations also count to the extent members comply. A trade association may monitor cartel compliance.
- *Wouters* (C-309/99): professional bodies such as a national Bar may be associations of undertakings.

### 8. Concerted practices
- A form of coordination which, **without reaching the stage of an agreement, knowingly substitutes practical cooperation for the risks of competition** (*ICI (Dyestuffs)*, Case 48/69, where dyestuff producers' simultaneous price increases were a concerted practice).
- *Suiker Unie* (Sugar Cartel, Joined Cases 40/73 etc.): producers coordinated to protect two Dutch producers on their home market. The result was price alignment, control of deliveries, and sales limits through basic and minimum quotas. A concerted practice may become apparent from the participants' behaviour.
- **Parallel conduct and its limits:** authorities must often infer coordination from circumstantial evidence because cartelists destroy records. But **parallel behaviour may be independent**. Kaplow's examples include people raising umbrellas when it rains, cars driving on the same side, and firms changing prices as input costs change. Where players are aware of each other this is "consciously parallel" but still independent. Matching a rival's strategy is not in itself an agreement or concerted practice.
- **Algorithms:** Ezrachi and Stucke note conscious parallelism at both human and machine level. Each human, without collusion, may programme algorithms to follow a rival's price increase, knowing that similar algorithms could produce supra-competitive equilibria.
- **Information exchange and public communications:** exchanges that remove uncertainty about intended conduct can be restrictions by object (*T-Mobile*). Pre-pricing communications were caught in *Bananas*. Kaplow: if public interchanges were per se legal, firms "could simply move their meetings from hotel rooms to joint press conferences". See cartels-leniency-and-information-exchange.md.

### 9. Effect on trade and appreciability
- **EU:** trade between Member States. **UK:** trade within the UK (or part of it).
- **De minimis:** an agreement escapes Art 101(1) if it has only an insignificant effect on the market (*Völk v Vervaecke*, cited in *Expedia* para 16). The Commission's De Minimis Notice ([2014] OJ C291/01) presumes no appreciable restriction where aggregate shares do not exceed **10%** (competitors) or **15%** (non-competitors). The default is 10% where classification is difficult, and 5% applies for cumulative foreclosure by parallel networks (paras 8–10).
- **But object restrictions are always appreciable:** an agreement that may affect trade and has an anti-competitive object constitutes, "by its nature and independently of any concrete effect", an appreciable restriction (*Expedia*, C-226/11, para 37). National authorities may apply Art 101(1) to agreements below the Notice thresholds (*Expedia* para 38).

### 10. Consequences
- Nullity of prohibited provisions (Art 101(2); s 2(4)). Fines (up to 10% of turnover). Damages claims. Criminal liability for individuals under the UK cartel offence.

## Debate and critique
- **Inferring agreement from parallelism.** The line between tacit collusion (conscious parallelism, lawful) and concerted practice (unlawful) is contested. Kaplow (*Competition Policy and Price Fixing*, 2013) argues that interfirm communications, including public statements, are important facilitating devices supporting an inference of coordination. Baker (*The Antitrust Paradigm*, ch 6, "Inferring Agreement and Algorithmic Coordination") is essential reading on how courts should infer agreement and deal with algorithms.
- **Algorithms.** If pricing algorithms independently reach supra-competitive equilibria, there may be no "concurrence of wills". Gal and Ezrachi and Stucke debate whether current concepts can capture this (see cartels-leniency-and-information-exchange.md).
- **Game theory and policy.** The prisoners' dilemma explains both why cartels form (repeated play) and why they break down (incentive to cheat), which leniency programmes exploit.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the parties** and whether they are undertakings (and in the same group).
2. **Classify the relationship:** competitors (horizontal) or supplier-distributor (vertical)?
3. **Find the form of collusion:**
   - written or oral agreement (birthday party agreement to raise prices by 15–21%; a golf-course persuasion to raise prices by 23%);
   - a trade association decision or recommendation (a note at a National Mango Board meeting);
   - a concerted practice (exchanges, signals such as "a seventeen-figure preceded an upward arrow", followed by parallel price rises).
4. **Single meeting or note:** one contact can suffice if it removes uncertainty (*T-Mobile*). Consider public distancing if a party did not object.
5. **Object or effect** (next note).
6. **Effect on trade and appreciability:** EU-wide or UK effects. Shares versus de minimis. An object restriction is appreciable regardless (*Expedia*).
7. **Exemption** (Art 101(3) / s 9; block exemptions).
8. **Consequences:** fines, nullity, damages, the UK criminal cartel offence for individuals, director disqualification.
9. **Trap:** parallel pricing alone, explicable by market conditions or algorithms matching rivals, is not enough. Look for contact or communication.
