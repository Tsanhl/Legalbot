# The Concept of Abuse and Objective Justification

## Overview
Once dominance is shown, the question is whether the conduct is an "abuse". Neither Art 102 TFEU nor s 18 CA 1998 defines abuse. They give a non-exhaustive list of examples (unfair prices, limiting output, discrimination, tying). The Court of Justice developed a general concept: abuse is an **objective** concept (*Hoffmann-La Roche*), covering recourse to methods different from those of "normal competition" or "competition on the merits", which hinder the maintenance or growth of competition. Abuse may be **exclusionary** (harming rivals and through them consumers) or **exploitative** (directly harming customers, e.g. excessive prices). Even conduct that looks abusive may escape the prohibition if it is **objectively justified**: objectively necessary, or producing efficiencies that outweigh the harm and benefit consumers (*Post Danmark I*). This note sets out the general framework that the specific abuse notes apply.

## The law

### 1. Classification of abusive practices (slides pp 6–7)
The slides group abuses as: discrimination; predatory pricing; exclusive dealing; long-term exclusive arrangements; tying or bundling; refusal (to supply / to deal); margin squeeze; excessive or unfair pricing. The handout warns no classification is complete. The same conduct may be competitive or abusive. A price cut may lower prices or be predatory. Tying (Windows/Media Player) may be an efficient enhancement or a disguised attempt to leverage power. This creates risks of **under-inclusiveness** (missing harmful practices) and **over-inclusiveness** (condemning beneficial ones).

### 2. The general definition of abuse
1. **Objective concept** – *Hoffmann-La Roche* (Case 85/76): abuse is an objective concept, confirmed in *Tomra* (C-549/10 P, para 17) and *TeliaSonera* (para 27). It does not require proof of intent (*Google Shopping* decision paras 333, 338).
2. **Methods different from normal competition** – abuse may consist in having recourse to methods different from those which condition normal competition in products or services based on traders' performance (*Hoffmann-La Roche* paras 6 and 91; *AKZO* para 69; *British Airways* C-95/04, para 66; *France Télécom* C-202/07 P, para 105; *Tomra* para 17). *Michelin I* (para 70) refers to traders' performance. *Deutsche Bahn* (T-229/94, para 78) to competition "on the basis of quality".
3. **Exclusionary effect** – Art 102 prohibits a dominant undertaking from adopting practices with an exclusionary effect on competitors that strengthen its dominance by means other than competition on the merits (*Post Danmark I* C-209/10, para 25).
4. **Not every exclusion is abusive** – competition on the merits may lead to the departure or marginalisation of less efficient competitors, less attractive on price, choice, quality or innovation (*Post Danmark I* paras 20–22; *TeliaSonera* para 24).
5. **Capability suffices** – Art 102 catches behaviour that tends to restrict competition or is capable of doing so, making market access impossible or harder (*Google Shopping* decision para 339). Under *Compagnie Maritime Belge*, failure to achieve the hoped-for result does not prevent abuse.
6. **No need for direct consumer harm** – harm through impact on competition or market structure is enough (*Continental Can* para 26; *British Airways*; *TeliaSonera*; *Post Danmark I* para 20; *National Grid v GEMA* [2010] EWCA Civ 114, para 85).

### 3. Intent
- Intent is not required, but it is not irrelevant. *AstraZeneca* (T-321/05, para 359): the objective nature of abuse does not make intention to resort to practices outside competition on the merits irrelevant. *Tomra* (paras 17–19): all relevant facts must be considered, including the motives behind the strategy. *Generics (UK) v CMA* (C-307/18, para 162) is cited by the CAT in *Advanz Pharma* for considering anti-competitive intent.
- Intent matters directly in predatory pricing (*AKZO*'s "plan for eliminating a competitor" for prices between AVC and ATC).
- Internal documents (emails, memos, CEO statements) are therefore valuable evidence in problem questions.

### 4. The as-efficient competitor (AEC) principle
- *Post Danmark I* frames abuse around whether a competitor as efficient as the dominant firm could compete.
- *Intel* (C-413/14 P, EU:C:2017:632, paras 138–139), as restated by the Court in *Google Shopping* (C-48/22 P, para 265): where the dominant undertaking submits, with supporting evidence, that its conduct was not capable of restricting competition, the Commission must assess whether there is a strategy aiming to exclude at least as efficient competitors. The AEC **test** (a formal price-cost test) is one tool among others. It is used "where that test is relevant", and its relevance is subject to judicial review (C-48/22 P, para 266). It is not required for non-pricing conduct such as self-preferencing (see self-preferencing-and-refusal-to-supply.md).

### 5. Objective justification
The legislation contains no express defence, but the case law accepts one (*Streetmap v Google* [2016] EWHC 253 (Ch), Roth J, paras 142–143). It has two forms:
1. **Objective necessity** – the conduct is objectively necessary (*Post Danmark I*, para 41).
2. **Efficiency defence** – the exclusionary effect is counterbalanced or outweighed by efficiency advantages that also benefit consumers (*Post Danmark I* para 41; *British Airways* C-95/04, para 86; *TeliaSonera* para 76). The conduct must be **proportionate**. Under the Commission Guidance Paper, relied on by Roth J (para 30), relevant efficiencies may include technical improvements in quality, not only price or cost.
- **Burden:** the evidential burden of raising the justification lies on the dominant undertaking (*Streetmap*; Reg 1/2003 Art 2 places the burden of proving infringement on the authority).

**Justifications rejected or limited in the case law (handout pp 38–39; slides pp 19–21):**
- **Meeting competition** – not a defence where a dominant liner conference selectively cut prices to match a rival (*Compagnie Maritime Belge* para 117). Also rejected in *Irish Sugar* (T-228/97, paras 186–189). A dominant firm may protect its commercial interests (*United Brands* para 189) but not where the aim is to strengthen dominance and abuse it (*British Airways* GC para 243; *France Télécom* T-340/03 para 185). Wanadoo had no absolute right to align its prices on competitors' (para 187).
- **Public health / safety** – *Tetra Pak* (T-83/91) and *Hilti* (T-30/89, paras 118–119): it is not for a dominant undertaking to take steps on its own initiative to eliminate products it considers dangerous or inferior.
- **Intellectual property** – *Microsoft* (T-201/04): IP coverage did not justify refusing to license interoperability information.
- **Regulatory procedures** – *AstraZeneca* (T-321/05, paras 671–672): a dominant firm cannot use regulatory procedures to block or hinder entry without legitimate grounds. But in *AstraZeneca* (C-457/10 P, para 135) pharmacovigilance obligations could objectively justify deregistering a marketing authorisation.
- **Legal advice** – reliance on legal advice does not avoid liability (*Advanz Pharma* [2023] CAT 52, para 431).
- **Portfolio pricing** (cross-subsidy across products) – rejected in *Allergan* [2023] CAT 56 (para 352).

### 6. Exclusionary versus exploitative abuse
- The *Hoffmann-La Roche* concept has been applied mainly to exclusionary conduct. It has been criticised for neglecting exploitative conduct such as excessive prices (handout p 37).
- UK law now strongly recognises exploitative abuse. The CAT in *Advanz Pharma* held that protecting customers against exploitation is a core aim of competition law (para 451), drawing on *London & South Eastern Railway v Gutmann* [2022] EWCA Civ 1077. See excessive-pricing-and-price-discrimination.md.

### 7. Unfair methods and deception
*Rambus* (COMP/38636, 2009): alleged "patent ambush" in standard-setting (non-disclosure of patents, then royalty claims). The case ended in commitments. The handout asks whether Art 102 adequately covers deceptive unfair practices, compared with FTC Act s 5 (unfair methods of competition) in the US, and whether a separate mandate against unfair methods of competition is needed.

## Key authorities
- *Hoffmann-La Roche* (Case 85/76) [1979]: abuse as an objective concept; methods different from normal competition (paras 6, 91).
- *Continental Can* (Case 6/72) [1973], para 26: harm via competition structure. Strengthening dominance may be abusive.
- *Michelin I* (Case 322/81) [1983], para 70; *AKZO* (C-62/86) [1991], para 69; *British Airways* (C-95/04 P) [2007], paras 66, 86.
- *France Télécom* (C-202/07 P) [2009], para 105: special responsibility; methods different from normal competition.
- *TeliaSonera* (C-52/09) [2011], paras 24, 27, 76.
- *Post Danmark I* (C-209/10) [2012], paras 20–25, 41: not all exclusion is abusive. Objective necessity and efficiency defence.
- *Tomra* (C-549/10 P) [2012], paras 17–19: objective concept; motives relevant.
- *AstraZeneca* (T-321/05) [2010], paras 359, 671–672; (C-457/10 P), para 135.
- *Intel* (C-413/14 P) [2017], paras 138–139 (as restated in C-48/22 P para 265): AEC analysis when the firm submits evidence of no capability.
- *Google Shopping* (C-48/22 P) [2024], paras 265–266: AEC test only where relevant.
- *Streetmap v Google* [2016] EWHC 253 (Ch), paras 142–143 (UK): objective justification and proportionality.
- *National Grid v GEMA* [2010] EWCA Civ 114, para 85 (UK): no need to prove direct consumer harm.

## Debate and critique
- **Form-based versus effects-based approach.** The Commission's Guidance Paper on its Art 82 (now 102) enforcement priorities introduced "anticompetitive foreclosure" leading to "consumer harm", the AEC benchmark and an efficiency defence. Chirita's 2009 European Competition Journal article examines these concepts (consumer welfare, foreclosure, consumer harm, the efficiency defence and balancing test, predation and tying). It questions how effective it is to reform Art 82 (now 102) by soft law, and how the Paper fits German competition law. The handbook's 2021 exam question pairs Bork's two theories of injury (collusion, and inflicting injury on rivals) with the European standard of "anti-competitive foreclosure leading to consumer harm".
- **Objective concept versus subjective intent.** The courts say intent is unnecessary but keep using it (*AKZO*, *AstraZeneca*, *Tomra*, *Intel*'s internal emails). Critics say this blurs the line between protecting competition and punishing "bad" firms. Defenders see intent as reliable evidence of likely effects.
- **Special responsibility and equality of opportunity.** The *Google Shopping* reasoning ("equality of opportunity") can be read as a non-discrimination norm (Chirita). Others see it as protecting competitors. *Post Danmark I* is the counterweight: less efficient rivals may be marginalised.
- **Exploitative abuse.** Werden argues that exploitative abuse (e.g. excessive pricing) is "a bad idea" (journal file title), because price regulation by courts is error-prone. The UK CAT nevertheless treats exploitation as a core aim (*Advanz*). See excessive-pricing-and-price-discrimination.md.
- **Unfair competition gap.** The *Rambus* discussion and the handout's "questions for further reflection" suggest Art 102 lacks a clear mandate against deceptive or unfair methods, compared with FTC Act s 5.
- **Past essays:** "The concept of abuse is an 'objective' concept", discussed with *AstraZeneca* T-321/05 and *Rambus* (May/June 2013); AG Wahl in *Intel* on exclusivity rebates (May/June 2017).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance first** (dominant-position.md).
2. **Name the type of conduct** and map it to Art 102(a)–(d) / s 18(2)(a)–(d): pricing (predation, rebates, margin squeeze, excessive or discriminatory prices); non-pricing (tying, refusal, self-preferencing, exclusivity, data terms).
3. **Is it competition on the merits?** Ask whether an as-efficient competitor could match it, whether it relies on the dominant firm's scale or position rather than performance, and whether it forecloses access.
4. **Effects/capability:** show the conduct is capable of exclusionary effects (actual effects not required). Use evidence of foreclosure: rivals displaced to page 3, exit threats, falling rival shares.
5. **Intent evidence:** leaked emails or memos (e.g. "eliminate X before price increases", "gradually increase subscription prices") support abuse, and intent is necessary for *AKZO* AVC–ATC predation.
6. **Objective justification:** list possible justifications (innovation, quality, security, privacy, sustainability, meeting competition, IP). Test each: necessity, efficiencies benefiting consumers, proportionality. Remember rejected defences (meeting competition with selective cuts; self-appointed safety policing; IP alone; legal advice).
7. **Exploitative angle:** consider unfair prices or trading conditions (s 18(2)(a)), e.g. intrusive data terms.
8. **Remedies and fine** (institutions-enforcement-and-procedure.md).
