# Vertical and conglomerate mergers

## Overview
Horizontal mergers (rivals in the same market) are treated as the most dangerous. Non-horizontal mergers are of two kinds: vertical (firms at different levels of the same supply chain) and conglomerate (firms in different but related, or unrelated, markets). Their effects are often efficient or neutral, but they can foreclose rivals, raise rivals' costs, or let a firm leverage market power into a neighbouring market. Under the EU Merger Regulation 139/2004 the test is the significant impediment to effective competition (SIEC), and the Commission's Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines (2008) structure the analysis around ability, incentive and effect. The UK applies the substantial lessening of competition test (see merger-procedure-and-jurisdiction.md).

## The law
**1. Definitions and economics (study materials).**
- *Vertical*: a merger between firms at different but complementary levels of the market for the same final product (for example a raw material supplier and a manufacturer). It does not by itself remove substitutes, but may increase market power by foreclosing third parties or facilitating collusion.
- *Backward integration* (acquiring a supplier) and *forward integration* (acquiring a customer or distribution). A monopolist supplier has no incentive to integrate forward into an already competitive downstream market, since it cannot produce more cheaply.
- Efficiencies of integration: lower production costs (e.g. continuous steel production saving heat), better planning and coordination, reduction of uncertainty and of incomplete information, assured supply, elimination of externalities, simplification of complex technical or legal relations, and avoidance of tax or price controls. Integration may also implement price discrimination where buyers' elasticities differ and resale is impossible.
- *Elimination of double marginalisation*: an independent upstream and downstream firm each earn a margin; the merged firm earns one, which may reduce costs and prices.
- *Conglomerate*: firms operating in different product markets (or the same product in different geographic markets). Forms of diversification: product extension (a sweet maker moving into chocolate bars), market extension (new geographic market) and pure diversification (unrelated products). Advantages: cross-subsidisation (capacity to fund predatory pricing against specialised rivals from profits elsewhere), economies of scope, spreading risk of demand fluctuation, tax exposure, internal capital market. Risks: diversified firms may underperform and be overstretched. A takeover may be a disguised vertical merger (acquiring the distribution network to foreclose a potential entrant).
- Conglomerate efficiencies: "one-stop shop" for customers wanting complementary products X and Y (tying of complementary products), and lower prices through bundling. Bundling is also a way to foreclose rivals: The block-booking example shows a monopolist raising total revenue by selling two films as a package when no perfect price discrimination is possible.

**2. Typology of effects.** Faull and Nikpay's table: horizontal non-coordinated (unilateral effects, potential competition) and coordinated (tacit coordination); non-horizontal non-coordinated (input foreclosure, customer foreclosure, conglomerate effects) and coordinated (vertically facilitated coordination). The discussion defines unilateral effects (merged entity can raise price or reduce output, quality, choice or innovation without coordinating), coordinated effects (greater ability of the merged firm and others to coordinate), vertical effects (foreclosure or coordination) and conglomerate effects.

**3. Foreclosure.** The Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines define foreclosure as any instance where actual or potential rivals' access to supplies or markets is hampered or eliminated as a result of the merger, reducing their ability or incentive to compete (para 18). It is "anti-competitive foreclosure" where it leads to higher prices to ultimate customers. Foreclosure can be absolute or partial (favouring some users). Three theories, each requiring existing substantial market power in one market that the merger allows to be exercised in another:
- *Input foreclosure*: upstream market power harms downstream competitors through supply conditions (refusal, higher prices, lower quality, delay). Example *General Electric/Honeywell* (COMP/M.2220, 2001): fear that Honeywell's engine starters could be used against engine rivals; the General Court rejected the theory because the starter was about 0.2 per cent of the engine price, so no ability to foreclose.
- *Customer foreclosure*: downstream market power harms upstream rivals through purchase conditions. The textbook says there is no clear example in the Commission's practice; *Telefónica UK/Vodafone UK/Everything Everywhere JV* (mobile wallet; COMP/M.6314, 2012) raised akin concerns (technical foreclosure treated as input foreclosure; commercial foreclosure as indirect) but the Commission found no ability or incentive, partly because rivals such as Google had counter-strategies.
- *Conglomerate effects*: leveraging into a closely linked market via technical tying (product designed not to work well with rivals' products) or commercial tying (rebates making it harder for rivals to sell). Example *Tetra Laval/Sidel* (COMP/M.2416, 2003): Tetra already dominant in carton packaging; fear of leveraging into PET packaging equipment by bundling. The General Court (T-5/02) and Court of Justice (C-12/03 P) required a prospective analysis with convincing evidence of the most likely outcome. *Intel/McAfee* (COMP/M.5984, 2011): cleared in Phase I with remedies on interoperability and non-impediment of rival security software on Intel chips.

**4. Ability, incentive, effect (Guidelines paras 32, 59, 94).**
- *Ability*: market power over something important to the rival (input, customers). Examples: *Itema/BarcoVision* (COMP/M.4874, 2008): alternative supplier Uster and small sensor cost share limited ability; *UTC/Goodrich* (COMP/M.6410, 2012): downstream engine rivals depended on Goodrich engine control parts; cleared after a divestiture of the small-engine controls business and an option for Rolls-Royce on a fuel nozzle R&D project.
- *Incentive*: compare profit lost upstream with profit gained downstream; a merged firm may value reducing downstream competition (raising rivals' costs). *Nokia/Navteq* (COMP/M.4942): foreclosure profitable only if Tele Atlas raised prices over 200 per cent, unrealistic. *Google/Motorola Mobility* (COMP/M.6381, 2012): no incentive to favour Motorola over the Android ecosystem, given Google's advertising model and Motorola's 5 per cent share.
- *Effect*: harm must reach customers at the level immediately below the lowest level where the merged entity competes. The fact that competitors suffer is not enough (Guidelines para 16): the Commission protects competition and customers, not competitors. Countervailing factors: buyer power, efficiencies such as elimination of double mark-ups. In *TomTom/Tele Atlas* (COMP/M.4854, 2008) the Commission's model found upward pressure on rivals' navigation device prices offset by downward pressure from the elimination of double mark-ups, leaving a small net decline. In *Advent/Maxam* (COMP/M.6411, 2012) joint control by other shareholders made foreclosure unlikely.

**5. Trends.** After the 2004 reform and the Non-Horizontal Merger Guidelines, the textbook says prohibitions have concentrated on horizontal unilateral effects; vertical and conglomerate theories are harder to establish because efficiencies are recognised and dominance is less central. Pre-reform non-horizontal prohibitions (*GE/Honeywell*, *Tetra Laval*) were annulled in whole or part by the EU Courts (alongside *Airtours/First Choice* and *Schneider/Legrand*). Remedies have still been imposed in vertical or conglomerate cases (Intel/McAfee, UTC/Goodrich).

**6. Digital conglomerates.** a review of "growth of conglomerates" lists many clearances and conditional clearances of big-tech acquisitions in different markets (Microsoft/LinkedIn, Google/Fitbit, Meta/Kustomer, Microsoft/Activision Blizzard) and CMA decisions (Facebook/Giphy prohibited). Chirita argues these conglomerates nurture harm to consumer privacy. See data-driven-mergers.md.

**7. Case study.** *Lloyds/HBOS* (2008, horizontal but included to show public interest override): the OFT found clear competition concerns (30-40 per cent joint share, unilateral effects from switching costs and customer inertia, removal of the only challenger), but the Government cleared on public interest grounds. *Lloyds TSB/Abbey National* (Competition Commission 2001) is used for barriers to switching.

## Key authorities
- Regulation 139/2004 (SIEC test); Commission Guidelines on the assessment of non-horizontal mergers (OJ 2008) and horizontal mergers (OJ 2004 C 31/5).
- *Tetra Laval/Sidel* (T-5/02; C-12/03 P); *GE/Honeywell* (T-210/01); *Airtours* (T-342/99); *Schneider/Legrand*.
- Commission decisions: UTC/Goodrich; Intel/McAfee; TomTom/Tele Atlas; Nokia/Navteq; Google/Motorola; Itema/BarcoVision; Advent/Maxam; Telefónica/Vodafone/EE JV.
- Nestlé/Perrier (oligopolistic dominance) and *Kali & Salz* (failing firm defence).

## Debate and critique
- **Protecting competition or competitors.** The Commission's insistence on customer harm responds to the risk of complaints by rivals; but this may under-protect markets from leveraging.
- **Effects-based approach.** More precise but more complex and longer decisions (Faull and Nikpay); the Courts' reversals of earlier vertical and conglomerate prohibitions demand strong evidence. Critics from the digital debate (Chirita) say the foreclosure test serves rivals and ignores exploitation of users.
- **Efficiencies and double marginalisation.** Offsetting mechanism may dominate in concentrated markets; but its force depends on pass-through.
- **Digital markets.** The traditional ability-incentive-effect test is challenged by data, ecosystems and network effects.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the merger: horizontal, vertical or conglomerate. Are the parties active upstream and downstream, or in neighbouring markets with complementary products?
2. Does a safe harbour apply? The discussion refers to simplified procedure thresholds (combined share under 15 per cent horizontally, under 25 per cent on vertically related markets).
3. Choose theory of harm: input foreclosure, customer foreclosure, conglomerate leveraging, access to competitively sensitive information, coordinated effects.
4. Test ability (market power over a key input or customers; share of cost in downstream product; alternatives), incentive (profit comparison), effect (harm to customers at the level below; entry barriers).
5. Weigh countervailing factors: double marginalisation, buyer power, efficiencies, entry.
6. Remedies: divestiture, interoperability or access commitments, firewalls.
7. Conclude on SIEC (EU) or SLC (UK) and refer to the likelihood of a Phase II.
