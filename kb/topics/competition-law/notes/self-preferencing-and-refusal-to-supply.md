# Self-Preferencing, Leveraging and Refusal to Supply (Google Shopping and Microsoft)

## Overview
A vertically integrated dominant platform can favour its own downstream service over rivals that depend on the platform. It may rank its own service higher, display it more attractively, or subject rivals to demoting algorithms. This is **self-preferencing**, a form of **leveraging** dominance from one market into a neighbouring one. The leading case is *Google Search (Shopping)*: the Commission's decision (AT.39740, 27 June 2017, fine €2.42bn), the General Court's judgment (T-612/17, 10 November 2021, ECLI:EU:T:2021:763) and the Court of Justice's Grand Chamber judgment dismissing Google's appeal (C-48/22 P, 10 September 2024, ECLI:EU:C:2024:726). The key doctrinal question, is whether such conduct must meet the strict *Bronner* conditions for a "duty to supply", and whether the as-efficient competitor test is required. The Court answered no to both. The older line on **refusal to supply** and IP, from *Microsoft* (T-201/04) on interoperability information, frames that contrast.

## The law

### 1. Refusal to supply / essential facilities (Bronner)
- *Oscar Bronner* (C-7/97) [1998] ECR I-7791: a refusal to supply is abusive only if (1) the input is **indispensable**, (2) the refusal is likely to **eliminate all competition** downstream, and (3) there is **no objective justification**. The test is strict because compelled access interferes with freedom of contract and property (as summarised in the C-48/22 P case file).
- **Later clarifications** (as summarised in the case file): *Deutsche Telekom* (C-152/19 P [2021]) – *Bronner* applies to outright refusals, not to unfair access terms such as margin squeeze. *Lietuvos geležinkeliai* (C-42/21 P [2023]) – indispensability is irrelevant where access is granted on unfair or discriminatory conditions.
- **UK:** in *Attheraces* (EWCA Civ 38), Mummery LJ, in light of *Bronner*, said Art 102 / Chapter II is not a law under which courts fix a fair price for access to an essential facility.

### 2. Microsoft – interoperability and IP (EU)
- **Commission decision** (COMP/C-3/37.792, 2004) and **GC** (*Microsoft v Commission*, T-201/04 [2007] ECR II-3601): three markets (PC operating systems, work group server operating systems, media streaming software). Microsoft refused to supply rivals with **interoperability information** needed to develop competing work group server OSs. Customers were increasingly locked into a homogeneous Windows solution, consumer choice was limited by an artificial interoperability advantage, and rivals were disadvantaged (para 650).
- **IP no justification:** the GC rejected the argument that IP coverage justified refusing to license. The public interest in effective competition allowed interference with the IP owner's exclusive right. Special responsibility applies irrespective of the cause of dominance (para 229).
- **Remedy:** disclose interoperability information within 120 days on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms, keep it updated, and set up an evaluation mechanism (Decision Art 5, as reproduced in textbook ch 15).
- A key question is whether a similar duty to disclose its search algorithm could be imposed on Google, and the tensions with IP.

### 3. Google Shopping – Commission decision (AT.39740, 2017)
**Markets and dominance**:
- Separate national markets for **general search** and for **comparison shopping services (CSS)**. CSSs are not substitutable with specialised search, search ads, merchant platforms (e.g. Amazon Marketplace, eBay) or offline tools.
- A free service is still an economic activity, because users "contribute to the monetisation of the service by providing data with each query" (paras 152, 158, 320). General search competes on relevance, speed, interface and index depth (para 160).
- Dominance in general search in all EEA countries since 2008 (Czech Republic since 2011). Shares were mostly above 90% and no rival exceeded about 4%. Barriers included huge investment, network effects on both sides of the platform, data-driven feedback, and low multi-homing.

**Abuse:**
- Google gave its own CSS (Product Universal, Shopping Unit) more favourable positioning and display in general search results in thirteen national markets.
- At the same time, rival CSSs appeared only as generic "blue links" subject to adjustment algorithms (e.g. **Panda**) that demoted them. Google's own service was not subject to those mechanisms.
- This diverted traffic from rivals to Google's CSS. Traffic from generic results (especially the top three to five) was a large share of rivals' traffic that could not be replaced (AdWords was not economically viable, nor were mobile apps or direct traffic).
- The conduct was capable of foreclosing rival CSSs, leading to higher merchant fees, higher consumer prices, less innovation and reduced consumer access to the most relevant services (paras 589–600).
- Actual exit or traffic decline need not be proved. Capability sufficed (para 602, citing *Post Danmark II*).
- **No novel abuse:** using dominance in one market to extend into adjacent markets is a well-established abuse outside competition on the merits (para 649). This was active conduct (favourable positioning and display), not a passive refusal of access, so *Bronner* was irrelevant because ending the infringement imposed no duty to supply (paras 650–652).
- **Objective justifications rejected** (paras 653–671): preserving search quality by demoting low-quality sites; quality improvement of the Shopping Unit; monetisation concerns; Charter rights (Arts 11, 16, 17). Google did not show it could not apply the same processes to its own service.
- **Remedy and fine:** equal treatment, meaning rival CSSs treated no less favourably than Google's own service, subject to the same processes and methods (paras 699–700). Fine €2.42bn including a 1.3 deterrence multiplier (paras 752–754). Duration was up to ten years (Germany, UK).

### 4. General Court (T-612/17, 2021)
As summarised in the case file, the GC largely dismissed Google's action. It upheld the abuse in the CSS markets but annulled the decision in so far as it found an infringement in the 13 general search markets based on anticompetitive effects there. It maintained the fine. The discussion cites GC para 183: leveraging may take various forms, but here it was an active discrimination against rivals, an exclusionary demotion.

### 5. Court of Justice (C-48/22 P, Grand Chamber, 10 September 2024)
Appeal dismissed in its entirety. The €2.424bn fine is final (case file). On the four grounds:
1. **Bronner inapplicable.** This was not a refusal to supply. Rivals had access (blue links), and the abuse lay in **discriminatory conditions of access**. Shopping Units were not a separate infrastructure. Requiring a firm to stop discriminating is less intrusive on contract and property than compelling supply.
2. **Competition on the merits.** The abuse was the **combination** of favourable display and simultaneous demotion, assessed in context: the importance of Google traffic to CSSs, user behaviour (clicking top results), and non-substitutability of that traffic. Discriminatory self-preferencing that leverages dominance and is capable of foreclosure is not competition on the merits. Presentation as a "quality improvement" does not change that.
3. **Causation / counterfactual.** No formal counterfactual is required. The Commission may rely on observed market evolution. The burden stays on the Commission, but the undertaking may submit a counterfactual to rebut. Google's counterfactual was incomplete because it modelled only Shopping Units and ignored demotion.
4. **AEC test.** Where a dominant firm submits evidence that its conduct could not foreclose, the Commission must assess the possible exclusion of as-efficient competitors (para 265, citing *Intel* paras 138–139). The AEC test is applied "where that test is relevant" (para 266). It was not required for this non-price discriminatory conduct.

### 6. Linked digital self-preferencing (Chirita)
- **Amazon:** misuse of non-public third-party seller data and Buy Box favouring. Commission commitments obliged Amazon to refrain from using seller data and to apply equal treatment for Buy Box and Prime eligibility. A CMA investigation also took place.
- **Apple App Store:** favouring its own apps in search, "Sherlocking" (cloning rival apps using confidential data), 30% commissions (Spotify/Apple Music).
- **Google Android:** Search and Chrome favoured on devices; Play Store as gatekeeper.
- **UK DMCCA 2024:** CMA conduct requirements may prevent an SMS firm from treating its own products more favourably than others' (see digital-markets-regulation-dma-dmcca.md). The DMA likewise targets self-preferencing.

## Debate and critique
- **Chirita – equal treatment.** The Commission's reasoning originates in a wider interpretation of non-discrimination that safeguards an **equal treatment norm**: Google should rank all CSSs the same way whatever their affiliation (Loyola Consumer L Rev 2021). Self-preferencing stems from conflicts of interest in vertical integration. In later work she describes Google's universal search as resembling an essential digital infrastructure, indispensable for as-efficient CSSs. Equal treatment of rivals exposed to algorithmic ranking "becomes a business expectation". She criticises the failure to value algorithmic consumer data and argues for procedural fairness (FRAND-like) in access terms.
- **Equal treatment versus freedom to prioritise one's own products.** The discussion cites Rooney against "equal" business rights that leave monopolists unable to prioritise their own offerings.
- **US approach – FTC 2013.** The FTC closed its Google investigation. It acknowledged that Google demoted all but one or two comparison shopping properties from page one for shopping queries. But it held that product design is an important dimension of competition, that "reasonable minds may differ" on search page design, and that second-guessing design with plausible procompetitive justifications risks harming consumers. Commissioner Rosch dissented in part, saying Google's power derived from data obtained by deceptive means. Patterson: the FTC ended its investigation because it accepted Google's business justification, not because there was no exclusionary harm.
- **Bork and Sidak** ("What does the Chicago School teach about Internet search and the antitrust treatment of Google?", 2012) is the Chicago-school defence. A key question is whether "academic defence of Google has been wrong" or "academic criticism has proved right".
- **Moot grounds (moot):** (a) the GC erred in upholding the decision despite failure to meet the legal test for a duty to supply; (b) the GC erred in holding the Commission did not need to examine whether the conduct could foreclose as-efficient competitors. The CJ's answers (points 1 and 4 above) give the Commission side. Google's side: the remedy (equal treatment) is in substance a duty to supply space, so *Bronner* should apply; and without an AEC analysis the Commission protects less efficient rivals (contrast *Post Danmark I*).
- **Novelty and fines.** Google argued there was "no precedent" and that it had first been offered commitments. The Commission and courts treated leveraging as well-established.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance** on the platform market (general search, app store, marketplace, OS).
2. **Classify the conduct:**
   - Outright refusal of access or information: apply *Bronner* (indispensability, elimination of competition, no justification). For IP, apply *Microsoft*.
   - Access granted but on discriminatory or unfavourable terms (ranking rivals on page 3 or 7, end of page 2, "Lioness algorithmic adjustment", higher commissions of 35% for rivals' apps): apply *Google Shopping*. *Bronner* is not needed.
3. **Leveraging:** identify the neighbouring market (own-brand groceries, cameras, AI assistants, CSS) and show traffic or visibility diverted from rivals.
4. **Context and capability:** importance of platform traffic to rivals, user click behaviour, non-substitutability (rivals' only alternative is to buy ads, "indispensable spending on Troogle Ads"). Actual exit is not needed. Observed market evolution suffices.
5. **AEC test:** not required for non-price self-preferencing. If the firm submits evidence of no foreclosure capability, address it.
6. **Objective justification:** quality, relevance, user experience, security, monetisation, freedom to conduct business. Apply the same standards to own and rival services. If the firm cannot show it could not apply equal treatment, the justification fails.
7. **Remedy:** equal treatment obligation, cease demotion, transparent ranking. Consider the DMCCA conduct requirement against self-preferencing (UK) and DMA obligations (EU gatekeepers).
8. **Data dimension:** if rankings or personalised discounts exclude rivals' brands using customer data, add data-abuse arguments.

- Error: distinguishing Microsoft (T-201/04) because access was never given; the decision relied partly on disrupting previous supply. Ending existing supply is more likely abusive (Guidance para 84); for platforms opened to third parties, Bronner indispensability need not be shown (Android Auto C-233/23).
