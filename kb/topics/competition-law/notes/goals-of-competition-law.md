# Goals of Competition Law

## Overview
Every substantive question in competition law (what counts as an abuse, when an agreement "restricts" competition, which mergers to block) depends on what the law is for. The discussion frames this as a contest between a narrow economic goal (consumer welfare / economic efficiency, associated with Robert Bork and the Chicago School) and a plural set of goals rooted in history and in the EU Treaties: undistorted competition, economic freedom, fairness, market integration, a social market economy, consumer choice, and now sustainability and data/privacy concerns. The position is that goals inevitably conflict and that competition law must rank them responsibly to keep a "fair and just balance".

## The law

### 1. Nature of competition law
- Competition law is public (administrative) law regulating the strategic conduct of private economic actors (and of states through aid/subsidies). It operates through public enforcement by authorities, with decisions reviewable by courts, alongside private litigation.
- Competition is pictured as a game or contest in the marketplace. Firms compete on price, output, quality and innovation. Those competing on the merits enjoy entrepreneurial freedom; those using anti-competitive practices infringe the law.
- Two "competition freedoms" appear: (a) entrepreneurial freedom of action (free competition) and (b) individual freedom of choice (free choice for consumers).

### 2. Historical roots (England, US, Europe)
The following timeline traces the development:
1. **Restraint of trade at common law (England).** *Dyer's case* (1414), a non-compete agreement; *Mitchel v Reynolds* (1711), where the question of what counts as "fair competition" was posed; the 1601 parliamentary pressure on Elizabeth I over monopolies; Coke's view that monopolies offend Magna Carta; the Anti-Monopoly Act, which came into force in 1624.
2. **Classical economics.** Adam Smith (1776): monopolists keep markets under-stocked and sell above the "natural price" of free competition; de Roover traced the idea back to Aristotle (who coined "monopoly") and medieval writers such as Aquinas (a monopolist pursues excessive gain to the public's detriment).
3. **1890 US Sherman Act** (same year as Marshall's *Principles of Economics*), and *Mogul Steamship Co Ltd v McGregor* [1892] AC 25, the common law that the Sherman Act's drafters later sought to codify.
4. **UK statutes:** Monopolies and Restrictive Practices (Inquiry and Control) Act 1948 (broad "public interest" criteria and an "abuse theory of monopoly"); Restrictive Trade Practices Act 1956; Monopolies Commission 1965; Competition Act 1998; Enterprise Act 2002; Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013.
5. **Europe:** Schuman Declaration 1950 (competition to counter price-fixing, quotas and market division); Treaty of Paris 1951 (ECSC); Treaty of Rome 1957; Treaty of Lisbon 2007.

### 3. The Sherman Act's drafting history and the "historical method"
- The original draft condemned arrangements that tend "to prevent full and free competition" or "to advance cost to the consumer". The Judiciary Committee replaced this, to codify common law (*Mogul Steamship*), with "contract ... in restraint of trade" and "monopolize or attempt to monopolize" (citing Millon).
- Senator Sherman's speeches combined consumer concerns (the "law of selfishness" disregarding the consumer) with political and social concerns (no "king" over the necessaries of life; inequality of wealth and opportunity). Senator Jones warned of a slide from corporate giantism to "monopolistic tyranny".
- Elzinga: there is "no single thread of efficiency" in the legislative history.

### 4. Contemporary EU goals
The discussion lists, with Treaty references:
1. **Economic freedom** – freedom to conduct a business (Art 16 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights).
2. **Open markets and free competition** – competition as macro-economic policy (Art 119 TFEU; Art 120 TFEU on efficient allocation of resources).
3. **Social market economy** – Art 3(3) TEU: "a highly competitive social market economy, aiming at full employment and social progress", plus a high level of environmental protection.
4. **Consumer choice** – Lande's thesis that antitrust is about consumer choice.
5. **Fair and balanced trade** – the TFEU Preamble refers to "fair competition".
6. **Static efficiency** (allocation of resources), **dynamic competition** (innovation, R&D, Art 3(3) TEU, Art 173 TFEU), **entrepreneurship** (protection of SMEs).
7. **Redistribution?** – contested (see Debate).

The key Treaty hook for "undistorted competition" after Lisbon is Protocol No 27 on the internal market and competition, read with Art 3(3) TEU (*TeliaSonera* para 20).

### 5. Case law on goals
- **EU:** the competition rules protect the competitive process and market structure, not only consumers directly; there is no need to prove direct consumer harm (see Key authorities).
- **UK:** ERRA 2013 s 25(3) – the CMA "must seek to promote competition, both within and outside the UK, for the benefit of consumers" (quoted in *CMA v Flynn Pharma* [2020] EWCA Civ 617 para 7).

## Key authorities
- *Continental Can* (Case 6/72) [1973] ECR 215: paras 25–26 – Arts 85 and 86 (now 101 and 102 TFEU) pursue the same aim of maintaining effective competition; the abuse prohibition covers practices harming consumers directly and also those harming them "through their impact on an effective competition structure". Foundation of the structural reading of EU goals.
- *Hoechst v Commission* (Cases 46/87 and 227/88) [1989] ECR 2859, para 19: the rules prevent competition being distorted to the detriment of the public interest, individual undertakings and consumers.
- *Konkurrensverket v TeliaSonera Sverige AB* (C-52/09) [2011] ECR I-527: para 20 (Art 3(3) TEU with Protocol 27 – a system ensuring competition is not distorted); para 22 (rules protect public interest, individual undertakings and consumers, "thereby ensuring the well-being" of the Union).
- *GlaxoSmithKline* (C-501/06 P) [2009] ECR I-9291: competition law protects not only competitors or consumers but "the structure of the market and, in so doing, competition as such"; and pursues market integration (para 61).
- *Post Danmark I* (C-209/10) [2012], para 20: the law targets practices harming consumers directly and also those harming them through their impact on competition. *Servizio Elettrico Nazionale* (C-377/20, ECLI:EU:C:2022:379) is paired with it in the discussion (function of Art 102 includes the well-being of the Union).
- *CMA v Flynn Pharma Ltd* [2020] EWCA Civ 617 (12 May 2020), para 7: CMA established by ERRA 2013 s 25; duty under s 25(3) to promote competition for the benefit of consumers.
- *R (Gallaher Group Ltd) v CMA* [2018] UKSC 25 (tobacco price-fixing settlement): fairness and equal treatment discussed (paras 6, 19, 25); para 31 – fairness, like equal treatment, is a fundamental principle of a democratic society but not necessarily directly translatable into a justiciable rule of law. Useful for the "fairness as a goal" debate in the UK.
- The discussion notes it lists only Court of Justice rulings because General Court rulings "can be overturned".

## Debate and critique
**Bork and consumer welfare.** Bork argued there is no "colourable" support for courts applying any value other than maximising consumer welfare; "competition" is a term of art for situations where consumer welfare cannot be increased; antitrust policy cannot be rational until its goal is settled (*The Antitrust Paradox*, pp 47, 58, 87–90). Consumer welfare, for Bork, is "merely another term for the wealth of the nation"; antitrust says nothing about how prosperity is distributed and cannot decide "who should be rich or poor" or how much to spend reducing pollution.

**Hovenkamp's reply.** Bork's "consumer welfare" was really total welfare (consumer plus producer surplus) and was "misnamed". A true consumer welfare principle does shift some wealth from producers to consumers, but not along the traditional rich/poor or capital/labour axes. Antitrust "has no statutory mandate and no tools for redistributing wealth". Hovenkamp also notes that no US court has actually based a judgment on Bork's welfare trade-off calculations, which Bork himself called "utterly insoluble" in practice.

**Lande.** Congress passed the antitrust laws primarily for distributive (wealth-transfer) rather than efficiency objectives, but not to secure fair overall distribution or help the poor. Lande also promotes "consumer choice" as the goal.

**Populist and progressive readings.** Phillips Sawyer: competition was a social good protecting democracy and formal equality. Langlois: agrarian populism and tariff politics shaped the Act. Hovenkamp: the Act reflected populist concern for small business and farmers.

**Orbach, "How Antitrust Lost Its Goal" (2013).** Orbach traces how "consumer welfare" was equated with efficiency and competition (via Bork), argues the history of the standard undermines its validity, and suggests that antitrust should let the term go and restore its lost goal of preserving competition.

**Chicago-school ideas** (via Olsen): Stigler's "efficient consumer", Friedman and Director's tolerance of "efficient" monopolies. Olsen argues the *Antitrust Paradox* "dethroned consumer choice".

**Redistribution and inequality.** Philippon (*The Great Reversal*): rising US concentration and markups raised prices by at least 8% over seventeen years; weak enforcement and lobbying hurt consumers and workers. Contrast Hovenkamp's view that antitrust cannot redistribute.

**Chirita's work.**
- *A Legal-Historical Review of the EU Competition Rules* (2014) 63 ICLQ 281: a historical-purposive reading of the drafting of the Treaty of Rome (from the Schuman plan and ECSC Treaty) used to answer contemporary questions: the goals of competition law, the object/effect distinction, a possible enforcement gap under Art 102, the link between unfair competition and non-discrimination, and the broader meaning of "distortion".
- *Competition policy's social paradox* (2018) European Competition Journal: challenges the rhetoric of efficiency as a goal; efficiency-driven, fierce competition sacrifices values like job creation; job cuts follow mergers; proposes reshaping the efficiency defence in mergers to focus on job creation (or at least balancing jobs lost and created) and opposes social dumping. The same point is made that inefficiency destroys jobs but efficiency does not always create them.
- *Neo-Brandeis and Antitrust: an Olive Branch for Consensus* (2024) Network Law Review: seeks consensus in the polarised US debate by widening consumer welfare beyond material prosperity to include consumer well-being and "sustainable efficiency", distinguishing core values from antitrust goals.

**Sustainability and other new goals.** The discussion quotes Simon Holmes (competition policy must play its part against climate change), the CMA's sustainability agreements guidance (January 2021) and CEO Sarah Cardell's emphasis on "fair deals" and sustainable growth, and the Commission's green-transition policy. See sustainability-and-competition-law.md.

**Legal formalism versus context.** Hovenkamp criticises formalism (law as a "closed system"); Posner contrasts legalism with pragmatic, consequence-based judging. The discussion frames competition law against its historical, economic, social and political context and identify teleological and dynamic interpretation in the case law.

**Essay framing.** Useful angles include: Bork's *Antitrust Paradox* and the legitimacy of consumer welfare and efficiency as EU goals, set against Commissioner Kroes's 2005 statement that the aim is to protect competition "as a means of enhancing consumer welfare and ensuring an efficient allocation of resources"; Bork's "two theories" of injury to competition. A useful question is whether the limits of competition law are tested by the Big Data sharing economy or by the green agenda, and what goal UK/EU law should pursue.

## Applying it to a problem question
Goals rarely decide a problem question alone, but they shape the arguments:
1. **Identify the regime(s):** UK (CA 1998, ERRA 2013 s 25(3) consumer-benefit duty, DMCCA 2024 for digital), EU (Arts 101/102 TFEU, Art 3(3) TEU, Protocol 27), and US if relevant (Sherman Act ss 1–2).
2. **Theory of harm:** is the concern direct consumer harm (higher prices, lower quality/choice, privacy) or harm to the competitive structure (exclusion of rivals)? Under EU law, harm to structure suffices (*Continental Can*, *Post Danmark I*, *TeliaSonera*); there is no need to prove direct consumer harm.
3. **Justifications:** efficiency arguments should be measured against the consumer-benefit standard. Social or environmental goals (sustainability, employment) are increasingly raised. Check whether the forum accepts them (see exemptions-article-101-3-and-section-9.md and sustainability-and-competition-law.md).
4. **Fairness:** EU law refers to fair competition, but UK courts treat fairness cautiously as a justiciable rule (*Gallaher* para 31).
5. **Trap:** do not assert that EU law follows Bork's total-welfare standard. The CJEU cases speak of undistorted competition, structure and well-being.
6. **MCQ points:** the Lisbon Treaty refers to a social market economy and (via Protocol 27) undistorted competition. The case law says the rules prevent distortion "to the detriment of the public interest, individual undertakings and consumers". The "no colourable support" quotation is Bork's.
