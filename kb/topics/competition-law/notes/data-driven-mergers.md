# Data-driven mergers and digital conglomerates

## Overview
Large technology firms have bought platforms whose main asset is user data ("big data"). Under the EU Merger Regulation (139/2004) the substantive test is the significant impediment to effective competition (SIEC), which looks at market power, prices, output and innovation, not at privacy or data protection. Most of the reviewed deals were cleared, often without conditions. Chirita (2020 chapter) argues that the standard analysis under-assesses harm from data accumulation, that data protection and consumer law are not adequate substitutes, and that public-interest grounds such as Art 21(4) EUMR should be used. This note supplements merger-control-substantive-assessment.md.

## The law and the practice
**1. Deals reviewed.** Google/DoubleClick (COMP M 4731, 2008; $3.1bn), TomTom/Tele Atlas (M 4854, 2008), Microsoft/Yahoo! Search (M 5727, 2010), Microsoft/Skype (M 6281, 2011; $8.5bn), Facebook/WhatsApp (M 7217, 2014; $19bn), Publicis/Omnicom (M 7023, 2014, collapsed), Telefonica UK/Vodafone UK/Everything Everywhere JV, Sanofi/Google JV, IMS/Cegedim and Microsoft/LinkedIn (M 8124, 2017; $26bn). All involved large user datasets.

**2. Data as the price of "free" services.** Users get a free service and pay with their data. Platforms are two- or multi-sided (a free side attracts users, the other side monetises, e.g. advertisers). Chirita argues the transaction remains a bilateral contract in law and that data has become a currency. The *Google Shopping* decision accepted that offering a service free can be a legitimate strategy for two-sided platforms. In *Publicis/Omnicom* the Commission distinguished marketing information, market research and media measurement and described big data analytics.

**3. Market definition.** In most decisions the Commission left the exact market open (a "Kaplowian" approach: Kaplow argues that market definition is unnecessary work), though it examined narrower markets (consumer communications apps divided by function, operating system and platform in *Facebook/WhatsApp* and *Microsoft/Skype*; search versus non-search advertising in *Microsoft/Yahoo*; digital map databases in *TomTom/Tele Atlas*). Market shares in fast-moving markets were treated as ephemeral. Chirita criticises over-reliance on market shares for innovative platforms from a Schumpeterian viewpoint, and the difficulty of inferring market power from shares in re-defined markets.

**4. Barriers to entry and switching.** Low barriers in *Facebook/WhatsApp* (despite portability and interoperability issues, network effects were not enough to shield the merged firm) and *Microsoft/Skype*; very high barriers in *Microsoft/Yahoo Search* (about $1bn investment); manageable switching costs in *Google/DoubleClick* and low in *Publicis/Omnicom*; reconfiguration costs only in *TomTom/Tele Atlas* though collecting map data is costly. The article says the most important barriers to data collection and sharing are in fact data protection and privacy laws.

**5. Innovation incentives.** The Commission assumed in *Facebook/WhatsApp* and *Microsoft/Skype* that short innovation cycles and easy switching would discipline the merged firm. Chirita argues that predictions have often proved inaccurate and that platforms can lose users through reputational damage (e.g. data breaches) rather than innovation. In *Microsoft/Yahoo Search* the Commission accepted Yahoo might lose the ability to innovate but thought the new platform would compete with Google.

**6. Theories of harm in the decisions (Chirita's map).**
- *Degradation of quality and targeted advertising.* Examined in *Facebook/WhatsApp* (change of privacy policy to use WhatsApp data for advertising) and *Microsoft/Yahoo Search* (degradation of organic search); dismissed because users could switch and many alternative advertising data sources exist. In *TomTom/Tele Atlas* the Commission found the merged entity likely able to increase prices, degrade quality or delay access for rivals, with confidentiality concerns later dismissed. In *Google/DoubleClick* degradation or bundling was also considered.
- *Horizontal effects.* Dismissed in *Microsoft/Skype* (not significant), *Facebook/WhatsApp* (only Facebook active in online advertising), the Telefonica JV and *IMS/Cegedim* (low shares), though effects were identified in syndicated primary market research.
- *Conglomerate effects (tying, bundling, loss of interoperability).* In *Microsoft/Skype* the Commission identified leverage from Windows and Office but the General Court in *Cisco and Messagenet v Commission* (T-79/12) found the theory "complex and abstract", noting conglomerates do not usually generate competition concerns. Dismissed also in *Microsoft/Yahoo Search*, *Publicis/Omnicom* and the Telefonica JV; *Sanofi/Google/DMI JV* found no leverageable position.
- *Exclusionary effects of data.* Foreclosure theories failed because rivals can collect similar data: in *Google/DoubleClick* DoubleClick's data was "relatively narrow" and not unique or non-replicable; in *Publicis/Omnicom* rivals had comparable access to big data analytics.

**7. Public interest route: Art 21(4) EUMR.** Member States may take appropriate measures to protect legitimate interests other than those considered by the Regulation, compatible with Community law; public security, plurality of the media and prudential rules are recognised, and "any other public interest" must be communicated to the Commission and assessed case by case. Chirita argues privacy considerations of economic weight could be a legitimate interest, so that no merger involving large amounts of user data should be cleared unconditionally; she accepts blocking on public-interest grounds is a last resort and open to criticism as political.

**8. UK and the later cases (from the materials).** The tables show EU and UK decisions involving the "big five" groups: EU clearances such as Facebook/WhatsApp, Google/DoubleClick, Microsoft/Skype, Apple/Shazam, Microsoft/GitHub, Amazon/MGM; conditions in Microsoft/LinkedIn, Meta/Kustomer, Google/Fitbit and Microsoft/Activision Blizzard (2023); Amazon/iRobot in EU Phase II (2023) and CMA Phase I clearance. CMA outcomes: Facebook/Giphy Phase II prohibition; Microsoft/Activision (streaming rights): Phase I undertakings in lieu and a Phase II prohibition in April 2023; Facebook/Instagram OFT Phase I clearance; Amazon/Deliveroo Phase II clearance.

## Key authorities
- Regulation 139/2004 (EUMR), including Art 21(4).
- Commission decisions as above; *Cisco Systems and Messagenet v Commission* (T-79/12).
- CMA decisions: *Facebook/Giphy*; *Microsoft/Activision*.
- EDPS Opinion (2016) on effective enforcement in a digital economy, urging cooperation between regulators.

## Debate and critique
- **Exclusion versus exploitation.** The focus has been on whether rivals can access data, not on exploitative harm to users from data use; extend foreclosure analysis to consumer harm (Chirita).
- **Consumer law and data protection fallacies.** Consumer law cannot control monopoly power or mergers; data protection law (GDPR from 25 May 2018) cannot block mergers, and a consent-based compliance checklist can be satisfied while users still have no real choice given weak bargaining power.
- **Quality and privacy as dimensions of competition.** Whether degraded privacy is a form of quality degradation. The Commission treated privacy issues as outside the Regulation ("without prejudice" to data protection law). A commentator (Holles de Peyer, cited) rejects consideration of privacy in mergers.
- **Under-enforcement of conglomerate theories.** The General Court's scepticism in *Cisco* against the later growth of conglomerates (the "growth of conglomerates" slides) and the later CMA prohibitions.
- **Economic versus political decisions.** The author says merger decisions are mostly economic and only exceptionally based on legal interpretation, and that privacy has economic weight.
- **Institutional cooperation.** the conclusion: better coordination among data, consumer and competition authorities.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Does the transaction meet EU or UK jurisdiction thresholds (see merger-procedure-and-jurisdiction.md)? Target may have low turnover but high data value, so consider share of supply and the UK test.
2. Market definition: identify all sides of the platform and do not assume zero price means no market; check whether the market can be left open.
3. Theories of harm: horizontal (overlap in data or advertising), vertical (data as input foreclosure), conglomerate (tying, bundling, loss of interoperability, leveraging data across markets), loss of innovation or potential competition, degradation of privacy as quality.
4. Evidence: replicability of the data, availability of alternative data sources, barriers to entry, switching and multi-homing, network effects, innovation cycles.
5. Remedies: divestiture, interoperability or data access commitments, conditions (as in Microsoft/LinkedIn, Google/Fitbit).
6. Public interest and privacy: Art 21(4) EUMR for Member States; consider whether regulators (DMA, DMCCA) now cover conduct (see digital-markets-regulation-dma-dmcca.md).
