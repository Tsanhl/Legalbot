# Sustainability and competition law

## Overview
Climate change and environmental harm have prompted the question whether competition law should accommodate, or even encourage, cooperation between competitors that reduces environmental damage. Such cooperation can look like a cartel (agreed standards, phase-outs, shared information) but may produce benefits that lie outside the relevant market and that traditional consumer-welfare analysis does not count. The debate focuses on how Art 101(3) TFEU and s 9 of the Competition Act 1998 treat "green" agreements, and whether "consumers" and "fair share" should be reinterpreted. Policy documents of the CMA (2021 guidance on environmental sustainability agreements) and the European Commission (2020 Report on Competition Policy) are the background. The material in the knowledge base is largely scholarly and so the note focuses on arguments.

## The law
**1. Starting point.** An agreement that restricts competition between rivals falls within Art 101(1) / Chapter I and is void unless exempted. Sustainability initiatives (joint standards, withdrawal of polluting products, codes of conduct) are therefore assessed under the usual tests (object or effect; four cumulative exemption conditions: efficiency, fair share to consumers, indispensability, no elimination of competition). See object-and-effect-restrictions.md and exemptions-article-101-3-and-section-9.md.

**2. The traditional fair-share condition.** Van Dijk (Dutch ACM chief economist): traditionally only consumers in the same relevant market as the cooperating competitors count, since they bear the harm, and "fair share" means they must be fully compensated, so that the net effect for them is at least neutral. This works for price cartels (no benefit, no exemption) and for R&D cooperation (benefits go to the same consumers) but works badly for a third category: cooperation to reduce negative externalities (emissions) whose benefits go far beyond the consumers of the product.

**3. Earlier practice recognising environmental benefits (Chirita's survey).** The written evidence to the CMA lists Commission exemptions where environmental gains featured in the technical or economic progress analysis, with the consistent finding that effective competition remained:
- *ACEC/Berliet* (1968): exclusive R&D for an electric bus engine, reducing pollution and CO2.
- *Carbon Gas Technologies* (1983): pooling of coal gasification know-how to reduce oil dependence.
- *BBC Brown Boveri/NGK* (1988): exclusive R&D for sodium-sulphur batteries for electric vehicles, ten years of exclusivity to recover costs.
- *Assurpol* (1992): co-reinsurance of environmental damage risks.
- *Ford/Volkswagen* (1993): joint venture for a multi-purpose vehicle with less hazardous materials and higher recyclability.
- *Exxon/Shell* (1994): joint venture for polyethene with reduced raw material and plastic waste.
- *Philips/Osram* (1994): joint venture to cut lead emissions and energy use.
- 1998-2000: voluntary standby-power reduction by consumer electronics makers, ACEA's CO2 emission commitments, noise and emission research, the UK Valpak packaging waste scheme, and *CECED* (2000), which retired low-efficiency washing machines and estimated CO2, sulphur and nitrous oxide savings and consumer payback within months.
- *Der Grüne Punkt/DSD* (2001): exclusivity for household packaging take-back.
She also notes the *Consumer Detergents* decision (2011), where a code of good environmental practice was used for unnecessary price discussions (a hard-core restriction), and the 2019 preliminary view that BMW, Daimler and VW colluded on emissions technologies; and says that such initiatives must be distinguished from disguised collusion.

**4. UK context.** Chirita's evidence responds to the CMA's September 2021 call for inputs and focuses on the Chapter I prohibition and s 9: environmental efficiencies could be recognised as quantitative or qualitative efficiencies passed on to consumers if the restriction is indispensable and does not eliminate competition. She proposes revised guidance on environmental efficiencies and a public register of environmental initiatives so authorities can distinguish genuine from sham greening.

**5. United States.** Brigham ("green product fixing") argues that US case law treats self-regulation through extra-governmental product standards as a Sherman Act violation, and that reductions in carbon emissions or pollution are not currently counted as procompetitive benefits; he proposes balancing the traditional analysis with environmental benefits that reach consumers as members of society.

## Key authorities
- Art 101(3) TFEU; CA 1998 s 9.
- CMA, Guidance on environmental sustainability agreements and competition law (27 January 2021); Commission Report on Competition Policy 2020, COM (2021) 373 (as cited by Chirita).
- Commission decisions listed above, notably *CECED*, *Exxon/Shell*, *Ford/Volkswagen*.
- Holmes, Middelschulte and Snoep (eds), *Competition Law, Climate Change & Environmental Sustainability* (Concurrences 2021): foreword by Frans Timmermans on the Green Deal; introduction by Suzanne Kingston posing questions such as whether competitors may agree to produce more energy-efficient goods at higher prices.
- Chirita, written evidence to the CMA (2022); van Dijk (ACM) in the Concurrences volume; Brigham (US comment).

## Debate and critique
- **Chirita.** Sustainability is an objective of competition policy; the field should move from consumer welfare alone to "ethical competition" and the greening of production, distribution, marketing and sale, without suspending competition rules or deregulating. Competition law has long engaged with environmental protection (the 1970s Commission reports and exemptions) but with fluctuating activity. She contrasts this with Bork's statement that competition has no ethical component and that antitrust cannot decide how much wealth to spend on reducing pollution.
- **Van Dijk.** Arguments for a new reading of "consumers" and "fair share" for sustainability agreements: urgency of emissions reduction and need for private initiatives; the traditional test ignores wider social benefits; sustainability is itself a dimension of competition; the polluter-pays principle (consumers of polluting goods bear the cost of reducing externalities); and a more abstract view that victims of pollution are "consumers" in a market for negative external effects. Arguments against: the slippery slope (employment or "national champions" could then claim the same), the risk of crowding out public regulation, and the need for modesty about competition policy's role. He supports the ACM's draft guidelines on sustainability agreements to take out-of-market benefits into account in well-defined circumstances.
- **Wider goals.** Whether economic efficiency alone is the standard (see exemptions note); Chirita's drafting-history reading supports flexibility.
- **Risk of greenwashing as cartel cover.** *Consumer Detergents* and the German carmakers case show enforcement concerns.
- **Regulation versus competition.** Chirita notes that competition awareness of green initiatives can alleviate but not remove the climate crisis, which needs effective regulation.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the agreement (competitors agreeing standards, phasing out products, sharing information about green technology) and classify it: is it a hard-core restriction (price, output, customer allocation) or an ancillary or minor restraint?
2. Does it restrict competition by object or effect? Look for exchanges of sensitive information or price coordination hidden in a green initiative (*Consumer Detergents*).
3. If restrictive, test the exemption: (a) efficiency (cleaner production, technical progress), (b) fair share to consumers: are in-market consumers compensated, or are benefits out-of-market (van Dijk's debate)? (c) indispensability: are less restrictive means available, such as individual targets (as in the ACEA CO2 commitment where each member kept its own target)? (d) competition not eliminated.
4. Note the evidence needed: cogent empirical evidence that the environmental benefit is linked to the restriction (see exemptions note).
5. Evaluate policy: whether current law accommodates the case, and whether guidance or reform is preferable.
