# Restrictions by object and by effect (Article 101(1) TFEU and Chapter I)

## Overview
Art 101(1) TFEU and the Chapter I prohibition in the Competition Act 1998 catch agreements, decisions and concerted practices that have as their "object or effect" the prevention, restriction or distortion of competition. The two are alternatives. If an agreement has an anti-competitive object, there is no need to prove its effects; if not, effects must be examined. The line between the two, and how much context is needed to classify a restriction as "by object", has been a major battleground since *Groupement des Cartes Bancaires*. the materials also compares the EU approach with the US per se and rule of reason, and with the "ancillary restraints" doctrine.

## The law
**1. Alternative conditions.** *Société Technique Minière v Maschinenbau Ulm* (Case 56/65): object and effect are not cumulative. The court looks at the precise purpose of the agreement in its economic context: the nature and quantity of the products, the position and importance of the parties, the nature of the agreement or its place in a series of agreements, and the severity of clauses giving protection. Later cases repeat that regard must be had to content, objectives and economic and legal context (*GlaxoSmithKline*, C-501/06 P, para 58; *Expedia* C-226/11, para 21; *Groupement des Cartes Bancaires (GCB)* C-67/13 P, para 53 referring to *Allianz Hungária* C-32/11, para 36).

**2. Restrictions by object.** The test is whether the content, objectives and context reveal "a sufficient degree of harm" to competition, so that it is redundant to prove effects (*BIDS* C-209/07; *T-Mobile* C-8/08; *GCB*). Certain collusion is by its nature harmful to the proper functioning of normal competition. Examples in the handout:
- Price-fixing and market-sharing, control of outlets (*European Night Services*, *Toshiba*, *Dole Food*).
- Exchanges of information capable of removing uncertainty about intended conduct (*T-Mobile*), including pre-pricing communications (*Bananas*). the materials quotes Kaplow that public communications can be as revealing as secret meetings.
- Encouraging competitors to leave the market and cut capacity (*BIDS*, where the arrangement aimed to remove about 75 per cent of excess capacity).
- Agreements limiting parallel trade, such as dual pricing for export (*GlaxoSmithKline*; also *Consten and Grundig*, *Miller International*, *BMW Belgium*, *Sot. Lelos kai Sia*); the CJEU held that consumers need not be shown to be deprived of the advantages of effective competition to find a restrictive object (*GSK* para 63).
- Fixed or minimum resale prices (the materials cites *Pronuptia*).
- Online sales bans and the like: *Pierre Fabre* (a de facto internet ban restricts passive sales: object); *Guess* (written authorisation for online sales, aimed at protecting the supplier's own site: object); *Ping v CMA* [2020] EWCA (ban on online sales of golf clubs: object, absent objective justification).
- Bilateral agreements between insurers and car repairers that could eliminate or seriously weaken competition (*Allianz Hungária*).

**3. Consequences of an object finding.**
- No need to take account of concrete effects (*Consten and Grundig*). Actual restriction need not occur. A concerted practice has an anti-competitive object where it has the potential to harm competition (*T-Mobile*).
- AG Kokott in *T-Mobile* (as quoted): an object restriction is not merely a rebuttable presumption; analogy with a drunk driver who commits an offence irrespective of whether anyone was endangered; actual effects matter only for fines and damages.
- Appreciability is automatic: an agreement that may affect trade and has an anti-competitive object is by its nature an appreciable restriction (*Expedia*), so the de minimis notice (market share thresholds 10 per cent between competitors, 15 per cent between non-competitors, default 10 per cent, 5 per cent for cumulative effect of parallel networks, as set out in the materials footnote) does not help.
- Intention is not required but may be considered (*GSK*, *GCB* para 54).

**4. Limits on "object": Groupement des Cartes Bancaires.** The CJEU held that the General Court wrongly treated the object as obvious from the formulae for the fees (a surcharge on members rapidly expanding their acquiring business and a charge on dormant members) without examining the degree of harm; free-riding was recognised as a legitimate concern. AG Wahl: only conduct whose harmful nature is proven and easily identifiable in the light of experience and economics should be by object; agreements with ambivalent effects, or ancillary restrictions necessary for a main objective, are not restrictive by object. The analysis of context should be limited to what is strictly necessary (*GCB* para 53, citing *Toshiba*). *Maxima Latvija* (C-345/14): a clause in commercial leases letting a tenant oppose other tenants was not among agreements harmful by their very nature; effect analysis was needed. *Delimitis v Henninger Bräu* (C-234/89): exclusive purchase commitments are assessed with the cumulative effect of similar contracts (the materials says object was not to restrict competition). *Metro SB-Grossmärkte* (Case 26/76): selective distribution is outside Art 101(1) where criteria are qualitative, objective and applied non-discriminatorily. *Coty* restated the *Metro* criteria for luxury goods. *Ping* (Rose LJ): *GCB* is the leading authority; *Pierre Fabre* did not create a special proportionality-based assessment for internet sales bans, and the CAT saw no contradiction between *Pierre Fabre* and *GCB*.

**5. Restrictions by effect.** If the content does not show sufficient harm, the court must consider the effects: that competition has been prevented, restricted or distorted to an appreciable extent (*GSK*). *STM*: use a counterfactual, what would have happened in the absence of the agreement. Example: *Sainsbury's Supermarkets v MasterCard* (CAT 2016; UKSC 2020 in the raw files): the UK MasterCard multilateral interchange fee was a price-fixing arrangement the OFT regarded as having a restrictive object, but ultimately was treated as a restriction by effect (the CAT: restriction by effect, not object). The analysis of effects looked at the default nature of the fee and rejected confining the inquiry to the acquiring market.

**6. Ancillary restraints.** A restriction directly related and objectively necessary to a main non-restrictive operation, and proportionate to it, falls outside Art 101(1) (Guidelines on Art 101(3), paras 28-31; *Métropole Télévision* T-112/99). The CAT used the four-step objective necessity test in *Sainsbury's* following the CJEU's *MasterCard* ruling: (i) a main operation not caught by the prohibition; (ii) an ancillary restriction inherent in that operation; (iii) without the restriction the main operation could not be carried out; (iv) necessary and proportionate. The UK MIF failed (a perfectly viable bilateral fee would have been agreed). This is not a rule of reason weighing of pro- and anti-competitive effects.

**7. Comparative: rule of reason and per se (US).** the materials explains that the US rule of reason (*Standard Oil* 1911) and per se rule developed through judicial experience; "naked" restraints (raising price or reducing output without integrative benefit, such as cartels) are condemned per se, and ancillary restraints are assessed under the rule of reason with a staged analysis (Hovenkamp's steps: output or price threat; naked or ancillary; market power; efficiencies; less restrictive alternatives; balancing). The NCAA television case condemned a naked limit on televised games. the materials Judge Brandeis' version has been among the costliest procedures.

## Key authorities
- *Société Technique Minière* (56/65): alternative conditions; counterfactual.
- *Consten and Grundig* (56 and 58/64): absolute territorial protection; effects unnecessary where object shown.
- *GlaxoSmithKline* (C-501/06 P): parallel trade limits; consumer harm not required.
- *T-Mobile* (C-8/08): potential to harm; information exchange. AG Kokott's opinion.
- *Groupement des Cartes Bancaires* (C-67/13 P): narrowing and context.
- *Allianz Hungária*, *Expedia*, *Maxima Latvija*, *Toshiba*: related guidance.
- *Pierre Fabre*, *Coty*, *Ping v CMA*, *Guess*: online sales bans.
- *Sainsbury's v MasterCard*: effects and ancillary restraints.

## Debate and critique
- **Presumption or rule?** Whether object means a rebuttable presumption (the CAT or GC approach) or an irrebuttable classification (Kokott). the materials tracks tension between formalism and economic effects, and the risk of mingling the two limbs.
- **How much context?** *GCB* requires an examination of context, yet only as strictly necessary. Critics (see the handout's summary of Wahl) want object limited to restrictions with clear, experience-based harm.
- **Internet bans.** *Pierre Fabre* arguably extended object to a sector-specific restraint, while *Metro/Coty* permits selective distribution for luxury; Ping shows the tension. the materials AG Mazák rejected public health and free-riding justifications.
- **Information exchange.** The reach of public communications as concerted practice (Kaplow).
- **US v EU.** The EU has no formal per se rule but treats object as functionally similar; the US rule of reason is criticised for cost. The EU ancillary restraint doctrine is not a rule of reason.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an agreement, decision or concerted practice between undertakings (see article-101-agreements-and-concerted-practices.md)?
2. Classify: horizontal price-fixing, market sharing, output limits, bid-rigging, information exchange removing uncertainty, export bans or limiting parallel trade, resale price maintenance, online ban. These are likely object restrictions. State the test: content, objectives, economic and legal context show sufficient harm (*GCB*).
3. If the restriction is ambiguous or ancillary (exclusive purchasing, lease clauses, selective distribution on qualitative criteria), consider effect: counterfactual, market structure, cumulative effects (*Delimitis*), market power.
4. Check ancillarity (necessary and proportionate to a legitimate main operation).
5. Appreciability: automatic for object; for effects look at market shares and the de minimis notice.
6. Then consider exemption (see exemptions-article-101-3-and-section-9.md), noting object restrictions rarely qualify.
7. Trap: do not require proof of consumer harm for object; do not treat object as incapable of any justification (but justification arises mostly at the Art 101(3) stage, and for selective distribution at the *Metro* stage).
