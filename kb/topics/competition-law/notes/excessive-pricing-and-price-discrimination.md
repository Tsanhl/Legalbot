# Excessive Pricing and Price Discrimination (Exploitative Abuse)

## Overview
Art 102(a) TFEU and s 18(2)(a) CA 1998 expressly prohibit "directly or indirectly imposing unfair purchase or selling prices or other unfair trading conditions". Art 102(c) / s 18(2)(c) prohibit "applying dissimilar conditions to equivalent transactions with other trading parties, thereby placing them at a competitive disadvantage". These are the basis for **exploitative** abuses (charging customers too much) and **discrimination**. EU enforcement has mostly focused on exclusion. The UK has become the main arena for excessive pricing, through a run of CMA pharmaceutical cases (*Phenytoin*/Pfizer–Flynn, *Liothyronine*/Advanz, *Hydrocortisone*/Allergan) and CAT and Court of Appeal judgments applying and refining the *United Brands* test. Digital markets add personalised (behavioural) price discrimination and "zero-price" exploitation through data. This note covers both.

## The law

### 1. The United Brands test for excessive/unfair prices (EU, applied in the UK)
From *United Brands v Commission* (Case 27/76), as applied by the CMA and CAT:
1. **Limb 1 – excessiveness:** is the difference between the costs actually incurred and the price actually charged excessive? Costs are split into direct costs, indirect costs and a reasonable rate of return.
2. **Limb 2 – unfairness:** if so, is the price unfair **either in itself or when compared with competing products**?
- The aim is to capture "trading benefits" that could not be obtained under normal and sufficiently effective competition, and a price that "bears no reasonable relation to the economic value" of the product ([2023] CAT 56, para 325).
- *United Brands* does not prescribe a particular cost benchmark and is not a "bright-line" test ([2023] CAT 52, para 229(4); [2023] CAT 56, para 324). It does not require comparing the benefits of competition with the harm from excessive prices ([2023] CAT 52, para 321).

### 2. UK case law
- ***Napp*** (CAT [2002] CAT 1, paras 390–392): prices are excessive if higher than would be expected in a competitive market with no effective competitive pressure to bring them down, and none likely. Methods include comparing price with cost, with the costs of the next most profitable competitor, and across markets.
- ***Attheraces v British Horseracing Board*** [2007] EWCA Civ 38: Mummery LJ held that abuse of dominance is concerned with distortion of competition and consumers' interests. It is not about suppliers making "excessive profits" over a reasonable return on cost, nor a law under which courts fix a "fair price". Limb 1 alone is not sufficient, because sellers must earn a proper return, true costs are hard to calculate, and producer surplus is to be encouraged. With *BHB Enterprises v Victor Chandler* [2005] EWHC 1074, charging above cost of production was held not abusive without consideration of all market conditions.
- ***Phenytoin* (Pfizer and Flynn):** the CMA fined about £90m (handbook) for excessive prices for a de-branded anti-epilepsy drug supplied to the NHS. The CMA found prices "unfair in themselves", bearing no reasonable relation to economic value, but disregarded market-comparator evidence. The CAT held the *United Brands* test had been misapplied. The CMA should have considered a realistic benchmark and evidence of comparators. The Court of Appeal (*CMA v Flynn Pharma* [2020] EWCA Civ 339, as cited in the handout; the goals material cites [2020] EWCA Civ 617) agreed that comparator evidence was needed, but held that the CMA has discretion over costing method and evidence. Economic value relates to willingness to pay, but customers' willingness to pay "cannot serve as an adequate definition in an abuse case", since otherwise value would be whatever an exploitative dominant firm could get away with. The CMA did not need to establish a hypothetical "workably competitive" price.
- ***Advanz Pharma* (Liothyronine) – *HG Capital, Cinven, Mercury, Advanz v CMA* [2023] CAT 52:**
  - Advanz bought Tertroxin for £1m in 1992 and de-branded it in 2007 to escape price regulation. It raised prices from £4.05 per pack (2007) to £247.87 (July 2017). NHS spend went from about £600,000 a year to over £30m by 2016. Comparators: £1.58 in the Czech Republic, £5.84 in Sweden.
  - The CMA fined £101m (Advanz £40.9m, Cinven £51.9m, HG Capital £8.6m). Advanz held a 100% share protected by regulatory barriers.
  - **Limb 1:** excess of 900% per pack in 2009, rising to about 2,450% in 2017. **Limb 2:** "unfair in themselves" given the disparity between price and economic and therapeutic value, a 6,021% increase over a decade, and the lack of regulatory or competitive constraint.
  - **Goals:** the CAT rejected the argument that s 18 protects only competition as an institution. Following *British Airways* and *London & South Eastern Railway v Gutmann* [2022] EWCA Civ 1077, protection of customers against exploitation is a core aim. Unfair pricing is a key harm, often more immediate and certain than other forms (paras 109, 318, 451).
  - **Defences rejected:** minor product improvements (dosages, shelf life, lactose-free, security of supply) were disproportionately small (para 321). There was no increase in costs or product improvement (para 428). Entry-incentivising prices were unreliable as a benchmark (paras 283, 306). Legal advice is no escape (para 431).
  - **Fine:** a 30% starting point was appropriate given the serious exploitative effect (para 446). The "unprecedented" penalty argument carried little weight (para 449). Director involvement justified a 10% uplift (para 456).
- ***Allergan* (Hydrocortisone) – *Allergan, Amdipharm, Advanz, Cinven, Auden McKenzie, Intas, Accord v CMA* [2023] CAT 56:**
  - £155.2m fine for 10mg and 20mg tablets, with duration multipliers of 10 and 8.25.
  - The CAT warned about inferring dominance from pricing power (para 268) and about courts determining "market prices" (para 300). The objective is to protect competition, not to impose outcomes inconsistent with properly operating markets (para 327(2)(i)).
  - Even so, prices above **Cost Plus** were abusive given an "extreme mismatch" between cost and price. Entrepreneurs should not expect profits above Cost Plus through illegitimate exploitation of market power (para 376).
  - Portfolio pricing (cross-subsidy) was rejected as a justification (para 352). The penalty was reduced pro rata for duration (para 377(2)).
  - On costs the CAT, following *Socrates Training* [2017] CAT 10, *Genzyme* and *Phenytoin*, rejected value-based allocation of common costs as circular. It accepted the CMA's return-on-capital-employed approach.

### 3. Price discrimination
- **Secondary-line discrimination (Art 102(c)):** applying different conditions to equivalent transactions, harming customers' competitiveness downstream. Fidelity rebates are also discriminatory in this sense (two buyers pay different prices for the same quantity depending on exclusivity; *Hoffmann-La Roche*).
- ***Post Danmark I* (C-209/10):** lower prices to a rival's customers. This is treated as exclusionary, assessed against incremental cost and the as-efficient competitor (see predatory-pricing-and-margin-squeeze.md).
- **Digital / behavioural discrimination:** algorithms using personal data (location, income, browsing, health) can segment consumers by willingness to pay. Chirita describes behavioural discrimination as "close to a contemporary interpretation of third-degree discrimination", where analytics identify user categories with different reservation prices.

## Debate and critique
- **Werden, "Exploitative abuse of a dominant position: a bad idea that now should be abandoned" (2021) 17 European Competition Journal.** Every exploitative abuse case is "a breach of faith in the market". Punishing exploitation weakens the rule of law because no rule or standard controls, so firms cannot know what is expected. Werden argues abandoning it would not disrespect Art 102's text, the drafters' intentions or Treaty goals. He notes the CJEU has used the term "exploitative abuse" only once (*Der Grüne Punkt*, C-385/07 P, para 32).
- **UK counter-trend.** The CAT in *Advanz* treats exploitation as a core aim and backs high fines. *Allergan* shows a Cost Plus benchmark can work where the mismatch is extreme. Caution remains (*Attheraces*; the CAT's warnings on price-setting by courts).
- **Pharma lifecycle.** An expert in *Advanz* described R&D, then exclusivity with high prices, then post-exclusivity price falls. Generic prices are typically 70% lower within six months, falling to 80–90% lower after four years. Liothyronine was an "outlier" caused by abuse and patient inertia.
- **Zero-price exploitation (Chirita).** Proving that a price of zero is "too high" under *United Brands* would be absurd (a probatio diabolica). The inquiry should instead target excessive disclosure of consumer data (procedural unfairness), consumers' loss of economic privacy (location, willingness to pay) and unfair terms.
- **Price discrimination: efficient or exploitative?**
  - Baker (*The Antitrust Paradigm*, 132): price discrimination is ubiquitous, especially on Internet platforms, and is often a natural way to recover high fixed costs of IT.
  - Ezrachi and Stucke (*Virtual Competition*, 115): Big Data enables segmentation approaching "perfect behavioural discrimination", and we may not know when we are victims.
  - Chirita: personalised price discrimination is an enforcement gap. Modern antitrust relied on classical theory. Vulnerable consumers are most at risk, so transparency and fairness are needed. As gatekeepers extract higher profits, the unfairness is exploitative.
- **Past exam topics:** online price discrimination as an abuse (Aug/Sept 2023); behavioural discrimination (Aug/Sept 2021); excessive pricing in pharma via s 18.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance:** high shares, regulatory barriers, captive customers (patients, locked-in users). Beware circularity: do not infer dominance solely from high prices (*Allergan*).
2. **Limb 1:** compare price with costs plus a reasonable return (Cost Plus / ROCE). Note facts such as "price above the market average since 2016" or "gradually increase subscription prices until further notice" (e.g. the Sunlight/Lodge cloud subscription question).
3. **Limb 2:** unfair in itself (no relation to economic value; huge increases without cost or quality changes), and/or compared with competing products or other markets (comparators across countries or rivals). Following *Flynn*, consider comparator evidence where available.
4. **Intent and documents:** memos planning price rises after a rival exits ("Step no 2: gradually increase our cloud subscription prices") support exploitation and exclusion together.
5. **Justifications:** investment recovery, quality improvements, R&D, entry incentives, portfolio pricing. Test proportionality (all rejected in *Advanz*/*Allergan* on the facts).
6. **Price discrimination:** identify different prices or terms for equivalent transactions. Is a downstream customer disadvantaged (Art 102(c))? Is the discrimination targeted at rivals' customers (exclusionary)? Is it algorithmic or personalised (exploitative)? For subscriptions (e.g. £150/£160 yearly AI or delivery packages), ask whether terms bundle data surrender with the price.
7. **Zero-price services:** reframe "price" as data. Argue unfair trading conditions (s 18(2)(a)) via excessive data collection or sharing (see abuse-of-consumer-data-and-privacy.md).
8. **Fine factors:** exploitative abuse is serious (a 30% starting point in *Advanz*). Director involvement is aggravating. Duration counts.
9. **Private action:** consumers or the NHS may claim damages, including UK CAT collective proceedings.
