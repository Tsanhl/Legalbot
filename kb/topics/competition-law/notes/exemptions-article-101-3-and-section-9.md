# Exemptions - Article 101(3) TFEU and section 9 Competition Act 1998

## Overview
An agreement that restricts competition by object or effect is not necessarily unlawful. Art 101(3) TFEU (EU) and s 9 of the Competition Act 1998 (UK, Chapter I) allow it to escape the prohibition if it brings efficiencies, passes a fair share to consumers, goes no further than necessary and leaves competition on the market. Without exemption a prohibited agreement is automatically void. Since Regulation 1/2003 the exception is directly applicable and decided by courts and authorities; the burden lies on the party relying on it. In practice object restrictions almost never qualify.

## The law
**1. The four cumulative conditions.** Art 101(3) provides that the prohibition may be declared inapplicable to an agreement (or category), decision or concerted practice which:
- (a) contributes to improving the production or distribution of goods or promoting technical or economic progress;
- (b) allows consumers a fair share of the resulting benefit;
- (c) does not impose restrictions that are not indispensable to those objectives;
- (d) does not afford the possibility of eliminating competition in respect of a substantial part of the products in question.
Section 9 CA 1998 replicates these conditions ("criteria for individual and block exemptions"). The UK Supreme Court summarises them in the same four steps in *Sainsbury's Supermarkets v Visa Europe Services* [2020] UKSC 24, para 107.

**2. How the system works.** Under Regulation 17/62 only the Commission could exempt individual agreements after notification. Regulation 1/2003 abolished notification and made the exception directly applicable, so national courts and authorities now apply it (*Sainsbury's* para 111). Any prohibited agreement that is not exempt is void. Exemption can be individual (self-assessment) or by block exemption, such as Regulation 2022/720 on vertical agreements: an exclusive distribution agreement is exempted if the supplier's and buyer's market shares each do not exceed 30 per cent of their relevant markets and no hard-core restrictions are present.

**3. Burden and standard of proof.** The party relying on exemption proves the four conditions (Regulation 1/2003, art 2; recital 5 leaves the standard to national law). In *Sainsbury's* the Supreme Court held that the standard is the civil balance of probabilities; the Court of Appeal's approach (nature of evidence informed by EU law and Commission practice; benefits must be causally linked to the restriction by facts and empirical analysis, not economic theory alone; a sufficiently direct causal link, Guidelines para 54) was not an enhanced standard of proof. The Supreme Court dismissed the schemes' appeal on this ground (paras 106-138).

**4. Condition one: efficiencies.** Must be objective advantages such as economies of scale (larger processing units, for example in petroleum) and scope (one firm producing several products). In *GlaxoSmithKline* (T-168/01) the court said the analysis must consider the factual arguments and evidence of the exemption request, including the nature of the sector. A transfer of wealth between producer and intermediary is not of interest to competition law, which is concerned with the final consumer's welfare (GC, as reported). Non-economic efficiencies are disputed; the materials lists past Commission practice: *CECED* (2000, phasing out least energy-efficient washing machines), *Exxon/Shell* (1994, reduced pollution as technical progress), *Matra Hachette* (maintenance of employment), *Metro I* (provision of employment), and *Ford/Volkswagen* (jobs and investment in a poor region and harmonious development).

**5. Condition two: fair share.** Consumers include all direct or indirect users, such as producers using inputs, wholesalers, retailers and final consumers (Guidelines). Traditionally only consumers in the same relevant market count, and they must be fully compensated so that the net effect is at least neutral (van Dijk, below). The benefit must be passed on in practice.

**6. Condition three: indispensability.** The restriction must be reasonably necessary to achieve the efficiencies. In *Sainsbury's* the CAT found the UK MIF was not necessary to the operation of the MasterCard scheme: bilateral negotiation between banks was a viable alternative. Severe restrictions generally fail the first three conditions (CMA, *Foster*, as cited in the handout).

**7. Condition four: no elimination of competition.** In *Sainsbury's* (CAT) the MIF eliminated competition: inter-market competition drove it upwards and inhibited bilateral negotiation. The CAT considered that benefits arising solely on the acquiring market could not outweigh the harm and that the MIF only works where cash is a substitute, which it is not online.

**8. Hard-core and object restrictions.** In *Ping v CMA* [2020] EWCA (Rose LJ) a hard-core restriction means the agreement as a whole does not benefit from block exemption and must be examined individually; no agreement is incapable of exemption in theory (*Matra Hachette*), but it is very unlikely an object restriction can satisfy all four criteria. The CMA in *Foster* said agreements with the object of restricting competition are very unlikely to benefit from individual exemption, and the party claiming it must provide the evidence. In *Ping* the CAT held the online ban could not benefit from individual exemption (no damage to brand or significant free-riding shown).

## Key authorities
- Art 101(3) TFEU; CA 1998 s 9; Regulation 1/2003 (art 2); Regulation 2022/720 (vertical block exemption); Commission Guidelines on the application of Art 101(3) (2004).
- *Sainsbury's Supermarkets v Visa/Mastercard* [2020] UKSC 24: burden, standard of proof, nature of evidence; CAT [2016] on the four conditions applied to the MIF.
- *GlaxoSmithKline* (T-168/01): efficiencies; wealth transfers.
- *CECED*, *Exxon/Shell*, *Matra Hachette*, *Metro I*, *Ford/Volkswagen*: non-narrow efficiencies.
- *Ping v CMA* [2020] EWCA; CMA, *Foster*: hard-core and object restrictions rarely exempted.

## Debate and critique
- **Efficiency only or wider goals?** the question is whether the defendant must show only economic efficiency, balanced against restrictive effects, or whether social, environmental and public health benefits can count. Chirita's study of drafting history says the original reference to "objectives of the treaty" invites flexibility in balancing other goals such as social ones. Her written evidence to the CMA surveys exemptions that rested on environmental benefits (see sustainability-and-competition-law.md).
- **Under-enforcement.** Chirita suggests the four cumulative conditions were meant to strengthen deterrence and that the supra-national individual exemption role was under-enforced.
- **Evidence burden.** Requiring cogent, empirical proof (*Sainsbury's*) makes exemption hard for defendants even though the standard remains balance of probabilities.
- **Effect of Regulation 1/2003.** Self-assessment gives less legal certainty to firms but allows national courts to apply Art 101(3).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Only reach exemption after finding an Art 101(1) / Chapter I restriction (see object-and-effect-restrictions.md).
2. Check block exemptions first: vertical agreements (Reg 2022/720: 30 per cent market-share caps on both sides and no hard-core restrictions such as RPM or restrictions on passive sales); other block exemptions. If hard-core, the whole agreement is outside the block exemption but can still be assessed individually.
3. Individual exemption: take each condition in order and say what evidence the parties must bring (efficiency, causal link, pass-on to consumers, indispensability, residual competition).
4. Identify the party with the burden (the defendant or the parties) and the standard (balance of probabilities, but empirical and cogent).
5. Conclude: object restrictions very unlikely to be exempt; effects-based agreements with documented efficiencies may be.
6. Trap: do not forget that failing exemption makes the agreement void and exposes the parties to fines and damages.
