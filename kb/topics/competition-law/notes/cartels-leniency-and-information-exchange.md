# Cartels - evidence, leniency, information exchange, cooperation agreements and the UK cartel offence

## Overview
Cartels are secret non-compete agreements among rivals. They sit at the hard end of the Art 101 TFEU / Chapter I Competition Act 1998 prohibition. The discussion covers how cartels are proved, how undertakings can escape or reduce liability (public distancing, leniency), what makes collusion easier, how information exchange and legitimate cooperation (R&D agreements) are treated, and the UK criminal cartel offence, ending with *Sainsbury's v MasterCard*. Jurisdiction: EU and UK (England and Wales).

## The law
### 1. What counts as a hard-core cartel
Hard-core cartels fix prices, share markets, restrict output, limit marketing, development or investment, and rig bids. The discussion groups the practices as price-fixing (identical or no discounts, fixed prices, fixed commissions or exchange fees), market-sharing (dividing national and European markets, cooperation to defend the home market against imports, sharing markets between Japanese and European firms), quotas (agreements on quantities, allocation of sales quotas, compensation schemes), and collusive tendering (exchange of information before tendering, allocating contracts by customer relationships, bid-rigging).

### 2. Proof: single overall agreements and evidence
- Cartels are typically oral and secret; hard evidence comes from videotape, confession or cooperation. Complex cartels of long duration are analysed as a **single overall agreement** (the discussion cites *Polypropylene* and *Limburgse Vinyl*).
- Evidential examples used: **Sasol** (a manuscript note of a May 2004 meeting with figures and arrows was read, in context, as an agreed slack-wax price increase); **Villeroy & Boch Austria** (a handwritten account of a September 1999 meeting prepared on the day by a company representative, contemporaneous and not made for the 2002 Leniency Notice application, did not need further corroboration); **Soliver** (meetings and notes, a record of a May 2002 telephone call about car-glass supply to named vehicle models; the applicant could reasonably assume the anti-competitive nature of the contacts, and sharp price rises indicated collusive contacts); **Aalberts Industries** (an isolated handwritten note of a call plus statements about a trade-fair meeting did not prove Simplex's participation, which could be an isolated incident).
- **Public distancing**: an undertaking is relieved of full responsibility if it publicly distances itself from the cartel (for instance where it does not abide by the outcome of the meetings).

### 3. Leniency
- **Commission (DG COMP)**: immunity from fines or a reduction for significant added value. Parties may file statements and submissions rather than attending to dictate oral statements. Conditions: cooperation, termination of the infringement, evidence.
- **CMA**: immunity from fines and from criminal prosecution for cooperating current or former employees and directors, and protection from director disqualification proceedings for cooperating directors.
- The discussion there is **no "one-stop-shop"** for leniency: applications must be made to each authority. The CMA offers whistleblower rewards of up to £250,000.

### 4. Factors facilitating collusion
High entry barriers; identical costs; no product differentiation; few firms; high concentration; slow technological advance; steady demand growth; low elasticity of demand; low frequency of sales.

### 5. Exchanges of information
An exchange of information may be used to predict future behaviour and coordinate conduct, or to support a cartel as a mechanism for monitoring or enforcing compliance. Authorities and cases: *Wood Pulp* (quarterly price announcements; parallel conduct cannot be inferred as concertation unless concertation is the only plausible explanation), *Aalborg Portland* (exchanges on prices, supply, demand), and *T-Mobile* (exchange between competitors capable of removing uncertainties about conduct is a restriction by object).

### 6. Cooperation agreements and R&D
Horizontal cooperation can raise concerns: loss of competition and higher prices; coordination through disclosure of strategic information; production or standardisation agreements shutting out third parties. For R&D agreements, the question is whether enough competing R&D pools remain. The R&D block exemption does not cover hard-core restrictions with the object of: restricting the parties' freedom to carry out R&D; limiting output or sales; fixing prices on sales of contract products or on licensing contract technology to third parties; restricting territories or customers to which the parties may passively sell; or refusing to meet demand from customers in their territories or making it hard for users or resellers to obtain contract products from other resellers.

### 7. The UK cartel offence (Enterprise Act 2002)
- **s 188(1)**: an individual commits an offence if they dishonestly agree with one or more others to make or implement, or cause to be made or implemented, arrangements between at least two undertakings that: fix a price for supply in the UK; limit or prevent supply or production in the UK; divide supply or customers between the undertakings; or are bid-rigging arrangements.
- **s 190**: on indictment, up to five years' imprisonment or a fine or both; on summary conviction, up to six months or a fine not exceeding the statutory maximum, or both.
- **s 188A**: circumstances where no offence is committed (the discussion gives the example where customers would be given relevant information about the arrangements before they enter into agreements).
- **s 188B** defences: the individual shows that at the time of the agreement they did not intend the nature of the arrangements to be concealed from customers or the CMA, or that before making the agreement they took reasonable steps to ensure the arrangements would be disclosed to professional legal advisers for advice.
- Examples: the **galvanised steel tanks** case (three individuals charged in 2014 with dishonestly agreeing to fix prices, share customers and rig bids); the **marine hose** cartel (a coordinator and two Dunlop Oil and Marine employees convicted at Southwark Crown Court on 10 June 2008; sentences of 3 years, 3 years and 2.5 years, with directors disqualified for 7, 7 and 5 years, and a costs order of £25,000 against one). Other CMA criminal and civil investigations (precast concrete drainage products, commercial vehicle manufacturers, air passenger fuel surcharge, rolled lead, groundworks, office design bid-rigging, estate agents) show the range.
- The discussion notes that the large EU cartel fines (figures of €2.9bn and €1.47bn are shown) dwarf typical CMA fines (for instance £9m for roofing lead, £36m for concrete drainage products).

### 8. Case study: *Sainsbury's Supermarkets v MasterCard* [2016] CAT
Sainsbury's claimed damages under Chapter I and Art 101 for the UK multilateral interchange fee (MIF) paid by acquirers to issuers and passed on to merchants. MasterCard argued the MIF was objectively necessary and proportionate. The OFT in 2005 had treated the UK MIF as a price-fixing agreement restricting competition (a default provision where no bilateral agreement exists). The CAT applied a four-part objective necessity test: (i) an operation not itself caught because of its pro-competitive effect (the scheme); (ii) a restriction inherent in but ancillary to it; (iii) the operation could not be carried out without the restriction; (iv) proportionality. It found a bilateral interchange fee would have been agreed without the MIF, so the UK MIF was not inherently necessary. It was a restriction by effect, and exemption was "very difficult to see": it was not necessary or indispensable, benefits were not passed on, and it eliminated competition. Sainsbury's recovered £68,582 (credit card overcharge) and £760,406 (debit cards) plus interest. The discussion on *MasterCard Inc.* add that a restriction of commercial autonomy falls outside the prohibition only if objectively necessary to a main operation and proportionate, meaning the operation would be impossible without it; if the operation is merely more difficult or less profitable, the restriction fails the test, since a wider reading would undermine the prohibition. They record the MIF as restricting price competition between acquiring banks to the detriment of merchants and cardholders and as not ancillary.

## Key authorities
- *Polypropylene*, *Limburgse Vinyl*: single overall agreement.
- *Sasol*, *Villeroy & Boch*, *Soliver*, *Aalberts*: evidence of participation.
- *Wood Pulp*, *Aalborg Portland*, *T-Mobile*: information exchange and concerted practice.
- Enterprise Act 2002 ss 188, 188A, 188B, 190.
- *Sainsbury's v MasterCard* [2016] CAT.

## Debate and critique
- Criminalisation: The discussion quotes the stance that cartels do such economic damage that imprisonment is expected as a deterrent. Whistleblower rewards and personal immunity incentivise reporting, though the lack of a leniency one-stop-shop imposes multiple filings.
- The line between lawful parallel conduct and concertation remains evidentially difficult (*Wood Pulp*).
- Objective-necessity arguments are rarely successful for price-related restraints (MIF cases).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify participants, level of the market, and the practice (price, quotas, markets, bids).
2. Is there an agreement/concerted practice (meetings, notes, calls)? Is it a single overall agreement? Is evidence contemporaneous and corroborated? Could the firm show public distancing?
3. Information exchange: is it capable of removing uncertainty about future conduct? Market structure (few firms, transparent, high barriers) supports concertation.
4. Leniency: who is first, what value added, which authority; note no one-stop-shop and criminal immunity from the CMA.
5. For individuals in the UK: s 188 elements (dishonesty, arrangement types), s 188A/188B defences, penalty under s 190.
6. For follow-on civil claims: overcharge, pass-on, and the objective-necessity test as in *Sainsbury's*.
