# Abuse of Consumer Data, Privacy and Competition Law

## Overview
Digital platforms often charge users nothing in money and are paid in **data** instead. the question is when collecting, combining and exploiting consumer data by a dominant firm is an abuse under Art 102 TFEU / s 18 CA 1998. The theory may be **exploitative** (excessive data extraction through unfair terms; behavioural price discrimination) or **exclusionary** (data accumulation and combination that forecloses rivals; self-preferencing using data). The landmark is *Meta Platforms v Bundeskartellamt* (C-252/21, Grand Chamber, 4 July 2023, ECLI:EU:C:2023:537). The Court held that a competition authority may find a GDPR breach where necessary to establish an abuse, and gave strict readings of GDPR legal bases for combining off-platform data. The topic connects competition, data protection (GDPR, Reg 2016/679) and consumer protection, and features in many problem questions about intrusive terms (Digi-Soft, Lodge, Megxa/Dronix, Speaktronics).

## The law

### 1. Zero price is still economic activity
- *Google Shopping* (AT.39740): free services do not prevent an economic activity. Users "contribute to the monetisation of the service by providing data with each query" (paras 152, 158, 320).
- *Meta* (C-252/21, paras 50–51): access to and use of personal data are of great importance in the digital economy. Facebook's business model is financed by personalised advertising based on user profiles. Personal data has become "a significant parameter of competition". Excluding data protection rules from competition analysis would disregard economic reality and undermine competition law's effectiveness.

### 2. Meta Platforms v Bundeskartellamt (C-252/21) – background
- The Bundeskartellamt (German FCO) prohibited Facebook from making use of the network by private users in Germany conditional on processing their **off-Facebook data** (from group services such as Instagram and WhatsApp, and from third-party websites and apps via interfaces and cookies) and linking it to their accounts without consent. It held this was an abuse of dominance under para 19(1) GWB because the processing was inconsistent with the GDPR's values and not justified under GDPR Arts 6(1) and 9(2) (paras 29–30). The Düsseldorf Higher Regional Court referred questions.
- On 31 July 2019, Meta introduced terms stating that users, instead of paying, agree to be shown advertisements (para 32).

### 3. Meta – the Court's rulings (operative part)
1. **Competition authority and GDPR:** a national competition authority examining an Art 102 abuse may find that the dominant firm's terms on data processing breach the GDPR, **where that finding is necessary to establish an abuse**. It must respect **sincere cooperation** (Art 4(3) TEU). It cannot depart from a data protection authority's decision on the same or similar terms. Where in doubt, or where those authorities are examining the terms, it must consult them and may proceed if there is no objection or reply within a reasonable time.
2. **Sensitive data (Art 9(1) GDPR):** collecting data from visits to sites or apps revealing special categories (the discussion mentions data from dating apps, political parties and health websites), linking it to the account and using it is processing special category data, prohibited in principle, even if the data concern another person.
3. **"Manifestly made public" (Art 9(2)(e)):** visiting such sites does not make data public. Clicking "Like"/"Share" or logging in does so only if the user has explicitly chosen beforehand, with full knowledge, to make the data accessible to an unlimited number of persons.
4. **Contract necessity (Art 6(1)(b)):** combining off-platform data is necessary for performance of the contract only if **objectively indispensable** for a purpose integral to the contract, so that the main subject matter cannot be achieved without it.
5. **Legitimate interests (Art 6(1)(f)):** only if users were informed of the interest, processing is strictly necessary, and a balancing does not show users' rights override it.
6. **Other bases:** the Court held such processing cannot in principle be regarded as necessary to protect vital interests or for a public-interest task, subject to verification by the referring court (para 155(7)).
7. **Consent and dominance (Art 4(11)):** dominance does not as such prevent valid consent. But it is an **important factor** in whether consent was freely given, which **the operator must prove** (paras 147–148). Users must be able to refuse particular processing not necessary for the contract without giving up the service entirely. They should be offered, "if necessary for an appropriate fee, an equivalent alternative" without such processing (para 150). Separate consent for off-Facebook data is appropriate given the scale and impact (para 151).

### 4. GDPR baseline
- Arts 7 and 8 EU Charter (privacy and data protection).
- Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, by a clear affirmative act. Silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity are not consent (recital 32).
- Consent is invalid where there is a clear imbalance between subject and controller (recital 43), or where the subject has no genuine choice (recital 42).
- The GDPR's informed-consent model draws on the Unfair Terms Directive 93/13/EEC (recital 42).
- GDPR fines: up to 2% or 4% of worldwide annual turnover (Art 83).
- Lengthy privacy policies: it would take each user on average 244 hours a year to read the privacy policies of sites they visit (McDonald and Cranor). Future uses are often hidden behind vague terms like "improving customer experience" (EDPS 2014).

### 5. Types of data-driven abuse (Chirita typology)
- **Data leveraging:** *Google Shopping* (an active discrimination, exclusionary demotion; GC para 183). Apple leveraging iOS and the App Store. Amazon using seller data to replicate products.
- **Data combination:** *Meta* (group and third-party data). *Google Android* (Chrome, Maps, YouTube, Gmail data for targeted ads). The DMA bans combining data from multiple sources without consent.
- **Unfair terms:** UK CAT class actions on Facebook's excessively long, complex terms extracting a "disproportionate and unnecessary" amount of data at zero price (1433/7/7/22). Apple's restrictive terms on app developers (1403/7/7/21).
- **Algorithmic and behavioural discrimination:** analytics segment users by willingness to pay using location, income and sensitive data (see excessive-pricing-and-price-discrimination.md).
- **Sherlocking and data misuse:** Apple cloning popular apps using confidential developer data. Amazon using aggregated seller data and controlling the Buy Box (commitments: no use of non-public seller data; equal treatment).
- **Sensitive data:** health and fitness (Apple Watch, Fitbit, Amazon Halo). Location (Find My, Nest, Maps). Voice assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant) collecting location and purchase data.

### 6. German Federal Court (as summarised by Hutchinson)
Users deserve protection from exploitation through undue data disclosure, based on the constitutional right to **informational self-determination**, even where the dominant firm's product is not essential. Users should have a free choice between a more personalised experience and one without "off-Facebook" data processing. Market conditions could be affected even without proof that terms differed from those under effective competition.

## Debate and critique
- **Exploitative or exclusionary? (Hutchinson, "Broad collection of consumer data by Big Tech: exclusionary or exploitative abuse?" (2024) 20 European Competition Journal 610).** Note: this file is labelled as a Chirita chapter but contains Hutchinson's article. The Bundeskartellamt treated Facebook's data collection as exploitative (excessive collection reducing privacy). The German Federal Court focused on informational self-determination. Some scholars (D'Amico) argue the case is really exclusionary: terms permitting extensive data harvesting work as tying to monopolise data and raise rivals' costs. Viewing it as exploitative blurs the line between competition authorities and data protection authorities. Hutchinson suggests the CJEU's emphasis on data as "crucial" may bolster treating large data sets like an "essential facility".
- **Chirita on exploitative data abuse.**
  - Under *United Brands*, proving a zero price is "too high" would be absurd. The focus should be excessive disclosure of consumer data (procedural unfairness).
  - Consumer privacy should include autonomy over economic preferences, bargaining over reservation prices, and control of physical and virtual location (which signals disposable income).
  - In *Facebook*, Art 102(a) on unfair conditions highlighted consumers' weaker bargaining power. Empowered consumers could prioritise economic privacy over personalisation.
  - "Facebook also made unfair disclosures of economic privacy to predict its users' reservation price" (Loyola 2021).
  - Digital dominance "cannot deliver equality of opportunity to entrepreneurs and consumers simultaneously".
- **The privacy paradox.** Consumers say they value privacy but overshare and sign up for free services. Data can be worth up to $5,000 per person per year to advertisers. "Free" services are paid for with data worth over €300bn (EDPS). Firms combine a consumer-choice posture with "law of the jungle" profiling.
- **"Big Data myths" (Grunes and Stucke).** It is wrong to say authorities should not intervene in two-sided data markets, should ignore loss of privacy as subjective, or should focus only on exclusion and not on exploiting consumers' naivety.
- **Sceptics.** Ohlhausen and Okuliar: competition law offers at best a convoluted, indirect way to protect privacy expectations, and unifying competition and consumer protection law risks destabilising antitrust consensus. Patterson (*Antitrust Law in the New Economy*, "Privacy as an information product") treats privacy as a dimension of product quality and information, opening with Tim Cook's criticism of firms "gobbling up everything they can learn about you and trying to monetize it".
- **Surveillance capitalism (Zuboff)** and data-opolies (Stucke): data extraction drifts from a "virtuous cycle" of improvement towards surveillance designed for control (Chirita).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance** of the data-collecting platform (zero price is no bar; use network effects, data scale, lock-in).
2. **Identify the data practice:** terms such as "agree to all terms and automatically sign in to any added settings"; "combines all data collected from its products or from third parties"; "shares location with any third-party merchants and advertising partners"; "voice data shared with prospective partners and merged with newly acquired AI ventures".
3. **Exploitative theory (Art 102(a) / s 18(2)(a)):** unfair trading conditions.
   - Consent: was it freely given, specific and informed? Dominance weighs against free consent, and the firm must prove validity (*Meta*).
   - Necessity: is the combination objectively indispensable to the service? Usually not.
   - Sensitive data (health, location, voice) aggravates.
   - Is there an equivalent less-personalised option, even for a fee?
4. **Exclusionary theory:** does data accumulation or combination entrench the platform and foreclose rivals (scale, raising rivals' costs, tying data collection to the core service)? Is data used to self-preference or replicate rivals?
5. **Discrimination theory:** personalised or behavioural pricing using the data.
6. **Interplay with GDPR:** an authority (CMA or Commission/NCA) may rely on a GDPR breach where necessary, cooperating with data protection authorities (*Meta* is an EU ruling binding on EU authorities; post-Brexit UK authorities may take it into account).
7. **Objective justification:** improving service, personalisation, AI training, security, "continued AI innovation depends on using customer data". Test necessity and proportionality, and whether users could opt out.
8. **Regulatory overlay:** DMA (no data combination without consent) and DMCCA conduct requirement against "using data unfairly", plus trust and transparency (see digital-markets-regulation-dma-dmcca.md). Consider consumer law and collective actions.
