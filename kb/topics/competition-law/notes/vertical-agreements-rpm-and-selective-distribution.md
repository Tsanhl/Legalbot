# Vertical agreements - RPM, selective distribution, exclusive dealing and the block exemption

## Overview
A vertical agreement is one between two or more undertakings at different levels of the production or distribution chain, for example a manufacturer and its distributor or retailer, concerning the purchase, sale or resale of goods or services. The same prohibition catches them as catches cartels: Art 101 TFEU in the EU and the Chapter I prohibition of the Competition Act 1998 in the UK (the US analogue is s 1 Sherman Act). Vertical restraints are treated more leniently than hard-core horizontal collusion in principle, but some, above all resale price maintenance (RPM) and bans on parallel trade or online sales, are treated as restrictions by object. the materials (the materials) stresses the tension between protecting inter-brand competition and the harm done to intra-brand competition.

## The law
### 1. Types of vertical restraint (as listed in the materials)
- **Exclusive dealing / single branding**: the buyer is obliged or induced to concentrate its orders for a product on one supplier (a "must stock item"). The Vertical Regulation 2022/720 definition of a non-compete obligation covers any obligation not to manufacture, purchase, sell or resell competing goods or services, or to buy more than 80% of the buyer's total purchases of the contract goods and substitutes from the supplier.
- **Exclusive distribution**: territorial protection for a distributor; absolute territorial protection and export bans are the problem.
- **Exclusive customer allocation**: supplier sells to a distributor who may resell only to a particular class of customers.
- **Selective distribution**: the supplier sells only to distributors chosen on specified criteria, who may not resell outside the network.
- **Franchising**, **exclusive supply**, **up-front access payments**, **tying**, and **RPM** (fixed or minimum resale prices). Maximum resale prices and recommended prices are allowed by Regulation 2022/720.
- The distinction between **intra-brand** restraints (how one manufacturer's brand is distributed: RPM, non-price restraints, discrimination) and **inter-brand** restraints (relations between brands: tying, exclusive dealing).

### 2. Competitive harm identified
The Guidelines on Vertical Restraints (as summarised) point to: anti-competitive foreclosure of suppliers or buyers; reduction of inter-brand competition between the supplier and its competitors; reduction of intra-brand competition between distributors of the same brand. Economic worries include forcing customers to concentrate orders on few suppliers, forcing distributors to resell at a minimum price, and discouraging entry by raising sunk costs. Benefits include lower transaction costs and the elimination of **double marginalisation** (where successive stages each add a monopoly margin).

### 3. Restrictions by object: parallel trade and territorial protection
Agreements that partition national markets are caught by object. the materials lists: *Consten and Grundig* (absolute territorial protection and export ban); *Miller International Schallplatten* (clause prohibiting exports); *Distillers* (dual pricing, export purchases at the gross price); *Tipp-Ex* (absolute territorial protection); *Sot Lelos kai Sia* (partitioning markets to prevent exports; parallel trade exerts pressure on prices to the benefit of health insurance funds and patients); *GlaxoSmithKline* (depriving final consumers of medicines reimbursed by national sickness schemes of price advantages). *AEG (Telefunken)* is cited under Art 101(1)(d): refusal to supply qualified retailers who did not accept its pricing policy. *Ford Werke* (refusal to supply right-hand-drive cars to German distributors at lower prices than in the UK) and *Volkswagen* (circulars telling distributors not to sell the Passat at discount) are cited in the selective-distribution context.

### 4. Selective distribution
- *Metro*: resellers chosen by objective, qualitative criteria applied uniformly and without discrimination fall outside Art 101(1).
- *Coty*: a contractual clause had a legitimate objective, was laid down uniformly, applied non-discriminatorily and went no further than necessary (the *Metro* criteria restated).
- *Google France*: a restriction on retailers of genuine products could not be justified where there was no confusion.
- *Guess*: advertising and online restrictions (ban on using the brand as a keyword, online sales without quality criteria, no sales to end users outside the allotted territory, cross-supply bans, minimum prices via a recommended pricelist and price monitoring) did not pursue a legitimate objective of protecting brand image; their object was to limit intra-brand competition and sales outside the territory.
- *Pierre Fabre*: a ban on internet marketing was a restriction of passive sales to end users outside the physical trading area, a restriction by object; AG Mazak rejected public-health and free-riding arguments.

### 5. *Ping v CMA* [2020] EWCA (Rose LJ)
Ping's policy prohibited online sales of golf clubs by account holders, with sales to follow a custom fitting in physical premises. The CMA and the CAT held it a restriction by object. Points taken from the materials:
1. In theory hard-core restrictions by object can meet the four exemption criteria, but in practice that is very unlikely.
2. *Groupement des cartes bancaires* is the leading authority on when a restriction is by object.
3. A ban on passive sales to end users is a hard-core restriction and removes the block exemption, but this does not of itself make it an object restriction; the CJEU took that further step in *Pierre Fabre* and confirmed it in *Coty*.
4. The CAT rejected arguments about brand damage and significant free-riding; custom fitting was a legitimate aim but an outright ban was unnecessary since less restrictive means (such as a clear notice) were available. Rose LJ agreed, and said a domestic court should not try to "tidy up" this area.
5. Warning noted: examining proportionality and alternatives risks crossing the line between the economic context and an analysis of effects.
6. *Gascoigne* (CA): the CAT was right to construe object restrictions narrowly.

### 6. Exemption
- The **Vertical Agreements Block Exemption Regulation 2022/720** exempts, for example, an exclusive distribution agreement where the supplier's share of the relevant selling market and the buyer's share of the relevant purchasing market each do not exceed 30%, and the agreement contains no hard-core restrictions.
- **Individual exemption** (Art 101(3) / s 9): the party claiming it must prove it. Severe restrictions are unlikely to qualify as they generally fail the first two conditions (objective economic benefits, fair share to consumers) and the indispensability condition. the materials presents this for RPM.

### 7. Resale price maintenance in practice
RPM is a restriction by object, whether it fixes or sets minimum resale prices directly or indirectly through threats, intimidation, warnings, monitoring and penalties. CMA case studies in the materials:
- *Online RPM in commercial refrigeration* (24 May 2016, Foster): a minimum advertised price (MAP) policy with disciplinary mechanisms (wholesale discount deduction, ceasing supply) and regular website monitoring; emails showed a reseller amending its advertised prices; the CMA found the aim was to reduce aggressive discounting and downward pressure on market price.
- *Online RPM in bathroom fittings* (10 May 2016, Ultra Finishing): a cap of 20% discount off RRP, enforced through withdrawal of a licence to use copyright images; the "Reseller Image Licence" was described as a smokescreen for managing prices; protecting a prestigious image was subsidiary to protecting resellers' margins.
- Others listed: Dar Lighting (£1.5m), Roland (£4m), Korg (£1.5m), Fender (£4.5m) for preventing online discounts.
- **Software and algorithms**: *Asus*, *Denon & Marantz*, *Philips* and *Pioneer* involved price-monitoring software used to detect and correct low reseller prices to prevent "price erosion". the materials also cites Ezrachi and Stucke's *Virtual Competition* on algorithms reacting within milliseconds, and a CMA case on posters and frames sold on Amazon UK where repricing software and "ignore" lists were used so parties did not undercut each other; this was an object restriction under Chapter I.

### 8. US comparison
Under the Sherman Act s 1 vertical restraints are judged for unreasonableness. the case law traces: *Dr Miles* (per se illegality of minimum RPM); *Schwinn* (vertical territorial restraint per se illegal) overruled by *Sylvania* (rule of reason); *Albrecht* (maximum RPM per se illegal) abandoned in *State Oil v Khan*; and *Leegin* (2007), which overruled *Dr Miles* so all vertical restraints now fall under the rule of reason (Kennedy J: the old rule was formalistic). Breyer J, dissenting for four justices, pointed to price rises of 19-27% during the era of fair-trade laws. The Robinson-Patman Act separately targets price discrimination between competing dealers.

## Key authorities
- *Consten and Grundig*: absolute territorial protection plus export bans; object restriction.
- *Metro SB-Grossmarkte*, *Coty*: criteria for lawful selective distribution.
- *Pierre Fabre*: internet sales ban in selective distribution; object restriction.
- *Ping v CMA* [2020] EWCA: object restriction; narrow construction; no tidying up.
- *Delimitis* (cited in the single-branding context): to assess the effects of a beer supply agreement; *Scholler Lebensmittel*: ice-cream supply tie.
- *Pronuptia*: franchise typology (service, production, distribution franchises; franchisor must communicate know-how and protect network identity).
- Regulation 2022/720 (VBER); Art 101 TFEU; Competition Act 1998 Chapter I and s 9.
- US: *Leegin*, *Sylvania*, *State Oil v Khan*.

## Debate and critique
- The core debate is whether RPM and online bans should be treated as object restrictions or analysed under an effects/rule-of-reason approach. The US Supreme Court chose the latter in *Leegin*; the EU and UK retain by-object treatment, though *Ping* shows judicial unease about the scope of "object".
- Free-riding justifications (retailers benefiting from pre-sale services) are repeatedly rejected on the facts (*Pierre Fabre*, *Ping*, *Guess*) though the literature accepts free-riding occurs.
- Brand-image arguments are treated sceptically where the real aim is to protect margins or limit intra-brand competition.
- Algorithmic monitoring and repricing make RPM easier to enforce and detect, raising questions on how concerted practices are proved.
- Lecture point: object restrictions are not a rebuttable presumption (AG Kokott in the the materials slides), yet exemption remains formally available.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an agreement or concerted practice between undertakings at different levels (not an agent or single economic unit)?
2. Identify the restraint: RPM (fixed/minimum vs maximum/recommended), territorial or customer limits, online sales ban, non-compete, selective criteria.
3. Is it a restriction by object (RPM, passive sales or export bans) or only by effect (then assess market structure, foreclosure, cumulative network effects as in *Delimitis*)?
4. Selective distribution: apply the *Metro* / *Coty* criteria (qualitative, uniform, non-discriminatory, proportionate).
5. Check the block exemption: market shares of both parties below 30% and no hard-core restriction (RPM and passive sales bans are hard-core). If hard-core, the block exemption is lost.
6. Consider individual exemption (Art 101(3)/s 9): burden on the parties; unlikely for severe restrictions.
7. Remedies: fines (CMA examples), nullity, damages; watch for online and algorithmic monitoring facts.
