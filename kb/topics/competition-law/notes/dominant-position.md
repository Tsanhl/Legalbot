# Dominant Position (Article 102 TFEU and CA 1998 Chapter II)

## Overview
Art 102 TFEU and s 18 CA 1998 (the Chapter II prohibition) do not condemn being big or dominant. They condemn the **abuse** of a dominant position. Dominance is the gateway: without it, unilateral conduct such as aggressive discounts, refusals or tying is generally lawful. This note covers the legal requirements of the prohibition, the definition of dominance, the role of market shares (including the *AKZO* 50% share and the very high shares in the case law), other indicators, the "special responsibility" that dominance brings, and how these play out in digital markets (*Google Shopping*). US law (Sherman Act s 2) targets monopolisation and attempts to monopolise instead. Abuse itself is covered in concept-of-abuse-and-objective-justification.md.

## The law

### 1. The texts
- **CA 1998 s 18(1)** (UK): "any conduct on the part of one or more undertakings which amounts to the abuse of a dominant position in a market is prohibited if it may affect trade within the United Kingdom". Section 18(2)(a)–(d) gives examples. Section 18(3) defines a dominant position as one "within the United Kingdom", and the UK includes "any part of it".
- **Art 102 TFEU** (EU): abuse by one or more undertakings of a dominant position "within the internal market or in a substantial part of it" is prohibited "in so far as it may affect trade between Member States". Examples (a)–(d) are identical to s 18(2): unfair prices or trading conditions; limiting production, markets or technical development to consumers' prejudice; discrimination placing trading parties at a competitive disadvantage; tying supplementary obligations with no connection to the contract.
- **Sherman Act s 2** (US): every person who shall "monopolize, or attempt to monopolize" any part of inter-State or foreign trade is guilty of a felony. Fines are up to $10m for a corporation and $350,000 for any other person, or imprisonment up to three years.

### 2. The legal requirements (handout p 33; slides pp 8–9)
1. There must be an **undertaking** (or more than one: "one or more undertakings").
2. It must hold a **dominant position**, meaning a position of economic strength on a properly defined relevant market.
3. The dominance must exist **within the internal market or a substantial part of it** (EU) or within the UK or part of it (UK).
4. It must **abuse** that position.
5. There must be an **effect on trade** between Member States (EU) or within the UK.

The slides stress that the key task is to identify abuse and distinguish legitimate from prohibited behaviour.

### 3. Definition of dominance
- A dominant position is "a position of economic strength enjoyed by an undertaking which enables it to prevent effective competition being maintained on the market by giving it the power to behave to an appreciable extent independently of its competitors, customers and ultimately its consumers" (*United Brands*, para 65; *Hoffmann-La Roche*, para 38; *Deutsche Telekom* C-280/08 P, para 170; *TeliaSonera* C-52/09, paras 23 and 79).
- The Commission's Guidance Paper on its Art 102 enforcement priorities kept this definition. "Independently" refers to the degree of competitive constraint on the undertaking (handout p 35).
- Acquiring dominance on the merits is not prohibited. *Continental Can* (para 26) adds that abuse may occur where a dominant undertaking strengthens its position so that the degree of dominance reached "substantially fetters competition".

### 4. Market shares as evidence
- **Very large shares held for some time** are in themselves evidence of dominance, save in exceptional circumstances (*Hoffmann-La Roche*, para 41; *AKZO*, para 60).
- *Hilti* (70–80%) and *Tetra Pak* (about 90%) shares were in themselves evidence of dominance.
- **AKZO:** a 50% share of organic peroxides (*AKZO Chemie*, C-62/86 [1991]). The slides list AKZO at 50%, and AKZO (para 60) is cited alongside Hoffmann-La Roche for the proposition that very large shares are themselves evidence of dominance.
- **Lower shares can suffice with other factors:** *United Brands* had about 35–45% of bananas, but seven times its next rival (Castle and Cooke). *Virgin/British Airways* (COMP/34.780) had 39.7%.
- **Case-law table (handout pp 33–34):** *Michelin I* 57–65% in the Netherlands against rivals at 4–8% ("immune to competition", para 56). *Soda Ash-Solvay* 70% in continental Europe and near monopoly in Italy, Spain and Portugal. *Compagnie Maritime Belge* 90% of liner shipping. *Irish Sugar* 88%. *Tetra Pak* 78% (GC) and 90% (CJ), seven times its closest rival.
- **Google Shopping (Commission, AT.39740):** a share between 70% and 80% is a clear indication of dominance (para 266). In fast-growing sectors with short innovation cycles large shares may be ephemeral (para 267). Art 102 still applies where the market shows no instability and "a rather stable hierarchy is established".

### 5. Other indicators of dominance
From the Unit I market-power material and the case law:
- **Barriers to entry and expansion:** legal (authorisations, IP, regulation), absolute (essential inputs or facilities), sunk costs, network effects. In *Advanz Pharma* (CAT, [2023] CAT 52) a 100% share combined with regulatory barriers (marketing authorisations) and captive patients.
- **Market structure:** gap to the next competitor (*United Brands*, *Tetra Pak*: seven times), fragmentation of rivals (*Michelin I*).
- **Countervailing buyer power:** relevant in *Imperial Chemicals* (T-66/01), where customers had alternatives, though the abuse was still found.
- **Unavoidable trading partner:** a very high share makes a firm an "unavoidable trading partner" (*Post Danmark II*; *Intel*).
- **Profitability and pricing behaviour:** the CAT in *Allergan* ([2023] CAT 56) warned that it is "extremely dangerous" to infer dominance merely from the ability to price without constraint (para 268), given the circularity where high prices are both the alleged abuse and the evidence of power. A patent holder may legitimately charge a premium.
- **Data, scale and strategic significance (digital):** the UK DMCCA 2024 uses the separate concept of "strategic market status": substantial and entrenched market power plus a position of strategic significance, with turnover thresholds (£25bn global or £1bn UK). It does not depend on Art 102-style dominance. See digital-markets-regulation-dma-dmcca.md.

### 6. Special responsibility
- A dominant undertaking has "a special responsibility not to allow its conduct to impair genuine undistorted competition" in the internal market (*France Télécom* C-202/07 P para 105; *TeliaSonera* para 24; *AstraZeneca* T-321/05).
- *Microsoft* (T-201/04, para 229): the special responsibility applies irrespective of the causes of the dominant position.
- *Google Shopping*: the special responsibility can be safeguarded only if "equality of opportunity is secured between the various economic operators" (Commission decision para 331).
- *Advanz Pharma*: a special responsibility over the pricing of liothyronine given the lack of competition and low elasticity of demand (para 350).

### 7. "One or more undertakings"
Art 102 and s 18 refer to "one or more undertakings". The Unit I material notes that a parent and its subsidiaries can form one undertaking. Collective dominance by legally independent firms is a further possibility that the sources here do not develop, so it is not covered.

## Key authorities
- *United Brands v Commission* (Case 27/76) [1978] ECR 207: classic definition of dominance (para 65). 35–45% of bananas, seven times its rival.
- *Hoffmann-La Roche v Commission* (Case 85/76) [1979] ECR 461: definition (para 38). Very large shares held for some time are evidence of dominance (para 41).
- *AKZO Chemie v Commission* (C-62/86) [1991] ECR I-3359: 50% share. Large shares evidence dominance (para 60).
- *Michelin I* (Case 322/81) [1983] ECR 3461: 57–65%, rivals 4–8%.
- *Hilti* (T-30/89) [1991]; *Tetra Pak II* (C-333/94 P) [1996]: shares of 70–80% and 90%.
- *Continental Can* (Case 6/72) [1973]: strengthening dominance can itself be abusive.
- *France Télécom* (C-202/07 P) [2009]; *TeliaSonera* (C-52/09) [2011]: special responsibility.
- Commission, *Google Search (Shopping)* AT.39740 (2017), paras 266–267: shares in fast-moving digital markets.
- *Advanz Pharma* [2023] CAT 52 and *Allergan* [2023] CAT 56 (UK): dominance from 100% share and regulatory barriers; caution about inferring dominance from pricing.

## Debate and critique
- **Shares as a proxy.** Kaplow and Lemley & McKenna doubt whether market shares in a defined market are a coherent proxy for power (see market-definition-and-market-power.md). The cellophane fallacy is a particular danger in dominance cases, where current prices may already be monopoly prices. The CAT's comments in *Allergan* reflect the same concern.
- **Dynamic digital markets.** Defenders of Big Tech argue that high shares are ephemeral in fast-innovating markets (the argument noted at para 267 of *Google Shopping*). Petit's "moligopoly" thesis (2022 exam question) argues that Big Tech firms compete with each other as an oligopoly rather than being entrenched monopolists. Against this, Stucke's "data-opolies" and the Commission's finding of a "stable hierarchy" support intervention. See big-tech-platform-cases-eu-us.md.
- **Special responsibility.** Critics see special responsibility as protecting competitors rather than competition. Supporters, including the materials' reading of *Google Shopping* as an equal-treatment norm, see it as securing equality of opportunity. Contrast *Post Danmark I*: competition on the merits may legitimately marginalise less efficient rivals.
- **Regulation versus Art 102.** The UK and EU have created ex ante regimes (DMCCA SMS, DMA gatekeepers) because Art 102's dominance-plus-abuse case-by-case model was seen as slow for digital platforms.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Define the market** for each jurisdiction (UK; EU) and note the shares given. Problem questions often give different shares (e.g. 53% EU / 75% UK; 51.96% EU / 60.34% UK; 85% UK / 77% EU for two decades; 30% EU / 50% UK).
2. **Apply the share benchmarks:**
   - 70%+ (stable): strong evidence in itself (*Hilti*, *Tetra Pak*, *Google Shopping*).
   - Around 50%: very large share treated as evidence of dominance (*AKZO*, para 60).
   - 40–50%: dominance is likely if rivals are small and fragmented (*United Brands*: seven times the next rival).
   - Under 40%: dominance is unlikely without strong additional factors (*Virgin/BA* at 39.7% is the low end in the slides). A 30% EU share (Techware question) makes EU dominance doubtful. Argue both ways.
3. **Stability over time:** "for two decades", "continuously consolidated" or "for over sixteen years" all support dominance and answer the "ephemeral" argument.
4. **Barriers:** network effects, data accumulation, pre-installation and default deals, IP, regulatory approvals, switching costs/lock-in.
5. **Corporate group:** aggregate parent and subsidiary (single economic unit), e.g. a parent's e-commerce platform and a subsidiary's AI products.
6. **Substantial part / effect on trade:** for EU-wide platforms this is straightforward. For UK, "the UK or any part of it".
7. **Digital:** consider whether the firm might also be designated with SMS under the DMCCA 2024 (separate test and thresholds).
8. **Then turn to abuse** and objective justification. State that dominance itself is not unlawful.
