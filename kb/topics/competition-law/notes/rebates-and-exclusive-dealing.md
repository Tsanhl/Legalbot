# Rebates and Exclusive Dealing by Dominant Undertakings

## Overview
Discounts are a normal part of competition, but a dominant firm's rebates can tie customers to it and shut rivals out. EU case law distinguishes three categories. (1) Pure **quantity rebates**, linked only to volume, are generally lawful because they reflect economies of scale. (2) **Exclusivity (fidelity) rebates**, conditional on buying all or most requirements from the dominant firm, were long treated as abusive almost per se (*Hoffmann-La Roche*). (3) **Other loyalty-inducing rebates** (individualised targets, retroactive schemes, long reference periods) are assessed on all the circumstances (*Michelin I*, *British Airways*, *Post Danmark II*). Since *Intel* the Court has accepted that where the dominant firm produces evidence that its rebates could not foreclose, the authority must examine their capacity to exclude an as-efficient competitor. Exclusive purchasing obligations themselves raise the same concerns. This topic is a recurring exam theme (AG Wahl's *Intel* opinion, essay May/June 2017; Global Trading's "premium discounting" problem question).

## The law

### 1. Exclusivity and fidelity obligations (Hoffmann-La Roche)
- A dominant undertaking that **ties purchasers by an obligation or promise** to obtain **all or most of their requirements exclusively** from it abuses its dominant position (*Hoffmann-La Roche*, para 89; *Tomra*, para 208).
- **Fidelity rebates** are discounts conditional on the customer obtaining all or most of its requirements from the dominant firm (*Tomra*, para 70). They were later called "exclusivity rebates".
- **Why harmful:** such obligations are "not based on an economic transaction which justifies this burden or benefit". They deprive the purchaser of choice of supply sources and deny other producers access to the market (*Hoffmann-La Roche*, para 90). They also apply dissimilar conditions to equivalent transactions, because two buyers pay different prices for the same quantity depending on whether they buy exclusively (Art 102(c)).

### 2. Quantity rebates
- Quantity rebates are linked solely to the volume purchased, are set to increase quantity, and apply to all purchasers. They are generally considered not to have the prohibited foreclosure effect, because they reflect efficiencies and economies of scale (*Michelin II* T-203/01, paras 56–58).
- **Labels do not decide:** calling a scheme a "quantity rebate" does not make it lawful. A rebate with a significant variation between lower and higher steps, a one-year reference period, and a discount fixed on total turnover in that period has the character of a loyalty-inducing system (*Michelin II*, para 95).
- A quantity rebate is compatible with Art 102 if the advantage is justified by the volume of business or economies of scale it allows the supplier (*Michelin II*, para 85 and following).

### 3. Third-category (loyalty-inducing) rebates – "all the circumstances"
- *Michelin I* (Case 322/81, para 73): consider all the circumstances, especially the criteria and rules for granting the discount. Investigate whether, by giving an advantage not based on any economic service justifying it, the discount tends to:
  1. remove or restrict the buyer's freedom to choose its sources of supply;
  2. bar competitors from access to the market;
  3. apply dissimilar conditions to equivalent transactions; or
  4. strengthen the dominant position by distorting competition.
- **Individual target rebates** (*Michelin I*): Michelin's annual target scheme did not require exclusivity, but a rival wishing to compete, especially at year end, had to offset the absolute value of Michelin's annual discount and so fix an extremely high percentage of its own. Exclusionary effects may arise from discounts linked to individually defined sales objectives.
- **Retroactive rebates** (*British Airways* T-219/99; C-95/04 P): BA's increased commission rates applied retrospectively to all tickets sold in the reference period and could rise exponentially. They were fidelity-building. The court had to investigate whether the rewards tended to restrict agents' freedom to sell the airlines of their choice (GC para 270). The fact that the hoped-for result was not achieved did not prevent abuse.
- **Long reference periods and "suction effect"** (*Post Danmark II*, C-23/14 [2015], paras 34–42): a long reference period increases pressure on the buyer to reach the threshold. Post Danmark's very large share made it an unavoidable trading partner, so competitors could not outbid its discounts. The scheme was capable of anticompetitive foreclosure. Such rebates are not exclusivity rebates but may have a fidelity-building effect.
- **Loyalty-inducing schemes** have foreclosure effects whether or not they discriminate. Such a scheme is "by its very nature, partitioning" (*Michelin II*, para 62 per the handout).

### 4. Objective justification of rebates
- Discounts by a dominant firm must rest on a **countervailing advantage which may be economically justified** (*Michelin II*, para 85, citing *Irish Sugar* para 114). The justification must be objective, not depend on the dominant firm's subjective assessment. Rewarding dealers for improving service quality or brand image was not enough (*Michelin II*, para 140).
- *British Airways* (CJ, para 86): the economic justification is assessed on all the circumstances. Ask whether the exclusionary effect is counterbalanced or outweighed by efficiency advantages that also benefit consumers. If it bears no relation to such advantages, or goes beyond what is necessary, it is abusive. BA's additional remuneration to agents was not an efficiency gain (GC para 280).
- **Meeting competition** was not recognised in *Imperial Chemicals* (T-66/01, paras 303–304).

### 5. Intel – from form to effects
- **Commission decision (COMP/C-3/37.990, 2009):** Intel gave OEMs (Dell, HP, NEC, Lenovo) conditional rebates and payments (and "naked restrictions" aimed at restricting the commercialisation of specific AMD products). Intel was an unavoidable trading partner, and AMD could compete only for the "contestable" share. The Commission ran an **AEC test**: the price of the contestable share minus the rebate was below average avoidable cost, so an as-efficient competitor would be foreclosed. Rebates reduced choice and innovation incentives (paras 923, 1603, 1616). Internal emails showed intent as part of a single continuous strategy.
- **General Court (T-286/09) [2014]:** treated exclusivity rebates as a separate category whose illegality needs no examination of the circumstances. Foreclosure capability need not be shown case by case (paras 72–82, 143–145).
- **AG Wahl (C-413/14 P, 20 October 2016):**
  - protection is for the competitive process, not competitors (para 41);
  - experience and economics do not show loyalty rebates are generally harmful, since rebates enhance rivalry (para 90);
  - exclusivity rebates should not be a separate category requiring no analysis of all the circumstances, and the GC erred (para 106);
  - accepting a low likelihood of harm would sanction form, not effects (para 118);
  - market coverage matters: the tied share was lower than in *Tomra* (39%) (para 137);
  - the GC failed to show that the rebates in all likelihood had an anticompetitive foreclosure effect (para 173).
- **Court of Justice (C-413/14 P, EU:C:2017:632):** as restated by the Court in *Google Shopping* (C-48/22 P, para 265, citing *Intel* paras 138–139), where the dominant undertaking submits, with supporting evidence, that its conduct could not restrict competition, the Commission must analyse the extent of dominance and assess the possible strategy to exclude as-efficient competitors. The module's summary file records that the CJ set aside the GC's judgment and treated the presumption against exclusivity rebates as rebuttable.

### 6. Contractual exclusivity and long-term deals
- *Imperial Chemicals (Soda Ash-ICI)* (T-66/01, 2010): with over 90% of the market, ICI granted substantial rebates conditional on customers buying all or an exceptionally substantial proportion of their soda ash requirements, for indefinite or prolonged periods. Documents proved the loyalty-inducing aim. The rebates did not reflect efficiencies or economies of scale. The abuse was found despite customers' countervailing buyer power and available substitutes (paras 222, 247, 277–279).
- Exclusive pre-installation or default deals (e.g. *Google Android*) apply the same logic in digital markets (see tying-and-bundling.md and big-tech-platform-cases-eu-us.md).

## Debate and critique
- **Per se versus effects.** Commentators cited in the handout (Dreher/Adam) criticised the per se treatment of loyalty rebates. AG Wahl's opinion is the leading effects-based statement: rebates are the essence of rivalry, and condemning them without likely effects punishes form. The exam quotation (para 106) invites you to trace the line from *Hoffmann-La Roche* through *Michelin*, *British Airways*, *Tomra*, *Post Danmark II* to *Intel*.
- **Is the AEC test right?** The handout notes criticism that the AEC test is formalistic, since it relies on a hypothetical as-efficient competitor and ignores actual harm to end users. Internal evidence of intent and consumer harm was abundant in *Intel*. The as-efficient competitor's position matters: unable to enter, already in the market and needing viability, or under competitive pressure (handout p 103). Geradin and Kellerbauer criticised the decision, and Banasevic and Hellström defended it.
- **Consumer choice and innovation.** The Commission in *Intel* emphasised free customer choice, reduced choice and innovation incentives, which the handout notes were "insufficiently quantified".
- **Unfair competition framing.** Faella (cited in the handout) suggests rebates, particularly producer or customer rebates, may be better analysed through unfair competition or economic-dependence rules.
- **Digital relevance.** Lump-sum payments for exclusive pre-installation (Global E-Sales/Better Life in the 2025 exam) and conditional "Megxa" discounts echo *Intel*'s "naked restrictions" and conditional payments.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance** of the rebate-granting firm (e.g. Global Trading at roughly 90% after tripling a 30% share; Sunlight at 85% UK / 77% EU).
2. **Classify the rebate:**
   - Pure volume, open to all, with no retroactivity: likely lawful.
   - Conditional on exclusivity or "all or most requirements" (e.g. a 65% rebate to select customers buying all their requirements exclusively): *Hoffmann-La Roche* category, presumptively abusive. After *Intel*, the firm may rebut with evidence, and the authority must then assess AEC foreclosure.
   - Individualised targets, retroactive, long reference periods, selective to an "inner circle" or loyalty-based ("four years of continuous and unwavering support"): third category, all the circumstances (*Michelin I*, *British Airways*, *Post Danmark II*).
3. **Selectivity and discrimination:** different discounts for equivalent customers (Art 102(c)). Cutting the discount once a rival enters (e.g. lowering from 45% to 4% when a rival launches) may show targeting.
4. **Effects:** market coverage, duration, unavoidable trading partner status, contestable share, AEC price-cost test if data available, evidence of rivals' declining shares or exit.
5. **Intent:** CEO memos ("reduce all but one customer discount", "increase prices upon rival's departure").
6. **Justification:** economies of scale, cost savings, meeting competition (weak), countervailing advantage. Apply proportionality.
7. **Related vertical issues:** a requirement that customers cap retail discounts (e.g. "maximum of 6% off sale") may be resale price maintenance under Art 101 / Chapter I (see vertical-agreements-rpm-and-selective-distribution.md).
