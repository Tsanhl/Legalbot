# Merger control - jurisdiction, substantive assessment (SIEC), theories of harm and procedure

## Overview
Merger control is administrative, ex ante control that can block or modify a transaction because increased concentration may bring higher prices, lower quality, inefficiency and a loss of consumer welfare. Unit IV of the discussion covers what counts as a merger or joint venture, the EU and UK jurisdictional thresholds, the economics of horizontal, vertical and conglomerate mergers, the legal test, case studies, the EU procedure, extra-territorial reach, and data-driven digital mergers. UK control is in Part 3 of the Enterprise Act 2002 (as amended by the Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013); EU control is under the EU Merger Regulation (the discussion refers to its recitals and to "Reg" thresholds without a citation number, so none is cited here).

## The law
### 1. What is a merger? Control and joint ventures
- A merger or concentration arises between previously independent undertakings through acquiring property rights or assets, or shares combined with shareholders' agreements, or **de facto control** (a qualified minority, or a minority shareholder with the right to manage).
- Forms of control: **legal** (majority of voting rights, or a minority with special rights to determine strategic direction); **factual** (a minority shareholder able to veto strategic decisions; attendance and voting patterns may show control over more than half the votes cast); **joint** (two or more exercising decisive influence).
- The CMA looks at **material influence**: a shareholding over 25% is presumed to amount to material influence (it can block special resolutions); shareholdings of 15% or more may also; board representation and particular expertise count.
- **Full-function joint ventures** must have the functions of an autonomous economic entity: the parents jointly control it; each has a veto over key strategic decisions; it has sufficient resources and independence and operates on a lasting basis. The decisive factor is whether the parent may influence the venture's strategy. Examples: *Vodafone Airtouch/Mannesmann* (veto rights over specific business decisions), *Boeing/Hughes* (strategic veto rights and the right to appoint three of five management positions).

### 2. Jurisdictional thresholds
- **EU general threshold**: aggregate worldwide turnover of all undertakings concerned above EUR 5bn, plus at least two undertakings each with EU-wide turnover above EUR 250m, unless each achieves two-thirds of its EU turnover in one Member State (two-thirds rule).
- **EU special (alternative) threshold**, used only if the general one is not met: worldwide turnover above EUR 2.5bn; EU-wide turnover of at least two undertakings above EUR 100m each; plus Member State level figures (combined EUR 100m in each of three Member States, and EUR 25m for at least two undertakings each in those states); two-thirds rule applies.
- **UK**: the **turnover test** (UK turnover of the target exceeds GBP 70m) or the **share of supply test** (businesses together supply or acquire at least 25% of goods or services in the UK or a substantial part of it; not met where the parties operate at different levels of the supply chain). The CMA has a discretion about size and significance, and an exception to the duty to refer markets of insufficient importance (a GBP 15m threshold is mentioned for Phase II).

### 3. The substantive test: SIEC
The EU test asks whether a concentration would **significantly impede effective competition (SIEC)** in the market or a substantial part of it, in particular by creating or strengthening a dominant position. SIEC separated substantive merger control from the question of dominance. Cases cited: *Aerospatiale* (strength of the new entity and effect on market structure), *Air France* (the transaction neither created nor strengthened dominance), *Schneider Electric* (new entity dominant but without significantly impeding effective competition). Market shares and concentration indicate market power; the **Herfindahl-Hirschmann Index (HHI)** sums squares of market shares, and its change (delta) approximates the effect of the merger. The Guidelines treat horizontal concerns as unlikely where post-merger HHI is below 1,000; or between 1,000 and 2,000 with delta below 250; or above 2,000 with delta below 150, absent exceptional circumstances (for instance a merger involving a potential or recent entrant or important innovator). Assessment compares post-merger conditions with the counterfactual (conditions without the merger).

### 4. Types of merger and theories of harm
- **Horizontal** (same market, same level; the discussion mentions a 15% joint share trigger for horizontal overlap). Concerns: elimination of a close rival and ability to raise price, easier collusion, entrenching an incumbent by acquiring an entrant. Factors: seller concentration, rivals' spare capacity, ease of entry, demand elasticity. Efficiencies must be merger-specific (examples: cost savings, economies of scale, R&D integration, bargaining power, complementary skills, interoperability). *Intel/McAfee*: commitments on interoperability and royalty-free access for competing security solutions.
- **Unilateral (non-coordinated) effects**: ability to raise price or reduce output, quality, choice or innovation without coordinating (*Airtours* on single dominance; the discussion notes an "enforcement gap" for non-dominant oligopoly effects and the risk of type I (prohibiting harmless mergers) and type II errors (clearing harmful ones). *Nestle/Perrier* is cited on oligopolistic dominance.
- **Coordinated effects**: ability of the post-merger firms and others to coordinate and exercise collective market power.
- **Vertical** (upstream/downstream; the discussion refers to joint shares of 25% or more). Often efficiency-enhancing (eliminating double marginalisation, assured supply, lower costs) but risks **foreclosure** (absolute or partial) of rivals from inputs or outlets, and coordination.
- **Conglomerate**: different products/markets with some commonality. Efficiency story (economies of scope, risk spreading) versus harm via bundling/tying (the film block-booking example), cross-subsidisation to prey on specialised rivals, and leverage.

### 5. Case studies
- *Ryanair/Aer Lingus*: Ryanair held 29% and bid for the rest; the two overlapped on 35 routes; prohibited. Closest competitors, Ryanair's presence linked to 5-10% lower Aer Lingus fares, barriers to entry, claimed efficiencies not verifiable, merger-specific or consumer-benefiting, and no adequate remedies.
- *Lloyds TSB/Abbey National* (Competition Commission): blocked a hostile takeover; Abbey held 5% of personal banking but was an important competitive force; low switching rates and switching costs hindered others replicating it.
- *Lloyds/HBOS*: cleared on public interest grounds although the OFT found clear competition concerns (30-40% joint share; unilateral effects from switching costs and customer inertia; removal of HBOS, the only player near the major banks in branch numbers).
- A table of EU decisions of unconditional clearance despite high combined shares (for example Norddeutsche Affinerie/Cumerio, Cargill/Degussa, UPM/Myllykoski/Rhein Papier) turns on rivals' spare capacity, customers' countervailing power and whether the parties were close competitors; contrasting cases with high shares where closeness and lack of rival capacity mattered (Kronospan/Constantia, Inco/Falconbridge, Oracle/Sun on removing a growing open-source constraint).
- *Deutsche Borse/NYSE Euronext*: in-depth investigation of exchange-traded European derivatives, a near-monopoly risk; entry difficult; efficiencies insufficient and unlikely to be passed on.

### 6. Extra-territorial application
Control is not tied to seat, principal activity or place of execution; competence follows turnover thresholds and substantial operations, so mergers of foreign undertakings are caught (an effects doctrine: *Boeing/McDonnell Douglas*, *General Electric/Honeywell*; extra-territorial reach confirmed in *Gencor* (1999)). Counterpoints: the public-international-law non-intervention principle and comity; thresholds operate as a presumption of insignificant effects, and may even give competence where no competitive effect arises (a foreign joint venture).

### 7. EU procedure
- **Mandatory ex ante notification** before implementation; **one-stop shop** (exclusive jurisdiction of the Commission, subject to referrals).
- Pre-notification contacts; notification on the regular CO form, short form or RS form for referrals.
- **Phase I**: 25 working days (extended to 35 with referral requests or remedies offered); outcomes: outside jurisdiction, clearance with or without commitments, or opening Phase II.
- **Phase II** (in-depth, about 90 working days, extendable; total Phase I and II about 115-160 working days): Statement of Objections, hearing before the Hearing Officer, Advisory Committee, decision by the College of Commissioners (simple majority): clearance, conditional clearance or prohibition. Commitments by day 65 of Phase II. A stop-the-clock mechanism allows extension of up to 20 working days. In-depth proceedings occur in a small proportion of cases (the discussion says about 3% or 10% in different places).
- **Simplified procedure** (about half of cases): combined share below 15%, or below 25% on vertical-related markets, or small joint ventures: clearance within 25 working days on a short form.
- **Procedural rights**: right to be heard through the SO; access to the file with business secrets removed (not before the SO, but key documents after proceedings open; no access to third-party submissions); State of Play meetings; oral hearing.
- **Fines**: up to 1% of aggregate turnover for incorrect or misleading information or failing to produce records; up to 10% for failing to notify before implementation.

### 8. Digital and data-driven mergers (summary only)
Reviewed deals (Facebook/WhatsApp, Microsoft/LinkedIn, Microsoft/Skype, Microsoft/Yahoo Search, Google/DoubleClick) frequently left market definition open, treated privacy as a data protection issue ("without prejudice" to data protection law), and rejected data-based theories of harm because other firms could collect similar data. An EDPS opinion urged cooperation between consumer, data and competition authorities. A table of CMA and EC decisions on large digital firms (for instance *Facebook/Giphy*, CMA Phase II prohibition; *Microsoft/Activision Blizzard*, EC conditions) illustrates the growing role of conglomerate theories. Detailed treatment is for a separate note on data-driven mergers.

## Debate and critique
- SIEC versus dominance test: enforcement gap in oligopoly; trade-off between type I and type II errors.
- Efficiency defence: verifiability, merger specificity and pass-on to consumers (failed in *Ryanair/Aer Lingus*).
- Whether privacy and data should be inside the competition assessment (Pamela Jones Harbour's dissent in *Google/DoubleClick* argued traditional analysis ignored privacy; the Commission treated it as data protection law).
- Extra-territoriality versus comity.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is it a concentration (acquisition of control, full-function JV)? Which type of control?
2. Jurisdiction: EU thresholds (general, special, two-thirds rule) or UK turnover/share-of-supply tests; one-stop shop and referrals.
3. Define the relevant markets; compute shares and HHI/delta.
4. Classify horizontal, vertical or conglomerate; analyse unilateral, coordinated, foreclosure and bundling effects; entry, countervailing power, spare capacity.
5. Efficiencies and counterfactual; failing firm if raised.
6. Remedies/commitments and procedure (Phase I/II, timelines, procedural rights, fines for non-notification).
