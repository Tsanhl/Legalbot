# Market Definition, Market Power and Basic Competition Economics

## Overview
Almost every competition case starts by asking where the "game" of competition is played: the relevant product and geographic market. Defining the market lets an authority compute market shares and concentration and infer market power. That inference underpins dominance under Art 102 TFEU / CA 1998 Chapter II, appreciability and block-exemption thresholds under Art 101 / Chapter I, and merger analysis. The module (the materials) teaches the orthodox tools (demand substitutability, the SSNIP test, HHI, barriers to entry) alongside the critiques of Kaplow and of Lemley & McKenna, who doubt whether market definition is a coherent exercise at all. It also introduces the economic models of perfect competition, monopoly and oligopoly and the efficiency concepts used throughout the module.

## The law

### 1. Jurisdictional "playing field" (slides; handout p 15)
- **CA 1998 Chapter I (s 2):** agreements that may affect trade within the UK and prevent, restrict or distort competition within the UK.
- **CA 1998 Chapter II (s 18):** abuse of a dominant position in a market if it may affect trade within the UK; "dominant position" means within the UK, and the UK includes "any part of it".
- **Art 101(1) TFEU:** practices incompatible with the internal market which may affect trade between Member States.
- **Art 102 TFEU:** abuse within the internal market "or in a substantial part of it".
- **Sherman Act s 1 and s 2 (US):** restraints of trade, and monopolisation of "any part of the trade or commerce among the several States, or with foreign nations".

### 2. The relevant product market
- **Definition:** all products and/or services regarded as interchangeable or substitutable by the consumer "by reason of the products' characteristics, their price and their intended use" (Commission Notice on the Definition of the Relevant Market [1997] OJ C327/5, para 7).
- **Demand-side substitutability and elasticity:** where consumers can easily switch, demand is elastic and a firm's ability to raise price is limited. Low substitutability locks consumers in (inelastic demand).
- **Aftermarkets:** spare parts and repair markets may be separate markets where customers are captive (*Hugin*, *Hilti*). They may instead form a single "systems" market where buyers take whole-life costs into account (*CEAHR*).

### 3. The SSNIP / hypothetical monopolist test
- Asks whether a small but significant non-transitory increase in price (in practice around 5–10%) would cause enough customers to switch to make the increase unprofitable. If so, the substitutes belong in the market. Slide example: "If the price of iPhones were raised in the UK by a small but significant amount, would you switch?"
- The test assumes prevailing prices are competitive.
- **Cellophane fallacy** (*United States v du Pont & Co*): du Pont had about 75% of US cellophane sales, but cellophane was only about 17.9% of flexible wrapping materials. Where the current price is already a monopoly price, customers will appear willing to switch at the margin, so the SSNIP test produces an overly wide market. The test cannot tell whether existing prices already reflect market power (handout p 22).

### 4. The geographic market
The area in which conditions of competition are sufficiently homogeneous. Lemley & McKenna's example shows the arbitrariness of binary boundaries: it is not true that a video store three miles away competes and one four miles away does not. A slightly larger number of consumers will simply travel three miles than four.

### 5. Market power
- **Definition used in the handout:** a firm has market power if it can profitably raise prices above the competitive level for a sustained period. The handout identifies three features: (1) a restriction of output, (2) higher corporate profits from the price increase, and (3) persistence over a sustained period.
- **Indicators:** number of competing suppliers, market shares and concentration, barriers to entry or expansion and potential competition, countervailing buyer power, product differentiation, and the nature of oligopolistic interaction.
- **HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index):** the sum of the squared market shares of all firms. local supermarkets exercise: Tesco 40%, Aldi 20%, Sainsbury's 20%, four firms at 5% each. HHI = 1,600 + 800 + 100 = 2,500, which is high concentration. Under the 2004 Horizontal Merger Guidelines (as summarised in the handout), concentration is low below 1,000, moderate between 1,000 and 1,800, and high above 1,800. Thresholds are presumptions or rules of thumb, not rigid decision rules.

### 6. Barriers to entry (slides p 13; handout p 24)
1. **Legal barriers:** tariffs, quotas, authorisations.
2. **Barriers to entry in Stigler's sense:** a cost of production borne by an entrant but not by incumbents.
3. **Absolute barriers:** exclusive or superior access to an essential input, facility or natural resource.
4. **Barriers to expansion:** for example sunk costs. Where existing rivals can quickly and cheaply expand output, they constrain any price rise.

### 7. Economic models (handout pp 27–30; slides pp 17–18)
- **Perfect competition:** no firm can influence the market price. Price equals marginal cost (and average cost under free entry), so economic profit is zero. Few real markets are perfectly competitive. Tesco's positive margin on bread shows we live with imperfect competition.
- **Monopoly** and **oligopoly** are the "bad" models (oligopoly is developed in game-theory form in Unit III).
- **Productive efficiency:** output produced at the lowest possible cost.
- **Allocative efficiency:** the relationship between the cost of producing the marginal unit and consumers' valuation of it (willingness to pay).
- **Social welfare** = consumer welfare + producer welfare. **Pareto efficiency:** no one can be made better off without making someone worse off.
- **Bork on the model:** perfect competition was never meant as a policy prescription. It is an error to think markets are inefficient merely because they depart from it, but antitrust should use the model as a guide to reasoning. Bork also rejected defining competition as "rivalry" (rivalry is not an end in itself, and every firm eliminates some rivalry internally) or as the "absence of restraints" (that would require destroying all contracts).

## Key authorities
- *United Brands v Commission* (Case 27/76) [1978] ECR 207: bananas form a distinct market from fresh fruit. Apples are not interchangeable and there is only relative substitutability with oranges (paras 29–30). Banana prices were affected by other fruit only in summer months, and in July by no more than 20% (para 32). Customers were locked in.
- *Hoffmann-La Roche v Commission* (Case 85/76) [1979] ECR 461: separate markets for vitamins A, B2, B3, B6, C, E and H, which are not interchangeable given their metabolising functions (para 23). The Court considered whether bio-nutritive and technological uses formed separate markets (para 29).
- *Europemballage Corp & Continental Can v Commission* (Case 6/72) [1973] ECR 215: separate markets for light containers for canned meat, for canned seafood, and metal closures for food packaging.
- *Michelin v Commission* (Case 322/81) [1983] ECR 3461: market for new replacement tyres for lorries, buses and similar vehicles. Interchangeability and the structure of demand separate heavy-vehicle tyres from car tyres (paras 35–36, 40).
- *British Airways v Commission* (T-219/99) [2003] ECR II-5917: an independent market for air travel agency services (para 94 describes what agents do).
- *France Télécom v Commission* (T-340/03) [2007] ECR II-107: low-speed and high-speed residential internet access were distinct. Migration from high to low speed was negligible (para 88). 80% of subscribers would not abandon high-speed access despite a 5–10% price rise (para 90).
- *Hugin v Commission* (Case 22/78) [1979] ECR 1869: a specific market for Hugin spare parts. Customers in the aftermarket were captive (para 7).
- *Hilti v Commission* (T-30/89) [1991] ECR II-1439: separate markets for nail guns, Hilti-compatible cartridge strips and Hilti-compatible nails (para 77).
- *CEAHR v Commission* (T-427/08) [2010] ECR II-5865: a single market for luxury/prestige watches and aftermarket services is possible if consumers can switch primary product to avoid aftermarket price rises (paras 82–84). After-sales costs are minor relative to the watch's price (paras 94–97). Repair and maintenance should not be treated as a separate market (paras 110–111).
- *PO Video Games / Nintendo* (COMP/35.587 etc.) [2003] OJ L255/33: distinct markets for static and hand-held consoles (paras 19–34). Owners faced substantial switching costs and were locked in for three to four years (para 46).
- *United States v du Pont & Co* (Cellophane): see the cellophane fallacy above.
- **UK OFT/CMA market definitions and fines** (handout table): *Napp Pharmaceutical* (2001; appeal dismissed by CAT [2002]) – sustained-release morphine tablets and capsules, £3.21m; *Aberdeen Journals* (2001; CAT [2003]) – local newspaper advertising in Aberdeen, £1m; *Genzyme* (CA98/3/03; CAT [2004]) – drugs for Gaucher disease, £3m; *English Welsh and Scottish Railways* (2006) – coal haulage by rail in Great Britain, £4.1m; *Loan pricing* (CE/8950/08) – lending and deposit products to large professional services firms, £28.59m; *Reckitt Benckiser* (CE/8931/2008) – NHS supply of alginate and antacid heartburn medicines, £10.2m; *Dairy retail pricing* (CE/3094/03; CAT [2012] CAT 31) – retail dairy through national multiples, £49.51m and £6.5m on Tesco; *BA/Virgin fuel surcharges* (CE/7691/06) – £58.5m; CMA *Residential estate agency services* (50235/2017) – estate agency services in Burnham, £370,084.

## Debate and critique
- **Kaplow, "Why (Ever) Define Markets?" (2010) 124 Harv L Rev 438.** Kaplow argues that the market definition/market share paradigm is always false as a means of inferring market power. Inferring power from shares in redefined markets is incoherent, and there is no way to define the market in the first place without already knowing the answer to the market power question it is meant to illuminate.
- **Lemley & McKenna, "Is Pepsi Really a Substitute for Coke?"** Binary in/out market definition treats an analogue reality as digital. Under a 5% SSNIP, if an eight-cent rise on a $1.60 Coke would not drive enough consumers to Pepsi, the two are not in the same market. IP-based differentiation and brand loyalty make small price changes ineffective (a Harry Potter reader is not satisfied by a cheaper Stephen King). They partly agree with Kaplow: antitrust needs some inquiry into who is affected, but not a formalistic mathematical framework. They want a continuum reflecting partial differentiation.
- **Hovenkamp, *The Antitrust Enterprise*.** Antitrust is not a business-tort statute and is poor at judging intent. In a competitive market (Italian restaurants in Manhattan) firms may lie about rivals, cut prices to the bone, poach chefs or merge. They can do almost anything "except divide markets or agree with each other about prices". Market structure, not unfairness, is what triggers antitrust.
- **Digital markets.** For zero-price, multi-sided services there is no obvious price to apply a SSNIP to, so other parameters (quality, data, attention) matter. The module explores this in the Big Tech units (see big-tech-platform-cases-eu-us.md and dominant-position.md).
- **Aftermarkets and lock-in.** *Hugin* and the Nintendo decision contrast with *CEAHR*. The question is whether whole-life-cost-aware buyers discipline aftermarket pricing. Hovenkamp's work on market power in aftermarkets is relevant reading.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the product.** Ask what customers would switch to if price rose 5–10% (SSNIP). Use characteristics, price and intended use (Notice para 7). Consider supply-side substitution where sources allow.
2. **Watch the cellophane fallacy** where the firm is already charging high prices (dominance cases). The current price may be supra-competitive.
3. **Zero-price or platform products:** consider quality, privacy or attention as the competitive parameters, and two-sided demand.
4. **Aftermarkets:** check lock-in, switching costs (Nintendo three to four years) and whether buyers assess whole-life cost (*CEAHR*). Otherwise spare parts or consumables may be a separate market (*Hugin*, *Hilti*).
5. **Geography:** the UK or a part of it (CA 1998 s 18(3)); the internal market or a substantial part (Art 102). Problem questions often give separate UK and EU shares (e.g. 53% EU / 75% UK). Analyse each jurisdiction separately post-Brexit.
6. **Compute concentration** where several shares are given (HHI). Use thresholds as rules of thumb.
7. **Barriers to entry and expansion:** network effects, data, IP, sunk costs, essential inputs.
8. **Then move to dominance** (dominant-position.md) or merger effects (merger-control-substantive-assessment.md).
