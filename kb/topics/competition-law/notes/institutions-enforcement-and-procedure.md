# Institutions, Public Enforcement and Procedure

## Overview
Competition law is mainly enforced publicly by administrative authorities: the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), the European Commission's Directorate-General for Competition (DG COMP), and in the US the Federal Trade Commission (with the Department of Justice). They investigate, raid, decide and fine, and their decisions are subject to judicial review: before the Competition Appeal Tribunal (CAT) in the UK, and the General Court and Court of Justice in the EU. In the EU, Regulation 1/2003 sets out the decentralised system in which the Commission, national competition authorities (NCAs) and national courts all apply Arts 101 and 102 TFEU. Since Brexit the UK applies only its domestic law (Competition Act 1998, Enterprise Act 2002, ERRA 2013, DMCCA 2024), but EU law still reaches UK firms doing business in the EU. Knowing the institutions, powers and remedies is essential to "advise X before the CMA and DG COMP" problem questions, especially those about appeals against fines.

## The law

### 1. The authorities (handbook section D; the materials slides)
- **CMA (UK):** its mission is "to make markets work well in the interests of consumers, businesses and the economy". It was established by ERRA 2013 s 25 and must "seek to promote competition, both within and outside the UK, for the benefit of consumers" (s 25(3), cited in *CMA v Flynn Pharma* [2020] EWCA Civ 617 para 7). Its functions (handbook p 12–13):
  1. investigating mergers that may lead to a substantial lessening of competition;
  2. market studies and market investigations;
  3. investigating breaches of competition law and bringing criminal proceedings for the cartel offence;
  4. enforcing civil and criminal consumer protection law;
  5. working with sector regulators that hold concurrent competition powers (gas, electricity, water, post, aviation, rail, communications, financial and healthcare services);
  6. regulatory appeals and references (price controls, licence terms);
  7. advising the public.
- **DG COMP (EU):** directorates for antitrust, cartels, mergers and state aid. The Commission's Legal Service represents it before the EU Courts. Two Hearing Officers safeguard the rights of defence.
- **US FTC:** its Bureau of Competition protects US consumers.
- **Networks:** the European Competition Network (ECN) links the Commission and NCAs. The International Competition Network (ICN) is the only international body devoted exclusively to competition enforcement (about 132 members from 120 jurisdictions per the handbook). It advocates superior standards and convergence.

### 2. Public enforcement powers (handbook p 11–12; slides)
1. **Fines** of up to 10% of annual worldwide turnover.
2. **Unannounced inspections ("dawn raids")**, unannounced because cartelists would otherwise destroy evidence.
3. **Administrative decisions**, subject to judicial review.
4. **Soft law:** guidelines and notices, which are themselves contestable.
- Fine examples given in the module: Microsoft €1.795bn, Intel €1.06bn, Google €2.42bn, €4.34bn and €1.49bn (pending appeals when the handbook was written; the slides total Google fines at €8.25bn); trucks cartel €2.93bn (MAN, Volvo/Renault, Daimler, Iveco, DAF) and Scania €880m; CMA Pfizer/Flynn about £90m for excessive pricing of an anti-epilepsy drug to the NHS; £370,000 from four Somerset estate agents for price-fixing; £56.8m collected in fines under CA 1998 in 2021. The CMA also secured the UK's first company director disqualification for a competition breach (online sales of posters and frames).
- Benefits: per the National Audit Office (as cited), the CMA generated direct financial benefits to consumers of more than 12 times its cost to the taxpayer.

### 3. EU: Regulation 1/2003 (key provisions)
- **Art 105 TFEU:** the Commission ensures the application of Arts 101 and 102, in cooperation with Member State authorities.
- **Art 1 – legal exception system:** agreements caught by Art 101(1) that do not meet Art 101(3) are prohibited, and those that meet it are not prohibited, "no prior decision to that effect being required". Abuse under Art 102 is prohibited without a prior decision.
- **Art 2 – burden of proof:** on the party or authority alleging infringement. The undertaking claiming Art 101(3) must prove its conditions.
- **Art 3 – relationship with national law:** NCAs and courts applying national law to conduct affecting inter-State trade must also apply Arts 101/102. National law may not prohibit agreements that are lawful under Art 101, but Member States may apply stricter national laws on unilateral conduct. This does not apply to national merger control.
- **Arts 5–6:** NCAs may order termination, interim measures, accept commitments and impose fines. National courts may apply Arts 101 and 102.
- **Art 7 – infringement decisions:** behavioural or structural remedies that are proportionate and necessary. Structural remedies are only allowed where no equally effective behavioural remedy exists, or where the behavioural remedy would be more burdensome. Complainants must show a legitimate interest.
- **Art 8 – interim measures:** for urgency due to risk of serious and irreparable damage to competition, on a prima facie finding.
- **Art 9 – commitments:** the Commission may make commitments binding, concluding there are no longer grounds for action. Proceedings can be reopened for a material change of facts, breach of commitments, or misleading information.
- **Art 10:** finding of inapplicability, in the Union public interest.
- **Arts 11–12:** cooperation and exchange of information (including confidential information) within the network. NCAs inform the Commission at the first formal investigative step and at least 30 days before a decision.
- **Arts 17–21 – investigations:** sector inquiries, requests for information, interviews, inspections of business premises (Art 20), and of other premises including directors' homes on reasonable suspicion of a serious violation (Art 21, by decision). Under Art 20(2) officials may enter premises, examine and copy books and records in any medium, seal premises, and ask staff for explanations. There is no power of forcible entry. Member States must give assistance, including police, and judicial authorisation may be needed (Art 20(6)–(8)).
- **Art 23 – fines:** up to 1% of total turnover for procedural breaches (incorrect or misleading information, broken seals, refusing inspections). Up to 10% of total turnover in the preceding business year for infringing Art 101/102, breaching interim measures, or breaking commitments. The amount reflects gravity and duration (Art 23(3)). Decisions "shall not be of a criminal law nature" (Art 23(5)). Members of an association that actively distanced themselves before the investigation started are protected from secondary liability for the association's fine (Art 23(4)).
- **Art 24:** periodic penalty payments (the textbook cites up to 5% of average daily turnover).
- **Art 27 – hearing and rights of defence:** parties must be heard on the objections, and decisions may rest only on objections they could comment on. They have access to the file, subject to business secrets, but not to internal Commission or NCA documents. Complainants are closely associated with the proceedings.
- **Art 30:** publication of decisions. **Art 31:** the Court of Justice has **unlimited jurisdiction** over fines and may cancel, reduce or increase them.
- **Example remedy (Microsoft, COMP/C-3/37.792, 2004):** disclose interoperability information on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms within 120 days, and offer a full-functioning Windows version without Windows Media Player within 90 days (textbook ch 15).

### 4. EU Courts
- The **General Court** hears annulment actions against Commission decisions at first instance. The **Court of Justice** hears appeals on points of law, not fact. National judges may request preliminary rulings on interpretation or validity of EU law. There is no formal doctrine of precedent (handbook p 13).

### 5. UK courts and statutes
- **CA 1998:** s 2 Chapter I prohibition (agreements and concerted practices with the object or effect of preventing, restricting or distorting competition within the UK). s 18 Chapter II prohibition (abuse of a dominant position). Under **ss 46 and 47** certain CMA decisions may be appealed to the **CAT**, which may confirm, set aside or vary the decision, remit the matter, or make any decision the CMA could have made (handbook p 14).
- **The CAT** hears merits appeals against CA 1998 decisions; damages claims, injunctions and approval of collective settlements (CA 1998 as amended by the Consumer Rights Act 2015); and reviews of Secretary of State/CMA merger and market investigation decisions under the Enterprise Act 2002.
- **Enterprise Act 2002 (as amended by ERRA 2013):** merger and market investigations. Phase 1 is carried out by the CMA, and a Phase 2 investigation must follow where there is a realistic prospect of a substantial lessening of competition (see merger-procedure-and-jurisdiction.md).
- The slides' court diagram: CAT, then High Court / judicial review, then UK Supreme Court (UK). General Court, then CJEU (EU).

### 6. Leniency (EU 2006 Notice, as set out in the textbook)
- **Immunity** goes to the first undertaking to submit evidence enabling a targeted inspection or a finding of infringement. Coercers of others are ineligible for immunity but may get a reduction.
- **Reductions** go to undertakings providing evidence of "significant added value". Contemporaneous, directly relevant and compelling evidence is valued more highly. Detail: cartels-leniency-and-information-exchange.md.

### 7. Private enforcement and Brexit
- Arts 101 and 102 are directly effective. National courts can award damages, apply Art 101(2) nullity, grant interim measures and award costs (Commission Complaints Notice para 16). *Courage v Crehan* (C-453/99) allows a party to an illegal agreement to claim damages unless it bore significant responsibility. The Damages Directive 2014/104/EU eases claims. See private-enforcement-and-damages.md.
- **Brexit:** after 31 December 2020 EU competition law no longer applies in the UK and the Commission cannot enforce in the UK. UK authorities may take account of EU developments or diverge. The Commission still has jurisdiction over UK firms' conduct within the EU (textbook 15.11).

- Commission Decision COMP/C-3/37.792 *Microsoft* (2004): example of behavioural remedies.

## Debate and critique
- **Are the Commission's powers excessive?** (textbook question). The Commission investigates, prosecutes and decides, its fines are large (up to 10% of turnover) and it can raid homes (Art 21). Safeguards include the hearing and access to file (Art 27), the Hearing Officers, the purpose limitation on information use and business secret protection, judicial authorisation for coercive measures (Art 20(8)), and the Court's unlimited jurisdiction over fines (Art 31). Fines are declared non-criminal (Art 23(5)), but their size invites the argument that criminal-standard protections should apply.
- **Robin Hood framing** (handbook): authorities take money from big corporations and cartelists for the public budget, though not directly into consumers' pockets.
- **Scale of protection:** the Commission protects far more consumers than the CMA (around 511.8 million v 65.6 million in the handbook's figures), which partly explains different fine levels.
- **Commitments v prohibition:** Art 9 commitments are quick but create no precedent and contain no finding of infringement. The 2018 Google Shopping exam question asks whether a fine was warranted given the Commission had first "selected the case for commitments".
- **Soft law:** guidelines bind the authority's discretion but are contestable. Problem answers should treat them as persuasive, not as law.
- **Post-Brexit divergence:** the UK may now take its own course (e.g. the DMCCA 2024 digital regime) while firms face parallel EU and UK proceedings. Problem questions often ask you to advise before both the CMA and DG COMP.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Which authority/authorities?** Check UK effects (CMA, CA 1998) and EU effects (Commission/NCAs, Arts 101/102). Post-Brexit, advise on both proceedings separately.
2. **Investigation stage:** what can the authority do? Requests for information, interviews, dawn raids at business premises and (with a decision and reasonable suspicion) at homes. Leaked emails and seized documents are typical evidence. Obstruction attracts procedural fines (1%).
3. **Outcome options:** infringement decision with behavioural/structural remedy (Art 7), interim measures (Art 8), commitments (Art 9), fine (Art 23(2)). Gravity and duration drive the amount, capped at 10% of turnover.
4. **Advising on an appeal to reduce a fine**: before the GC/CJ (unlimited jurisdiction, Art 31) or the CAT (ss 46–47: may set aside or vary). Grounds include: errors in market definition or dominance; failure to prove the abuse or its effects; procedural breaches (rights of defence, access to file, Art 27); novelty of the theory of harm (legal certainty); duration and gravity miscalculated; parental liability contested; cooperation or leniency credit.
5. **Burden of proof:** the authority proves the infringement, and the undertaking proves Art 101(3)/s 9 efficiencies and objective justification (Reg 1/2003 Art 2).
6. **Complainants:** competitors and consumer bodies (e.g. BEUC in the Digi-Soft question) can complain if they show a legitimate interest (Art 7(2)) and are associated with the proceedings (Art 27(1)). Consumers can also bring private damages claims before the CAT.
7. **Individuals:** in the UK, consider the criminal cartel offence and director disqualification (see cartels-leniency-and-information-exchange.md).
