# Private enforcement and damages

## Overview
Competition law is enforced not only by authorities (see institutions-enforcement-and-procedure.md) but by private parties in national courts, who can seek damages, declarations of nullity and injunctions. Damages claims are often "follow-on" claims after an authority's decision, but may be stand-alone. The key English authority is the Supreme Court's decision in *Sainsbury's v Visa and Mastercard* on interchange fees, covering proof of loss, pass-on and mitigation. The note also shows the EU baseline (*Courage v Crehan*, the 2014 Damages Directive) and a US comparison (indirect purchasers, *Apple v Pepper*).

## The law
**1. Direct effect and the advantages of national courts.** Arts 101 and 102 TFEU are directly effective and can be invoked in national courts. According to the Commission Notice on complaints (2004), para 16, as reproduced in the textbook, national courts can award damages for loss suffered as a result of an infringement; rule on claims based on contracts examined under Art 101; apply the nullity sanction in Art 101(2); are usually better placed to adopt interim measures; allow combination with national-law claims; and normally award costs to the successful claimant, which the Commission cannot.

**2. *Courage Ltd v Crehan* (C-453/99) [2001] ECR I-6297.** A tied tenant of two pubs argued the beer-tie clause infringed Art 101. The Court of Justice held that: (i) a party to a contract liable to restrict or distort competition can rely on the breach to obtain relief from the other party; (ii) Art 101 precludes a national rule barring a party to such a contract from claiming damages merely because he is a party to it; (iii) EU law does not preclude a national rule that prevents a party relying on his own unlawful act to obtain damages where he bears significant responsibility for the distortion. The Supreme Court in *Sainsbury's* (para 185) cites *Courage* for the proposition that the right of persons who suffer loss to obtain damages contributes importantly to effective competition in the EU.

**3. Damages Directive 2014/104/EU.** It governs actions for damages under national law for infringements of competition law and makes it easier for private applicants by addressing procedural obstacles. The textbook gives no more detail than this.

**4. UK.** After Brexit EU competition law no longer applies in the UK (31 December 2020), though UK courts still decide EU-law-based claims relating to earlier periods, as in *Sainsbury's*. Claims arise under the Chapter I prohibition and Art 101 equivalents; the Competition Appeal Tribunal hears damages claims, injunctions and collective settlements (existing note on institutions).

**5. *Sainsbury's Supermarkets Ltd v Visa Europe Services LLC and Mastercard* [2020] UKSC 24.** Retailers (Sainsbury's; Asda, Argos and Morrisons "AAM") claimed damages for the multilateral interchange fees (MIFs) set by Visa and Mastercard, which are passed from acquirers to merchants in the merchant service charge. The Supreme Court decided five issues:
- *(i) Restriction.* The Court dismissed the schemes' appeal; default MIFs infringed Art 101(1) (restriction of competition in the acquiring market).
- *(ii) Standard of proof for exemption.* Balance of probabilities; no enhanced "robust" standard, though the nature of evidence accepted is informed by EU practice (see exemptions-article-101-3-and-section-9.md). Appeal dismissed.
- *(iii) Fair share.* In the two-sided market, "consumers" for the fair-share condition are the merchants who bear the restriction; they must be compensated in full; it is not the purpose of competition law to allow harm in one market to be offset by benefits to those in another. Appeal dismissed.
- *(iv) "Broad axe" and pass-on.* Merchants may claim the overcharge on the merchant service charge as the prima facie measure of loss. They need not prove loss of overall profits. The legal burden lies on the defendants to establish that the merchants recovered the cost by passing it on to customers, but once pass-on is raised there is a heavy evidential burden on the merchants to produce evidence of how they dealt with recovery. The court applies the compensatory principle, avoiding under- and over-compensation, but accepts estimates where precision would be disproportionately costly (paras 216-217, drawing on *Devenish Nutrition v Sanofi-Aventis*). EU law (via *Courage*, referring to *Hans Just*) recognises pass-on as relevant but does not import the strict restitution rules for unlawful taxes; the domestic rules apply subject to equivalence and effectiveness (paras 184-189).
- *(v) Remission.* AAM's cross-appeal was allowed: the schemes bore and failed the burden of showing that a MIF at any positive level satisfied Art 101(3). The CAT, awarded Sainsbury's the overcharge difference (£68,582 for credit cards and £760,406 for debit cards plus interest) over what it would have paid without the UK MIF.

**6. US comparison.** *Apple Inc v Pepper* (US Supreme Court 2019, 13 May): iPhone owners who bought apps from Apple's App Store were direct purchasers under *Illinois Brick Co v Illinois* and could sue Apple under Clayton Act s 4 (any person injured in business or property); the rule that indirect purchasers cannot sue was not extended to consumers buying directly from the alleged monopolist, even if the developers set app prices. The case shows how the direct/indirect purchaser rule controls who can sue. (The raw files also include US private actions such as *Epic Games v Apple*, *Lambrix v Tesla* (right to repair class action) and *Xockets v Nvidia*, not analysed here.)

## Key authorities
- *Courage v Crehan* (C-453/99) [2001]: right to damages, even for a party to the agreement, subject to significant responsibility.
- Directive 2014/104/EU (damages actions).
- *Sainsbury's v Visa/Mastercard* [2020] UKSC 24: pass-on, burden, estimates, exemption proof.
- Commission Notice on complaints 2004 (OJ C101/05), para 16.
- *Apple v Pepper* (2019); *Illinois Brick*.

## Debate and critique
- **Compensatory principle versus proportionality.** *Sainsbury's* tries to avoid both over- and undercompensation but allows estimates, which may be seen as favouring claimants who can prima facie rely on the overcharge, while defendants face the burden of pass-on. The decision balances the information asymmetry (merchants hold the evidence).
- **Pass-on defence.** The defence aims at avoiding unjust enrichment but complicates litigation; the Supreme Court rejects borrowing the strict approach from tax restitution cases (*Société Comateb* line) because those cases concern a different legal basis.
- **Standing.** The US direct purchaser rule (*Illinois Brick*, *Apple v Pepper*) restricts claims; EU law (*Courage*) takes a wider view of who may claim.
- **Procedural hurdles.** The Directive was introduced because national procedure often prevented claims; the textbook leaves the details.
- **Role of private enforcement.** Complements public enforcement and deters; see the Commission's Notice (cost orders, interim measures).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the claimant (direct purchaser, indirect purchaser, competitor, contracting party) and the infringement (typically established by a decision, as a follow-on claim, or proven in court).
2. Court or tribunal: the CAT or High Court in England; Commission Notice benefits of national courts.
3. Contracting party claimant: *Courage* allows the claim unless the party bears significant responsibility.
4. Loss: prima facie the overcharge; compare with the counterfactual (what would have been paid absent the infringement).
5. Defendant's pass-on argument: legal burden on the defendant; claimant must disclose how it recovered costs; courts estimate.
6. Defences to liability: exemption under Art 101(3) / s 9 (burden on defendant, cogent evidence).
7. Remedies: damages with interest, injunctions, nullity; consider limitation and costs (not covered in the sources).
8. Trap: do not confuse EU rules on tax reimbursement with competition damages; do not require the claimant to prove net loss of overall profits.
