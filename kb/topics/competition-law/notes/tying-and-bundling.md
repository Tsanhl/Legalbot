# Tying and Bundling

## Overview
Tying is where a dominant firm makes the sale of one product (the tying product, in which it is dominant) conditional on the customer also taking another, distinct product (the tied product). The condition may be contractual, technical (integration that cannot be removed) or achieved through pre-installation requirements. The textual hook is Art 102(d) TFEU / s 18(2)(d) CA 1998: making contracts subject to supplementary obligations which by their nature or commercial usage have no connection with the contract's subject. Tying can be efficient (integration and convenience), but it can also leverage dominance into a neighbouring market and foreclose rivals. EU law built its test in *Hilti* and *Tetra Pak II* and refined it in *Microsoft* (Windows Media Player), *Microsoft (tying)* (Internet Explorer) and *Google Android*. Tying of software, hardware and search is a recurring exam theme (May/June 2016 essay; problem questions involving pre-installed assistants, apps that cannot be uninstalled, and default search deals).

## The law

### 1. The four-step test (Microsoft)
From the Commission's *Microsoft* decision (COMP/C-3/37.792, para 794) and the GC in *Microsoft v Commission* (T-201/04) [2007] (para 869):
1. **Two separate products**, the tying and the tied product.
2. **Dominance** in the tying product market.
3. **Coercion:** the undertaking gives customers no choice to obtain the tying product without the tied product.
4. **Foreclosure** of competition.
Plus: **no objective justification**.

### 2. Separate products
- If there is no demand for the tied product alone, the products are not distinct (*Microsoft* T-201/04).
- **Independent suppliers** specialising in the tied product are serious evidence of a separate market (*Tetra Pak II* para 36; *Hilti* para 67; *Tetra Pak I* para 82, cited in *Microsoft* para 927).
- Even highly complementary products may be distinct where consumers wish to obtain them from different sources (*Microsoft* paras 917–933).
- **Commercial usage or a natural link is no defence by itself:** even when tying is consistent with commercial usage, or there is a natural link between the products, it may be abusive unless objectively justified (*Tetra Pak II* para 37; *Microsoft* paras 941–942).

### 3. Coercion and technical tying
- In *Microsoft*, customers were not forced contractually or induced by pricing to take Media Player. Windows was supplied with WMP that could not be removed. This **technical bundling or product integration** was a tie under Art 102(d) (handout p 42).
- *Hilti*: making the sale of patented cartridge strips conditional on taking a matching quantity of nails left consumers no choice of nail supplier. This was abusive and also exploitative (Eurofix-Bauco/Hilti, para 75).
- *Tetra Pak II*: tying packaging machinery to cartons was abusive. Tetra Pak could not justify it on its own initiative by technology, product liability, public health or reputation (C-333/94 P, paras 35–37).

### 4. Foreclosure
- **WMP:** because Windows was ubiquitous, bundling made rival media players less attractive. There was "a reasonable likelihood" that tying would lessen competition (para 1089). The Commission did not rely on a speculative theory. It examined the actual effects of the bundle on the streaming media player market and how the market was likely to evolve (para 868). Proof of direct consumer harm was unnecessary. Significant consequences for rival producers were enough under Art 102(b).
- **Internet Explorer (*Microsoft (tying)*, COMP/C-3/39.530, 2009):** tying IE to Windows gave an artificial distribution advantage rival browsers (e.g. Opera) could not match. Free downloads did not cure it because vendors had to overcome users' inertia and switching barriers (searching, choosing, installing, lack of technical skills). Tying also distorted web development, since developers targeted IE. The remedy benefited alternative browsers (the handout cites Robinson for criticism of this decision).
- **Google Android (Commission AT.40099, C(2018) 4761, 18 July 2018; fine €4.34bn per the handbook):** the GC (T-604/18, ECLI:EU:T:2022:541, 14 September 2022) dealt with a single and continuous infringement including product bundles (pre-installation of Google Search and Chrome as a condition for licensing the Play Store), exclusivity payments and anti-fragmentation obligations.
  - The GC upheld the tying findings. Pre-installation conditions gave a significant competitive advantage that competitors could not offset (headnotes 8–9, 14).
  - It **annulled** the finding on revenue-share agreements conditional on exclusive pre-installation of Google Search across device portfolios. The Commission's analysis of market coverage and AEC offsetting capacity was insufficient (headnote 13).
  - It set the fine at **€4,125,000,000**, with Alphabet jointly and severally liable for €1,520,605,895, and otherwise dismissed the action (operative part).

### 5. Objective justification and efficiencies
- The tying test allows efficiencies to be considered under objective justification. In *Microsoft* it was hard to show the efficiencies outweighed the loss of consumer choice in the tied market.
- The handout notes that because WMP's price was zero, cross-subsidisation from the other side of the market was overlooked.
- Safety, quality and reputation claims made on the dominant firm's own initiative were rejected (*Tetra Pak*, *Hilti*).
- **Remedy in *Microsoft*:** within 90 days, offer a full-functioning Windows version without WMP. Microsoft kept the right to also offer the bundle (textbook ch 15).

### 6. US comparison
*United States v Microsoft* (2001) is in the module materials as the US counterpart on browser integration under Sherman Act s 2 (see big-tech-platform-cases-eu-us.md).

## Key authorities
- *Eurofix-Bauco/Hilti* [1988] OJ L65/19, para 75; *Hilti* (T-30/89) [1991]: nails tied to cartridge strips.
- *Tetra Pak II* (C-333/94 P) [1996], paras 35–37: machinery and cartons; commercial usage no defence.
- Commission *Microsoft* (COMP/C-3/37.792) [2004/2007 OJ L32]; GC *Microsoft* (T-201/04) [2007] ECR II-3601, paras 801, 868–869, 917–942, 1089: WMP tying; four-step test.
- Commission *Microsoft (tying)* (COMP/C-3/39.530), 2009: Internet Explorer.
- Commission *Google Android* (AT.40099), 2018; GC *Google and Alphabet v Commission* (T-604/18) [2022]: partial annulment; fine reduced to €4.125bn.
- Art 102(d) TFEU / s 18(2)(d) CA 1998.

## Debate and critique
- **Efficiency versus leverage.** The same integration may be "a natural and efficient enhancement" or "a disguised attempt to leverage" power (handout p 40). Over-inclusive enforcement may chill product design. Under-inclusive enforcement may let ecosystems entrench.
- **Separate demand test in digital ecosystems.** When the tied product is free (WMP, browsers, search apps, AI assistants), the price is zero and the harm may lie in data or advertising revenues on the other side of the market. The handout criticises the *Microsoft* analysis for overlooking cross-subsidisation.
- **Edelman, "Does Google Leverage Market Power through Tying and Bundling?" (2015) 11 J Comp L & Econ 365.** Edelman documents Google's pattern of tying to enter new sectors, compel use of its services and often dominate rivals. Examples include requiring Google+ accounts to comment on YouTube, and Android restrictions requiring manufacturers that install any Google service (e.g. Maps, YouTube, Play) to install others. He concludes Google's tying tactics are suspect under antitrust law. This is useful support for the *Android* reasoning.
- **Pre-installation and defaults.** The IE and Android cases accept that user inertia gives pre-installed products a decisive advantage even if alternatives are free to download. The case law treats this as an artificial distribution advantage.
- **Effects and AEC.** The *Android* annulment on revenue-share agreements shows the courts now require a proper AEC and market coverage analysis for payment-based exclusivity, while technical and contractual ties are judged on capability to foreclose.
- **Exam angle:** essays have asked whether intervention under Art 102 against tying of ICT hardware/software and search engines is justified (May/June 2016). The materials' materials link tying to self-preferencing and data combination.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Dominance in the tying market** (e.g. mobile OS, app store, PC OS, search).
2. **Distinct products:** is there separate demand for the tied item (digital assistant, photo/video maker, camera app, browser, cloud storage)? Independent suppliers exist? Then the products are distinct.
3. **Coercion:** contractual condition (pre-install X to get Y), technical integration (an app "that cannot be uninstalled"), or default settings and "automatic sign-in to have agreed to any added settings".
4. **Foreclosure:** ubiquity of the tying product, user inertia, network and data effects, and rivals' inability to match distribution. Evidence such as rival apps relegated or rivals forced to pay high commissions supports this.
5. **Bundled payments and exclusivity:** lump-sum payments for exclusive pre-installation are analysed like exclusivity rebates. Expect the AEC/market-coverage scrutiny of *Android* and *Intel* (see rebates-and-exclusive-dealing.md).
6. **Justification:** integration benefits, security, quality, "personalised experience". Ask whether these are necessary and proportionate and whether they could be achieved without the tie (offer an untied version as in the *Microsoft* remedy).
7. **Remedy and fine:** unbundled version, choice screens, removal of conditions. The fine is reviewable under unlimited jurisdiction (the GC reduced the *Android* fine).
8. **Data angle:** where bundling lets the firm combine data across services, add an exploitative or data-based theory of harm (abuse-of-consumer-data-and-privacy.md). For UK SMS firms, DMCCA conduct requirements can prohibit requiring users to use other products alongside the relevant digital activity.
