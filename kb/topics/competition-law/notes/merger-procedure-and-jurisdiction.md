# Merger procedure and jurisdiction (EU Merger Regulation and UK Enterprise Act)

## Overview
Before a merger can be assessed on its substance (see merger-control-substantive-assessment.md), it must come within the jurisdiction of the reviewing authority: it must be a "concentration" involving a change of control, and it must meet the turnover or share-of-supply tests. The EU runs a mandatory, suspensory, "one-stop shop" regime administered by the Commission under Regulation 139/2004 (EUMR). The UK has a voluntary, non-suspensory regime under the Enterprise Act 2002, now administered by the CMA. This note sets out the jurisdictional building blocks, the referral mechanisms and the procedural timetable.

## The law
### A. EU Merger Regulation
**1. Concentration (Art 3).** A concentration arises on a change of control on a lasting basis from (a) a merger of previously independent undertakings or parts of them, or (b) acquisition of direct or indirect control of the whole or parts of other undertakings (by securities, assets, contract or other means). Control is the possibility of exercising "decisive influence" through rights, contracts or other means, notably ownership or rights to use assets, or rights or contracts conferring decisive influence on the composition, voting or decisions of the organs of the undertaking (Art 3(2)). Faull and Nikpay: sole control is the power to determine, or veto, strategic commercial decisions (positive or negative sole control; de jure or de facto, such as through attendance rates at shareholder meetings, as in *News Corp/Premiere*); joint control exists where shareholders must reach a common understanding because each can block strategic decisions (veto rights or joint exercise of voting rights). Art 3(5) excludes some temporary holdings by financial institutions, insolvency office-holders and certain financial holding companies. Minority shareholdings without control (structural links) are outside the Regulation although they may harm competition.

**2. Joint ventures.** A joint venture only counts if it performs on a lasting basis all the functions of an autonomous economic entity (the full-functionality test): operational autonomy and sufficient resources (finance, staff, assets, dedicated management), not a mere specific function within the parents' businesses; selling to or buying from parents is relevant to its autonomy; a venture of short, finite duration does not operate on a lasting basis (Faull and Nikpay paras 5.112-5.138). Related transactions may be treated as a single concentration, and two or more transactions between the same persons within two years are treated as one (Art 5(2)).

**3. Community (EU) dimension (Art 1).** Two alternative sets of turnover thresholds:
- *Art 1(2)*: combined aggregate worldwide turnover of all undertakings concerned above EUR 5,000 million, and aggregate EU-wide turnover of each of at least two undertakings above EUR 250 million.
- *Art 1(3)* (if Art 1(2) not met): combined worldwide turnover above EUR 2,500 million; combined turnover above EUR 100 million in each of at least three Member States; in each of those three states, turnover of each of at least two undertakings above EUR 25 million; and EU-wide turnover of each of at least two undertakings above EUR 100 million.
- Both subject to the *two-thirds rule*: no EU dimension if each undertaking achieves more than two-thirds of its EU-wide turnover in the same Member State.
The thresholds are a proxy for economic resources and an objective, predictable jurisdictional test, not evidence of market power (Faull and Nikpay 5.154-5.156). Turnover of the whole corporate group is counted (Art 5).

**4. One-stop shop and referrals.** Concentrations with an EU dimension are reviewed exclusively by the Commission (and not national authorities), but referrals redistribute cases:
- *Art 4(4)* (pre-notification, request of the parties): parties argue the concentration may significantly affect competition in a distinct market within a Member State, which can then ask for the case; the Member State has 15 working days to respond.
- *Art 4(5)*: parties to a concentration without EU dimension but capable of review in at least three Member States may ask the Commission to examine it; any competent Member State can veto within 15 working days.
- *Art 9*: after notification, a Member State may inform the Commission within 15 working days that the concentration threatens to affect significantly competition in a distinct market in that state (or affects such a market not being a substantial part of the common market); the Commission may deal with it or refer it.
- *Art 22*: one or more Member States may ask the Commission to examine a concentration without EU dimension that affects trade between Member States and threatens to significantly affect competition in the requesting states, within 15 working days of notification or being made known; other states may join.
- Art 21(3)-(4): Member States may not apply national competition law to concentrations with an EU dimension, but may take appropriate measures to protect legitimate interests other than those considered by the Regulation (public security, plurality of the media, prudential rules, and other interests communicated to the Commission) (see data-driven-mergers.md).

**5. Notification and suspension (Arts 4 and 7).** Concentrations must be notified to the Commission before implementation and after the agreement, public bid announcement or acquisition of a controlling interest; notification may also be made on a good-faith intention to conclude an agreement. Joint notification for mergers and joint control, otherwise by the acquirer. A concentration must not be implemented before notification or clearance (the standstill obligation, Art 7(1)); public bids are an exception if notified without delay and voting rights are not exercised. Derogations are possible (Art 7(3)); a transaction in breach is valid only if a clearance decision is made (Art 7(4)). Pre-notification contacts with the Commission, the Form CO and requests for waivers are standard practice (Faull and Nikpay).

**6. Phases.**
- *Phase I* (Arts 6 and 10): within 25 working days from receipt of a complete notification (35 if a Member State requests referral under Art 9(2) or the parties offer commitments). Outcomes: not within the Regulation; compatible (no serious doubts), possibly with commitments; or initiation of proceedings where serious doubts exist (Art 6(1)(c)).
- *Phase II* (Arts 8 and 10(3)): decision within 90 working days from initiation, 105 if commitments are offered (unless offered within 55 working days), with extensions up to 20 working days in total; periods suspended if information must be requested by decision. Outcomes: compatible (Art 8(1)), compatible with conditions and obligations (Art 8(2)), or incompatible (Art 8(3)); if already implemented, the Commission can order dissolution or restoration (Art 8(4)). A Statement of Objections, access to file and oral hearing are standard (study materials). The Commission's decision declaring compatibility covers restrictions directly related and necessary (ancillary restraints).
- *Simplified procedure* (lecture): used in about half of cases, for example where combined shares are below 15 per cent horizontally or 25 per cent in vertical relations or for small joint ventures.
- *Fines* (Art 14 and lecture): up to 1 per cent of aggregate turnover for incorrect or misleading information or incomplete production of records; up to 10 per cent for failing to notify or implementing without clearance.
- *Appeals* to the General Court and then the Court of Justice.

**7. Extra-territorial reach (study materials).** The EUMR applies irrespective of where the undertakings are established, provided the turnover thresholds are met (recital 11, "substantial operations" in the Community). The effects doctrine was applied in *Boeing/McDonnell Douglas* and *GE/Honeywell*, and the General Court accepted extra-territorial application in *Gencor* (1999). Non-intervention and comity (bilateral agreements) limit this; foreign undertakings are not exempt from notification.

### B. United Kingdom (Enterprise Act 2002, as amended)
**8. Structure.** The Enterprise Act 2002 (merger provisions in force 20 June 2003) removed most decisions from the Secretary of State, replacing the older public interest test with a competition test (substantial lessening of competition, SLC). The Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013 abolished the OFT and the Competition Commission and created the CMA (full effect 1 April 2014), introduced statutory time limits for Phase I and for undertakings in lieu of reference (UILs), new information-gathering powers and enhanced initial enforcement orders (Parker and Majumdar). The CAT hears appeals.

**9. Voluntary regime.** Parties do not have to notify even where thresholds are met, and the regime is non-suspensory; the Government considered and rejected mandatory notification in its 2011 consultation, keeping the voluntary system for flexibility and lower burden, but strengthened the CMA's powers to prevent pre-emptive integration (initial enforcement orders and hold-separate orders; the consultation floated fines up to 10 per cent of turnover for breach).

**10. Jurisdiction (relevant merger situation).** Two or more enterprises cease to be distinct and either (i) the merged parties' share of supply of goods or services is 25 per cent or more in the UK or a substantial part of it, or (ii) the target's UK turnover exceeds GBP 70 million (the figures given in the 2016 text and the materials; the Government deliberately made the share of supply test a quick jurisdictional test and not an economic market-share test). Check later amendments to the thresholds, which the sources do not cover.

**11. Phases and remedies.** Phase I (originally OFT, now CMA) decides whether to clear, accept UILs or refer to a Phase II investigation (previously the Competition Commission: 24 weeks plus up to 8 weeks) where a relevant merger situation may result in SLC; exceptions to the duty to refer include markets of insufficient importance and customer benefits outweighing the SLC. Parker and Majumdar report that from June 2003 to March 2014 the OFT cleared 734 mergers unconditionally, accepted UILs in 54 and referred 119 to Phase II. The Secretary of State may issue an intervention notice on public interest grounds (national security, media plurality, financial stability).

## Key authorities
- Regulation 139/2004: Arts 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14, 21, 22.
- Commission Jurisdictional Notice (as cited in Faull and Nikpay); Notice on case referral (OJ 2005 C56/2).
- *Gencor v Commission* (1999) (extra-territoriality, per lecture); *News Corp/Premiere*; *BASF/Ineos/Styrene/JV* (full-function example).
- Enterprise Act 2002 Part 3; Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013.

## Debate and critique
- **Mandatory versus voluntary notification (UK).** Voluntary notification is flexible and cheap but risks completed anti-competitive mergers that are hard to unwind; the Government (2011 consultation) preferred strengthening interim powers to mandating notification.
- **Thresholds.** Turnover thresholds are easy to apply but may miss deals with low turnover and high value (digital start-ups); the UK's share of supply test is meant to be a jurisdictional shortcut, not an economic test. Faull and Nikpay stress that referrals (Arts 4(4), 4(5), 9, 22) act as a corrective mechanism.
- **Minority interests.** The EUMR does not capture acquisitions of non-controlling minority shareholdings (structural links) even though they can harm competition.
- **Extra-territoriality and comity.** The effects doctrine versus the principle of non-intervention.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is it a concentration? Look for a change of control (sole or joint) on a lasting basis; check Art 3(5) exclusions; for a JV, apply full-functionality.
2. EU dimension? Add up worldwide and EU turnover of the whole groups; test Art 1(2), then Art 1(3), then the two-thirds rule. Who are the "undertakings concerned" (acquirer and target, not the seller)?
3. If EU dimension: notification before implementation, standstill, Phase I (25 working days) and Phase II if serious doubts; possible Art 9 referral to a Member State.
4. If no EU dimension: national rules; consider Art 4(5) and Art 22 referrals.
5. UK: relevant merger situation (enterprises ceasing to be distinct, share of supply 25 per cent or UK turnover of target above GBP 70 million per the sources), voluntary notification, CMA Phase I then Phase II, UILs, initial enforcement orders.
6. Consider outcome and consequences: clearance with commitments, prohibition, dissolution, fines for failure to notify or gun-jumping.
7. Trap: do not treat the turnover thresholds as indicating market power, and do not forget that the standstill applies even where the parties think the deal is harmless.
