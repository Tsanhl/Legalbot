# Bunge Corp v Tradax Export SA [1981] UKHL 11, [1981] 1 WLR 711 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Also reported at [1981] 2 All ER 513. Check pinpoints against the report before citing them. Sources differ on who was buyer and seller and on the contract quantity, so this note leaves those details out.

## Overview
House of Lords (Lord Wilberforce gave the leading speech). The case concerns **classification of contract terms**: a stipulation about time in a mercantile (commodity) contract was held to be a **condition**, so any breach entitled the other party to terminate, whatever the seriousness of the consequences.

## Facts
A GAFTA-form contract for soya bean meal, to be shipped in three monthly instalments from a Gulf of Mexico port. Clause 7 required at least 15 consecutive days' notice of the vessel's readiness to load. For the disputed instalment notice was due by 13 June 1975 but was given on 17 June, four days late. The other party, Tradax, treated this as a breach entitling it to terminate and claimed damages, the market price having fallen. Bunge argued that a four-day delay was not serious enough to justify termination.

## Held
- The notice requirement was a **condition**; breach justified termination.
- Time stipulations in mercantile contracts are generally treated as conditions, and precise compliance is required where that reflects the parties' intention and the need for certainty.
- The House rejected the argument that termination depends on whether the breach deprived the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit of the contract (the *Hong Kong Fir* approach) for a term of this kind.
- The word "condition" need not be used; a term that, fairly read, has that effect suffices. Courts should not classify terms as conditions too readily.

## Why it matters
- Leading authority on **conditions in mercantile contracts** and on the limits of the innominate-term approach.
- Supports commercial certainty: parties who need strict time compliance can rely on it.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the term and ask whether it is a condition (by statute, by clear contract wording, or by well-established treatment of such terms).
2. For time clauses in commodity or mercantile contracts, expect condition status unless the wording points elsewhere.
3. If it is a condition, termination follows from any breach; if not, consider the *Hong Kong Fir* seriousness test.
