# Exemption Clauses and Unfair Terms

## Overview
An **exclusion clause** tries to remove one party's liability for breach of contract or for a tort (such as negligence), or to remove a remedy. A **limitation clause** caps that liability or remedy rather than removing it. Both are known as exemption clauses. They are legitimate tools for allocating risk, but they can be used oppressively, especially against consumers. English law controls them in two layers:
- **Common law:** is the clause incorporated, and does it, properly construed, cover the loss?
- **Statute:** the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (UCTA) for business contracts and non-consumer notices, and Part 2 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA) for trader–consumer contracts. The CRA also has a general fairness test for consumer terms that goes well beyond exemption clauses.
The law stated is that of England and Wales. The CRA's fairness regime comes from EU Directive 93/13/EC.

## The law

### Step 1: Is the clause a term of the contract?
1. Apply the ordinary incorporation rules (see `terms-of-the-contract.md`):
   - signature (*L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394), subject to misrepresentation of the clause's effect (*Curtis v Chemical Cleaning* [1951] 1 KB 805);
   - reasonable notice given at or before contracting (*Olley v Marlborough Court* [1949] 1 KB 532; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* [1971] 2 QB 163) in a contractual document (*Chapelton v Barry UDC* [1940] 1 KB 532);
   - heightened notice for onerous or unusual clauses (*Interfoto* [1989] QB 433; *Thornton*);
   - course of dealing (*Hollier v Rambler Motors* [1972] 2 QB 71; *McCutcheon v MacBrayne* [1964] 1 WLR 125).

### Step 2: Does the clause, properly construed, cover what happened?
2. **The modern approach.** Exclusion and limitation clauses are read by the ordinary principles of interpretation (see `interpretation-of-contracts.md`), subject to the principle that **clear words** are needed before a party is taken to have given up a valuable right.
   - Lord Leggatt in *Triple Point Technology Inc v PTT Public Co Ltd* [2021] UKSC 29, [107]–[111] explained the shift by two forces: UCTA removed the need for artificial interpretive devices, and the modern contextual approach of *ICS* discarded the "old intellectual baggage".
   - Lord Denning's "swansong" in *George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds* [1983] QB 284 is to the same effect.
3. **Contra proferentem.** Ambiguity is resolved against the party who drafted or relies on the clause.
   - *Hollier v Rambler Motors*: Salmon LJ held that a clause saying the garage was not responsible for fire damage did not plainly cover fire caused by the garage's own negligence. Fires have many causes, and much plainer language was needed.
   - *Hut Group Ltd v Nobahar-Cookson* [2016] EWCA Civ 128 (Briggs LJ): parties are not lightly taken to have cut down important remedies without clear words.
   - See also *Transocean Drilling UK Ltd v Providence Resources Ltd* [2016] EWCA Civ 372, which the materials lists among the key cases.
   - **Consumer contracts:** "if a term in a consumer contract, or a consumer notice, could have different meanings, the meaning that is most favourable to the consumer is to prevail" (CRA s 69(1)).
4. **Negligence: the *Canada Steamship* guidelines** (*Canada Steamship Lines Ltd v The King* [1952] AC 192, Lord Morton):
   - (i) if the clause expressly refers to negligence (or a synonym), it is given effect;
   - (ii) if not, ask whether the words are wide enough in their ordinary meaning to cover negligence, resolving doubt against the proferens;
   - (iii) if they are wide enough, ask whether the clause could cover some other, not fanciful, ground of liability. If so, it is generally confined to that other ground.
   General words such as "howsoever arising" are not an express reference to negligence (*Shell Chemicals v P & O Roadtanks* [1995]).
5. **The decline of *Canada Steamship*.**
   - Lord Hoffmann in *HIH Casualty and General Insurance v Chase Manhattan Bank* [2003] UKHL 6 put the question as whether the parties "must have thought it went without saying" that words wide enough to cover negligence did not do so.
   - Lord Bingham in the same case said the courts should "ordinarily" not infer that a party gave up rights without clear terms.
   - The guidelines are not to be applied "mechanistically" (*Mir Steel UK Ltd v Morris* [2012] EWCA Civ 1397). They are now "more relevant" to indemnity clauses (*Persimmon Homes v Ove Arup* [2017] EWCA Civ 373).
   - In *Triple Point*, Lord Leggatt said the three-limb test is "steadily losing [its] last vestiges of independent authority". McKendrick expects it to give way to a simple clear-words requirement.
6. **Fundamental breach is a rule of construction, not a rule of law.**
   - Lord Denning's rule of law in *Harbutt's Plasticine v Wayne Tank and Pump* [1970] 1 QB 447 was a misreading of *Suisse Atlantique* [1967] 1 AC 361.
   - In *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827, a security firm's employee started a fire at the factory he was guarding. The House of Lords overruled *Harbutt's* and held that, on its natural meaning, the wide exclusion clause covered even this. This was a commercial contract, the fee was modest relative to the potential liability, and the owner could be expected to insure.
   - Lord Bridge said *Photo Production* gave "the final quietus" to the doctrine (*George Mitchell* [1983] 2 AC 803).
   - Today the gravity of a breach simply makes it less likely that the clause was meant to cover it, unless its language is clear (*Mott Macdonald v Trant Engineering* [2021] EWHC 754 (TCC)).
7. **Limitation clauses** are construed less strictly than total exclusions. They must be clear and are read contra proferentem, but the "specially exacting standards" do not apply (Lord Fraser in *Ailsa Craig Fishing Co Ltd v Malvern Fishing Co Ltd* [1983] 1 WLR 964). McKendrick doubts the logic: a £1 cap is in effect a total exclusion. Evans LJ in *BHP Petroleum v British Steel* [2000] preferred a sliding scale, under which the more extreme the effect, the clearer the words must be.

### Step 3: Statutory control (which statute?)
8. **Consumer or business?** UCTA no longer applies to consumer contracts or consumer notices: for example, ss 3 and 6 each now state that they do not apply to a consumer contract. These are governed by CRA Part 2. Part 2 applies to a contract between a **trader** and a **consumer** (s 61; the materials cites s 62(1)):
   - a "trader" is a person acting for purposes relating to their trade, business, craft or profession (s 2(2));
   - a "consumer" is "an individual acting for purposes that are wholly or mainly outside the individual's trade, business, craft or profession" (s 2(3)).
   UCTA's Part I applies to England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
9. **Identify the liability first.** UCTA does not regulate clauses in the abstract. It applies only once the defendant is shown to be liable on a recognised basis: negligence, so s 2; breach of contract on written standard terms, so s 3; sale or hire-purchase implied terms, so ss 6–7 (McKendrick).

### UCTA 1977 (business-to-business and non-consumer cases)
10. **Business liability only (s 1(3)).** Sections 2–7 apply only to liability arising from things done in the course of a business or from occupation of business premises. Section 6 extends to any contract of sale (s 6(4)). "Negligence" means breach of a contractual duty of reasonable care or skill, or of the common law duty of care (s 1(1)).
11. **Section 2, negligence.**
   - Liability for death or personal injury from negligence **cannot** be excluded or restricted by a term or notice (s 2(1)).
   - For other loss or damage, exclusion is allowed only if the term or notice satisfies the requirement of reasonableness (s 2(2)).
   - Agreement to or awareness of the term is not of itself voluntary acceptance of the risk (s 2(3)).
   - *Phillips Products Ltd v Hyland* [1987] 1 WLR 659: a plant-hire condition making the hirer responsible for the supplied driver's negligence fell within s 2(2) and was unreasonable. Contrast *Thompson v T Lohan (Plant Hire) Ltd* [1987] 1 WLR 649, which the materials pairs with *Phillips*. Both are discussed in McKendrick on whether clauses that "transfer" or "share" liability exclude or restrict it.
12. **Section 3, standard terms.** Where one party "deals ... on the other's written standard terms of business", the other cannot, except so far as the term is reasonable: (a) exclude or restrict liability for its own breach; or (b) claim to be entitled to render a performance substantially different from that reasonably expected of it, or no performance at all.
   - "Standard" terms are those the proferens "habitually" uses (*Chester Grosvenor Hotel v Alfred McAlpine* (1992)). Substantial modification may mean the terms were not employed on that occasion.
   - In *African Export-Import Bank v Shebah Exploration* [2017] EWCA Civ 845, the Court of Appeal identified four elements: the term is written, it is a term of business, it is the other party's standard term, and the parties dealt on it. It must be shown that the terms are invariably or usually used.
13. **Sections 6 and 7, goods.**
   - Liability for breach of the title term (SGA 1979 s 12) cannot be excluded at all.
   - Liability for breach of description, quality, fitness and sample (ss 13–15) can be excluded only if the term is reasonable.
   - Section 7 applies equivalent rules to other contracts under which goods pass.
14. **Section 13, anti-avoidance.** Where the Act prevents exclusion, it also prevents:
   - making liability or its enforcement subject to restrictive or onerous conditions;
   - excluding rights or remedies, or penalising someone for pursuing them;
   - excluding rules of evidence or procedure;
   - excluding or restricting liability by terms that exclude or restrict the relevant obligation or duty.
   *Stewart Gill Ltd v Horatio Myer & Co Ltd* [1992] QB 600 is listed in the materials. McKendrick gives a clause excluding a right of set-off as an example of what s 13(1)(b) might catch. Section 13 extends ss 2 and 6–7 only; it has no independent role.
15. **The reasonableness test (s 11).**
   - The term must have been "a fair and reasonable one to be included having regard to the circumstances which were, or ought reasonably to have been, known to or in the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made" (s 11(1)). The test applies at the time of contracting.
   - For ss 6–7, regard must be had to the Schedule 2 guidelines (s 11(2)), though courts use them generally:
     - relative bargaining strength and alternative sources of supply;
     - any inducement, or the chance to contract elsewhere without a similar term;
     - whether the customer knew or ought to have known of the term;
     - whether compliance with any condition was practicable;
     - whether the goods were specially made.
   - For a cap on liability, the court must consider the resources available to meet the liability and the availability of insurance (s 11(4)).
   - The burden of proving reasonableness is on the party relying on the term (s 11(5)).
   - Notices are tested by whether reliance is fair and reasonable when liability arose (s 11(3)).
16. **How the courts apply reasonableness.**
   - **The trial judge's decision is respected.** *George Mitchell (Chesterhall) Ltd v Finney Lock Seeds Ltd* [1983] 2 AC 803 (HL): seeds costing £201.60 were the wrong variety and of inferior quality, and the crop was worthless. A clause limiting liability to replacement or refund was effective as a matter of construction, but failed the statutory reasonableness test (under the modified s 55 SGA, then in force). The "decisive factor" was that the clause was not enforced in practice. There was evidence that neither party expected it to be applied literally, and reliance on it was recognised as unreasonable. Contrast *Schenkers Ltd v Overland Shoes Ltd* [1998] 1 Lloyd's Rep 498, where give-and-take in practice did not stop reliance on the clause. Lord Bridge said appellate courts should treat the trial judge's decision "with the utmost respect" and not interfere unless it rested on an erroneous principle or was "plainly and obviously wrong".
   - **Commercial parties are taken to know their own interests.** In *Watford Electronics Ltd v Sanderson CFL Ltd* [2001] EWCA Civ 317, Chadwick LJ said that where experienced businessmen of equal bargaining power negotiate, "they should ... be taken to be the best judge of the commercial fairness of the agreement". The court should not interfere unless one party took unfair advantage or the term was so unreasonable it could not properly have been understood. The court must first construe the clause, because its scope affects whether it is reasonable.
   - **Free agreement between equals is decisive.** *Goodlife Foods Ltd v Hall Fire Protection Ltd* [2018] EWCA Civ 1371 (Coulson LJ): terms freely agreed by commercial parties of broadly equal bargaining power, where the claimant could have used another supplier, are of "primary importance".
   - **Other factors** in McKendrick: the meaning and width of the clause; equality of bargaining power (including over the terms, not just price); the clause tested as a whole at the time of contracting; the availability of insurance rather than the actual cover; the practice of not relying on the clause; bundling two different losses into one clause; and that a sensible cap is more likely to be reasonable than a total exclusion.

### CRA 2015 Part 2 (trader–consumer contracts)
17. **Blacklisted exclusions.**
   - **Negligence (s 65):** a trader cannot exclude or restrict liability for death or personal injury from negligence. Knowing of or agreeing to the term is not voluntary acceptance of risk. "Negligence" includes breach of the common duty of care under the Occupiers' Liability Act 1957.
   - **Goods (s 31):** a term is not binding to the extent it would exclude or restrict liability under ss 9–17, 28 or 29 (e.g. satisfactory quality, fitness for purpose, description, right to supply, delivery, passing of risk). This includes terms that restrict remedies, impose onerous conditions, penalise the consumer, or prevent the obligation arising.
   - **Digital content (s 47).**
   - **Services (s 57):** liability under s 49 (reasonable care and skill) cannot be excluded. A restriction is not binding if it would stop the consumer recovering the price paid; otherwise the fairness test may apply.
18. **The fairness test.**
   - "An unfair term of a consumer contract is not binding on the consumer" (s 62(1)).
   - A term is unfair "if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations under the contract to the detriment of the consumer" (s 62(4)).
   - Fairness is judged by the nature of the subject matter and all the circumstances when the term was agreed, including the other terms (s 62(5)).
   - Unlike the Directive, the Act also covers individually negotiated terms.
   - The provisions that make certain liabilities wholly unexcludable (e.g. s 31) prevail over the general test (s 62(8)).
19. **Interpreting good faith and significant imbalance.**
   - *Director General of Fair Trading v First National Bank plc* [2001] UKHL 52, [2002] 1 AC 481:
     - Significant imbalance exists if a term is "so weighted in favour of the supplier as to tilt the parties' rights and obligations under the contract significantly in his favour" (Lord Bingham).
     - Good faith means "fair and open dealing". Openness requires terms expressed fully, clearly and legibly with no "concealed pitfalls or traps". Fair dealing means not taking advantage of the consumer's necessity, inexperience or weak bargaining position.
     - Lord Steyn rejected a purely or predominantly procedural reading of good faith and saw a "large area of overlap" between the two requirements.
   - *Aziz v Caixa d'Estalvis de Catalunya, Tarragona i Manresa* (Case C-415/11) [2013] (CJEU; the materials gives a different case number):
     - Compare the consumer's position with what national law would give without the term.
     - Ask whether the trader, dealing fairly and equitably, could reasonably assume that the consumer would have agreed to the term in individual negotiations.
   - *ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67: an £85 charge for overstaying two hours' free parking was not unfair. Any imbalance was not contrary to good faith, because ParkingEye had a legitimate interest in the charge and a reasonable motorist would have agreed to it. Lord Toulson dissented: no evidence showed that consumers would have agreed, and the charge applied even for overstaying by a minute.
20. **The grey list (s 63, Sch 2 Part 1).** An indicative, non-exhaustive list of terms that may be regarded as unfair. In the materials's breach handout this includes paras 7 and 8 of Part 1.
21. **The core terms exemption (s 64).** A term cannot be assessed for fairness to the extent that it specifies the main subject matter, or the assessment is of the appropriateness of the price compared with what is supplied. But this applies only if the term is **transparent** (plain and intelligible language and, if written, legible) and **prominent** (brought to the consumer's attention so that an average consumer would be aware of it) (s 64(2)–(4)).
   - *First National Bank*: a default-interest term was not a core term. The exemption must be read "restrictively", or "endless formalistic arguments" would frustrate the scheme (Lord Steyn).
   - *Office of Fair Trading v Abbey National plc* [2009] UKSC 6: unauthorised overdraft charges were part of the price and could not be assessed for their adequacy against the services supplied.
   - McKendrick thinks the new "transparent and prominent" requirement may expose terms like those in *Abbey National* to more searching review.
22. **Transparency and the court's own duty.** Written consumer terms must be transparent (s 68). The court must consider fairness even if neither party raises it, provided it has enough legal and factual material (s 71). Regulators, now the Competition and Markets Authority, enforce Part 2 under Sch 3 (s 70).

## Key authorities
- *Photo Production v Securicor* [1980] AC 827 (HL): fundamental breach is a matter of construction. A wide clause covered a deliberate fire in a commercial contract.
- *Canada Steamship v The King* [1952] AC 192 (PC): three-limb guidelines for negligence. Now in decline (*Triple Point*).
- *HIH Casualty v Chase Manhattan* [2003] UKHL 6: the parties' intention governs; the guidelines are not rigid rules.
- *Triple Point Technology v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29: clear words are needed to give up valuable rights; the old special rules are largely obsolete.
- *Hollier v Rambler Motors* [1972] 2 QB 71 (CA): contra proferentem applied to a fire clause; no course of dealing.
- *Ailsa Craig v Malvern Fishing* [1983] 1 WLR 964 (HL): limitation clauses are construed less strictly.
- *George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds* [1983] 2 AC 803 (HL): leading case on reasonableness; appellate restraint.
- *Watford Electronics v Sanderson* [2001] EWCA Civ 317; *Goodlife Foods v Hall Fire Protection* [2018] EWCA Civ 1371: commercial parties of equal strength are the best judges of fairness.
- *Phillips Products v Hyland* [1987] 1 WLR 659 (CA): a liability-shifting clause fell within s 2(2) and was unreasonable.
- *Stewart Gill v Horatio Myer* [1992] QB 600 (CA): s 13 anti-avoidance (listed in the materials).
- *DGFT v First National Bank* [2001] UKHL 52: meaning of good faith and significant imbalance; core terms exemption read narrowly.
- *OFT v Abbey National* [2009] UKSC 6: bank charges were price terms.
- *ParkingEye v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67: a legitimate interest made a parking charge fair.
- UCTA 1977 ss 1(3), 2, 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, Sch 2. CRA 2015 ss 2, 31, 47, 57, 61–65, 68–71, Sch 2.

## Debate and critique
- **Defence or definition?** The courts and UCTA (except ss 3(2)(b) and 13(1)) treat exclusion clauses as *defences* to breach of a primary obligation. Brian Coote (*Exception Clauses*, 1964) argues that many clauses help *define* the obligation. His "Type A" clauses prevent particular primary rights arising (a horse warranted sound "except for hunting"). His "Type B" clauses qualify rights that do arise (caps, time limits). On this view, controlling clauses as defences is conceptually confused, and s 13(1)'s extension to duty-defining terms shows the difficulty: almost every term helps define obligations (Chitty's examples include a decorator who excludes the garage doors). The Q&A text links this debate to *Impact Funding Solutions v Barrington Support Services* [2016] UKSC 57.
- **Freedom of contract vs intervention.**
  - Should the law police business-to-business exemption clauses at all? *Photo Production*, *Watford* and *Goodlife* show strong deference to commercial risk allocation. Peel, 'Reasonable Exemption Clauses' (2001) 117 LQR 545, argues that *Watford* struck the right balance.
  - Adams and Brownsword, 'The Unfair Contract Terms Act: A Decade of Discretion' (1988) 104 LQR 94, contrast the non-interventionist approach of *Photo Production* with the interventionist one of *George Mitchell*.
  - The reasonableness test is fact-sensitive and largely left to trial judges. This gives flexibility but makes outcomes hard to predict.
- **The rise and fall of artificial construction.** Before 1977, courts used contra proferentem, *Canada Steamship* and fundamental breach as hidden controls on unfairness. Lord Denning, in *George Mitchell* in the Court of Appeal, described the judges stabbing the idol of freedom of contract in the back with the weapon of "the true construction of the contract". He accepted that after UCTA the courts should no longer need "gymnastic contortions". With statutory control in place, the courts have returned to ordinary interpretation. The question for essays is whether the special rules should now be abandoned entirely (Lord Leggatt) or kept as useful presumptions.
- **Limitation vs exclusion.** The *Ailsa Craig* distinction is criticised as unconvincing. A sliding scale based on how extreme the clause's effect is (*BHP Petroleum*) may be more coherent.
- **Consumer protection under the CRA.**
  - Its drafting departs from the Directive, notably by covering negotiated terms.
  - The core terms exemption, and how far *First National Bank* and *Abbey National* survive the "transparent and prominent" requirement, is a key essay point. Davies (2010) 69 CLJ 21 reads *Abbey National* as based on consumer choice rather than consumer protection.
  - Dean (2002) 65 MLR 773 criticised the courts' failure to refer questions to the CJEU. After Brexit, references are no longer possible, and the EU origins of Part 2 may matter less over time.
  - Devenney [2018] JBL 485 reflects on the CRA reforms.
- **Enforcement.** Susan Bright's work (e.g. 'Winning the battle against unfair contract terms' (2006) 20 Legal Studies 331, as cited in the materials; and her chapter in Burrows and Peel, *Contract Terms* (2007)) shows the importance of regulatory, as opposed to litigated, control. Over 5,000 terms were changed or abandoned after OFT investigation in 2000–2005. Common targets were quality exclusions, financial penalties and unclear language.
- **The B2B/B2C divide.** The Concentrate text notes that defining consumers as "individuals" leaves small businesses with only UCTA's reasonableness protection. It asks whether the reformed regime achieved its aims and whether the CRA drafting is clear.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the clause and what it does.** Does it exclude, limit, or impose a condition or time-bar? Note whether it mentions negligence.
2. **Identify the liability.** Is it negligence (in tort or a contractual duty of care), strict contractual liability, or a statutory implied term? Which clause covers which liability?
3. **Incorporation.** Signature, notice (timing, contractual document, reasonable steps, onerous terms), or course of dealing?
4. **Construction.**
   - Read the clause in context, requiring clear words for loss of valuable rights (*Triple Point*).
   - Apply contra proferentem to ambiguity, and in consumer cases CRA s 69.
   - For negligence, consider the *Canada Steamship*/*HIH* approach: does the clause mention negligence, and is there a realistic other head of liability?
   - Construe limitation clauses less strictly (*Ailsa Craig*).
   - Serious or deliberate breach is only a construction point (*Photo Production*).
5. **Which statute?**
   - Is the claimant an individual acting wholly or mainly outside their business, contracting with a trader? Then apply the CRA.
   - Is it a business liability? Then apply UCTA.
6. **UCTA:**
   - Death or personal injury from negligence: void (s 2(1)).
   - Other negligence loss: reasonableness (s 2(2)).
   - Written standard terms: s 3. Are they the defendant's habitually used terms?
   - Sale of goods: s 6. Title is non-excludable; quality is subject to reasonableness.
   - Check s 13 for disguised exclusions.
   - Then apply s 11: the burden is on the defendant; test at the time of contracting; Sch 2 factors; insurance and resources for caps; bargaining power and alternatives; whether the clause was relaxed in practice (*George Mitchell*; contrast *Schenkers*).
7. **CRA:**
   - Death or personal injury: void (s 65).
   - Goods, digital content or services liabilities: ss 31, 47, 57.
   - Otherwise apply the s 62 fairness test: significant imbalance and good faith (*First National Bank*, *Aziz*, *ParkingEye*), grey list, core terms exemption only if transparent and prominent (s 64).
   - The court must raise fairness itself (s 71).
8. **Consequence.** An unfair or unreasonable term is not binding or is ineffective. The rest of the contract normally continues.
9. **Traps:**
   - Applying UCTA to a consumer contract.
   - Forgetting s 11(5) (burden on the defendant).
   - Testing reasonableness at the time of breach (UCTA tests at contracting).
   - Assuming fundamental breach defeats a clause.
   - Overlooking the s 2(1)/s 65 absolute bans.
   - Missing that a clause about price may still be reviewable if not transparent and prominent.
10. **Facts that change the outcome:**
   - Whether there was equal bargaining power and alternative suppliers.
   - Whether the claimant was a consumer.
   - Whether the clause mentions negligence.
   - Whether the clause was brought to attention before contracting.
   - Whether insurance was available, and to whom.
   - Whether the defendant enforced the clause in practice.
