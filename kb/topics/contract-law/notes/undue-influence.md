# Undue Influence

## Overview
Undue influence is an **equitable** doctrine (English law). It lets a court set aside a contract or gift where one party's influence over the other has been wrongfully exercised. Unlike duress, there need not be any specific threat or illegitimate pressure. The doctrine typically works inside relationships of trust and confidence, where the influence is subtle and hard to prove directly. That is why the evidential **presumption** matters so much. The modern law is mostly made in **three-party "surety wife" cases**: a husband's undue influence (or misrepresentation) leads his wife to charge the family home to a bank for his debts. The question there is whether the bank can enforce the charge. The only remedy is **rescission**, subject to the bars.

## The law

### 1. What undue influence is
- Wilde JP in *Hall v Hall* (1868) LR 1 P & D 481 described it as pressure of whatever character, if exercised so as "to overpower the volition without convincing the judgment".
- Lord Nicholls in *Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge (No 2)* [2001] UKHL 44, [2002] 2 AC 773 said equity, "as a court of conscience", ensures that one person's influence over another is not abused. Everyday persuasion is fine, but the law limits the means that may properly be used. Equity supplemented the common law's narrow doctrine of duress by reaching other unacceptable forms of persuasion. Influence is "undue" whenever the consent it procures "ought not fairly to be treated as the expression of a person's free will". No more precise definition is possible.
- Lord Clyde in *Etridge*: it is "more easily ... recognised when found than exhaustively analysed in the abstract".
- Lord Millett in *National Commercial Bank (Jamaica) v Hew* [2003] UKPC 51 took a **defendant-focused** view. Equity intervenes for unconscionable conduct, where one party exploits influence gained from the relationship. "Equity does not save people from the consequences of their own folly; it acts to save them from being victimised by other people" (drawing on Lindley LJ in *Allcard v Skinner*). A transaction that is fair *as between the parties* will not be set aside, however commercially disadvantageous it is.
- *Hewett v First Plus Financial Group* [2010] EWCA Civ 312 (attributed in the handout to Briggs J): undue influence does not require that the victim made no decision of her own or that her will was completely overborne. A conscious exercise of will may still be vitiated.
- *Nature Resorts Ltd v First Citizens Bank Ltd* [2022] UKPC 10, [10]–[15] (Lord Briggs and Lord Burrows) gives a modern summary. Setting aside illegitimate threats, which now belong to duress, undue influence covers the case where, because of the relationship, B has such influence over A that A does not exercise a free judgment independent of B.

### 2. Classification: from *O'Brien* to *Etridge*
- **Old scheme** (*Barclays Bank v O'Brien* [1994] 1 AC 180, taken from *BCCI v Aboody*):
  - Class 1: actual undue influence.
  - Class 2: presumed undue influence, split into **2A** (relationships automatically raising a presumption of influence) and **2B** (a relationship of trust and confidence proved on the facts).
- **After *Etridge***: there is **one** concept of undue influence. "Actual" and "presumed" are two **ways of proving** it, not two forms (confirmed in *Nature Resorts*, [11]). The claimant bears the burden of proving either that:
  - the defendant exerted undue pressure (the old Class 1, outside duress cases); or
  - the defendant failed to safeguard the claimant's interests in the transaction (the old Class 2).
- What remains of the "presumption" (lecture's summary of *Etridge*):
  - (a) a **legal presumption** in certain specially protected relationships (the old 2A). Lord Nicholls called it "irrebuttable" that one party had *influence*, not *undue* influence. *Nature Resorts* says it is better described as a legal rule;
  - (b) an **evidential presumption** (an inference). The court draws legitimate inferences from proof of a relationship of influence plus a transaction that calls for explanation. The **evidential burden then shifts** to the defendant to show the claimant's consent was "informed and free".
- The future of a separate "2B" category is doubtful. Lord Hobhouse said it "should not be adopted", Lord Scott doubted its usefulness, and Lord Clyde was hostile to presumptions generally (McKendrick).

### 3. Presumed undue influence: the elements
Proving (1) and (2) gives prima facie evidence of abuse of influence. That stands as undue influence unless (3) the presumption is rebutted.

**(1) Trust and confidence (a relationship of influence).**
- *Protected relationships (automatic)*:
  - spiritual adviser and follower (*Allcard v Skinner* (1887) 36 Ch D 145);
  - doctor and patient (*Dent v Bennett* (1839) 4 My & Cr 269 in the materials; *Mitchell v Homfray* (1881) in *Nature Resorts*);
  - solicitor and client (*Wright v Carter* [1903] 1 Ch 27);
  - parent and (young) child (*Bainbrigge v Browne* (1881); *Lancashire Loans v Black* [1934] 1 KB 380);
  - McKendrick also lists guardian and ward, and trustee and beneficiary.
  
  **Not** included: husband and wife (*Bank of Montreal v Stuart* (1911); confirmed in *Etridge*, [19]), and bank and customer (*National Westminster Bank v Morgan* [1985] AC 686).
- *Relationship proved on the facts*:
  - customer and bank manager (*Lloyds Bank v Bundy* [1974] 3 All ER 757 / [1975] QB 326, CA): Mr Bundy relied on the bank manager for financial advice and was persuaded to charge his home for the debts of his son's company. The charge was set aside;
  - *National Westminster Bank v Morgan* (customer and bank, where the claim failed);
  - junior employee and employer (*Credit Lyonnais Bank Nederland v Burch* [1997] 1 All ER 144);
  - elderly widower and housekeeper (*Re Craig* [1970] 2 All ER 390);
  - young pop singer-songwriter and manager (*O'Sullivan v Management Agency & Music* [1985] QB 428);
  - divorced woman and her new partner (*Leeder v Stevens* [2005] EWCA Civ 50);
  - non-English-speaking widow and her son (*Abbey National Bank v Stringer* [2006] EWCA Civ 338);
  - frail elderly great-uncle and great-nephew (*Goodchild v Bradbury* (2006));
  - elderly donor and the neighbour caring for him (*Hammond v Osborn* (2002)).
  
  A wife *may* prove trust and confidence in her husband over financial affairs. In *Etridge* one appeal involved a Hasidic couple where the wife's upbringing prepared her for "subservience and obedience", and the presumption arose (Lord Scott).

**(2) A transaction that "calls for explanation".**
- *Etridge* replaced Lord Scarman's **"manifest disadvantage"** test (*Morgan*) with the older test of Lindley LJ in *Allcard*: the gift must be "so large as not to be reasonably accounted for on the ground of friendship, relationship, charity, or other ordinary motives on which ordinary men act" (*Nature Resorts*, [12]). The handout attributes the "so large" passage to Cotton LJ, but McKendrick's extract places it in Lindley LJ's judgment.
- Lord Nicholls warned that it would be "absurd" to presume that every Christmas or birthday gift from child to parent, or every solicitor–client transaction, was procured by undue influence. A contract that is substantively very unfair to A falls on one side of the line, and a Christmas present on the other (*Nature Resorts*).
- McKendrick suggests the change may be one of label rather than substance.
- In the ordinary case, a wife's guarantee of her husband's business debts **is** explicable by the relationship, so it does not by itself call for explanation (*Etridge*). This makes two-party claims by wives hard to win at trial.

**(3) Has the presumption been rebutted?**
- The defendant must show the transaction was the free exercise of independent will.
- The classic method is proof of **independent advice**. Lord Halisham (as the handout spells it) in *Inche Noriah v Shaik Allie Bin Omar* [1929] AC 127 asked whether the nature and effect of the transaction were fully explained to the donor by some independent and qualified person, so completely that the court is satisfied the donor acted independently and with full appreciation.
- Advice is **neither necessary nor conclusive**:
  - it need not be legal advice, and it is sometimes insufficient (lecture);
  - Treitel (quoted in McKendrick) says it must be competent and based on all the relevant facts. Where the influence is very strong or the gift very large, the adviser may need to approve the transaction and the advice may need to be followed;
  - in *Nature Resorts* the presumption was rebutted *without* advice, because the claimant had considerable commercial experience and understood the transaction;
  - *Smith v Cooper* [2010] EWCA Civ 722 is listed in the materials on this point, without detail.
- Lindley LJ in *Allcard*: in the face of a rule forbidding consultation with outsiders, the gifts could not stand without proof that the claimant could have obtained independent advice and knew she could. Lindley LJ doubted that even this would suffice without proof that she was free to act on the advice.

### 4. Actual undue influence
- The claimant gives **direct proof** that the defendant's conduct, within the relationship, stopped the claimant from exercising free and independent judgment (*Nature Resorts*, [11]). Forms include bullying, harassment, domination and control (McKendrick). the materials asks two questions: is there clearly exploitation (*Lloyds Bank v Bundy*; *BCCI v Aboody* [1990] 1 QB 923; *Drew v Daniel* [2005] EWCA Civ 507)? And was the claimant **induced** to contract because of it (*Aboody*; *UCB Corporate Services v Williams* [2002] EWCA Civ 555)?
- **No need to show that the transaction calls for explanation or is disadvantageous.** Lord Browne-Wilkinson in *CIBC Mortgages v Pitt* [1994] 1 AC 200 called actual undue influence "a species of fraud": the victim may have it set aside as of right. In *Pitt* the husband told the bank the loan was for a holiday home and to pay off the mortgage, but used it to buy shares. This was actual undue influence, but the bank was not affected (see section 5).
- **Overlap with duress.** Older "actual undue influence" cases built on threats, such as *Williams v Bayley* (1866) LR 1 HL 200 (bankers implied they could prosecute a father's son for forgery, and the father gave a mortgage), would now be treated as **duress** (*Times Travel* [2021] UKSC 40, [9], [90]; *Nature Resorts*, [10]). They may still be pleaded as undue influence (*Libyan Investment Authority v Goldman Sachs* [2016] EWHC 2530 (Ch)). But a pressure claim that would fail as duress is unlikely to succeed as undue influence (*KSH Farm v KSH Plant* [2021] EWHC 1986 (Ch)). McKendrick expects actual undue influence to survive for subtler kinds of impropriety.

### 5. Three-party cases: when is the creditor affected?
Typical facts: A (the husband) unduly influences B (the wife) to contract with C (the bank), usually a guarantee or mortgage of the home securing A's business debts. B's transaction with C is voidable only if:
- **(a)** the undue influence (or misrepresentation) by A is established (actual, or presumed on proof of trust and confidence plus a transaction calling for explanation); **and**
- **(b)** C is affected, either because A acted as **C's agent** in obtaining the signature (*Aboody*; *O'Brien*), or because C had **actual or constructive notice** of A's wrong.

**When is the bank "put on inquiry"?** (*Etridge*, Lord Nicholls)
- Whenever a wife stands surety for her husband's debts or his company's debts. This applies even where she is a shareholder or officer of the company. Examiners like this point.
- The same applies to unmarried couples, heterosexual or homosexual, where the bank knows of the relationship ([47]). It also covers non-sexual relationships (*Burch*). In general, the bank is put on inquiry in "every case where the relationship between the surety and the debtor is non-commercial" ([84]). Commercial sureties, such as those paid a fee or guaranteeing a group company's debts, are outside this.
- **Not** where the loan is to husband and wife **jointly** for joint purposes, unless the bank knows it is really for the husband's purposes (*CIBC v Pitt*; *Etridge* [48]).

**the materials's summary of constructive notice:**
1. the bank must be aware that the influenced party is in a relationship of trust and confidence with the influencer, so there is a substantial risk of undue influence;
2. the transaction must not be to the financial advantage of the influenced party;
3. the bank must have failed to take reasonable steps to satisfy itself that the transaction was entered into freely and with knowledge of the full facts.

**Reasonable steps** (the materials's list, from Lord Nicholls at [79]):
- the bank should communicate **directly** with the wife and tell her it requires **written confirmation from a solicitor acting for her** that she understands what she is doing;
- it should send her, or her solicitor, the relevant **financial information** about the husband's loan application;
- it should **not proceed** until it receives the solicitor's confirmation that she has been properly advised.

The bank need not meet the wife itself. It can rely on the solicitor's confirmation unless it knows or ought to realise the advice was not given. McKendrick notes that the duties on **solicitors** (Lord Nicholls at [65], [74]) are in many ways more onerous. Lord Nicholls had described past "independent" advice for wives as a "fiction" and a "charade".

**Balancing aim.** Lord Bingham (*Etridge*, [2]) said a wife must not charge her interest in the home without fully understanding the transaction and knowing the decision is hers. Lenders, though, must be able to lend in ordinary cases "in reasonable confidence" that properly obtained security will be enforceable. The minimum requirements should be "clear, simple and practically operable".

### 6. Remedy: rescission
- The only remedy is rescission, subject to the bars (as in the handout): **impossibility** (*Clarke v Dickson* (1858)), **affirmation** (*Peyman v Lanjani* [1984]), **lapse of time / laches** (*Leaf v International Galleries* [1950]) and **third-party rights** (*Car and Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965]).
- *Allcard*: the claimant was unduly influenced but lost her claim through **delay** after leaving the order (about six years). Cotton LJ dissented on this point. The majority did not decide whether to call it acquiescence, laches or affirmation. Lindley LJ would in principle have allowed recovery only of property not already spent on the sisterhood's purposes.
- Restoring the parties: the victim must give back what they received (*Dunbar Bank v Nadeem* (1998)). Where exact restoration is impossible, the court does what is practically just. In *Cheese v Thomas* [1994] 1 All ER 35 (CA), an 86-year-old contributed £43,000 and his great-nephew £40,000 (by mortgage) to buy an £83,000 house in the nephew's name. The nephew defaulted, and the house sold for £55,000. The transaction was set aside, and the proceeds were split in proportion to contributions because it had been meant to benefit both. See also *Halpern v Halpern (No 2)* [2007] EWCA Civ 291 on a relaxed approach.
- Damages are generally unavailable, though the Q&A guide notes the comparison with *Mahoney v Purnell* [1996] 3 All ER 61. If undue influence were classified as a wrong, compensatory damages might become available (Birks; see Debate).

## Key authorities
- *Allcard v Skinner* (1887) 36 Ch D 145 (CA): a wealthy woman joined a sisterhood whose rules required obedience to the Lady Superior (whose voice was "the voice of God") and banned consulting outsiders without leave. She gave away her property. She left in 1879 but did not sue until 1885. Held: presumed undue influence, arising from the relationship, the vows and especially the rule against outside advice, without any wrongdoing by the Lady Superior. But the claim was barred by delay. Key points: the actual/presumed distinction (Cotton LJ's "two classes"; Lindley LJ's "two groups"); Lindley LJ's principle of protecting people from being victimised, not from their own folly; the "ordinary motives" test now adopted by *Etridge*; and Bowen LJ's "fetter placed upon the conscience of the recipient".
- *Williams v Bayley* (1866) LR 1 HL 200: mortgage "extorted ... by undue pressure" (an implied threat to prosecute the son). Now seen as lawful act duress.
- *Inche Noriah v Shaik Allie Bin Omar* [1929] AC 127 (PC): the standard for rebutting by independent advice.
- *Re Craig* [1970]; *O'Sullivan v MAM* [1985]; *Lloyds Bank v Bundy*: examples of relationships proved on the facts. *Bundy* is also where Lord Denning proposed a general "inequality of bargaining power" principle.
- *National Westminster Bank v Morgan* [1985] AC 686 (HL): bank and customer is not a protected relationship. Introduced "manifest disadvantage", later dropped by *Etridge*. Rejected a general doctrine of inequality of bargaining power.
- *BCCI v Aboody* [1990] 1 QB 923 (CA): actual undue influence and inducement; the classification later adopted in *O'Brien*.
- *Barclays Bank v O'Brien* [1994] 1 AC 180 (HL): Mr O'Brien misrepresented that the charge was limited to £60,000 and short-term, and the bank's instructions to explain the documents were ignored. The bank had constructive notice and could not enforce. In the end this was a misrepresentation case, not undue influence. It set up the "notice" framework, largely overtaken by *Etridge*.
- *CIBC Mortgages v Pitt* [1994] 1 AC 200 (HL): actual undue influence needs no proof of disadvantage. A joint loan does not put the bank on inquiry.
- *Credit Lyonnais v Burch* [1997] 1 All ER 144 (CA): a junior employee gave security for her employer's debts. Extends "on inquiry" beyond sexual relationships.
- *Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge (No 2)* [2001] UKHL 44, [2002] 2 AC 773 (HL): eight conjoined appeals (seven bank enforcement cases and one claim against a solicitor). The leading statement: a single concept; evidential presumption; the "calls for explanation" test; husband and wife not a protected relationship; the bank on inquiry in all non-commercial surety cases; detailed steps for banks and solicitors.
- *UCB Corporate Services v Williams* [2002] EWCA Civ 555: inducement in actual undue influence. Cited in the Q&A guide on underhand dealing that might have dissuaded the claimant.
- *Hammond v Osborn* (2002) (CA): gifts of nearly £300,000, over 90% of an elderly donor's liquid assets, which also triggered a large tax bill, to a neighbour carer. The presumption arose and was not rebutted, as he had no advice.
- *R v Attorney-General for England and Wales* [2003] UKPC 22: an SAS confidentiality contract. The majority (Lord Hoffmann) required an inference of unfair *exploitation*. A contract any soldier could reasonably be asked to sign was not one, even without legal advice. Lord Scott, dissenting, said the hierarchical military relationship made it a classic presumed case and the MoD should have offered independent advice.
- *National Commercial Bank (Jamaica) v Hew* [2003] UKPC 51: Lord Millett's two elements (a relationship capable of influence, and abuse of it). A defendant-sided view.
- *Pesticcio v Huet* [2004] EWCA Civ 372: Mummery LJ said the basis of intervention is public policy, not a wrongful act. A transaction may be set aside even though the recipient's conduct cannot be criticised. A claimant-sided view.
- *Drew v Daniel* [2005] EWCA Civ 507; *Leeder v Stevens* [2005]; *Abbey National v Stringer* [2006]; *Smith v Cooper* [2010]: listed in the materials (relationships, exploitation, rebuttal) without further detail in the sources.
- *Goodchild v Bradbury* (2006): a hospitalised, frail great-uncle gave land to his great-nephew, who sold it to a developer for £1,800 using a solicitor arranged by the developer. Both transfers were set aside.
- *Hewett v First Plus* [2010] EWCA Civ 312: the will need not be wholly overborne.
- *Nature Resorts v First Citizens Bank* [2022] UKPC 10: the modern summary. Rebuttal is possible without advice where the claimant is commercially experienced. Undue influence need not be a civil wrong ([15]).
- *Cheese v Thomas* [1994]: proportionate restitution on rescission.

## Debate and critique
- **Defendant-sided or claimant-sided?** This is the central essay debate.
  - *Defendant-sided (wrongdoing or exploitation)*: Lindley LJ's "victimised" language in *Allcard*; Lord Nicholls's "abuse" and "connotation of impropriety" in *Etridge*; Lord Hoffmann in *R v A-G* ("unfair exploitation"); Lord Millett in *Hew* ("abuse", "victimisation", "exploitation"). McKendrick infers from these that some form of wrongdoing appears to be indispensable.
  - *Claimant-sided (impaired autonomy)*: Birks and Chin, "On the Nature of Undue Influence" (1995), read *Allcard* as presuming no threats. The claimant was "excessively spell-bound" and her capacity to judge her own interests in relation to the order was impaired. Birks ((2004) 120 LQR 34) argues for "innocent undue influence". If an innocent misrepresentation is enough to rescind, "relational paralysis" should be enough too, and proving wrongdoing should matter only where the claimant seeks to shift a loss (damages). *Pesticcio* and *Nature Resorts* ([15]) support this.
  - *Problems with each*: Chen-Wishart ([2006] CLP 231) argues *Allcard* cannot realistically support a claimant-sided story, since the nun knew what was expected and acted from willing, enthusiastic devotion. Treating her as unable to manage her affairs would be patronising and would deny her freedom to express her faith. Equally, a wrongdoing reading struggles with the finding that the Lady Superior exerted no actual pressure.
  - *Relational middle way*: McKendrick suggests that both elements are relevant and courts weigh the evidence as a whole. Chen-Wishart ("Undue Influence: Beyond Impaired Consent and Wrongdoing Towards a Relational Analysis", in *Mapping the Law*, 2006) calls for analysis of the relationship, the appropriateness of the transaction, the claimant's motives and the defendant's behaviour. The Concentrate guide points to Devenney and Chandler, "Unconscionability and the Taxonomy of Undue Influence" [2007] JBL 269, and Morgan, *Great Debates: Contract Law*.
  - *Status of the debate*: *Nature Resorts* followed *Pesticcio* without discussing *R v A-G* or *Hew*, which are Privy Council decisions of equal standing. McKendrick therefore says English law does not yet unequivocally treat undue influence as something other than a wrong. *In re Naidoo* [2023] EWHC 500 (Ch) recognises that it can exist without wrongdoing.
- **What is being "presumed"?** It is unclear whether the presumption is of dependence, influence or exploitation. Lord Hobhouse said only the relationship of trust is presumed; there is "no presumption properly so called" of abuse, only a factual inference ([104]). The language of "presumption" confuses more than it helps (McKendrick).
- **Undue influence and duress: merge?** Threat cases have moved to duress (*Times Travel*; *Nature Resorts*). Lord Hodge in *Times Travel* built lawful act duress on equity's treatment of reprehensible conduct in undue influence. The Q&A guide asks whether the two should be merged. Remember the different vocabulary: undue influence speaks of trust, confidence and free judgment; duress speaks of illegitimate pressure, causation and alternatives.
- **Gender, family and the *Etridge* compromise.**
  - *Etridge* tries to protect vulnerable sureties without choking off lending secured on family homes (Lord Bingham's balance).
  - McKendrick notes it makes it much harder for wives to win at trial: there is no presumption from marriage, and a guarantee of a husband's debts is usually explicable.
  - Protection rests largely on solicitors' advice. Lord Nicholls admitted this had previously been a "charade", and McKendrick asks whether it will work in practice.
  - The handout's optional readings take a critical, contextual line: Chen-Wishart, "The O'Brien Principle and Substantive Unfairness" (1997) 56 CLJ 60; Auchmuty, "The Rhetoric of Equality and the Problem of Heterosexuality" in *Feminist Perspectives on Contract Law* (2005); Burns, "The elderly and undue influence inter vivos" (2018) Legal Studies. The sources give only titles, so their arguments are not summarised here.
- **Unconscionability and inequality of bargaining power.**
  - Lord Denning in *Bundy* tried to unify duress, undue influence and unconscionable bargains under inequality of bargaining power. *Morgan* rejected this, and *Times Travel* ([26]) confirmed there is no such general doctrine.
  - A separate, narrow equitable doctrine of **unconscionable bargains** exists (McKendrick ch 20). It covers transactions with "poor and ignorant persons" (*Fry v Lane* (1888) 40 Ch D 312; *Cresswell v Potter* [1978] 1 WLR 255), or those under some serious disadvantage, where the stronger party exploited that weakness "in a morally culpable manner" and the bargain is "overreaching and oppressive", not merely hard or improvident (Peter Millett QC in *Alec Lobb (Garages) v Total Oil* [1983] 1 WLR 87, adopted by Lord Hodge in *Times Travel* [23]–[24]; *Strydom v Vendside* [2009] EWHC 2130 (QB), [39]). Unequal bargaining power alone is not enough; unconscientious advantage must be taken of the disabling condition (*Boustany v Pigott* (1993) 69 P & CR 298, PC). Dillon LJ in the Court of Appeal in *Alec Lobb* [1985] 1 WLR 173 said courts interfere only in exceptional cases where the strong should not be allowed to "push the weak to the wall".
  - Scholars try to bring the cases together. Bamforth identifies four recurring factors: serious disadvantage, actual or constructive fraud, lack of independent advice, and disadvantageous terms. Capper identifies three: relational inequality, transactional imbalance and unconscionable conduct. McKendrick warns that multi-factor approaches risk inconsistent outcomes.
  - Collins explains the classical reluctance: freedom of contract permits procedural, not substantive, scrutiny. So courts dress up concern about unfair terms as procedural defects. The "calls for explanation" requirement is one place where substantive unfairness openly enters undue influence, through evidence.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Two-party or three-party?** Identify who influenced whom, and who the contract or gift is with. If B is the influencer and C the counterparty, you need both steps: establish B's undue influence against A, **then** C's notice or agency.
2. **Actual undue influence?** Look for direct evidence of domination, bullying, deception or manipulation of a dependent person (for example, a medium exploiting a bereaved believer, as in the Q&A guide's *Lyon v Home* and *Nottidge v Prince* examples). Show it induced the transaction (*Aboody*; *UCB v Williams*). There is no need to show disadvantage (*Pitt*). If the "influence" is really a threat, consider **duress** instead or as well.
3. **Presumed undue influence.**
   - *Relationship*: is it a protected category (solicitor and client, doctor and patient, spiritual adviser, parent and young child)? Spouses and bank–customer are **not** protected. If not protected, is trust and confidence proved on the facts? Look for reliance for advice, dependence, age, illness, language barriers, isolation, or one party managing the other's affairs (*Bundy*, *Burch*, *Re Craig*, *Stringer*, *Hammond*).
   - *Calls for explanation*: consider the size of the gift relative to the donor's wealth, the absence of benefit, the risk to the home, and concealed interests (such as the influencer's undisclosed directorship of the recipient). Is it explicable by ordinary motives (a Christmas present, or a spouse supporting the family business)?
   - *Rebuttal*: was there independent, informed, competent advice, given with knowledge of the facts? Was the claimant free to act on it? Is there other evidence of free judgment, such as commercial experience (*Nature Resorts*)? Was the act spontaneous? (*Re Brocklehurst's Estate* [1978] Ch 14, cited in the Q&A guide, warns against interfering with eccentric generosity absent fraud.)
4. **Three-party analysis.**
   - Was the creditor put on inquiry? It is if the relationship is non-commercial and the surety is not acting for a joint purpose. A joint loan, as in *Pitt*, does not put it on inquiry unless the bank knows the real purpose. A wife who is a director or shareholder of the husband's company still counts.
   - Did the creditor take the *Etridge* steps: direct contact, nomination of a solicitor, disclosure of financial information, and written confirmation of advice? If it did, it is protected unless it knew or ought to have known that the advice was defective. Any claim then lies against the solicitor.
   - Alternatively, was the influencer the creditor's agent?
5. **Remedy.** Rescission. Check the bars:
   - **delay** after the influence ends (*Allcard*: six years). Time may run from when the influence ceases or, if there was fraud, from discovery (Q&A guide);
   - **affirmation**;
   - **third-party rights**;
   - **impossibility**: money already spent, but note the flexible approach of *Cheese v Thomas* and *Halpern*.
   
   The claimant must give back benefits received (*Dunbar Bank v Nadeem*). Damages are generally unavailable.
6. **Also consider:** misrepresentation by the influencer (*O'Brien* was decided on misrepresentation; see `misrepresentation.md`), unconscionable bargain doctrine for poor, ignorant or disadvantaged claimants, and duress for threats.

**Typical traps:**
- Saying husband and wife is a protected relationship.
- Using "manifest disadvantage" as the test. It is now "calls for explanation / not explicable by ordinary motives".
- Forgetting that the bank is protected if it followed the *Etridge* steps.
- Treating independent advice as automatically conclusive, or as always required.
- Ignoring delay (*Allcard*).
- Mixing duress vocabulary with undue influence vocabulary.
