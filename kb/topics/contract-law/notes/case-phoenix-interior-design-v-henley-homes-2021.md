# Phoenix Interior Design Ltd v Henley Homes plc [2021] EWHC 1573 (QB) (case note)

> Case note written from secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base. The judge (Freedman J)
> and the date (9 June 2021) come from case listings found in search results. Check the judgment before citing
> pinpoints or the precise wording of the clause.

## Overview
High Court (Queen's Bench Division), Freedman J, 9 June 2021. A recent illustration of two issues: **incorporation of
standard terms** where the documents referred to terms that were not physically attached, and the **UCTA 1977
reasonableness test** applied to an unusual exclusion clause in a supplier's terms.

## Facts
Phoenix, an interior design firm, provided design services and furnishings for the refurbishment of the Dunalastair
Hotel in the Scottish Highlands, described in the commentary as a five-star hotel. The relationship broke
down and the defendants withheld the balance of the price, relying on delays and the quality of the furnishings, and
counterclaimed. Phoenix relied on its standard terms. Its first proposal said the terms were "overleaf" when they were
in fact supplied as a separate document; the terms had also been handed over in hard copy earlier, and the final
contract document said it was subject to Phoenix's standard terms. One of those terms removed Phoenix's liability under
any warranty, condition or guarantee if the full price had not been paid by the due date.

## Held
- The standard terms **were incorporated**: a reasonable person would understand the reference to mean the terms
  Phoenix had already supplied, and the defendants put forward no competing terms.
- The exclusion clause was **unreasonable under UCTA** (applying s 11 and the Schedule 2 guidelines), so it was
  ineffective. It was unusual and went much further than a standard anti-set-off clause; it was not drawn to the
  defendants' attention; it was exorbitant, because a slight late or short payment could remove all redress for
  defective goods; and it was impracticable because it fixed no clear payment date.
- Phoenix therefore could not use the clause to defeat the defendants' counterclaim for defective goods.
- One account reports that the court found a "five-star" finish was not part of the contract specification and that
  Phoenix succeeded on that point. The accounts consulted do not set out the final figures, so the overall money
  outcome is not stated here.

## Why it matters
- Shows that standard terms can be incorporated despite a defective reference in the contract documents, but that
  unusual or harsh clauses remain subject to the reasonableness test.
- Emphasises prominence and explanation of unusual clauses, and that a narrower clause (such as an ordinary
  anti-set-off clause) may have been enough to protect the supplier.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Are the standard terms incorporated (signature, reference, course of dealing, notice)?
2. If so, does UCTA apply and does the clause meet the reasonableness test?
3. Consider prominence, bargaining position, whether the clause is unusual for the trade, and its effect on the
   other party's remedies.
