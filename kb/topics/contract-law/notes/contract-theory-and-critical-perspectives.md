# Contract Theory and Critical Perspectives

## Overview
This note gathers the "big picture" material the module uses to frame all the substantive topics. It covers what a contract is, where the English law of contract came from, the competing values of freedom/sanctity of contract and fairness, and the theoretical debate between *promise-based* (autonomy) accounts and accounts that stress *fair exchange*, reliance and collective values. The introductory and revision lectures contain no substantive content of their own: they direct students to re-read Chen-Wishart ch 1 and Anson ch 1 and to connect the doctrines, paying particular attention to doctrines that are easily confused. The material below is therefore drawn from McKendrick ch 1 (with Adams and Brownsword), Atiyah's lecture "Contract and Fair Exchange" (1985) 35 UTLJ 1, and Atiyah's review of Fried, *Contract as Promise* ((1981) 95 Harv L Rev 509). It is essay material and a way of tying the module together.

## The law
Theory does not give "rules" in the usual sense. This section sets out the doctrinal skeleton the theories try to explain, as McKendrick describes it.

1. **No formal definition.** English law has no formal definition of a contract and, without a Code, has not needed one. Textbook definitions are illustrative only:
   - Anson: the law of contract is "provisionally" the branch of law that determines when a promise is legally binding.
   - Treitel: a contract is an agreement giving rise to obligations enforced or recognised by law, distinguished by being based on agreement. Treitel qualifies this in three ways: (i) the law often looks at the *objective appearance* of agreement; (ii) some obligations, such as terms implied by law, are imposed, not agreed; (iii) freedom of contract has been limited to protect weaker parties.
2. **Historical origin.** English contract law grew around the form of action of *assumpsit*, not from an underlying theory. After the Common Law Procedure Act 1852 abolished the forms of action, nineteenth-century treatise writers (Pollock, Anson) rationalised the case law, drawing heavily on continental jurists (Simpson, "Innovation in Nineteenth Century Contract Law" (1975) 91 LQR 247). The shift from "promise broken" to "contract broken" reflects the will theory of Pothier and Savigny (McKendrick ch 3).
3. **The classical outline**, still broadly the formal structure:
   - **Formation and enforceability:** agreement (offer plus mirror-image acceptance), consideration (something of value given in return; adequacy irrelevant; gratuitous promises unenforceable unless in a deed, with some protection through estoppel), and ICLR.
   - **Parties:** privity, modified by the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 for benefits but not burdens.
   - **Policing doctrines:** mistake, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, illegality, frustration.
   - **Breach and remedies:** expectation damages and termination for breach of an important term. Specific performance is exceptional, so the law gives "a money substitute for performance, not performance itself".
4. **General principles and specific regimes.** The general principles claim to apply to all contracts, but many contracts (employment, landlord and tenant, consumer credit) are regulated separately. McKendrick says the claim to generality should not be "taken too seriously".
5. **Incremental change.** Anson (1879) would recognise the outline today. Freedom of contract and freedom *from* contract remain the underlying norms, and consideration remains central. But there is now more open concern with procedural and substantive fairness:
   - duress and undue influence flourished in the late twentieth century;
   - statute regulates fairness (UCTA 1977; Consumer Rights Act 2015 Part 2);
   - freedom from contract has been eroded "covertly", e.g. restitution for pre-contract work (*British Steel v Cleveland Bridge* [1984] 1 All ER 504) and finding contracts by benevolent interpretation (*Blackpool & Fylde Aero Club v Blackpool BC* [1990] 1 WLR 1195).

   English law still refuses to recognise a duty to negotiate with reasonable care or in good faith. McKendrick suggests it should consider openly recognising what it already does indirectly. Collins argues contract law has been *transformed* from its classical ideals, a change not visible in formal doctrine.
6. **Transactional and transnational context** (McKendrick):
   - many "rules" are default rules displaced by the contract's own terms (boilerplate, exclusion, force majeure, entire agreement and agreed damages clauses);
   - standard forms raise battle-of-the-forms problems, and one-sided consumer terms that the common law largely failed to control were left to Parliament (UCTA, via a private member's Bill; CRA 2015, from an EU Directive);
   - EU influence diminishes after the UK left the EU;
   - the UK has not ratified the CISG (Vienna Convention);
   - the Unidroit Principles and PECL are non-binding statements that parties may incorporate or use as models.

## Key authorities
Cases and statutes used in the theoretical literature as illustrations (detail is in the doctrinal notes):
- *Lloyds Bank v Bundy* [1975] QB 326: a father's guarantee and mortgage was set aside. Lord Denning proposed a doctrine of inequality of bargaining power (relief where a party without independent advice contracts on very unfair terms or for grossly inadequate consideration when bargaining power is "grievously impaired"). Sir Eric Sachs focused on independent advice. Atiyah uses the case to show procedural and substantive unfairness blending together.
- *National Westminster Bank v Morgan* [1983] (cited by Atiyah as a recent sign of life in equitable relief).
- *Liverpool CC v Irwin* (HL): an implied term of reasonable care to maintain the common parts of a council block. Necessity, not reasonableness, was the stated test, but Atiyah argues the content of the duty was fixed by the low rent.
- *Staffordshire Area Health Authority v South Staffordshire Waterworks*: a 1929 water supply contract "for all times hereafter" was held terminable on reasonable notice, which in effect let the price rise (Atiyah).
- *The Atlantic Baron*: a threat to withhold delivery of a ship unless the price was increased was capable of being economic duress, even though there was a small variation that might have been consideration (Atiyah).
- *Universe Tankships v ITWF* [1983] AC 366: in Atiyah's account, the House of Lords was moving away from the "overborne will" theory of duress.
- *Cresswell v Cresswell*; *Backhouse v Backhouse*: separating wives' transfers of the home at an undervalue were set aside (Atiyah).
- *Ocean Island* case (Megarry V-C): the "benefit and burden" principle (Atiyah).
- *Mountford v Scott* [1975] Ch 258: nominal consideration remains valid in English law (Atiyah).
- *Hoffman v Red Owl Stores* (US) and *Box v Midland Bank* [1979] 2 Lloyd's Rep 391 (its nearest English equivalent): liability for unbargained-for reliance (Atiyah on Fried).
- *Luxor (Eastbourne) v Cooper* [1941] AC 108: an estate agent's commission is payable only on sale. Atiyah asks what, if anything, the owner "promised".
- *Jacob & Youngs v Kent* (US, "Reading pipes"); *Boulton v Jones* (1857) (Bramwell B): restitution versus autonomy (Atiyah on Fried).
- Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 14(6) (then the definition of merchantable quality, with price as a relevant circumstance): used by Atiyah as an example of implied value for money.
- Consumer Rights Act 2015 Part 2; UCTA 1977: statutory fairness controls (McKendrick).

## Debate and critique

### 1. Market-individualism v consumer-welfarism (Adams and Brownsword (1987) 7 LS 205, in McKendrick)
- **Market-individualism.** Contract facilitates *competitive* exchange.
  - (a) Security of transactions is reflected in the objective approach, caution about subjective mistake, protection of third-party purchasers, and expectation damages.
  - (b) The rules should be clear and predictable. The postal acceptance rule is the "model" here.
  - (c) The law should defer to commercial practice, e.g. incorporation by reasonable notice, *Hillas v Arcos* on certainty, *The Eurymedon*, and the convenience-based shop-display rules.
  - Its individualist side rests on freedom of contract (partner-freedom and term-freedom) and sanctity of contract. Sanctity explains the refusal to review adequacy of consideration, necessity as the test for implied terms, the hard line on mistake and frustration, caution about economic duress and inequality of bargaining power, and reluctance to release parties for minor uncertainty.
  - The line between misrepresentation (actionable) and non-disclosure (prudent bargaining) captures the philosophy.
- **Consumer-welfarism** favours consumer protection, fairness and reasonableness, with closer regulation of consumer contracts and somewhat more of commercial contracts. Its principles include:
  - constancy (no "blowing hot and cold": estoppel);
  - proportionality (penalties, exemption clauses);
  - bad faith;
  - no profit from one's own wrong;
  - unjust enrichment (penalties, cost-of-performance damages, *White & Carter*);
  - the better loss-bearer;
  - exploitation (unconscionability, but non-intervention between equals);
  - a fair deal for consumers;
  - informational advantage;
  - responsibility for fault;
  - paternalism.

  Its greatest success is the reasonableness regime for exemption clauses under UCTA. Its weakness is pluralism: without a ranking of its principles, outcomes are unpredictable.
- McKendrick's framing: the classical law rests on freedom and sanctity of contract, but it was never committed to them exclusively. Minors' limited capacity and illegality show an older concern with protecting the weak and with public policy.

### 2. Atiyah, "Contract and Fair Exchange" (1985) 35 UTLJ 1
- **Target.** The dogma that the *adequacy of consideration is immaterial*, so courts only police the bargaining *process* (fraud, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, which are traditionally explained as defects of consent or "cognitive weaknesses"), not the *result*.
- **Thesis.** Contract law is in fact "greatly concerned with substantive fairness of exchange". Courts pursue it, often without statute, wherever there is a *gross* imbalance, not every trivial one.
- **Preliminary points.**
  - (i) Public demand for "fair wages, fair prices, fair rents" is powerful and has shaped statute, so it would be surprising if judges were untouched by it.
  - (ii) The adequacy principle concerns only *validity*. Courts can still achieve fairness by *adjusting* obligations instead of invalidating the contract.
  - (iii) Procedural and substantive fairness cannot be separated. Fraud and threats are condemned largely because they produce one-sided deals, and a grossly one-sided deal leads courts to infer procedural impropriety.
- **Illustrations.**
  - *Offer and acceptance*: the rule that a bare "I accept" binds the acceptor as fully as the offeror, and the suspicion of options and of requirement and output contracts, reflect a belief that it is unfair for one party to be bound while the other is free.
  - *Implication and construction*: the price affects the quality a buyer can expect under the statutory merchantability definition; an actor's right to perform as well as be paid; *Liverpool CC v Irwin* (the rent shaped the landlord's duty); *Staffordshire AHA* (the inflation-hit water contract was ended on notice).
  - *Election maxims*: you cannot "approbate and reprobate", must elect between affirming and avoiding, and take the burden with the benefit (*Ocean Island*). These preserve the integrity of the exchange.
  - *Procedural doctrines that are really substantive*: *Bundy*, *Cresswell* and *Backhouse* (the need for independent advice is inferred from the gross unfairness itself, which is circular); *Atlantic Baron* (moving from consideration to economic duress is "functionally equivalent" to using consideration without the adequacy limit).
- **Normative defence.**
  - Against the subjective-value sceptic (who says there is no fair exchange beyond a free one): procedures shape results, so if there is no just result in the abstract there is no just procedure either. Even proceduralists assume a *substantive* rule that executory bargains bind, and that rule has distributive effects.
  - Courts fix fair prices all the time: tort damages, reasonable-price contracts, implied terms, compulsory acquisition. This is not just parasitic on the market, because some things (personal injury) have no market and the court sets the rules of any hypothetical bargain (*Bracewell v Appleby*).
  - Market imperfections justify relief: a consumer may assume a dealer charges market prices (Paley); monopoly, including the "bilateral monopoly" in contract modifications and salvage; sales of information; externalities (Macneil's "agents in a world without principals").
  - Overriding consent is not illegitimate. Consent given without effective choice or adequate information is weak, and insisting that consent binds whatever its reasons is "formalistic if not fetishistic".
  - Conclusion: once the law cares about procedures, as it must, it is "compelled to take an interest in the substantive justice of contracts".

### 3. Fried's promise principle and Atiyah's critique (review in (1981) 95 Harv L Rev 509)
**Fried's position** (as reported by Atiyah):
- Contract rests on the liberal "promise principle": autonomous persons may bind themselves by invoking the *convention* of promising, and the state should respect this.
- Expectation damages follow naturally. A promisor who has simply changed his mind deserves no relief, because relief would disrespect his original choice.
- Unbargained-for reliance (*Hoffman v Red Owl*) and restitution are explained by *other* principles: tort, and a duty not to mislead or harm carelessly.
- Consideration is incoherent: if adequacy is for the parties, why refuse one-sided promises? Fried expects the promise principle to be recognised more openly.
- Mistake and frustration fill *gaps* using fairness or administrative principles, not risk-sharing, since redistribution belongs to taxation.
- Duress depends on the offer being wrongful, and property rights rest on moral foundations.
- Procedural unconscionability is treated as mistake or good faith. Substantive unconscionability is generally rejected, except in cases of market failure (cartels, barriers to entry, information problems).

**Atiyah's criticisms:**
- Fried's concessions are so large that the promise principle justifies only "a very small part of contract law". He reclassifies large parts as tort or restitution.
- Liability for unintended consequences, and much of mistake and frustration, is gap-filling. Standard forms and signatures on multi-page printed contracts strain any idea of intention.
- Fried's paradigm, a face-to-face swap ("my cow Rose for your $80"), is too simple.
- **Remedies**: the law enforces *exchanges*, not bare promises; it gives damages, not performance; and the **mitigation** rule often removes the remedy altogether. Fried's description of mitigation as an altruistic duty that "shields the promise-breaker" is unconvincing.
- **Obligation first, promise after**: in the estate agent case (*Luxor v Cooper*), what was "promised" is a legal construct fixed by policy.
- **Classical theory's exclusionary side**: no promise meant no liability, out of respect for autonomy. Fried keeps the affirmative side but drops the exclusionary side when he endorses reliance and restitution liability (*Hoffman*; *Jacob & Youngs*, which Bramwell B's autonomy reasoning in *Boulton v Jones* would have resolved the other way). Fried cannot explain why another person's choice to rely is attributable to the non-promisor.
- **Collective and egalitarian values**: anti-discrimination law and the City Takeover Code's equal-price rule override freely made contracts. Enforcing *executory* contracts presupposes a distribution of entitlements (the promisee owns the promise, and the promisor may not change his mind). That is not distributively neutral, and it favours good planners. Respect for autonomy does not obviously require giving conclusive weight to a *past* choice over a *present* one (compare liberal divorce law).
- **Paternalism**: welfare-state laws show sympathy for people who misjudge risk. "Respect" for the autonomy of a person of low bargaining skill can ring hollow when a smart operator exploits them.
- **Atiyah's conclusion**: treat promises as *prima facie*, not conclusively, binding. The presumption rests on likely mutual benefit and likely reliance, and can be rebutted by fraud, coercion and, increasingly, unfairness. Because contract reflects diverse and conflicting values, it cannot be reduced to a single principle.

### 4. Further strands from the textbook chapters
- **Objectivity**: Lord Steyn (certainty and predictability) and Stevens (agreements are communicative acts) give different justifications. Howarth and Vorster debate *whose* perspective counts.
- **Will theory**: Ibbetson calls the import of will theory into the common law "a mess". Paley's objective theory sat badly with meeting-of-minds theory.
- **Offer and acceptance as formalism** (Collins), against the courts' preference for clear rules applied flexibly (McKendrick ch 3).
- McKendrick on the postal rule: the choice of rule is "not a value-free exercise", even if its later application looks mechanical.

### Connecting themes for revision (the brief of lecture 20)
- Fairness doctrines (duress, undue influence, unconscionability, statutory control of terms) against sanctity of contract.
- Objectivity versus subjectivity across offer and acceptance, mistake, ICLR and interpretation.
- Reliance-based liability (promissory estoppel, misrepresentation) versus promise-based liability (consideration, expectation damages).
- Remedies: expectation damages as the "next best thing" to performance; mitigation; the exceptional nature of specific performance.

## Applying it to a problem question
Theory is mainly for essays, but it helps in problems too:
1. **Spot the policy choice.** When a rule is uncertain (an overtaken postal acceptance, revoking a unilateral offer, silence, uncertainty of terms), name the competing values, such as security of transactions against fairness or protection of the weaker party, before choosing an answer.
2. **Grossly unequal bargains.** Where the facts show a gross imbalance, check every doctrine that might give relief: duress (especially pressure to modify a contract), undue influence or the need for independent advice, misrepresentation, implied terms and construction, penalties, statutory controls (UCTA, CRA). Atiyah's point is that courts often reach substantive fairness through these "procedural" routes.
3. **Commercial versus consumer contexts.** Use the market-individualist and consumer-welfarist lens to predict how strictly a court will apply a rule.
4. **Essay structure.** Set out the classical position (freedom and sanctity; adequacy irrelevant), then the critique (Atiyah: substantive fairness is everywhere; Adams and Brownsword: two ideologies), then the defence (Fried's autonomy; certainty, as with Lord Steyn), and finish with a reasoned view, e.g. Atiyah's "prima facie binding" model or McKendrick's call for open recognition of what courts already do covertly.
5. **Trap.** Do not present a theory as the law. Cite the doctrinal authority first and use theory to evaluate it.
