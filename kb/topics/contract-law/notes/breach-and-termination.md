# Breach of Contract and Termination

## Overview
A breach of contract is a failure, without lawful excuse, to perform a contractual obligation. Liability is generally **strict**: fault is irrelevant unless the obligation is only to take reasonable care. Every breach gives a right to damages, at least nominal. Only some breaches also give the innocent party the **self-help** right to terminate, meaning to refuse further performance and treat itself as discharged. Termination is powerful because no court order is needed. It is also dangerous: a party who terminates without the right to do so is itself in repudiatory breach and may face a large damages claim. The core questions are:
- what counts as a breach;
- when it gives a right to terminate (conditions, warranties, innominate terms; entire and dependent obligations);
- how the right is exercised or lost (election and affirmation);
- how anticipatory breach works, including the *White & Carter* problem.
The law stated is that of England and Wales.

## The law

### 1. Remedies map and what termination is
1. **Self-help vs judicial remedies.** Termination is a self-help remedy. Damages (expectation, reliance, restitutionary, negotiating), the action for an agreed sum, specific performance and injunctions are judicial remedies. Parties may also agree remedies. The innocent party still needs a court to recover damages, and the contract-breaker may go to court to dispute a termination.
2. **Terminology.** Courts use "rescinded", "discharged", "repudiated", "brought to an end" and "rejected" loosely. Lord Wilberforce warned in *Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd* [1980] AC 827 that calling termination "rescission" causes confusion.
3. **Termination for breach is not rescission for vitiating factors.** Lord Porter in *Heyman v Darwins Ltd* [1942] AC 356 (approved in *Johnson v Agnew* [1980] AC 367): on acceptance of a repudiation, the injured party "is discharged from further performance and may bring an action for damages, but the contract itself is not rescinded".
   - Termination operates **prospectively only**.
   - Rescission for misrepresentation unwinds the contract for all purposes.
   - Some clauses survive termination, e.g. arbitration clauses (*Heyman*) and possibly confidentiality clauses.
4. **Termination for breach is not discharge by frustration.** Frustration ends the contract automatically, without either party being in breach (see `frustration.md`). *Photo Production* also rejected the doctrine of fundamental breach as a rule of law.
5. **Effect of termination** (*Photo Production*):
   - Accrued obligations remain enforceable.
   - Both parties are discharged from performance falling due after termination.
   - The contract-breaker's unperformed primary obligations are replaced by a secondary obligation to pay compensation, including loss of bargain.
   - Damages for loss up to termination are also recoverable.
   - Restitutionary claims are possible, though much more limited for the party in breach.
6. **Employment contracts.** *Geys v Société Générale, London Branch* [2012] UKSC 63: the Supreme Court rejected automatic termination and affirmed the election approach. A repudiation does not end an employment contract until it is accepted.

### 2. When is termination available?
7. **Kinds of breach.**
   - An *actual* breach occurs when performance is due.
   - An *anticipatory* breach occurs before performance is due. It may be by **renunciation** (words or conduct showing an intention not to perform all or part) or **impossibility** (disabling oneself from performing).
   - Breaches may be *total* or *partial*. A total breach always allows termination. Whether a partial breach does depends on the status of the term broken.
8. **Contingent conditions vs promissory obligations.**
   - A **contingent condition** is an event on which both parties' obligations depend but for which neither is responsible. If a *condition precedent* fails, the obligation never arises. If a *condition subsequent* occurs, the obligation is discharged. Neither is a breach.
   - A **promissory obligation** is a promise to perform. Its breach may or may not allow termination.
   - Treitel, '"Conditions" and "Conditions Precedent"' (1990) 106 LQR 185, and English (2021) 137 LQR 630 are in the further reading.
9. **Independent vs dependent obligations.**
   - If the innocent party's obligation is *independent* of the other's performance, it must still perform and can only claim damages. *Taylor v Webb* [1937] 2 KB 283: a tenant's covenant to pay rent is independent of the landlord's covenant to repair.
   - If obligations are *dependent*, breach may allow termination. SGA 1979 s 28: unless otherwise agreed, delivery and payment are "concurrent conditions".
10. **Entire vs divisible dependent obligations.**
   - An **entire** obligation must be completely performed before the other party's obligation to pay arises (*Cutter v Powell* (1795) 6 TR 320). *Re Hall & Barker* (1878): a shoemaker who agreed to make a pair of shoes "cannot offer you one shoe and ask you to pay one half the price".
   - The rule can be harsh (*Sumpter v Hedges* [1898] 1 QB 673; but see McFarlane and Stevens, 'In Defence of Sumpter v Hedges' (2002) 118 LQR 569). It is softened by:
     - **substantial performance** (*Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952] 2 All ER 176): a party who has substantially performed recovers the price less damages for the defects (McKendrick, discussing *Ruxley*);
     - restitution for the contract-breaker in limited cases;
     - SGA 1979 s 30(1): if the seller delivers short, the buyer may reject, but if it accepts the goods it must pay at the contract rate.
   - A **divisible** obligation always supports damages, and supports termination depending on the status of the term.
11. **Conditions, warranties and innominate terms.**
   - **Condition:** an essential term. Any breach allows termination regardless of consequences, plus damages up to termination and for loss of the bargain.
   - **Warranty:** a subsidiary term. Damages only; the contract continues, so there are no loss-of-bargain damages.
   - **Innominate (intermediate, "wait and see") term:** termination depends on the consequences of the breach. Diplock LJ's test in *Hong Kong Fir Shipping Co Ltd v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha Ltd* [1962] 2 QB 26: "Does the occurrence of the event deprive the party who has further undertakings to perform of substantially the whole benefit which it was the intention of the parties as expressed in the contract that he should obtain as the consideration for performing those undertakings?"
   - Upjohn LJ explained why seaworthiness cannot be a condition: it is broken by the slightest defect, such as a missing nail or medical supplies. It would be "contrary to common sense" to let the charterer terminate for such trifles.
   - The bar is high, because Diplock LJ drew the analogy with frustration (*Telford Homes v Ampurius Nu Homes* [2013] EWCA Civ 577). The test is applied at the date of termination.
   - Relevant factors (McKendrick): the intended benefit, the loss suffered, the cost of cure, the value already received, the breaching party's willingness to put things right, the likelihood of further breach, and the adequacy of damages.

### 3. How are terms classified?
12. **By statute.**
   - Conditions: SGA 1979 s 12(1) (right to sell), s 13(1) (correspondence with description), s 14(2) and (2C) (satisfactory quality, with exceptions for defects drawn to the buyer's attention or that examination ought to reveal), s 14(3) (fitness for a particular purpose made known, unless there is no reasonable reliance on the seller), s 15(2) (sale by sample).
   - Warranties: s 12(2), (4) and (5) (freedom from encumbrances, quiet possession).
   - **Slight breaches in non-consumer sales:** under SGA s 15A (the materials prints this passage under the heading "s 12(4)"), where a non-consumer buyer could otherwise reject for breach of ss 13–15 but the breach "is so slight that it would be unreasonable for him to reject them", it is treated as a breach of warranty. This can be excluded (s 15A(2)); see also s 30(2A). Its main target is *Arcos v Ronaasen* [1933] AC 470. There, staves for cement barrels were mostly slightly thicker than the contract's half inch. They were still usable, but the buyer was allowed to reject the whole consignment under s 13, apparently because the market price had fallen. Compare *Re Moore & Co and Landauer & Co* [1921] 2 KB 519.
   - **Consumer contracts** under the CRA 2015 do not use the condition/warranty labels. Instead they give a structured menu of remedies: s 19 (consumer's rights to enforce terms about goods), s 20 (right to reject), s 22 (short-term right to reject, generally within 30 days), s 24 (price reduction or final right to reject), s 28 (delivery), s 42 (digital content), s 54 (services).
13. **By judicial decision.** Precedent may fix a term's status. *The Mihalis Angelos* [1971] 1 QB 164: a charterparty statement of when the vessel was "expected ready to load" is a condition.
14. **By the parties.**
   - Parties are generally free to make any obligation a condition. Lord Wilberforce in *Bunge Corp v Tradax Export SA* [1981] 1 WLR 711 said it is open to them to agree that "any breach shall entitle the party not in default to treat the contract as repudiated".
   - *Lombard North Central plc v Butterworth* [1987] QB 527: a computer lease made time of payment "of the essence". Late payment was therefore a breach of condition. The penalty-type clause (cl 6) was unenforceable, but the lessor recovered arrears and future instalments as common law loss-of-bargain damages. Contrast *Financings Ltd v Baldock* [1963] 2 QB 104, where there was no condition and only arrears to the date of termination were recoverable.
   - **Courts control party classification in three ways:**
     - They may read "condition" in a non-technical sense. In *L Schuler AG v Wickman Machine Tool Sales Ltd* [1974] AC 235, a "condition" that the distributor visit six named manufacturers weekly (around 1,400 visits over 4½ years) was held not to allow termination for a single missed visit. Lord Reid said "the more unreasonable the result the more unlikely it is that the parties can have intended it". Lord Wilberforce dissented.
     - They may read down termination clauses. In *Rice (t/a Garden Guardian) v Great Yarmouth BC* [2003] TCLR 1 (CA), a clause allowing termination for breach "of any of its obligations" was not applied literally to a four-year maintenance contract, and the council's termination was wrongful.
     - They may treat termination under a clause as less potent than termination for breach of a true condition.
   - But clear words are needed to *exclude* the common law right to terminate by an express termination clause (*Stocznia Gdynia SA v Gearbulk Holdings Ltd* [2009] EWCA Civ 75).
15. **By the courts (default).**
   - If a term has not been classified, the default is that it is **innominate** unless the contrary is indicated. Hamblen LJ in *Grand China Logistics v Spar Shipping* [2016] EWCA Civ 982 said a term is innominate "unless it is clear that it is intended to be a condition or a warranty". See also *Ark Shipping v Silverburn Shipping (The Arctic)* [2019] EWCA Civ 1161.
   - *Cehave NV v Bremer (The Hansa Nord)* [1976] QB 44: an express term requiring shipment "in good condition" was innominate. Buyers who rejected a £100,000 cargo of citrus pulp pellets over partial damage (after the market fell), and then bought the same goods for about £30,000 and used them as intended, were confined to damages.
   - *Reardon Smith Line v Yngvar Hansen-Tangen* [1976] 1 WLR 989: a wrong yard number in a ship's description did not justify rejection. Lord Wilberforce doubted *Arcos*.
   - But time clauses in mercantile contracts will usually be conditions. *Bunge v Tradax*: a buyer's duty to give 15 days' notice of readiness of the vessel was a condition, so the sellers could terminate for notice given five days late. The House of Lords stressed commercial certainty, that the parties traded as both buyers and sellers, the interdependence of the obligations, business experience, and the difficulty of assessing damages. It rejected the argument that a condition requires every breach to deprive the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit. See also *The Naxos* [1990] 1 WLR 1337.

### 4. Electing to terminate, and losing the right
16. **Election.** A repudiatory breach does not itself end the contract. It gives the innocent party a choice: terminate (accept the repudiation) or affirm. An unaccepted repudiation is "a thing writ in water" (Asquith LJ, *Howard v Pickford Tool Co* [1951] 1 KB 417).
   - Acceptance needs no particular form, but must "clearly and unequivocally" convey that the contract is treated as at an end (Lord Steyn, *Vitol SA v Norelf Ltd* [1996] AC 800). Silence is generally not enough.
   - A termination is valid if a right to terminate existed, even if a wrong reason or no reason was given.
17. **Time to decide.** The innocent party need not choose at once. It may wait or negotiate, but must remain ready and willing to perform. Rix LJ in *Stocznia Gdanska SA v Latvian Shipping Co* [2002] EWCA Civ 889 recognised a "middle ground" while the innocent party makes up its mind. If it does nothing for too long, it may be treated as having affirmed. Meanwhile an anticipatory repudiation may be overtaken by frustration or by the innocent party's own breach, or the repudiating party may resume performance. Reserving rights expressly is prudent. There is no "third choice" to affirm yet be excused from performing (*Fercometal SARL v Mediterranean Shipping Co SA* [1989] AC 788).
18. **Loss of the right to terminate.**
   - The right is lost by affirmation, which must be unequivocal and is in principle irrevocable. It generally requires knowledge of the breach and of the right to choose. The law does not require the innocent party to "snatch at a repudiation" (*Yukong Line v Rendsberg* [1996]).
   - It may also be lost by operation of law, or by estoppel.
   - A **continuing** refusal to perform after affirmation can be a fresh repudiation that the innocent party may then accept (*Johnson v Agnew* [1980] AC 367; *The Fortune Plum* [2013] EWHC 1355 (Comm)).
19. **Good faith of the contract-breaker.** Cases such as *Woodar v Wimpey* [1980] 1 WLR 277 and *Federal Commerce v Molena Alpha* [1979] AC 757 are hard to reconcile. *Eminence Property Developments Ltd v Heaney* [2010] EWCA Civ 1168 states the test: viewed objectively from the position of a reasonable person in the innocent party's position, has the contract-breaker "clearly shown an intention to abandon and altogether refuse to perform the contract"? Motive matters only so far as a reasonable person would know of it. On the facts, a vendor's mistaken notice to complete, which was obviously a clerical error, was not repudiatory.

### 5. Anticipatory breach and affirmation
20. **Anticipatory breach gives an immediate cause of action.**
   - *Hochster v De La Tour* (1853) 2 E & B 678: a courier engaged from 1 June was told on 11 May his services were not needed. He could sue on 22 May.
   - *Frost v Knight* (1872) LR 7 Ex 111: Cockburn CJ said the promisee has "an inchoate right to the performance" and a right to have the contract "kept open as a subsisting and effective contract".
   - Devlin J in *Universal Cargo Carriers v Citati* [1957] 2 QB 401: anticipatory breach "was not devised as a whip" for deliberate contract-breakers only. It "covers all breaches that are bound to happen".
21. **Affirming and performing: *White and Carter (Councils) Ltd v McGregor* [1962] AC 413.** The claimants agreed to advertise the defendant's business on litter bins for three years. Before performance was due to begin, the defendant repudiated and asked them to cancel. The claimants refused to accept the repudiation, performed for the full period without trying to find substitute advertisers, and sued for the price. The House of Lords (3:2) allowed the claim in debt; no duty to mitigate applies to a debt claim. Two limits appear in Lord Reid's speech:
   - (i) **Co-operation.** Affirmation-and-perform is impossible if performance needs the contract-breaker's co-operation.
   - (ii) **Legitimate interest.** A party with "no legitimate interest, financial or otherwise, in performing the contract rather than claiming damages" may be confined to damages.
   Lords Morton and Keith dissented, giving the example of an expert who travels abroad to write an unwanted report.
22. **Developing the legitimate interest limit.**
   - *The Alaskan Trader* [1984] 1 All ER 129: shipowners who repaired a vessel at a cost of about £800,000 and kept it fully crewed for charterers who no longer wanted it had no legitimate interest. They were confined to damages.
   - Simon J in *Ocean Marine Navigation v Koch Carbon (The Dynamic)* [2003] EWHC 1936 (Comm) summarised the rule. The burden is on the contract-breaker. It is not discharged merely by showing that the benefit to the innocent party is small compared with the loss to the contract-breaker. The exception applies only in extreme cases where damages are adequate and keeping the contract alive would be unreasonable.
   - Cooke J in *The Aquafaith* [2012] EWHC 1077 (Comm) spoke of insistence that is "wholly unreasonable", "extremely unreasonable" or "perverse".

## Key authorities
- *Photo Production v Securicor* [1980] AC 827 (HL): effect of termination (primary obligations replaced by secondary ones); fundamental breach rejected as a rule of law.
- *Heyman v Darwins* [1942] AC 356 (HL); *Johnson v Agnew* [1980] AC 367 (HL): acceptance of repudiation discharges future performance; it is not rescission ab initio.
- *Geys v Société Générale* [2012] UKSC 63: the election approach applies to employment contracts.
- *Hochster v De La Tour* (1853); *Frost v Knight* (1872): anticipatory breach.
- *Cutter v Powell* (1795); *Sumpter v Hedges* [1898] (CA); *Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952] (CA): entire obligations and substantial performance.
- *Taylor v Webb* [1937]: independent covenants.
- *Arcos v Ronaasen* [1933] AC 470 (HL): rejection for a trivial breach of a statutory condition. Now limited by SGA s 15A for non-consumers.
- *Hong Kong Fir v Kawasaki* [1962] 2 QB 26 (CA): innominate terms; the "substantially the whole benefit" test. An unseaworthy ship with 17 of 24 months left did not justify termination.
- *The Mihalis Angelos* [1971] 1 QB 164 (CA): "expected ready to load" is a condition.
- *Schuler v Wickman* [1974] AC 235 (HL): the label "condition" is not conclusive.
- *The Hansa Nord* [1976] QB 44 (CA): an express quality term was innominate; no rejection for a minor breach.
- *Bunge v Tradax* [1981] 1 WLR 711 (HL): time clauses in mercantile contracts are usually conditions; certainty matters.
- *Lombard North Central v Butterworth* [1987] QB 527 (CA): "time of the essence" makes late payment a breach of condition with loss-of-bargain damages.
- *Rice v Great Yarmouth BC* [2003] (CA): a broad termination clause was read down.
- *Stocznia Gdanska v Latvian Shipping* [2002] EWCA Civ 889: time to elect.
- *Vitol v Norelf* [1996] AC 800 (HL): the form of acceptance of a repudiation.
- *White and Carter v McGregor* [1962] AC 413 (HL): affirmation and claim for the price; limits of co-operation and legitimate interest.
- *The Alaskan Trader* [1984]; *The Dynamic* [2003]; *The Aquafaith* [2012]: the legitimate interest limit applied.
- SGA 1979 ss 11(4), 12–15A, 28, 30, 34–35A; CRA 2015 ss 19, 20, 22, 24, 28, 42, 54, Sch 2 Pt 1 paras 7–8 (as listed in the materials).

## Debate and critique
- **Certainty vs fairness.** McKendrick identifies a "tension at the heart of English law".
  - One strategy focuses on the *nature of the term* (conditions and warranties). It favours certainty and suits commercial parties who need to know at once whether they may terminate (*Bunge*).
  - The other focuses on the *consequences of breach* (innominate terms). It gives proportionate, fair outcomes but makes litigation harder to predict.
  - Classifying more terms as innominate may in practice deter termination. Risk-averse advisers will not recommend termination when a wrongful termination could itself be a repudiation (*Telford Homes*; *Urban I (Blonk Street) v Ayres* [2013]). Reported cases on the dangers of wrongful repudiation include *Reardon Smith*, *Hong Kong Fir* and *The Hansa Nord*.
- **Opportunistic termination.** *Arcos*, *The Hansa Nord* and *Hong Kong Fir* all involved termination driven by a falling market. Brownsword, 'Retrieving Reasons, Retrieving Rationality?' (1992) 5 JCL 83, explores "good" and "bad" reasons for withdrawing. Andrews, 'Breach of Contract: A Plea for Clarity and Discipline' (2018) 134 LQR 117, is also in the further reading. Should motive matter? The law generally says no: a valid right to terminate can be exercised for any reason. *Eminence* allows motive in only as part of the objective assessment of repudiation.
- **Is the condition/warranty distinction illogical?** The Q&A text's essay question says prior classification prevents remedies from reflecting the actual consequences of breach.
  - Against that: freedom of contract, the needs of commodity markets (*Bunge*, *The Mihalis Angelos*), and statutory moderation (s 15A, whose words "slight" and "unreasonable" themselves create uncertainty).
  - Lord Wilberforce's dissent in *Schuler* defends "aggressive, insistent punctuality".
  - Combining innominate terms with the orthodox categories lets courts tread a middle path.
  - Further reading: Carter (1981) 40 CLJ 219; Treitel, 'Types of Contractual Terms' (2002); Nolan on *Hong Kong Fir* in *Landmark Cases* (2008).
- **Party-created conditions and *Lombard*.** Treitel ([1987] LMCLQ 143) and Bojczuk ([1987] JBL 353) criticise allowing loss-of-bargain damages where termination follows a minor breach made a "condition" by an express term. Opeskin ((1990) 106 LQR 293) supports the decision. McKendrick thinks *Lombard* was correctly decided, harsh though it is, because the law does not distinguish conditions created by the parties from those arising under the general law. Outside the Consumer Credit Act 1974, hirers are left to "the chill winds of the common law". Whittaker, 'Termination Clauses' (2007), is in the further reading.
- **White & Carter.** The decision allows wasteful performance and sidesteps mitigation. Its "legitimate interest" limit has been called "unintelligible and elusive" (Q Liu, 'The White & Carter Principle: A Restatement' (2011) 74 MLR 171). Liu proposes a test that weighs the wastefulness of continued performance against the innocent party's performance interest, with a "further good reason" (non-pecuniary interest, liability to third parties, lack of a real alternative) tipping the balance, and with the gap between benefit and cost needing to be "completely out of proportion". Carter (2012) 128 LQR 490 asks how unreasonable conduct must be. O'Sullivan, 'Repudiation: Keeping the Contract Alive' (2017), is also in the reading.
- **Nature of termination.** Is termination a matter of election, or of discharge? See Carter, 'Discharge as the Basis for Termination for Breach of Contract' (2012) 128 LQR 283; Peel, 'The Termination Paradox' [2013] LMCLQ 519; Morgan, 'Repudiatory Breach: Inability, Election and Discharge' (2017) 76 CLJ 11; Wilmot-Smith, 'Termination after Breach' (2018) 134 LQR 307. *Geys* confirms election even for employment contracts.
- **Anticipatory breach as a concept.** Dawson, 'Metaphors and Anticipatory Breach of Contract' (1981) 40 CLJ 83, and J C Smith, 'Anticipatory Breach of Contract' (in Lomnicka and Morse (eds), *Contemporary Issues in Commercial Law*), question how a promise can be broken before performance is due. *Frost v Knight* answers that the promisee has a present right to have the contract kept open. McKendrick notes that recognising the breach also serves the repudiating party's interest in reducing losses.
- **Strict liability.** Liability for breach is generally strict, justified by voluntary assumption of the obligation. It contrasts with fault-based tort and with some civilian systems. Parties can qualify it by "reasonable endeavours", exclusion or force majeure clauses. Statutory terms (e.g. SGA s 14) impose strict liability to protect buyers.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Has there been a breach?** Identify the term, express or implied, and interpret it. Is there a lawful excuse (a prior breach by the other side, frustration)? Is the obligation strict or only one of reasonable care?
2. **Actual or anticipatory?** If before performance is due, look for a clear renunciation (words or conduct, judged objectively; *Eminence*) or self-disablement.
3. **Is the obligation entire, dependent or independent?**
   - Independent: damages only.
   - Entire: no payment for part performance unless there is substantial performance (*Hoenig*) or an exception applies (e.g. SGA s 30(1) acceptance).
4. **Classify the term broken:**
   - Statute (SGA ss 12–15; but s 15A for slight breaches by non-consumer sellers; the CRA remedy scheme for consumers)?
   - Precedent (*The Mihalis Angelos*)?
   - The parties' label, subject to *Schuler*, or a termination clause, subject to *Rice* and clear words for excluding common law rights?
   - The nature of the term (time clauses in commodity contracts: *Bunge*)?
   - Otherwise innominate: does the breach deprive the innocent party of substantially the whole benefit (*Hong Kong Fir*), weighing the factors above?
5. **Advise on election.**
   - If the right to terminate exists: accept clearly (*Vitol*), or reserve rights while deciding (*Stocznia*).
   - Watch for conduct that amounts to affirmation.
   - Note the risk of wrongful termination if the term may be innominate.
6. **If the innocent party wants to affirm and perform** (e.g. sue for the price): can it perform without the other's co-operation? Does it have a legitimate interest (*White & Carter*; *The Alaskan Trader*; *The Dynamic*)?
7. **Consequences.** Termination is prospective. Accrued rights survive (*Photo Production*). Loss-of-bargain damages are available for breach of condition or repudiation (*Lombard*). Then go to `damages.md` and `specific-remedies.md`.
8. **Traps:**
   - Treating a contract label "condition" as conclusive.
   - Forgetting that termination is not rescission ab initio.
   - Advising termination for a breach of an innominate term without assessing seriousness.
   - Forgetting s 15A and the CRA in sale of goods cases.
   - Assuming *White & Carter* lets any innocent party run up costs.
9. **Facts that change the outcome:**
   - Whether the market has moved (motive and opportunism).
   - How much of the contract period is left and whether the defect can be cured (*Hong Kong Fir*).
   - Whether time is "of the essence".
   - Whether the innocent party kept performing or demanded performance without reserving rights.
   - Whether the innocent party needs the other's co-operation to perform.

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: conceding that a contractor who abandoned an entire contract may be paid for work done. Abandonment bars recovery for part-performance; work left on land is not free acceptance (Sumpter v Hedges [1898] 1 QB 673). Work done only reduces completion-cost damages. - Common error: saying abandoning work is never repudiation on its own. Unexplained abandonment can be, if objectively it shows an intention no longer to be bound (Freeth v Burr (1874) LR 9 CP 208); only a short or explained pause may fall short. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1855/q03#2]
