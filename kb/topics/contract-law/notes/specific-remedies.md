# Specific Remedies - Action for the Agreed Sum, Specific Performance and Injunctions

## Overview
"Specific" remedies try to give the innocent party the performance itself, rather than money in place of it. The discussion covers three of them:
1. **The action for the agreed sum** (a debt claim). This is the most common claim for breach of contract.
2. **Specific performance**, an equitable order to perform a positive obligation.
3. **Injunctions**, which are prohibitory or mandatory.

In English law (England and Wales), specific performance and injunctions are equitable and discretionary. Specific performance is treated as secondary to damages and is barred where damages are adequate. Contracts for the sale of land are the main exception. The leading modern case is *Co-operative Insurance Society Ltd v Argyll Stores (Holdings) Ltd* [1998] AC 1, which explains the "constant supervision" bar. The broader essay theme is whether English law protects the claimant's right to performance or only its money value.

## The law

### 1. Action for the agreed sum
1. **Nature of the claim.**
   - It is a claim for a debt. The claimant need not prove loss, only that it has earned the sum by due performance.
   - Because it is a debt claim, the mitigation rule does not apply. Either the money is owed or it is not (McKendrick ch 22).
   - Damages can be claimed as well, for example for loss caused by late payment.
2. **Entire obligations.** Where payment depends on complete performance, partial performance earns nothing (*Cutter v Powell* (1795); *Sumpter v Hedges* [1898]).
   - **Substantial performance** is an exception. The claimant recovers the price less a cross-claim for damages, as the trial judge applied it in *Ruxley*.
   - The discussion lists *Hoenig v Isaacs* [1952] 2 All ER 176 under this head.
3. ***White & Carter (Councils) Ltd v McGregor*** [1962] AC 413.
   - **Facts:** a garage owner repudiated a three-year contract for advertisements on litter bins on the very day it was renewed. The advertisers refused to accept the repudiation and displayed the advertisements anyway. They then sued for the full price under a clause that made the whole sum due on breach.
   - **Held:** this was a claim in debt, so it succeeded, and mitigation was irrelevant.
4. **Limits from *White & Carter***. An innocent party cannot affirm and claim the price where:
   - **(a) the contract-breaker's co-operation is needed** to carry on performing. Co-operation is read broadly, covering passive as well as active co-operation (*Hounslow LBC v Twickenham Garden Developments* [1971] Ch 233); or
   - **(b) the innocent party has "no legitimate interest, financial or otherwise, in performing the contract rather than claiming damages"** (Lord Reid).
   If affirmation is not possible, the innocent party must sue for damages, and mitigation then applies.
5. **The legitimate interest limit in practice.**
   - In *Hounslow*, Megarry J held that Lord Reid's limit forms part of the ratio, because without Lord Reid there was no majority.
   - The test is whether insisting on performance is "wholly unreasonable" (Concentrate). The burden is on the party in breach.
   - *The Alaskan Trader* (*Clea Shipping Corp v Bulk Oil International* [1984] 1 All ER 129): the charterers said they had no further use for the ship. The owners spent about £800,000 on repairs and kept the ship fully crewed and idle at the charterers' disposal. Held: they had no legitimate interest in claiming hire rather than damages, so the hire was recoverable by the charterers subject to damages.
   - The discussion also lists *Reichman v Beveridge* [2006] EWCA Civ 1659 here.

### 2. Specific performance and the adequacy of damages
1. **Definition and character.**
   - Specific performance is "the remedy available in equity to compel a person actually to perform a contractual obligation" (Chitty, quoted by McKendrick).
   - It is discretionary, not available as of right. But the discretion is structured by "well-established principles" (Lord Hoffmann in *Argyll*).
   - McKendrick notes it is often really substitutionary, because performance ordered after breach is necessarily late.
2. **Secondary to damages.**
   - Specific performance will not be ordered where damages, or an award of the agreed sum, are an adequate remedy.
   - Lord Hoffmann in *Argyll*: specific performance is "traditionally regarded in English law as an exceptional remedy", part of Chancery's jurisdiction where common law remedies were inadequate.
   - In *Makdessi* [2015] UKSC 67, Lord Neuberger said the minimum condition is that the innocent party "should have a legitimate interest extending beyond pecuniary compensation" (Concentrate).
3. **When are damages inadequate?** (McKendrick)
   - The loss is hard to quantify or prove: *Adderley v Dixon* (1824) 1 Sim & St 607.
   - The claimant could recover only nominal damages: *Beswick v Beswick* [1968] AC 58. The discussion notes the case can also be analysed from a different perspective; see the privity note.
   - The subject matter is **unique**. Kronman (1978) argues uniqueness is the "most important common feature" of the cases. Uniqueness may be:
     - **physical:** heirlooms, antiques, art. In *Falcke v Gray* (1859) 4 Drew 651, the court said two rare china jars would qualify, though it refused the order on the facts. *Phillips v Lamdin* [1949] concerned an Adam door. *Gregor Fisken Ltd v Carl* [2020] EWHC 1385 (Comm), approved [2021] EWCA Civ 792, concerned a unique original Ferrari gearbox;
     - **commercial:** in *Sky Petroleum Ltd v VIP Petroleum Ltd* [1974] 1 WLR 576, Goulding J granted interim relief to keep petrol supplies flowing during an unusual market in which the buyer could not get supplies elsewhere. The supplier was "for practical purposes" the plaintiffs' "sole means of keeping their business going". In *Behnke v Bede Shipping* [1927] 1 KB 649, a ship of "practically unique value" to the buyer was ordered to be delivered. The discussion also lists *Société des Industries Metallurgiques v Bronx Engineering* [1975] 1 Lloyd's Rep 465. McKendrick says it is unresolved whether commercial uniqueness is enough in general.
   - Other examples (Concentrate):
     - *AB v University of XYZ* [2020] EWHC 2980 (QB): there was no realistic way to assess damages for a student denied legal representation at a disciplinary hearing;
     - *Thames Valley Power v Total Gas and Power* [2005] EWHC 2208 (Comm): a 15-year gas supply with no equivalent substitute source (the discussion lists it as *Harvela Investment Ltd v Total Gas and Power Ltd* [2006] 1 Lloyd's Rep 441, which appears to be the same report);
     - *Gravelor Shipping v GTLK Asia M5* (2023): where the defendant is "not good for the money".
4. **Statute for goods.**
   - Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 52(1): in an action for breach of a contract to deliver **specific or ascertained** goods, the court "may, if it thinks fit" order specific performance, without giving the defendant the option of keeping the goods and paying damages.
   - Section 52 now applies only to non-consumer sales. Consumers have special remedies under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (ss 19, 23, 42–43, 54–55 and 58 are; their content is not set out in the sources read).
5. **Land contracts.**
   - Specific performance is conventionally ordered for the sale or other disposition of an interest in land. It is treated as a primary remedy for **both** parties, without proof that damages are inadequate.
   - The traditional explanation is that each piece of land is unique (*Sudbrook Trading Estate v Eggleton* [1983] 1 AC 444).
   - The discussion asks some questions:
     - Is every plot unique? Think of identical new houses on an estate.
     - Should the buyer's purpose (resale, investment) matter?
     - Why does the **seller**, who only wants money, also get specific performance? Perhaps as a reciprocal remedy.
   - In Canada, *Semelhago v Paramadevan* [1996] 2 SCR 415 (Sopinka J) rejected uniqueness: property is "mass produced" much like consumer products.
   - *Rainbow Estates Ltd v Tokenhold Ltd* [1999] Ch 64 (Lawrence Collins QC): subject to avoiding injustice or oppression, the remedy should be available when damages are inadequate or, "in the more modern formulation", when specific performance is the appropriate remedy.

### 3. Other bars to specific performance
Each bar is discretionary, and they often work together.
1. **Want of mutuality.**
   - Fry's old rule required mutuality at the time of contracting. Buckley LJ rejected it in *Price v Strange* [1978] Ch 337. The relevant time is now the **judgment**.
   - The court will not compel the defendant if it cannot also ensure that the claimant's unperformed obligations will be performed, unless damages would adequately protect the defendant.
   - *Rainbow Estates*: lack of mutuality is a discretionary bar, not an absolute one.
2. **No consideration, or only nominal consideration.** "Equity will not assist a volunteer" (*Cannon v Hartley* [1949] Ch 213; *Jeffreys v Jeffreys* (1841)).
3. **Impossibility, physical or legal.** *Ferguson v Wilson* (1866); *Warmington v Miller* [1973] QB 877. Specific performance will also not be ordered if the defendant cannot comply (*Watts v Spence* [1976] Ch 165, Concentrate).
4. **Uncertainty.** *Joseph v National Magazine Co* [1959] Ch 14; *Argyll*.
   - The order must be precise enough for the defendant to know what to do. Lord Upjohn in *Morris v Redland Bricks* [1970] AC 652, quoted in *Argyll*: the defendant must know "exactly in fact what he has to do".
   - An obligation can be certain enough to be valid, or to found damages, yet too imprecise to enforce specifically.
5. **Severe hardship to the defendant.** *Wroth v Tyler* [1974] Ch 30.
   - *Patel v Ali* [1984] Ch 283: after the contract, the seller lost a leg to cancer, had more children, and her husband was imprisoned. There had also been more than four years' delay that was not her fault. Goulding J left the buyers to damages.
   - Hardship can be a ground "only in extraordinary and persuasive circumstances". Mere money difficulties are no excuse.
   - McKendrick suggests hardship alone, without the delay, would probably not have been enough.
6. **Claimant's conduct: "clean hands".** *Lamare v Dixon* (1873) LR 6 HL 414; *Chappell v Times Newspapers* [1975] 1 WLR 482.
   - The claimant must not have taken unfair advantage, for example by negotiating with a drunk, or acted dishonestly (Concentrate).
   - In *Quadrant Visual Communications v Hutchison Telephone* [1993] BCLC 442, the claimant's "trickery" in failing to disclose information barred relief.
7. **Delay (laches).** *Milward v Earl of Thanet* (1801); *Glasbrook v Richardson* (1874); *Lazard Bros v Fairfield Properties* (1977).
8. **Avoidance of waste.** *Tito v Waddell (No 2)* [1977] Ch 106; *Argyll*. Specific performance may be refused where the cost of performance is wholly out of proportion to the benefit.
9. **Termination.** Specific performance cannot be granted once the claimant has terminated the contract for breach (*Johnson v Agnew* [1980] AC 367).
10. **Parties cannot fetter the discretion.** A contractual term saying that specific performance is available does not bind the court (*Quadrant Visual*: the court is not a "rubber stamp"). Rowan (2010) 126 LQR 448 argues such terms should carry more weight.

### 4. Contracts for personal services
1. **General rule.** Personal service contracts are not specifically enforced.
   - *Chappell v Times Newspapers*: forcing parties without mutual trust to work together is "a plain recipe for disaster".
   - Fry LJ in *De Francesco v Barnum* (1890) 45 Ch D 430: courts must be "jealous, lest they should turn contracts of service into contracts of slavery".
2. **Statute protecting the employee.** Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992 s 236: no court may, by specific performance or injunction, "compel an employee to do any work or attend at any place for the doing of any work". This covers injunctions as well as specific performance.
3. **Megarry J in *C H Giles & Co Ltd v Morris*** [1972] 1 WLR 307:
   - The reluctance is a "strong reluctance rather than a rule".
   - It is rooted in human nature. A singer ordered to sing might sing flat, and contempt would be a useless weapon.
   - Not every contract with an element of personal service should automatically be refused.
4. **Against the employer.**
   - Statutory unfair dismissal remedies include reinstatement and re-engagement (Employment Rights Act 1996 ss 113–117). They are rarely used, and an employer can ultimately refuse by paying enhanced compensation under s 117 (McKendrick).
   - At common law, injunctions restraining dismissal have been granted where trust and confidence survive: *Hill v C A Parsons & Co Ltd* [1972] Ch 305, where a union had forced the dismissal.
   - *Powell v Brent LBC* [1988] ICR 176 (Ralph Gibson LJ): an employer will not be required to keep an employee unless the order is otherwise just **and** the employer still has sufficient confidence in the employee's ability and other attributes. Confidence is judged on the facts.
   - In *Irani v Southampton HA* [1985] ICR 590 and *Robb v Hammersmith and Fulham LBC* [1991] ICR 514, the injunctions preserved the employee's status and salary while the employee undertook not to turn up for work.
   - *Ashworth v Royal National Theatre* [2014] EWHC 1176 (QB) is.
5. **Signs of change.** In *Geys v Société Générale* [2012] UKSC 63, Lord Wilson asked whether today's "more impersonal, less hierarchical" employment relationships call for review. In *Johnson v Unisys* [2001] UKHL 13 at [35], Lord Hoffmann said employment gives "a livelihood but an occupation, an identity and a sense of self-esteem".

### 5. Constant supervision
1. **The traditional bar.** *Ryan v Mutual Tontine Westminster Chambers Association* [1893] 1 Ch 116: a landlord's covenant to provide a porter "constantly in attendance" was not specifically enforced.
   - Lord Esher MR: a long-continuing contract performed day to day would need constant superintendence.
   - Lopes LJ: the court cannot compel performance that requires it to "watch over and supervise its execution".
2. ***Posner v Scott-Lewis*** [1987] Ch 25 (Mervyn Davies J): specific performance of a covenant to employ a resident porter was ordered. He asked three questions:
   - (a) is what must be done sufficiently defined?
   - (b) would enforcing compliance require an unacceptable degree of superintendence?
   - (c) what hardship or prejudice would each side suffer if the order were made or refused?
   McKendrick asks which side of Lord Hoffmann's activity/result line the case falls on.
3. ***Co-operative Insurance Society v Argyll Stores (Holdings) Ltd*** [1998] AC 1 (HL):
   - **Facts:** Argyll closed a loss-making Safeway supermarket, the anchor store in a Sheffield shopping centre. That broke a lease covenant to keep the premises open for retail trade during usual business hours. The lease had about 19 years left. The Court of Appeal ordered Argyll to trade. The House of Lords restored the trial judge's refusal, leaving the landlord to damages.
   - **What supervision really means:** not officers literally watching, but the risk of an **indefinite series of rulings** on whether the order is being obeyed, each enforced by the heavy-handed, quasi-criminal sanction of contempt.
   - Running a business under "a sword of Damocles" is "no way to run a business", and repeated contempt applications are expensive for the parties and the courts.
   - **Activity v result:**
     - Orders to **carry on an activity** (running a business) attract these objections most.
     - Orders to **achieve a result** (building works, repairs) can be checked once at the end, which is why building and repairing covenants have been enforced (*Wolverhampton Corp v Emmons* [1901]; *Jeune v Queens Cross Properties* [1974]).
   - **Imprecision:** "keep open for retail trade" said nothing about the level, area or kind of trade.
   - **Oppression and injustice:** forcing someone to trade at a loss could let the claimant extract far more than the value of performance. Lord Hoffmann quoted Sharpe on the claimant's bargaining position.
   - **Waste and continuing hostility:** an order "yokes the parties together in a continuing hostile relationship", whereas damages end the litigation.
   - None of these reasons alone is decisive, but together they justify the settled practice. Lord Hoffmann warned that the Court of Appeal had misread Lord Wilberforce's remarks in *Shiloh Spinners v Harding* [1973] AC 691, and that Megarry J in *Tito v Waddell (No 2)* had read them the same way.
4. **After *Argyll*.** Courts have enforced well-defined building and repair obligations (*Rainbow Estates*). In *Thames Valley Power*, the difficulty of supervising a gas supply contract with five years left to run was seemingly passed over (Concentrate).

### 6. Injunctions
1. **Nature.** Injunctions are equitable and discretionary, like specific performance. They are either:
   - **prohibitory:** restraining a threatened or future breach of a negative undertaking; or
   - **mandatory:** ordering the defendant to undo a breach and restore the position.
2. **Prohibitory injunctions are normally granted.** For a clear breach of a negative covenant, the adequacy of damages is generally irrelevant.
   - *Araci v Fallon* [2011] EWCA Civ 668: on the day of the Derby, a jockey was restrained from riding a rival horse, in breach of his retainer. An injunction is granted as a matter of course unless it is oppressive or causes particular hardship. The "balance of convenience" test does not apply to a clear, uncontested breach of a negative covenant. Elias LJ said adequacy of damages is not generally relevant.
3. **Mandatory injunctions are granted less readily.** Damages are often awarded in lieu, especially where undoing the breach would be costly or wasteful.
4. **No indirect specific performance of personal services.** A prohibitory injunction will not be granted if it would amount to specific performance of a personal service contract. The key cases:
   - ***Lumley v Wagner*** (1852) 1 De GM & G 604, listed as a key case. A negative stipulation in a personal service contract was enforced by injunction.
   - ***Warner Bros Pictures Inc v Nelson*** [1937] 1 KB 209. An actress had promised not to act for others and not to "engage in any other occupation". The first promise was enforced by injunction. The second was not, because it would have forced her to work for Warner or starve (Concentrate).
   - ***Page One Records v Britton*** [1968] 1 WLR 157. An injunction was refused where, in practice, it would have compelled performance. The defendant must have a reasonable alternative way to earn a living.
   - ***Warren v Mendy*** [1989] 1 WLR 853, and ***LauritzenCool AB v Lady Navigation*** [2005] EWCA Civ 579, are. The Concentrate guide cites *Warren v Mendy* for the rule that personal service contracts will not usually be specifically enforced.
   - Other routes (Concentrate):
     - enforcing a valid restraint clause, if it is not an unreasonable restraint of trade (*Fitch v Dewes* [1921]; *Littlewoods v Harris* [1977]);
     - implying a negative stipulation in contracts that are not for personal service (*Associated Portland Cement v Teigland Shipping* [1975]);
     - severing an over-wide stipulation and enforcing part of it (*Warner v Nelson*).
5. **Damages in lieu, and combined claims.** Senior Courts Act 1981 s 50 lets the court award damages in addition to, or in substitution for, specific performance or an injunction (formerly Lord Cairns' Act). Section 49 allows combined claims, as in *Experience Hendrix v PPX* [2003] EWCA Civ 323. On negotiating damages in lieu, see the penalties note.

- *Hounslow LBC v Twickenham Garden Developments* [1971] Ch 233: the co-operation limit; Lord Reid's limits are part of the ratio.
- *The Alaskan Trader* [1984] 1 All ER 129: no legitimate interest where affirming is wholly unreasonable.
- *Falcke v Gray* (1859) 4 Drew 651; *Behnke v Bede Shipping* [1927] 1 KB 649; *Sky Petroleum v VIP Petroleum* [1974] 1 WLR 576: uniqueness, physical and commercial.
- *Beswick v Beswick* [1968] AC 58: specific performance where damages would be nominal.
- Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 52: specific performance for specific or ascertained goods, non-consumer sales only.
- *Price v Strange* [1978] Ch 337: mutuality is judged at the date of judgment.
- *Patel v Ali* [1984] Ch 283: hardship together with delay.
- TULRCA 1992 s 236: no compulsion of employees.
- *C H Giles v Morris* [1972] 1 WLR 307; *De Francesco v Barnum* (1890); *Chappell v Times Newspapers* [1975]: personal services.
- *Hill v C A Parsons* [1972] Ch 305; *Powell v Brent LBC* [1988] ICR 176: injunctions against employers.
- *Ryan v Mutual Tontine* [1893] 1 Ch 116; *Posner v Scott-Lewis* [1987] Ch 25: supervision.
- *Co-operative Insurance v Argyll* [1998] AC 1: the leading case; activity v result; contempt; oppression.
- *Rainbow Estates v Tokenhold* [1999] Ch 64: a more flexible "appropriate remedy" approach.
- *Lumley v Wagner* (1852); *Warner Bros v Nelson* [1937] 1 KB 209; *Page One Records v Britton* [1968] 1 WLR 157: negative stipulations.
- *Araci v Fallon* [2011] EWCA Civ 668: prohibitory injunctions as a matter of course.
- Senior Courts Act 1981 ss 49–50.

## Debate and critique
- **Should specific performance be routine?**
  - *For:* Schwartz (1979) 89 Yale LJ 271. Damages under-compensate more often than is supposed. A request for specific performance is itself evidence that damages are inadequate. Promisees should choose the remedy.
  - *Against:*
    - Kronman (1978): the present rules match what parties would choose and so cut negotiating costs, because only for unique goods would parties want specific performance. Schwartz criticises this.
    - Burrows: mitigation does not apply to specific performance, and compelled performance intrudes more on liberty.
    - Smith (1997) 60 MLR 360: forced performance is "self-defeating", because the bonds created by a voluntary undertaking can only be realised by voluntary performance.
  - McKendrick's answers: the promise was given voluntarily; claimants rarely want performance from a hostile party; and parties' remedial preferences depend on context.
- **Adequacy is relative.** Chen-Wishart ("Specific Performance and Change of Mind", 2017) notes damages are often treated as "adequate" even when they do not put the victim where performance would have. Think of the limits of cost of cure, remoteness and mitigation. So whether damages are "adequate" depends on how generous the damages rules are. Compare the performance-interest debate (Friedmann, Coote) in the damages notes.
- **Common law v civil law.** Lord Hoffmann in *Argyll* questioned the supposed contrast with civilian systems, where performance is the primary remedy. In those systems coercion often works through financial penalties rather than contempt.
- **Land.** Is uniqueness still a convincing rationale for treating land as automatically entitled to specific performance (*Semelhago*)?
- ***White & Carter*.** The decision is attacked as unfair, saddling the defendant with performance it did not want, and as economically wasteful. Courts have confined it through the co-operation and legitimate interest limits (Liu (2011) 74 MLR 171, cited by McKendrick).
- **Efficient breach and moral obligation.** Further reading (Friedmann on good faith and remedies, Smith, Katz, Nadler) frames the question as whether contract creates a duty to perform or a choice between performing and paying. The sources only list these works; this note does not summarise them.
- **Employment.** Is the "slavery" rationale outdated given modern, impersonal employment (*Geys*; *Johnson v Unisys*)?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **What does the claimant want?** The price, performance, or to stop something?
2. **Claim for the price:**
   - Has the sum been earned by due performance? Is the obligation entire, and has there been substantial performance?
   - If the defendant repudiated before performance: can the claimant affirm and perform without the defendant's co-operation? Does it have a legitimate interest in doing so, or would that be wholly unreasonable (*Alaskan Trader*)?
   - If affirmation is not open, the claim is for damages and mitigation applies.
3. **Specific performance:**
   - Are damages adequate? Is there a substitute on the market? Is the subject matter physically or commercially unique (land, rare goods, sole supplier)? Would damages be nominal (*Beswick*) or impossible to assess?
   - For goods: are they specific or ascertained (s 52)? Is the buyer a consumer (Consumer Rights Act 2015)?
   - Go through the bars:
     - personal service (TULRCA s 236);
     - supervision: is the order to carry on an activity or to achieve a result (*Argyll*)?
     - is the obligation precise enough?
     - mutuality at the date of judgment;
     - volunteer;
     - impossibility;
     - hardship and delay (*Patel v Ali*);
     - clean hands;
     - laches;
     - waste or disproportionate cost;
     - has the contract already been terminated?
   - Remember the discretion. Even where specific performance is refused, damages in lieu are available (s 50).
4. **Injunction:**
   - Is there an express or implied negative covenant?
   - Prohibitory: generally granted unless oppressive (*Araci*).
   - In a personal service setting: would the injunction indirectly compel work? Does the defendant have another way to earn a living (*Warner* against *Page One*)? Can an over-wide covenant be severed? Check restraint of trade.
   - Mandatory: consider cost and waste; damages in lieu are likely.
5. **Traps:**
   - treating specific performance as available as of right;
   - forgetting that land is the exception;
   - assuming "constant supervision" is an absolute bar after *Argyll*;
   - forgetting that mitigation does not apply to a debt claim, but does apply once the claim becomes one for damages.
