# Damages for Breach of Contract - The Compensatory Measure

## Overview
Damages are the default remedy for breach of contract in English law (England and Wales). Specific performance and injunctions are equitable and discretionary; damages are claimed as of right. This note covers the first part of Lectures 7 and 8: what contract damages are for, how the lost "expectation" or "performance" interest is measured (difference in value against cost of cure), when damages are assessed, recovery for non-pecuniary loss such as distress and loss of amenity, and the claimant's option to claim wasted expenditure ("reliance damages"). The limits on recovery (remoteness, mitigation, contributory negligence) are in `damages-limits-remoteness-mitigation-contributory-negligence.md`. Negotiating damages, account of profits and agreed damages clauses are in `damages-negotiating-damages-account-of-profits-and-penalties.md`.

Damages matter because nearly every contract problem question ends with "what can the claimant recover?". The Concentrate guide suggests a standard order: breach; any valid agreed damages clause; the measure; causation; remoteness; non-pecuniary loss; mitigation.

## The law

### 1. Damages are compensatory, not punitive
1. The traditional view in the materials is that contract damages are (usually) **compensatory**. They aim to make good the claimant's loss caused by the breach, not to punish the contract-breaker. Punitive damages are not available for breach of contract: *Addis v Gramophone Co Ltd* [1909] AC 488.
2. If there is a breach but no loss, the claimant still gets damages, but only **nominal** damages.
3. In *Morris-Garner v One Step (Support) Ltd* [2018] UKSC 20, Lord Reed's 12-point summary (para 95, as reported in McKendrick) restated these points:
   - damages are awarded as of right, not as a matter of discretion;
   - they are meant to compensate for loss;
   - where the claimant's interest is purely financial and no loss is proved, only nominal damages are available;
   - courts will be "tolerant of imprecision where the loss is incapable of precise measurement".
4. McKendrick notes a "pressure" on the *Addis* bar from *Kuddus v Chief Constable of Leicestershire Constabulary* [2001] UKHL 29 (a tort case on the categories in *Rookes v Barnard*). He leaves open whether contract law will ever admit punitive damages, citing Cunnington (2006) 26 LS 369.

### 2. The expectation (performance) interest
1. The classic statement is Parke B in *Robinson v Harman* (1848) 1 Exch 850, at 855: the claimant is "so far as money can do it, to be placed in the same situation, with respect to damages, as if the contract had been performed".
2. McKendrick prefers Friedmann's label "performance interest" to Fuller and Perdue's "expectation interest". He argues that the courts' commitment to the performance interest is real but "less than whole-hearted".
3. **Minimum legal obligation.** Where the defendant could have performed in several ways, damages are assessed on the assumption that it would have chosen the way least onerous to itself and least beneficial to the claimant (lecture).
4. **Market measure in sale of goods** (Concentrate):
   - non-delivery: the extra cost of buying a substitute at market price on the date of non-delivery (Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 51(3));
   - non-acceptance: the contract price minus the market price (s 50(3));
   - if the market has not moved against the claimant, only nominal damages are recoverable unless some other loss, such as lost profit on the sale, is shown.

### 3. Date of assessment
1. The general rule is that damages are assessed **at the date of breach** (*Johnson v Agnew* [1980] AC 367, cited by McKendrick). The rule assumes the claimant can go into the market at that date and buy substitute performance.
2. The rule is not absolute. Lord Wilberforce in *Johnson v Agnew* said the court may fix another date if following the rule would cause injustice. Later dates have been used:
   - where the claimant could not reasonably have discovered the breach until later;
   - where there was no available market (*Hooper v Oates* [2013] EWCA Civ 91);
   - where the claimant could not afford a substitute in a rising market (*Wroth v Tyler* [1974] Ch 30).
3. *Golden Strait Corp v Nippon Yusen Kubishika Kaisha (The Golden Victory)* [2007] UKHL 12, [2007] 2 AC 353, majority of 3:2:
   - **Facts:** charterers repudiated a seven-year charter in December 2001. The charter contained a war cancellation clause (cl 33). The Second Gulf War broke out in March 2003, an event that would have entitled the charterers to cancel.
   - **Held:** the owners could not recover for the period after March 2003. The compensatory principle requires the claimant to get the value of the contractual benefit it actually lost, and the court should not shut its eyes to known later events. Lord Scott said certainty is a "desideratum" but not a principle.
   - **Dissent:** Lords Bingham and Walker said the decision undermines certainty and finality and gives contract-breakers an incentive to delay settlement.
4. *Bunge SA v Nidera BV* [2015] UKSC 43 confirmed the general application of the same compensatory principle (McKendrick; Concentrate).

### 4. Difference in value versus cost of cure
Two methods can protect the performance interest:
- **Difference (diminution) in value:** the gap between the value of what was promised and the value of what was received. This protects the expected increase in the claimant's wealth.
- **Cost of cure:** the cost of getting the promised performance from someone else. This protects the performance itself.

The two usually come to much the same figure. When they diverge sharply:
1. Cost of cure will **not** be awarded if it is unreasonable, or if the claimant does not intend to carry out the cure (lecture).
2. *Radford v De Froberville* [1977] 1 WLR 1262 (Oliver J): a person who contracts for something that serves their interests, "be they commercial, aesthetic or merely eccentric", may in principle recover the cost of obtaining it elsewhere. This is subject to the proviso that the claimant is seeking compensation for a genuine loss, not using a technical breach to secure an "uncovenanted profit".
3. *Ruxley Electronics and Construction Ltd v Forsyth* [1996] AC 344 (HL) is the leading case:
   - **Facts:** a pool was built 6 ft deep instead of the agreed 7 ft 6 in. The difference in value was nil, and the only cure was a rebuild at £21,560. The trial judge found that the shortfall did not impair use and awarded £2,500 for "loss of amenity". The Court of Appeal awarded the cost of cure; the House of Lords restored the trial judge's award.
   - **Lord Jauncey:** cost of cure was out of all proportion to the benefit. Reasonableness goes to what the loss actually is. Giving the owner the cost of a new pool while he keeps a serviceable one would be a "gratuitous benefit". He gave Lord Bridge's example of yellow bricks laid where blue were specified.
   - **Intention:** intention to reinstate is a factor, not a passport. The owner's undertaking to use the money to rebuild did not make cost of cure reasonable.
4. McKendrick's reading of *Ruxley*:
   - Unreasonableness came from two factors together: the high cost of the work, and the lack of benefit to the owner. If the owner had wanted the depth for diving, the result would probably have been different.
   - The £2,500 award was not actually challenged, so the case is no authority on the size of loss-of-amenity awards.
   - Consumer groups criticise it as a "licence" for builders to deliver something different and pay a trifling sum.
   - The courts may nonetheless be slow to call cost of cure unreasonable, because the contract-breaker should not put "unreasonable obstacles" in the victim's way.
5. **Two readings of the loss-of-amenity award** (lecture; McKendrick):
   - (a) Lord Mustill's "consumer surplus": the law must cater for cases where the value of the promise to the promisee exceeds the financial gain from full performance. This is a broader recognition of the performance interest, endorsed in similar terms by Lord Reed in *Morris-Garner* at [39]–[40].
   - (b) Lord Lloyd's narrower view: an instance of "mental distress" damages under the pleasure-contract exception. the materials suggests this exception is the preferable explanation.
   - In *Farley v Skinner*, their Lordships gave three different explanations of *Ruxley*. Lord Scott's analysis was the most radical (McKendrick).

### 5. Loss of a chance
- *Chaplin v Hicks* [1911] 2 KB 786 (CA):
  - **Facts:** the claimant was one of 50 finalists, selected from 6,000 entrants, competing for 12 prizes. In breach of contract she was denied a fair chance of an interview.
  - **Held:** the £100 awarded by the jury was upheld. Fletcher Moulton LJ said that being in a limited class of competitors is "something of value", and the jury must estimate it even though the chance was only about one in four.
- The case shows the courts will try to overcome valuation difficulties rather than leave the claimant with nominal damages.

### 6. Non-pecuniary loss (distress, disappointment, inconvenience)
1. **General rule: no damages for "mental distress"** (*Addis*). Bingham LJ in *Watts v Morrow* [1991] 1 WLR 1421, at 1445: a contract-breaker is not in general liable for "distress, frustration, anxiety, displeasure, vexation, tension or aggravation".
2. **Exception 1: pleasure or peace of mind as an object of the contract.** Damages are available where the object of the contract is to provide pleasure, relaxation, peace of mind or freedom from molestation.
   - The original test (*Watts*) required this to be the "very object" of the contract.
   - *Farley v Skinner* [2001] UKHL 49, [2002] 2 AC 732 relaxed it: it is enough that this is an **important** object, not the predominant one.
   - Examples:
     - *Jarvis v Swan's Tours Ltd* [1973] QB 233: a disastrous holiday;
     - *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* [1975] 1 WLR 1468;
     - *Heywood v Wellers* [1976] QB 446: solicitors negligently failed to stop molestation;
     - *Hamilton Jones v David & Snape* [2003] EWHC 3147 (Ch): Neuberger J awarded £20,000 where the solicitors' negligence let the husband take the children abroad, and peace of mind was an important object of the retainer.
3. **Exception 2: physical inconvenience and discomfort.** Damages are available for physical inconvenience and discomfort caused by the breach, and for distress directly related to it (*Watts v Morrow*; *Perry v Sidney Phillips & Son* (1982), cited in Concentrate). *Farley* read "physical inconvenience" widely enough to cover aircraft noise.
4. *Farley v Skinner*:
   - **Facts:** a buyer specifically asked his surveyor whether a house near Gatwick would suffer from aircraft noise. The surveyor said it was unlikely. It was a problem, but the house was worth no less.
   - **Held:** the trial judge's award of £10,000 was restored. Peace of mind on this point had been made an important object of the contract.
   - The case left a distinction between an "ordinary surveyor's contract" (no recovery, as in *Watts*) and one where the client specifically asks about a matter of amenity (McKendrick).
   - One speech in the extract describes the discomfort as "real" but "not very great" and treats £10,000 as on the high side. McKendrick warns that *Farley* is not a "green light" for higher awards.
5. **Commercial contracts:** distress damages are normally unavailable where the object of the contract is profit-making activity (*Hayes v James & Charles Dodd* [1990] 2 All ER 815; *Bliss v SE Thames RHA* [1987] ICR 700, both cited in Concentrate). Obiter remarks in *Farley* suggest the business/non-business label should not be decisive.
6. **Loss of reputation:**
   - Damages for loss of reputation as such cannot be recovered in a contract claim (*Addis*).
   - Financial loss caused by loss of reputation may be recoverable: *Malik (Mahmud) v BCCI SA* [1998] AC 20 (lecture). The Concentrate guide adds *Anglo-Continental Holidays v Typaldos Lines* [1967] 2 Lloyd's Rep 61 for injury to commercial reputation.
7. Physical injury caused by breach is recoverable if not too remote (*Grant v Australian Knitting Mills* [1936] AC 85, cited in McKendrick).

### 7. Reliance damages (wasted expenditure)
1. A claimant may claim the expenditure wasted because of the breach instead of lost profits. This is typical where profits are too speculative to prove.
2. **Status of reliance damages.** In *Omak Maritime Ltd v Mamola Challenger Shipping Co (The Mamola Challenger)* [2010] EWHC 2026 (Comm), Teare J held that reliance losses are a "species of expectation losses", not a fundamentally different head of claim.
3. *Anglia TV v Reed* [1972] 1 QB 60 (CA):
   - **Facts:** the lead actor repudiated, and the film was abandoned.
   - **Held:** Anglia could not prove its lost profit, so it claimed its wasted expenditure. It recovered all of it, **including expenditure incurred before the contract was made**, because Reed must have contemplated that such expenditure would be wasted if he broke the contract.
   - **Criticism:** Ogus (1972) 35 MLR 423 called pre-contract recovery a "doubtful proposition", because the expenses were not incurred in reliance on the promise. He suggested a cut-off at the point of "substantial agreement".
4. **The bad-bargain limit.** A claimant cannot use a reliance claim to escape a losing bargain.
   - *C & P Haulage v Middleton* [1983] 1 WLR 1461 (CA): a licensee was ejected ten weeks early and claimed the cost of improvements. He failed, because the improvements would have been lost anyway when the six-month licence ended. His loss flowed from his own bad bargain, not the breach.
   - The burden is on the contract-breaker to prove that the claimant would not have recouped its expenditure (*CCC Films (London) Ltd v Impact Quadrant Films Ltd* [1985] QB 16; McKendrick cites Leggatt J's "principle of reasonable assumptions" in *Yam Seng* [2013] EWHC 111 (QB)). That burden is not easy to discharge.
   - In *The Mamola Challenger* the owners re-let the ship at a higher market rate and so more than made good their loss. Awarding them wasted expenditure would have left them better off than performance, so it was refused.

## Key authorities
- *Robinson v Harman* (1848) 1 Exch 850: the classic statement of the expectation measure.
- *Addis v Gramophone Co Ltd* [1909] AC 488: no punitive damages, no damages for injured feelings, no damages for loss of reputation as such.
- *Golden Strait v NYK (The Golden Victory)* [2007] UKHL 12: the breach-date rule is flexible; later events that would have ended the contract cut damages (3:2).
- *Bunge SA v Nidera BV* [2015] UKSC 43: confirms the *Golden Victory* compensatory principle.
- *Radford v De Froberville* [1977] 1 WLR 1262: cost of cure where the claimant wants performance for non-commercial reasons; the "uncovenanted profit" proviso.
- *Ruxley Electronics v Forsyth* [1996] AC 344: cost of cure refused as unreasonable and disproportionate; £2,500 loss of amenity awarded instead.
- *Chaplin v Hicks* [1911] 2 KB 786: damages for loss of a chance.
- *Watts v Morrow* [1991] 1 WLR 1421: the general bar on distress damages and the two exceptions.
- *Jarvis v Swan's Tours* [1973] QB 233: distress damages for a ruined holiday.
- *Farley v Skinner* [2001] UKHL 49: an "important object" is enough; physical inconvenience read widely; £10,000.
- *Hamilton Jones v David & Snape* [2003] EWHC 3147 (Ch): £20,000 distress damages against solicitors.
- *Malik (Mahmud) v BCCI* [1998] AC 20: financial loss from damaged reputation can be recovered.
- *Anglia TV v Reed* [1972] 1 QB 60: wasted expenditure recoverable, including pre-contract spending.
- *C & P Haulage v Middleton* [1983] 1 WLR 1461: no recovery of reliance loss to escape a bad bargain.
- *CCC Films v Impact Quadrant Films* [1985] QB 16: burden on the defendant to show the bargain was a losing one. the materials gives the citation as [1995] QB 16; McKendrick gives [1985] QB 16.
- *The Mamola Challenger* [2010] EWHC 2026 (Comm): reliance loss is a species of expectation loss.
- Listed in the materials without further detail in the sources: *Johnson v Gore Wood* [2001] 1 AC 1 and *Johnson v Unisys Ltd* [2001] UKHL 13 (non-pecuniary loss). McKendrick's specific performance chapter cites *Johnson v Unisys* only for Lord Hoffmann's remark on how much employment matters to a person.

## Debate and critique
- **Fuller and Perdue v Friedmann.** Fuller and Perdue's 1936–37 Yale articles, called by McKendrick probably the most cited contract article ever written, identified three interests: expectation, reliance and restitution. They argued the reliance interest has a strong claim to protection. Friedmann ("The Performance Interest in Contract Damages" (1995) 111 LQR 628) argues their terminology degraded the right to performance: a promisee contracts to get performance, not to get back its outlay. McKendrick finds Friedmann "convincing".
- **Does English law protect performance?** Friedmann reads *Ruxley* as resting on *pacta sunt servanda*. The opposing view is that *Ruxley* did not protect the performance interest, since Mr Forsyth never got his deep pool. Specific performance would not have been ordered either, which suggests English law protects the financial equivalent of performance rather than performance itself (Coote, "Contract Damages, Ruxley and the Performance Interest" [1997] CLJ 537).
- **Consumer surplus.** Harris, Ogus and Phillips (1979) 95 LQR 581 and Lord Mustill in *Ruxley* recognise a non-monetary value placed on performance. Mullen (2016) 36 OJLS 83 addresses how to quantify it. Rowan [2017] CLJ 616 addresses the relevance of the promisee's intention to cure. (These are on the materials reading list; the sources do not summarise Mullen's or Rowan's arguments.)
- **Distress damages.** Capper (2002) 118 LQR 193 considers *Farley* and its relationship with *Ruxley*. Chandler and Devenney [2007] LS 126 argue that inconvenience and disappointment should be compensated as part of lost expectation. McKendrick, "Breach of Contract and the Meaning of Loss" [1999] CLP 53, argues for damages for non-financial losses. The Concentrate author notes the remaining limits are better explained by policy than by remoteness.
- **Certainty or accuracy in assessment.** *The Golden Victory* pits the compensatory principle against commercial certainty and finality. Lord Mustill's article (2008) 124 LQR 569 doubts the courts' ability to stop contract-breakers delaying. Dyson and Kramer (2014) 130 LQR 259 argue there is no true "breach date rule".
- **Pre-contract expenditure.** Ogus's critique of *Anglia TV* is that such spending is incurred at the claimant's own risk while negotiations are still going on.
- **Essay angle.** "Damages fully compensate the victim of breach": discuss. Use nominal damages, cost-of-cure limits, the distress bar, and the limits in the companion note to show under-compensation. Use *Blake* and negotiating damages to show the exceptions.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Breach and causation.** Identify the term broken and the loss caused. Note concurrent causes (see the companion note on limits).
2. **Agreed damages clause?** If there is a valid liquidated damages clause it fixes the sum, so deal with that first (see the penalties note).
3. **Choose the measure.**
   - Expectation: lost profit, or the market difference in a sale.
   - Defective performance: difference in value or cost of cure. Ask whether cure is reasonable and proportionate, and whether the claimant intends to cure (*Ruxley*, *Radford*).
   - If cost of cure is disproportionate and the difference in value is nil, consider loss of amenity or consumer surplus.
   - If profits are speculative, consider reliance damages, including pre-contract spending (*Anglia TV*). Then check whether the defendant can prove a bad bargain (*C & P Haulage*, *CCC Films*).
   - Lost opportunity: *Chaplin v Hicks*.
4. **Minimum legal obligation.** If the defendant had a choice of performance methods, assume the cheapest.
5. **Date of assessment.** Use the breach date, but check whether later events (a termination right, war, a market change) would have cut performance short (*Golden Victory*, *Bunge*).
6. **Non-pecuniary heads.** Was pleasure, relaxation or peace of mind an important object? Holidays, weddings, solicitors protecting a client from harm, and a specific request to a surveyor all qualify. Was there physical inconvenience? Commercial parties will rarely recover. Reputation: only consequential financial loss.
7. **Then apply the limits:** remoteness, mitigation, contributory negligence.
8. **Common traps:**
   - treating *Ruxley* as authority on the amount of loss-of-amenity awards;
   - forgetting nominal damages when there is no loss;
   - allowing a reliance claim that would beat the expectation position (*Mamola Challenger*);
   - assuming a company can get distress damages.
