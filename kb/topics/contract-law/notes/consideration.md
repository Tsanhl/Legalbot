# Consideration

## Overview
In English law (England & Wales) a promise that is not made by deed is only enforceable as a contract if the promisee has given something in return for it: consideration. The doctrine separates bargains (enforceable) from bare gratuitous promises (unenforceable unless in a deed). Consideration is not a step-by-step process like offer and acceptance; it is a set of rules and principles which can apply singly or together, and which critics say are not fully coherent. In a problem question the task is to decide whether what was given or promised counts as *good* consideration, paying special attention to past consideration, performance of existing duties, and part payment of debts. The doctrine is also the starting point for promissory estoppel (see `promissory-estoppel.md`) and privity (see `privity-and-third-party-rights.md`).

## The law

### 1. What consideration is
1. **Benefit or detriment.** The classic definition is Lush J's in *Currie v Misa* (1875) LR 10 Exch 153, 162: consideration may be some right, interest, profit or benefit to one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other. Either benefit to the promisor or detriment to the promisee will do; the law does not insist on both, though usually they are the same thing seen from two sides (Treitel, as quoted by McKendrick). In a sale, the buyer's payment is a detriment to the buyer and a benefit to the seller, and vice versa for delivery.
2. **The price of the promise (bargain).** Pollock's definition, approved by the House of Lords in *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co v Selfridge* [1915] AC 847, 855, treats consideration as the act or forbearance (or promise of it) that is "the price for which the promise of the other is bought".
3. **Given in return for the promise / requested.** The act relied on must be done at the express or implied request of the promisor; it is not enough that it was a foreseeable result of the promise (McKendrick, "establishing the necessary link"; *Combe v Combe* [1951] 2 KB 215).
4. **Consideration v condition.** A promisor may attach a condition (e.g. "come and collect it") that is not the price of the promise. Whether something is consideration or a mere condition can be hard to tell: *Chappell & Co v Nestlé* [1960] AC 87.
5. **Executory and executed consideration.** Executory consideration is a promise exchanged for a promise (both to be performed in future); executed consideration is an act actually performed in exchange for a promise (typical of unilateral contracts).
6. **Necessity.** Consideration is needed to enforce all oral and written agreements not under seal (deed). It is what gives an agreement legal enforceability.

### 2. The three core rules
The rules can be grouped as follows (McKendrick):
1. Consideration must be **sufficient** but need not be **adequate**.
2. Consideration must not be **past**.
3. Consideration must **move from the promisee**.

### 3. Sufficiency, not adequacy
- The courts do not weigh the value exchanged. A party may stipulate for whatever consideration it chooses: *Chappell v Nestlé* (chocolate wrappers were part of the consideration even though Nestlé threw them away). Lord Somervell's dictum: a peppercorn remains good consideration even if the promisee dislikes pepper and throws the corn away.
- Value is judged largely from the promisor's own point of view: *Bainbridge v Firmstone* (1838) 8 A & E 743 (permission to weigh boilers was consideration; the court would not inquire into what benefit the defendant expected); *Haigh v Brooks* (1839) 10 A & E 309 (a guarantee later found to be worthless was still consideration; the promisor was the only judge of its value to him).
- Fairness is not policed through consideration but through other doctrines such as duress and undue influence (McKendrick).
- **But the consideration must be real**:
  - Refraining from a course of action one never intended to pursue is not consideration: *Arrale v Costain Civil Engineering Ltd* [1976] 1 Lloyd's Rep 98, 106.
  - A motive is not consideration: *Thomas v Thomas* (1842) 2 QB 851 (the testator's wish that his widow live in the house was the motive; her promise to pay £1 and keep the house in repair was the consideration).
  - Types of "unreal" consideration: impossibility (*Hall v Cazenove* (1804) 4 East 477, charterparty stipulation tied to a date already past was nugatory); a genuine compromise of a claim honestly made is good consideration even if the claim might not have succeeded (*Cook v Wright* (1861) 1 B & S 5: the reality of the claim and the bona fides of the compromise are what matter); uncertainty as to what is promised (*White v Bluett* (1853) 23 LJ Ex 36: son's promise to stop complaining about his father's will was not consideration; the Concentrate guide explains he had no right to complain anyway, so it was not something capable of economic expression).

### 4. Past consideration
1. **Rule.** An act done before the promise is given is not given in exchange for it and cannot support it: *Roscorla v Thomas* (1842) 3 QB 234 (warranty given after the sale of a horse unenforceable); *Eastwood v Kenyon* (1840) 11 A & E 438 (husband's later promise to pay a debt incurred to bring up his wife was a gratuitous promise; Lord Denman CJ refused to enforce promises merely on the basis of moral obligation).
2. **Courts look at the real sequence of events, not the wording**: *Re McArdle* [1951] Ch 669 (document said "in consideration of your carrying out" work, but the work had already been done).
3. **Exception: previous request ("implied promise to pay").** Rooted in *Lampleigh v Brathwait* (1615) Hob 105 (a courtesy done at the promisor's request can support a later promise, because the promise "couples itself" with the earlier request) and *Re Casey's Patents* [1892] 1 Ch 104 (Bowen LJ: a past service raises an implication that it was to be paid for, and the later promise fixes the amount). Restated by Lord Scarman in *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614, 629 (PC) (the discussion spells it "Pau On"). The three conditions:
   - (a) the act was done at the promisor's request;
   - (b) both parties understood that the act was to be paid for by payment or some other benefit; and
   - (c) the payment or benefit would have been legally enforceable had it been promised in advance.
4. The crucial factual question is whether the context supports an implied promise to pay: likely for professional/commercial services, unlikely in a domestic or social setting (Concentrate). Distinguish a unilateral contract: past consideration is *act then promise*; a unilateral contract is *promise then requested act*.

### 5. Consideration must move from the promisee
1. Only the person who provided consideration can enforce the promise: *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861) 1 B & S 393 (son could not enforce his father-in-law's promise made to his father because he had given no consideration).
2. The consideration need not move *to* the promisor; it is enough that the promisee confers a benefit on a third party at the promisor's request (*Bolton v Madden* (1873) LR 9 QB 55, cited by McKendrick).
3. Now read with the **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999**: a third party with a right under the Act need not provide consideration (see `privity-and-third-party-rights.md`).

### 6. Performance of an existing duty
**(a) Duty imposed by law (public duty).**
- Performing, or promising to perform, a duty one already owes by law is not consideration: *Collins v Godefroy* (1831) (subpoenaed witness could not enforce a promise of payment for attending).
- Narrow exception: doing more than the legal duty requires is consideration: *Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC* [1925] AC 270 (extra police cover during a strike).
- Possible wide exception: *Ward v Byham* [1956] 1 WLR 496, where Lord Denning said performing an existing duty should be good consideration because it benefits the person to whom it is given (the majority instead found the mother had done more by promising the child would be well looked after and happy; Concentrate). Lord Denning took a similar view in *Williams v Williams* (1957), unless enforcement would be against public policy.

**(b) Contractual duty owed to a third party.**
- Performing or promising to perform a contractual duty already owed to a third party *is* good consideration: *Scotson v Pegg* (1861) 6 H & N 295 (spells it "Scotston"); *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* (the promise to the company not to sell shares supported the defendants' indemnity); *New Zealand Shipping Co v AM Satterthwaite (The Eurymedon)* [1975] AC 154 (PC) (stevedores' unloading, already owed to the carrier, was consideration for the shipper's promise of exemption).

**(c) Contractual duty owed to the promisor ("promise to pay more").**
1. **Traditional rule:** no consideration where the promisee does only what it was already contractually bound to do: *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317 (promise to share deserters' wages among the remaining crew unenforceable; their contracts already covered such a contingency). The case has two reported rationales: no consideration, and/or a policy against extortion (Concentrate; see Luther (1999) 19 LS 526 on the different reports).
2. **Doing more than the contract requires** is fresh consideration: *Hartley v Ponsonby* (1857) 7 E & B 872 (ship so undermanned that the crew were entitled to refuse to sail on; carrying on was more than their duty); *Hanson v Royden* (1867) LR 3 CP 47 (promotion with extra tasks).
3. **Practical benefit:** *Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd* [1991] 1 QB 1 (CA). Glidewell LJ's test (at 15-16), in summary:
   - (i) A has contracted with B to do work or supply goods/services for payment;
   - (ii) before A has finished, B has reason to doubt that A will or can complete;
   - (iii) B then promises A extra payment in return for A's promise to perform on time;
   - (iv) B thereby obtains in practice a benefit, or avoids a disbenefit;
   - (v) B's promise is not obtained by A's economic duress or fraud;
   - (vi) then the benefit to B is capable of being consideration.
   In *Roffey* the benefits were avoiding a late-completion penalty under the main contract and avoiding the trouble of finding a replacement subcontractor. *Stilk* was not overruled; duress now does the policing work that the strict consideration rule used to do.
4. Later applications of practical benefit: *Pitt v PHH Asset Management Ltd* [1994] 1 WLR 327 (promise to exchange contracts; removal of a "nuisance" factor); *Attrill v Dresdner Kleinwort Ltd* [2011] EWCA Civ 229 (employees continued working after promise of a bonus pool).

### 7. Part payment of a debt ("promise to accept less")
1. **The rule in Pinnel's Case.** Paying a smaller sum is not good discharge of an undisputed larger debt: *Pinnel's Case* (1602) 5 Co Rep 117a (obiter); *Foakes v Beer* (1884) 9 App Cas 605 (HL) (ratio: a judgment creditor who agreed to instalments could still claim interest). Lord Blackburn doubted the rule, saying business people recognise that prompt part payment can be more beneficial than insisting on the whole, but the House kept it.
2. **Practical benefit does not apply to part payment** (orthodox position): *Re Selectmove Ltd* [1995] 1 WLR 474 (CA) (company's promise to pay PAYE arrears by instalments and future tax as it fell due was no consideration; extending *Roffey* would leave *Foakes* without effect, and only the House of Lords or Parliament could do that); *Collier v P & MJ Wright (Holdings) Ltd* [2007] EWCA Civ 1329, [2008] 1 WLR 643 (recorded as "Collier v Wright (Holdings) Ltd").
3. **The MWB v Rock saga.**
   - CA: *MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd v Rock Advertising Ltd* [2016] EWCA Civ 553 accepted that part payment alone is no consideration, but held that an *additional* practical benefit could be, here keeping Rock as licensee and avoiding empty premises. Arden LJ said there could be no coherent distinction between promises to pay debts and promises to do work, and adopted Chen-Wishart's "collateral unilateral contract" analysis (MWB bound only while Rock kept to the new schedule).
   - SC: *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 allowed the appeal on a different ground: the contract's "no oral modification" clause meant the oral variation was not binding. Lord Sumption (at [18]) declined to decide the consideration point. He said the points of distinction between *Roffey* and *Foakes* were arguable but "somewhat forced", and that *Foakes* was "probably ripe for re-examination", but only by an enlarged panel in a case where the decision would be more than obiter.
   - After the SC, Kerr J in *Simantob v Shavleyan* (2018) treated himself as bound by *Selectmove*: a practical benefit cannot be consideration for accepting part payment (Concentrate).
4. **Ways to discharge the whole debt despite the rule:**
   - any difference in performance at the creditor's request: earlier payment, payment at a different place, or something in kind ("a horse, hawk or robe": *Pinnel's Case*; Jessel MR in *Couldrey v Bartrum* (1881) 19 Ch D 394: a creditor may take "a horse, or a canary, or a tomtit" but not 19s 6d in the pound);
   - a composition agreement with other creditors;
   - part payment by a third party (*Hirachand Punamchand v Temple* (1911)): it would be a fraud on the third party for the creditor then to sue for the balance;
   - promissory estoppel (see `promissory-estoppel.md`; *High Trees*; *D & C Builders v Rees* [1966] 2 QB 617).

## Key authorities
- *Currie v Misa* (1875) LR 10 Exch 153: classic benefit/detriment definition.
- *Dunlop v Selfridge* [1915] AC 847 (HL): approves Pollock's "price of the promise" definition; also leading privity case.
- *Pillans v Van Mierop* (1765) 3 Burr 1663: Lord Mansfield treated consideration as merely evidential, so a written commercial undertaking was enforceable. Presented in the discussion as a "heresy" that did not survive (see *Eastwood v Kenyon*).
- *Chappell & Co v Nestlé* [1960] AC 87 (HL): wrappers were part of the consideration; adequacy irrelevant.
- *Thomas v Thomas* (1842) 2 QB 851: motive distinguished from consideration; £1 plus repairs was enough.
- *Bainbridge v Firmstone* (1838); *Haigh v Brooks* (1839): subjective value; the courts will not reassess the bargain.
- *Combe v Combe* [1951] 2 KB 215 (CA): husband promised ex-wife an annual allowance; her not applying to court for maintenance was not consideration because he had not requested it. No contract (and estoppel could not create a cause of action).
- *Roscorla v Thomas* (1842); *Re McArdle* [1951]; *Eastwood v Kenyon* (1840): past consideration rule.
- *Lampleigh v Brathwait* (1615); *Re Casey's Patents* [1892]; *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614 (PC): the previous request exception and its three conditions.
- *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861): consideration must move from the promisee.
- *Shadwell v Shadwell* (1860) 9 CBNS 159: majority held the nephew's marriage (already promised to his fiancée) was consideration for the uncle's annuity promise. The discussion asks whether the uncle really benefited and whether the promise really induced the marriage.
- *Collins v Godefroy*; *Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC* [1925] AC 270; *Ward v Byham* [1956] 1 WLR 496: public duty.
- *Scotson v Pegg* (1861); *The Eurymedon* [1975] AC 154 (PC): duty owed to a third party is good consideration.
- *Stilk v Myrick* (1809); *Hartley v Ponsonby* (1857): existing contractual duty to promisor; going beyond it.
- *Williams v Roffey Bros* [1991] 1 QB 1 (CA): practical benefit test (Glidewell LJ).
- *Foakes v Beer* (1884) 9 App Cas 605 (HL); *Re Selectmove* [1995] 1 WLR 474 (CA): part payment no consideration; practical benefit not extended.
- *MWB v Rock Advertising* [2016] EWCA Civ 553 (CA) / [2018] UKSC 24 (SC): CA found practical benefit; SC decided on the no-oral-modification clause and left *Foakes* for another day.
- *Couldrey v Bartrum* (1881) 19 Ch D 394: anything other than a smaller sum of money can be accord and satisfaction.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the doctrine necessary?** McKendrick lists five criticisms: it is too narrow (Dawson: a "formula of denial" for promises with nothing exchanged); highly technical (Treitel's account runs past 100 pages); out of touch with commercial reality (though easily satisfied by nominal sums such as £1 business sales, or avoided by a deed; letters of credit are hard to explain); hard to reconcile with theories based on promise or will (S A Smith); and too broad, doing work better done by duress, unconscionability, estoppel and intention to create legal relations. Lord Goff in *White v Jones* [1995] said English contract law is seen as "hampered" by an unnecessary doctrine of consideration.
- **Defences of the doctrine.** Chen-Wishart (*Reciprocity and Enforceability*) argues it expresses a deep instinct for reciprocity between equals and keeps the state out of gift-giving. Lord Steyn (extrajudicially, (1997) 113 LQR 433) saw no need for radical reform: once a serious commercial agreement is shown there is "virtually a presumption" of consideration.
- **Academic accounts.** Ibbetson: consideration is a historical leftover from tensions between the old forms of action, an anachronism. Treitel: it gives promise-enforcement a sound conceptual basis in the relationship between benefit and detriment. Atiyah ("Consideration: A Restatement"): it is mostly a label applied after the fact to the court's policy choice in each case; courts should look openly at the reasons that make it just to enforce a promise. Treitel replied that Atiyah's approach is too vague to be workable. Maitland's line, "The forms of action we have buried, but still they rule us from their graves", captures the historical critique. Other reading: Horwitz (1974) 87 Harv L Rev 917 and Simpson (1979) 46 U Chi L Rev 533 (history of adequacy); Adams & Brownsword (1990) 53 MLR 536.
- **Inventing consideration.** Treitel (quoted by McKendrick) describes courts "inventing" consideration (e.g. *Shadwell*, *Ward v Byham*, *Chappell*). This shows the doctrine is flexible, but also that it may be a cover for policy.
- **Incoherence between *Roffey* and *Foakes*.** A practical benefit can support a promise to pay more but (orthodoxly) not a promise to accept less. Arden LJ in *MWB* called the distinction incoherent. Lord Sumption in the SC thought the distinctions forced and *Foakes* ripe for review. Concentrate notes the *Roffey* "benefit" comes from the promisor's own promise, not from anything the promisee gives. That is hard to square with the rule that consideration must move from the promisee (Colman J in *South Caribbean Trading v Trafalgar Beheer BV* (2004)) and makes the doctrine close to meaningless if benefit is judged subjectively. Chen-Wishart's "collateral unilateral contract" reading explains *Roffey* as buying actual performance rather than the right to it. Further reading: Coote, "Consideration and Variations: A Different Solution" (2004) 120 LQR 19; Shaw-Mellors (2016) JBL 696; Shaw-Mellors and Poole (2018) JBL 101; Peel (1994) 110 LQR 353 on *Selectmove*; Foster and Reilly [2018] Coventry LJ 53 on the SC's refusal to address part payment; *Ma Hongjin v SCP Holdings* (2020) (Singapore CA reaffirmed consideration for variations).
- **Reform.** The Law Revision Committee, Sixth Interim Report (Cmnd 5449, 1937) proposed that promises inducing detrimental reliance be enforceable, the past consideration rule be abolished, promises to perform existing duties be enforceable where public policy allows, and agreements to accept part payment be binding absent improper pressure or fraud. None was enacted. The Singapore Court of Appeal's coda in *Gay Choon Ing v Loh Sze Ti Terence Peter* [2009] 2 SLR 332 surveyed alternatives (estoppel, duress, undue influence, unconscionability, a writing requirement) and concluded that keeping a "somewhat dilute" doctrine alongside those doctrines may be the practical answer. International restatements, such as the Principles of European Contract Law, have no consideration requirement (McKendrick).
- **Reflection questions.** How does Combe differ from a stranger who plays the violin uninvited on your lawn and demands payment? Are parties always "free and able to judge for themselves" (Lord Denman in *Haigh*)? Should fairness be discussed within consideration at all?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the promise to be enforced** and who is promisor and promisee. The same party can be both, depending on which promise is sued on.
2. **Is it a formation promise or a variation (alteration) promise?** Variations raise the existing-duty and part-payment rules.
3. **Is there a deed?** If so, no consideration is needed.
4. **What did the promisee give or promise?** Was it requested by the promisor (a price), or merely a condition or a gratuitous act (*Combe*)?
5. **Sufficiency.** Ignore adequacy. Check for "unreal" consideration: motive (*Thomas*), forbearing from something never intended (*Arrale*), nothing of value (*White v Bluett*). An honest compromise of a doubtful claim is fine (*Cook v Wright*).
6. **Timing.** Was the act done before the promise? If so, apply the *Pao On* three-stage test (request; understood to be paid for; would have been enforceable). Look for professional or commercial context. Trap: confusing past consideration with acceptance of a unilateral offer.
7. **Existing duties:**
   - public duty: did the promisee go beyond it (*Glasbrook*)?
   - duty owed to a third party: good consideration (*Scotson v Pegg*, *Eurymedon*, *Pao On*).
   - duty owed to the promisor and promise to pay more: did the promisee do anything extra (*Hartley*)? If not, apply *Roffey*'s steps: doubt over completion, practical benefit to the promisor (penalty avoided, no need to find a replacement), and **no duress or fraud**. Always flag possible economic duress if the promisee threatened not to perform (see the duress note).
8. **Part payment:** start with *Pinnel*/*Foakes* and *Selectmove*. Look for anything extra (earlier date, different place, chattel, third-party payment, composition). Discuss the *MWB* CA reasoning and the SC's doubts, and *Simantob*. Typical trap: a parent or relative paying part of the debt (*Hirachand*).
9. **If no consideration, move to promissory estoppel** (only for variations, only as a defence; see `promissory-estoppel.md`). Do not jump straight to estoppel without first analysing consideration.
10. **Third-party angle:** if the claimant gave no consideration because they are not a party, consider the 1999 Act (`privity-and-third-party-rights.md`).
