# Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893] 1 QB 256 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Also cited as [1892] EWCA Civ 1 (Court of Appeal, decided December 1892). Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Lindley, Bowen and A L Smith LJJ, all agreeing in result). The leading case on **unilateral contracts**: an advertisement promising a reward can be an offer to the world, accepted by performing its conditions.

## Facts
The company advertised its smoke ball, promising £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using it as directed, and stating that £1,000 had been deposited at a bank to show sincerity. Mrs Carlill bought a ball, used it as directed and caught influenza. The company refused to pay.

## Held
- The advertisement was a genuine offer, not a mere "puff" or invitation to treat; the bank deposit showed the promise was meant seriously.
- An offer can be made to the world at large and ripens into a contract with whoever performs its conditions.
- Performance was acceptance; notification of acceptance was not required because the offer, by implication, dispensed with it.
- The terms were not too vague: whichever construction of the time limit applied (the judges differed), Mrs Carlill succeeded.
- There was consideration: her use of the ball as directed (inconvenience) and the company's benefit from increased sales.
- The company was liable to pay the £100.

## Why it matters
- Standard authority on offers to the world, acceptance by conduct and the dispensing of notice.
- Illustrates how a clear statement of intention (the deposit) supports finding legal intention.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the statement an offer or an invitation to treat or puff? Look at specificity and indications of seriousness.
2. Who can accept, and how (performance rather than notice)?
3. Identify consideration and test any vagueness argument.
