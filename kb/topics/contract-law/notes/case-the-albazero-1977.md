# Albacruz (Cargo Owners) v Albazero (Owners) (The Albazero) [1977] AC 774 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1977] AC 774 ([1976] 3 All ER 129) before
> citing paragraphs or Lord Diplock's exact formulation of the exception, which is not reproduced here.

## Overview
House of Lords, decided in 1976 (one source gives 28 July 1976; the exact day is not confirmed) and reported [1977] AC
774. Panel as named by the sources: Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Diplock, Lord Simon of Glaisdale and Lord Fraser of
Tullybelton; Lord Diplock gave the leading speech. A time charterer, who had suffered no loss itself, could not recover
the value of cargo owned by others. The case is the source of the exceptions to the rule that a claimant can recover
only its own loss, relied on later in *Linden Gardens* and *Panatown*.

## Facts
The claimants had time chartered the *Albacruz* from the defendant owners under a five-year charterparty (Shell Time
B). The defendants also owned a sister ship, the *Albazero*. The *Albacruz* was totally lost with a cargo of crude
oil. The claimants arrested the *Albazero* and claimed the value of the cargo. Property in the cargo had passed to
indorsees of the bills of lading, who, not the claimants, bore the loss. The indorsees' own claim under the bill of
lading was time-barred under the Hague Rules one-year limit. Brandon J upheld the claim in the claimants' favour
following *Dunlop v Lambert*; the Court of Appeal also allowed the claimants to recover the full value of the cargo,
although Roskill LJ doubted the rule while following precedent.

## Held
- The House of Lords reversed and allowed the owners' appeal. The charterers, having suffered no loss, were not
  entitled to substantial damages.
- The rule in *Dunlop v Lambert* (a consignor may recover substantial damages for loss to a consignee who is a stranger
  to the carriage contract) was not extended to a case where the owner of the goods has a separate contract of
  carriage with the shipowner under the bill of lading. Lord Diplock's compromise limited the rule to cases of that
  kind.
- Commentary treats Lord Diplock's speech as identifying the exceptions to the general rule, which later cases adapted.

## Effect
- It is the root of the "narrow ground" for third-party loss: in *St Martin's* the House of Lords adapted the
  *Albazero* exception for building contracts.
- It confirms that a claimant who has not suffered the loss itself ordinarily can recover only nominal damages.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Start from the general rule: a claimant can recover substantial damages only for its own loss.
2. Identify whether the claimant contracted for the benefit of a third party who bears the loss.
3. Ask whether the third party has its own direct contract with the defendant covering the same ground. If so, the
   *Dunlop v Lambert* exception is not extended (*The Albazero*).
4. In a construction setting, move to the *St Martin's* / *Panatown* analysis; elsewhere, consider the 1999 Act.
