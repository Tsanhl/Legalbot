# Alfred McAlpine Construction Ltd v Panatown Ltd [2001] 1 AC 518 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Also reported at [2000] 4 All ER 97. Check the
> report ([2001] 1 AC 518; [2000] UKHL 43) before relying on the detail of any individual speech.

## Overview
House of Lords, July 2000 (several sources give 27 July; one gives 28 July, so the day is not confirmed). Panel: Lord
Clyde, Lord Goff, Lord Jauncey, Lord Browne-Wilkinson and Lord Millett. By a majority of 3 to 2 (Lords Goff and
Millett dissenting) the House allowed McAlpine's appeal and held that Panatown could not recover substantial damages
in respect of loss suffered by a related company, because that company had its own direct remedy.

## Facts
Panatown, the employer, contracted with Alfred McAlpine for the construction of an office building and car park. The
land belonged to Unex Investment Properties Ltd (UIPL), a sister company of Panatown, which was not a party to the
building contract. McAlpine had also given UIPL a **duty of care deed**, under which UIPL had direct rights against
McAlpine. The work was defective and Panatown, which had no proprietary interest, claimed substantial damages. The
Court of Appeal (1998) had rejected McAlpine's "no loss" argument and held that Panatown could recover.

## Held
- The House reversed the Court of Appeal. The existence of UIPL's direct right of action under the duty of care deed
  was fatal to Panatown's claim on the "narrow ground": the narrow ground (the extension of the *Albazero* exception
  adopted in *St Martin's*) did not apply where the third party had its own remedy.
- Sources describe the deed as the decisive feature: without it the case would have fallen within *St Martin's*.
- **Broad ground.** The broad ground is Lord Griffiths' idea in *St Martin's*, that the employer suffers loss in its
  own right from not receiving the performance it bargained for. Lords Goff and Millett (dissenting) accepted it and
  would have decided for Panatown on that basis. Lords Jauncey and Browne-Wilkinson held that, whatever its status,
  it could not help Panatown because UIPL's direct remedy under the deed displaced it. Lord Clyde also rejected the
  claim and raised doubts about the principle itself. The House therefore did not settle whether the broad ground
  exists, though commentary treats it as now generally accepted.
- Open points noted by commentary: whether repairs must be carried out before recovery, and whether consequential
  losses are recoverable.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the contracting employer and the third party who owns or uses the property.
2. Ask first whether the **narrow ground** (*St Martin's* / *Linden Gardens*, from *The Albazero*) applies: the
   contractor knew the property would be owned or occupied by others who could not sue on the contract.
3. Check whether the third party has its own direct remedy, for example a collateral warranty or duty of care deed.
   If so, the narrow ground is likely unavailable (*Panatown*).
4. Where there is no direct remedy, consider arguing the broad ground, but note the doubt about its status.
5. Consider the 1999 Act as an alternative: it may let the third party sue directly.
