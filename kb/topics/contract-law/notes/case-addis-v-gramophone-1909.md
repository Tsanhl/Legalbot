# Addis v Gramophone Co Ltd [1909] AC 488 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> The accounts differ on how the dismissal was carried out, so that detail is left out. Check the report before citing pinpoints. Later case law has narrowed the rule; check current authority.

## Overview
House of Lords, decided 26 July 1909 ([1909] UKHL 1). The case is the origin of the rule that damages for wrongful dismissal do not compensate for **injured feelings or harm from the manner of dismissal**.

## Facts
Addis managed the Gramophone Company's business in Calcutta under a contract terminable on six months' notice. The company dismissed him in circumstances he described as harsh and humiliating. He sued for wrongful dismissal, claiming his salary and commission and damages for the manner of dismissal, including harm to his reputation and prospects of finding work. The Court of Appeal allowed damages for the manner of dismissal.

## Held
- The House of Lords reversed. Damages for the **manner of dismissal**, including injured feelings and the harm to reputation or employment prospects flowing from it, could not be recovered in an action for breach of contract.
- Recovery was limited to the direct financial loss from the breach (for example pay and commission for the notice period). Such wrongs would need a separate cause of action, for example defamation.
- Exemplary damages are not available for breach of contract (an observation attributed to Lord Atkinson in the Wikipedia account).

## Why it matters
- Underpins the general rule against damages for mental distress in contract, now subject to exceptions (see *Farley v Skinner*).
- Later decisions have read the rule narrowly, as restricting recovery for loss caused by the manner of dismissal rather than barring all reputational loss, and statutory unfair dismissal compensation operates alongside it.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify whether the claim is for the manner of dismissal or breach, not for the termination itself.
2. State the *Addis* rule, then examine whether the claim is better framed as financial loss, or whether a later exception applies.
3. Cross-refer to *Farley v Skinner* for distress where freedom from it was an important object of the contract.
