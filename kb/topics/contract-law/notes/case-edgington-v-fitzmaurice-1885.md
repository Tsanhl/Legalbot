# Edgington v Fitzmaurice (1885) 29 Ch D 459 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them. (One source prints the volume as 24 Ch D; the other three, and the usual citation, give 29 Ch D.)

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Cotton, Bowen and Fry LJJ), 1885. The case holds that a statement of **present intention** is a statement of fact capable of being a misrepresentation, and that a misrepresentation need not be the **sole** inducement.

## Facts
Company directors issued a prospectus inviting subscriptions for debentures, saying the money would be used for alterations to buildings, for horses and vans and to expand the business. In truth the money was needed to pay off pressing debts. Edgington subscribed; he also mistakenly believed the debentures gave him a charge over the company's property. The company failed and he sued the directors in deceit.

## Held
- The directors were liable in deceit. The stated purpose of the loan was a false statement of fact, even though it was about their intentions.
- Bowen LJ: a misstatement of a person's state of mind is a misstatement of fact; "the state of a man's mind is as much a fact as the state of his digestion".
- It is enough that the false statement **played a part** in inducing the claimant; it need not be the only reason he acted. His own mistaken belief did not prevent recovery.

## Why it matters
- Authority that statements of intention (present state of mind) can found liability if the speaker did not hold that intention.
- Authority on **materiality and inducement**: a claimant who has more than one reason for contracting can still rely on the misstatement.
- Useful alongside *Redgrave v Hurd* (reliance despite opportunity to check).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify statements of intention or opinion and ask whether the speaker really held that intention or belief; if not, there is a false statement of fact.
2. Ask whether it influenced the claimant, not whether it was the only influence.
3. Move on to the state of mind of the maker (fraud: *Derry v Peek*) to determine the remedy.
