# Certainty of Terms and Intention to Create Legal Relations

## Overview
An offer and an acceptance do not by themselves make an enforceable contract. The formula is: Agreement + Intention to Create Legal Relations (ICLR) + Certainty + Privity + Consideration = Enforceable Contract. This note covers two of those requirements, as English law (England & Wales) applies them:
- **Certainty.** The agreement must be precise and complete enough for the court to enforce what the parties actually (objectively) agreed, without writing the contract for them.
- **ICLR.** The parties, judged objectively, must have intended legal consequences. This works through presumptions: *against* legal relations in domestic and social agreements, and *for* them in commercial agreements.

Both requirements show the same tension. Courts must not "make the contract for the parties", but they also do not want to be "the destroyer of bargains". McKendrick (ch 1) notes that ICLR causes few practical problems in commercial settings because the presumption there is strong. Its main function is to keep contract law out of domestic and social life.

## The law

### Part A: Certainty

1. **The requirement.** To be enforceable, an agreement must be precise enough, read against the whole context of the negotiations, for the court to be confident that it is enforcing the parties' own intentions (objectively assessed), not intentions the court has imputed to them.
   - Viscount Maugham, *Scammell v Ouston* [1941] AC 251: "In order to constitute a valid contract the parties must so express themselves that their meaning can be determined with a reasonable degree of certainty."
   - Lord Tomlin, *Hillas v Arcos* (1932) 147 LT 503: the court must balance matters so that, without violating essential principle, "the dealings of men may as far as possible be treated as effective", and the law does not incur "the reproach of being the destroyer of bargains".
2. **Two categories**: (1) **vagueness**, where the terms are too vague to bear a definite meaning; and (2) **incompleteness**, where an essential term has been left unresolved, e.g. an "agreement to agree".

#### Vagueness
- *Scammell v Ouston* (HL): a van was ordered "on the understanding that the balance of the purchase price can be had on hire-purchase terms over a period of 2 years". "Hire-purchase terms" could not be given a definite meaning, so there was no contract, even though four lower-court judges had found one. Lord Russell said the plaintiffs' "fivefold choice" of ways to explain the contract was "embarrassing but eloquent". McKendrick notes such cases are relatively rare (*Scammell v Dicker* [2005] EWCA Civ 405). For a modern example of an unenforceable agreement to agree, see *Barbudev v Eurocom Cable* [2012] EWCA Civ 548.
- Viscount Maugham added that courts are "very willing" to imply terms in commercial documents between parties familiar with their trade (trade custom: *Shamrock SS Co v Storey* (1899)).

#### Incompleteness: May and Butcher v Hillas
- *May and Butcher Ltd v The King* [1934] 2 KB 17n (HL, decided 1929): an agreement to buy surplus tentage at prices "to be agreed upon from time to time" was not binding. Price was essential and had been left for future agreement, and the arbitration clause did not save it. Sale of Goods Act s 8(2) did not apply because the contract was not *silent* on price.
- *Hillas v Arcos* (HL): an option to buy 100,000 standards of softwood in 1931, priced at 5% below the official price list, was binding once exercised, even though details were left open. The previous year's dealings and the agreed formula filled the gaps.
- *Foley v Classique Coaches* [1934] 2 KB 1 (CA): petrol was to be supplied "at a price to be agreed … from time to time". The agreement was binding: a reasonable price was implied, there was an arbitration clause, and the parties had acted on it for three years (Greer LJ).
- Scrutton LJ thought *May and Butcher* was enforceable and *Hillas* was not, the reverse of the House of Lords in both. This shows how hard it is to extract principle. Macneil called the search for coherent principles a "fool's errand", and Rix LJ (*Mamidoil-Jetoil v Okta* [2001] 2 Lloyd's Rep 76, [69]) stressed that the answer depends on the facts.

#### Techniques that save an agreement (McKendrick 4.3)
1. **Machinery agreed by the parties.**
   - Leaving a term to *one party* can be valid (Viscount Dunedin in *May and Butcher*: price "to be settled by the buyer"). An implied limit may stop that power being used dishonestly, for an improper purpose, capriciously or arbitrarily: *Paragon Finance v Nash* [2001] EWCA Civ 1466 (variable interest rates).
   - Leaving a term to a *third party or arbitrator* also works. Courts are slower to find uncertainty where there is a wide arbitration clause (*Queensland Electricity Generating Board v New Hope Collieries* [1989] 1 Lloyd's Rep 205, PC: uncertainty arguments "exert minimal attraction"), with *May and Butcher* as the notable exception.
2. **Machinery that breaks down.** *Sudbrook Trading Estate v Eggleton* [1983] 1 AC 444 (HL): an option to buy "at such price … as may be agreed upon by two valuers" survived the landlord's refusal to appoint a valuer. Where the machinery is subsidiary and not essential, the court substitutes its own, here an inquiry into fair value. The position is different where a *named* valuer, or one with special knowledge, is essential. On the other side of the line is *Gillatt v Sky Television* [2000] 1 All ER (Comm) 461 (CA): "as determined by an independent chartered accountant" was integral, and since the machinery had never been invoked there was nothing for the court to substitute.
3. **Statute.** Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 8: the price may be fixed by the contract, left to be fixed in an agreed manner, or determined by course of dealing (s 8(1)). If it is not, the buyer pays a reasonable price (s 8(2)), and what is reasonable is a question of fact (s 8(3)). It applies only where the contract is silent.
4. **Severance.** *Nicolene v Simmonds* [1953] 1 QB 543: "the usual conditions of acceptance apply" was meaningless (there were no usual conditions) and could be struck out, leaving the contract intact. Denning LJ distinguished a *meaningless* clause, which can be ignored, from a clause *yet to be agreed*, which may mean there is no contract, and warned that defaulters would otherwise scan contracts "to find some meaningless clause on which to ride free". The more important the term, the less likely it can be severed (the contrast with *Scammell*).
5. **Implication of terms.** *Wells v Devani* [2019] UKSC 4: the estate agent's commission (2% plus VAT) had been agreed by phone without saying when it was payable. The Supreme Court implied that it was payable on completion, out of the sale proceeds. Lord Kitchin said there is no rule that a contract must exist before a term can be implied: where the parties intended to be bound and have acted on the agreement, a term may be implied to make an otherwise incomplete agreement enforceable, provided it is necessary for business efficacy or obvious.
6. **Performance and reliance.** "When much has been done, the Courts will do their best not to destroy the bargain" (Lord Denning, *F & G Sykes (Wessex) v Fine Fare* [1967] 1 Lloyd's Rep 53). Steyn LJ in *G Percy Trentham v Archital Luxfer* [1993] 1 Lloyd's Rep 25 said execution makes it easier to imply a term or to treat a matter as inessential. Reliance is evidence, not a guarantee: in *May and Butcher* a £1,000 deposit did not help. Courts will not impose an agreement the parties never reached (*RTS Flexible Systems* [2010] UKSC 14, [47]). In *British Steel v Cleveland Bridge* [1984] 1 All ER 504, Robert Goff J found no contract despite performance and used restitution instead.

### Part B: Intention to create legal relations

1. **Objective test.** Whether the parties intended to be legally bound is judged objectively, not by their states of mind: Lord Bingham in *Edmonds v Lawson* [2000] 2 WLR 1091, 1099; *Maple Leaf Marco Volatility Master Fund v Rouvroy* [2009] EWCA Civ 1334 (McKendrick). There is a limit: a party who actually *knows* the other did not intend legal relations cannot rely on objective appearances, by analogy with "snapping up" (*Attrill v Dresdner Kleinwort* [2013] EWCA Civ 394, [86]).
2. **Origins.** Simpson credits *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 with first recognising the doctrine. Atkin LJ in *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571 firmly established it.
3. **Two presumptions**:
   - *Social, family or domestic agreements*: presumption **against** ICLR, rebuttable by an objective inference from the language and circumstances.
   - *Commercial agreements*: presumption **for** ICLR, rebuttable by the party denying it. Clear evidence is needed (*Edwards v Skyways* [1964] 1 WLR 349, 355). McKendrick says the domestic presumption is easier to rebut than the commercial one.
4. **Categories**: *domestic* means relationships between partners (spouses, ex-spouses, cohabitants); *family* means other family ties, e.g. parent and child; *social* means friendship. McKendrick notes there is no formal line between social and domestic, and the categories overlap.

#### Domestic agreements
- **Spouses living together in amity.** No intention is presumed. *Balfour v Balfour*: a husband working in Ceylon promised his wife, who stayed in England on medical advice, £30 a month. The promise was unenforceable. Atkin LJ relied on floodgates ("the small courts of this country would have to be multiplied one hundredfold") and on the law's limited role in marriage ("natural love and affection … counts for so little in these cold Courts"). Duke LJ reasoned instead from lack of consideration, so the ratio is debatable. Lord Upjohn in *Pettitt v Pettitt* [1970] AC 777 said *Balfour* stretched the doctrine "to its limits". McKendrick thinks the presumption applies equally to cohabitants.
- **Separated or separating spouses.** Intention is presumed. *Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211 (Lord Denning MR): when spouses are separated or about to separate, "they do not rely on honourable understandings. They want everything cut and dried."
- **Parent and child.** *Jones v Padavatton* [1969] 1 WLR 328: a mother promised maintenance if her daughter gave up her Washington job to read for the Bar in England, later replaced by a house the daughter could live in. The mother recovered possession. Danckwerts and Fenton Atkinson LJJ said this was a family arrangement resting on good faith. Salmon LJ thought there was a contract at first, but it lasted only a reasonable time (no more than five years). The daughter's detrimental reliance was outweighed by the presumption combined with the vague terms.
- **Rebutting the domestic presumption** (McKendrick; the list is not closed):
  - context, e.g. agreements to share ownership of the home or bank accounts (*Granatino v Radmacher* [2010] UKSC 42, [142]), or a family business (*Snelling v John G Snelling Ltd* [1973] 1 QB 87);
  - relationship breakdown (*Merritt*);
  - detrimental reliance (*Parker v Clark* [1960] 1 WLR 286: a couple sold their home to move in with an elderly couple who promised to leave them the house. Devlin J found a contract).

#### Social agreements
- Atkin LJ's examples: an agreement to go for a walk, or an offer of hospitality. *Lens v Devonshire Club* (1914): the winner of a golf competition could not sue for the prize. Contrast newspaper competitions (*O'Brien v MGN*).
- **Rebuttal.** *Simpkins v Pays* [1955] 1 WLR 975: a lodger, her landlady and the landlady's granddaughter entered a weekly newspaper competition together, sharing costs. They had a binding agreement to share the £750 prize because there was "mutuality". McKendrick reads this as rebuttal through shared expense and reliance, and through the agreement not being about running the household.
- **Car-sharing.** *Coward v Motor Insurers' Bureau* [1963] 1 QB 259: regular lifts to work in return for a contribution were not presumed contractual, given everyday hazards such as illness and shift changes. *Albert v Motor Insurers' Bureau* [1972] AC 301 (Lord Cross) took a different view: a contract may exist, at least for completed journeys, and the unlikelihood of anyone suing does not decide the question. The discussion presents *Albert* as rebutting the presumption.

#### Commercial agreements
- **Strong presumption.** It is especially strong where a term is added to an existing contractual relationship such as employment (*Attrill*, [80]).
- *Esso Petroleum v Commissioners of Customs & Excise* [1976] 1 WLR 1 (HL): World Cup coins were "given" with four gallons of petrol. A bare majority found ICLR. Lord Simon said a commercial promoter should not be allowed to call its promotion a mere puff. Viscount Dilhorne disagreed, because the coins had little intrinsic value. It was a tax case and is often used to show how strong the presumption is.
- *Edwards v Skyways* [1964]: calling a redundancy payment "ex gratia" did not rebut the presumption. The words only denied any *pre-existing* liability.
- **Ways to rebut the commercial presumption** (list, with McKendrick's examples):
  - express declarations or honour clauses: *Rose and Frank Co v JR Crompton & Bros* [1925] AC 445 (an "honourably pledge" clause meant no contract). Scrutton LJ (CA) said such clauses are not contrary to public policy. Ousting the courts' jurisdiction over a binding contract is objectionable, but agreeing that there will be no contract at all is not;
  - advertising puffs (contrast *Carlill*, where the £1,000 deposit showed seriousness);
  - "subject to contract" wording (*RTS Flexible Systems* [2010] UKSC 14);
  - letters of intent and letters of comfort (listed without further detail);
  - implied agreements inferred from conduct: *Baird Textile Holdings v Marks & Spencer* [2001] EWCA Civ 274. Mance LJ: a contract needs (a) agreement on essentials with enough certainty and (b) ICLR, both judged objectively. ICLR is normally presumed once there is an express agreement, but where a contract is to be *implied* from conduct, the party alleging it must show it is necessary. If the parties might have acted the same way without a contract, none is implied;
  - collective agreements: *Ford Motor Co v AUEFW* [1969] 2 QB 303. Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992 s 179 conclusively presumes a collective agreement is not intended to be enforceable unless it is in writing and expressly says it is;
  - purely social settings: *Blue v Ashley* [2017] EWHC 1928 (Comm) (a jocular promise of £15m in a pub was not contractual); *MacInnes v Gross* [2017] EWHC 46 (QB) (an informal dinner conversation).
- **Burden of proof.** The claimant must prove an agreement. Once an otherwise enforceable commercial agreement is shown, the defendant must prove there was no ICLR (McKendrick on *Baird*).

## Key authorities
- *Scammell v Ouston* [1941] AC 251 (HL): "hire-purchase terms" was too vague, so no contract.
- *Hillas v Arcos* (1932) 147 LT 503 (HL): gaps were filled from prior dealings and the agreed formula, so the agreement was enforced.
- *May and Butcher v The King* [1934] 2 KB 17n (HL): price "to be agreed" meant no contract.
- *Foley v Classique Coaches* [1934] 2 KB 1 (CA): a reasonable price was implied, helped by the arbitration clause and three years of performance.
- *Sudbrook Trading Estate v Eggleton* [1983] 1 AC 444 (HL): the court substituted machinery for valuation machinery that was not essential.
- *Nicolene v Simmonds* [1953] 1 QB 543 (CA): a meaningless clause was severed.
- *Wells v Devani* [2019] UKSC 4: a term may be implied into an otherwise incomplete agreement the parties acted on.
- Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 8: reasonable price where the contract is silent.
- *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571 (CA): presumption against ICLR between spouses living together in amity.
- *Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211 (CA): separating spouses, so ICLR presumed.
- *Jones v Padavatton* [1969] 1 WLR 328 (CA): family arrangement, presumption not rebutted.
- *Parker v Clark* [1960] 1 WLR 286; *Simpkins v Pays* [1955] 1 WLR 975: domestic and social presumption rebutted.
- *Coward v MIB* [1963] 1 QB 259; *Albert v MIB* [1972] AC 301: lift-sharing.
- *Edwards v Skyways* [1964] 1 WLR 349: commercial presumption; "ex gratia" not enough to rebut.
- *Esso v Customs & Excise* [1976] 1 WLR 1 (HL): promotional coins.
- *Rose and Frank v Crompton* [1925] AC 445 (HL): honour clause.
- *Baird Textile v Marks & Spencer* [2001] EWCA Civ 274: no implied contract from conduct.
- *RTS Flexible Systems v Müller* [2010] UKSC 14: objective approach; "subject to contract".
- TULRCA 1992 s 179: collective agreements.

## Debate and critique
- **Is May and Butcher still good law?** The New Zealand Court of Appeal in *Fletcher Challenge Energy v ECNZ* [2002] 2 NZLR 433 (Blanchard J) said it stands only for the proposition that an agreement which leaves out an essential term, and any means of fixing it, is not a contract, and doubted it would be decided the same way today. Following McLauchlan ((1998) 18 NZULR 77), it said an agreement to agree is not void if the parties supplied a workable formula, standard or machinery. The question becomes whether the parties intended to be bound. McKendrick welcomes the direction but warns that an intention-based test is itself imprecise. Scrutton LJ (in *Hillas*, CA) complained that law and commercial practice were "getting wider apart", driving business to arbitration.
- **Arbitration clauses.** McKendrick asks why parties should be able to leave important matters to an arbitrator when they cannot leave them to a High Court judge.
- **Reform models.** The Unidroit Principles provide (i) no contract where a party insists on agreement on specific matters first, and (ii) a contract can exist despite terms deliberately left open if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonable alternative way to fill them. These are more liberal than *May and Butcher*, but broadly drafted.
- **Market-individualism.** Adams and Brownsword (in McKendrick ch 1) see the *Hillas v Arcos* approach to certainty as deference to commercial practice, typical of market-individualism. Resisting release from contracts for "relatively unimportant uncertainty" reflects sanctity of contract.
- **Rationale of the domestic presumption.** Atkin LJ's reasons were general policy (floodgates, and keeping the law out of the family), not the Balfours' actual intentions. Intention matters only to rebuttal. Freeman ("Contracting in the Haven: *Balfour v Balfour* Revisited", 1996) argues that modern family law has embraced private ordering and autonomy, so the Victorian premise of *Balfour* no longer reflects reality. Douglas and others are not persuaded. McKendrick prefers *Balfour*'s policy values to those of Freeman and Giddens (who proposed binding parent–child "parenthood contracts"), and cites Kahn-Freund's view that *Balfour* lets "the realities of life" shape the legal rule.
- **Executed v executory.** Hedley ((1985) 5 OJLS 391) argues the question "is there a contract?" cannot be answered in the abstract. Jack and Jill agreeing to split a dinner bill would probably not be bound to go to dinner, but Jack's refusal to pay his half after they have eaten looks quite different. Be wary of blanket statements that there was no ICLR.
- **ICLR and consideration.** *White v Bluett* (1853): a son's promise to stop complaining was held not to be consideration, but McKendrick suggests a modern court might explain the case through ICLR. Unger ((1956) 19 MLR 96) argues that lack of consideration better explains domestic agreements. Duke LJ's reasoning in *Balfour* also turns on consideration.
- **Should ICLR be abolished?** Halson (ed), *The Law of Contract* (Butterworths) argues ICLR is unnecessary, since a proposal not intended to be binding is not an *offer* at all, so the issue belongs inside offer, acceptance and consideration. McKendrick replies that this does not remove the doctrine but moves it, making the law more complex. He predicts ICLR's role will *grow* as consideration weakens after *Williams v Roffey* [1991] 1 QB 1. Rajah JC in *Chwee Kin Keong* said "the marrow of contractual relationships should be the parties' intention to create a legal relationship". Other critics say the doctrine rests on a fiction because parties often have no discernible intention either way.
- **Overlap between certainty and ICLR.** Vague terms may show both that the parties did not agree on essentials and that they did not intend to be bound (*Baird*; *Jones v Padavatton*, where Salmon LJ's analysis turned on duration).

## Applying it to a problem question
**Certainty**
1. Identify the vague or missing term. Is it *vague* (the *Scammell* type) or *left to be agreed* (the *May and Butcher* type)?
2. Is the term essential? Could the contract work without it, so that it can be severed (*Nicolene*)?
3. Did the parties supply a formula, standard, prior dealings, trade custom, or a decision-maker (one party, a valuer, an arbitrator)? If the machinery failed, was it essential (*Sudbrook* v *Gillatt*)?
4. Is it a sale of goods silent on price (s 8)?
5. Has the agreement been acted on? Performance and expenditure favour enforcement (*Foley*, *Sykes*, *Trentham*, *Wells v Devani*), but are not conclusive (*May and Butcher*, *British Steel*). Mention restitution as a fallback where there is no contract.

**ICLR**
1. Classify the context: domestic, family, social or commercial. Mixed facts, e.g. a family business or friends doing business, need careful argument.
2. State the presumption and who bears the burden.
3. Look for rebutting factors:
   - domestic: separation (*Merritt*), property-sharing, a business context, detrimental reliance (*Parker*), seriousness and certainty of terms (contrast *Jones v Padavatton*);
   - commercial: express wording ("honour", "subject to contract", "not legally binding"), and wording that does *not* rebut ("ex gratia", *Edwards*), a jocular or social setting (*Blue v Ashley*), collective agreements (s 179), promotional puffs (*Esso* split; *Carlill*).
4. Consider whether the claim is executed or executory (Hedley): a claim for repayment after performance is easier to support than a claim to force future performance.
5. Remember the knowledge limit (*Attrill*): a party who knew the other did not intend to be bound cannot rely on the objective appearance.

*Traps:* treating the presumptions as rules of law that cannot be displaced; forgetting that ICLR is objective; assuming a deposit or performance automatically cures uncertainty; applying s 8(2) where the contract does provide for price (as in *May and Butcher*).
