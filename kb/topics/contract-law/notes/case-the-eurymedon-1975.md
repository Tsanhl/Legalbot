# New Zealand Shipping Co Ltd v A M Satterthwaite & Co Ltd (The Eurymedon) [1975] AC 154 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the Board's opinion was not read. Check [1975] AC 154 (also [1974] UKPC 1;
> [1974] 1 NZLR 505) before citing. The decision was by a majority, but which members dissented is not confirmed by
> the sources consulted, so it is left out.

## Overview
Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, on appeal from New Zealand, judgment 25 February 1974, reported [1975] AC
154. Panel: Lord Wilberforce, Lord Hodson, Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Simon of Glaisdale and Lord Salmon. Lord
Wilberforce gave the majority opinion. By a majority (the decision was **not unanimous**) the Board held that
stevedores, who were not parties to the bill of lading, could rely on a clause in it that protected the carrier and
extended to independent contractors.

## Facts
A drilling machine was shipped from Liverpool to Wellington under a bill of lading. The bill contained a clause
protecting the carrier which was expressed also to cover its servants, agents and independent contractors (a
"Himalaya" clause). The carrier and the stevedoring company were in the same corporate group. The stevedores negligently damaged the
machine while unloading it, and the owner sued them in tort. The relevant protection was a one-year time bar: the
carrier, and its servants, agents and independent contractors, were discharged unless suit was brought within one
year of delivery. The owner sued the stevedores after the year had expired, and the stevedores relied on the clause. The
obstacle was privity, as the stevedores were not parties to the bill of lading, as in *Scruttons v Midland Silicones*.

## Held
- The stevedores could rely on the clause. The Board treated the Himalaya clause as showing an offer, made through the
  carrier acting as agent, that the stevedores would have the protection if they did the unloading.
- The bill of lading operated as a unilateral contract between the shipper and the stevedores which became binding
  when the stevedores unloaded the goods.
- **Consideration.** The stevedores' performance of unloading supplied consideration, even though they were already
  bound to the carrier to do that work. Performance of an existing obligation owed to a third party can be good
  consideration for the new promise.
- The conditions in Lord Reid's test from *Scruttons* (the clause must show it protects the third party, that the
  carrier contracts as agent for them, that the carrier has authority or later ratification, and that any
  consideration problem is overcome) were treated as satisfied.

## Effect
- It allows an exclusion or limitation clause drafted as a Himalaya clause to protect third parties (stevedores,
  subcontractors) through the device of agency and a unilateral contract, without the 1999 Act.
- The 1999 Act, s 1(6), now makes it possible for a third party to rely on an exclusion or limitation clause in a
  contract directly, where it applies.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify whether the contract has a clause extending protection to servants, agents or independent contractors.
2. If the contractor sued is a third party, explain that privity is the obstacle and apply the *Eurymedon* analysis:
   agency, unilateral contract, and consideration by performance.
3. Add the alternative statutory route under the 1999 Act (s 1 and s 1(6)).
4. Check that the clause is effective in its own right (incorporation, construction, UCTA).
