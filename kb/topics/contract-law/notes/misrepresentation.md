# Misrepresentation

## Overview
Misrepresentation is the first of the "vitiating factors". These are doctrines that let an innocent party escape from, or get compensation for, a contract that was properly formed (offer, acceptance, consideration and so on) but has a defect in how it came about. The question is what happens when one party entered the contract because they relied on a false statement made by the other. The contract is **voidable**: the misled party may rescind it, and depending on the representor's state of mind (fraudulent, negligent or innocent) may also or instead get damages. The topic matters because it sits where contract, tort and restitution meet. Most of its controversy comes from the Misrepresentation Act 1967, especially the "fiction of fraud" in s 2(1). The law stated is that of England and Wales.

### Where misrepresentation fits among the vitiating factors
The discussion sets out the different consequences that vitiating factors can have:
- **Void** (for example some mistakes): the contract is treated as never having existed, and the parties must return what they received.
- **Voidable** (misrepresentation, duress, undue influence): the innocent party may choose to **affirm** the contract or to **rescind** it. Rescission restores both parties to their pre-contract positions. Until rescission, the contract stands.
- **Discharged** (frustration): future obligations fall away automatically at the moment of frustration, with no damages for not performing them.
- **Unenforceable** (illegality, public policy): the court will not compel performance.

McKendrick (ch 17) describes misrepresentation as, in effect, the law of *induced* mistake. Relief rests not on the claimant's mistake as such but on the fact that the other party's false statement caused it.

## The law

### 1. Starting point: no general duty of disclosure
- English law does not generally require contracting parties to disclose information to each other (*Keates v Cadogan* (1851); see also *Fox v Mackreth* (1788) and *Turner v Green* [1895], both). Each party is expected to find out what it needs and to bear the risk of its own decision. One reason given is that if parties had to reveal their bargaining position, the incentive to bargain would disappear.
- Viscount Maugham in *Bradford Third Equitable Benefit Building Society v Borders* [1941]: "mere silence, however morally wrong, will not support an action of deceit" (quoted by McKendrick). The key word is *mere*, because something more than silence will do.
- The position changes when one party interferes with the other's decision-making by supplying false information, whether deliberately or not.

### 2. Classifying a pre-contractual statement
The discussion puts statements made in negotiations into three groups:
1. **A "puff"**: so vague or exaggerated that it played no part in the decision to contract. It is not actionable.
2. **A representation**: not intended to be a term, but it influenced the other party's decision to contract.
3. **A term** (or a warranty in a collateral contract): the maker guaranteed its truth.

Categories 2 and 3 can both be misrepresentations. Only a statement that is *also* a term gives remedies for breach of contract **as well as** for misrepresentation. Section 1(a) of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 keeps the right to rescind for misrepresentation alive even after the misrepresentation "has become a term of the contract". It does not remove the breach-of-contract remedies (*Salt v Stratstone Specialist Ltd* [2015] EWCA Civ 745, per McKendrick). The test for whether a statement is a term is objective intention (*Heilbut, Symons & Co v Buckleton* [1913] AC 30); see `terms-of-the-contract.md`.

### 3. The definition of an actionable misrepresentation
The working definition is a false and unambiguous statement, or misleading conduct, about existing or past fact or law, made by one party (the representor) to the other (the representee) before or when the contract was concluded, which induced the representee to contract. McKendrick's version is "an unambiguous false statement of fact which is addressed to the party misled and which induces that party to enter into a contract", possibly with a further requirement of materiality. Each element is taken in turn below.

**(a) A false and unambiguous statement, or misleading conduct.**
- *Unambiguous*: an ambiguous statement is generally not enough. The exception is where the maker intends the false meaning and the recipient reasonably takes it that way (McKendrick).
- *Conduct counts.* Denning LJ in *Curtis v Chemical Cleaning & Dyeing Co* [1951] 1 KB 805: any behaviour, "by words or conduct", that conveys a false impression is enough. Examples:
  - *Gordon v Selico* (1986): covering up dry rot in a flat before it was viewed.
  - *Spice Girls Ltd v Aprilia World Service BV* [2000] EMLR 478 (first instance); [2002] EWCA Civ 15 (CA): taking part in a commercial shoot amounted to a representation that the group's members did not know of any existing declared intention by one of them to leave during the contract period, when one member had already decided to leave.
  - Implied representations in everyday dealings: a buyer who orders goods represents an intention to pay; a diner who orders a meal represents an ability to pay (*DPP v Ray* [1974] AC 370, cited by McKendrick).
  - Lord Campbell LC in *Walters v Morgan* (1861): even "a nod or a wink" intended to induce a false belief can be enough.
- *A true statement is not a misrepresentation.* Check the facts carefully before assuming a statement was false.

**(b) Silence: the exceptions.** The discussion lists five situations where silence or partial disclosure can found a claim:
1. **Half-truths**: a statement that is literally true but misleading because it leaves something out (*Dimmock v Hallett* (1866) LR 2 Ch App 21: farms said to be let, but the tenants had given notice to quit). In *Nottingham Patent Brick & Tile Co v Butler* (16 QBD 778), a solicitor said he was "not aware" of restrictive covenants without revealing that he had not checked. Lord Chelmsford in *Peek v Gurney*: "half a truth will sometimes amount to a real falsehood".
2. **Change of circumstances**: a statement that was true when made but becomes false before the contract is concluded must be corrected (*Davies v London and Provincial Marine Insurance Co* (1878) 8 Ch D 469; *With v O'Flanagan* [1936] Ch 575). The representation is treated as *continuing* until the contract is made.
3. **Active concealment of a defect** (*Schneider v Heath* (1813) 3 Camp 506: a ship kept afloat, out of dry dock, so buyers could not see its worm-eaten bottom).
4. **Fiduciary or confidential relationships.**
5. **Contracts uberrimae fidei** (of the utmost good faith), the leading example being insurance (*Lambert v Co-operative Insurance Society* [1975]). For consumer insurance, the Consumer Insurance (Disclosure and Representations) Act 2012 s 2 replaces the consumer's duty of disclosure with a duty to take reasonable care not to misrepresent (McKendrick).

**(c) Of existing or past fact.** The statement must be false before or when the representee contracts. A statement that is accurate at signing but later becomes untrue gives no remedy. A buyer who wants protection against later changes must negotiate contract terms for it. The exceptions are where the representor *knew* (not merely believed) that the fact would change, or where the change happened before the contract was made (*With v O'Flanagan*).

**(d) Fact or law, not opinion or intention (subject to qualifications).**
- *Facts*: for example a car's mileage, what a site is like, who painted a picture.
- *Law*: the old rule that statements of law could not be misrepresentations is no longer followed. Misrepresentations of law are actionable (*Pankhania v Hackney LBC* [2002] EWHC 2441 (Ch); mistake of law: *Brennan v Bolt Burden* [2004] EWCA Civ 1017). Depending on the facts, a statement of law may be only an opinion (*Bolt Burdon Solicitors v Tariq* (2016), per the Concentrate guide). The discussion also includes legal attributes (title, licences) and existing laws and regulations, foreign law included.
- *Opinion*: an honestly held opinion is generally not actionable (*Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177, below). However:
  - when the facts are not equally known to both sides, an opinion from the person who knows the facts best often implies that they know facts justifying it (Bowen LJ in *Smith v Land and House Property Corp* (1884) 28 Ch D 7: a tenant described as "a most desirable tenant" who was in fact in arrears);
  - an opinion from someone with special skill or knowledge may be actionable (*Esso Petroleum Co Ltd v Mardon* [1976] QB 801: an expert's forecast of a petrol station's throughput);
  - The discussion adds that an opinion may be actionable where the speaker was better placed to check it but did not (*Brown v Raphael* [1958] Ch 636);
  - an opinion that is not honestly held misstates a fact, namely the speaker's state of mind.
- *Intention, promises, predictions*: these are not generally statements of existing fact. A statement of intention does, however, represent the speaker's *present* state of mind. If the speaker never had the stated intention, or knew they could not perform, that is a misrepresentation. *Edgington v Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459, per Bowen LJ: "the state of a man's mind is as much a fact as the state of his digestion."

**(e) Addressed to the party misled.** The representation must be aimed at the claimant, intending that they act on it (*Peek v Gurney* (1873) LR 6 HL 377). A statement passed on by chance to someone else is not enough. But if the maker *intends* it to be passed on, the eventual recipient can sue (*Pilmore v Hood* (1838); see also *Commercial Banking of Sydney v RH Brown* [1972], bank-to-bank information meant for a customer, and *Clef Aquitaine v Laporte Materials* (2001)). In *Taberna Europe CDO II plc v Selskabet* (2016), the court warned against loosening this requirement in the internet age (Concentrate).

**(f) Inducement (reliance).** The representee must show a causal link between the misrepresentation and the decision to contract.
- It need not be the only or main reason. Being one of the reasons is enough (*Edgington v Fitzmaurice*, where the claimant was also influenced by his own mistaken belief that the loan was secured).
- The discussion breaks reliance into four parts:
  1. the representation must be *material* (*Smith v Chadwick* (1884));
  2. it must be *known* to the representee (*Horsfall v Thomas* (1862): a defect in a gun was concealed, but the buyer never inspected it, so the concealment could not have induced him);
  3. it must be *intended to be acted on* (*Peek v Gurney*);
  4. it must actually be *acted on*: there is no inducement if the representor corrected the statement and drew the correction to the representee's attention before the representee relied on it (*Peekay Intermark Ltd v ANZ Banking Group* [2006] EWCA Civ 386).
- *Reliance on one's own judgment* defeats inducement. In *Attwood v Small* (1838), buyers of a mine relied on their own experts' reports rather than the vendor's figures. The Concentrate guide warns that after *Edgington* this applies only where the claimant relied *entirely* on their own investigation.
- *The chance to discover the truth does not defeat inducement* (*Redgrave v Hurd* (1881) 20 Ch D 1: a buyer of a solicitor's practice did not examine the papers he was offered). Constructive notice is not enough; only actual knowledge of the truth defeats reliance. The claimant's carelessness may instead reduce damages through contributory negligence (see 6 below).
- *Burden and presumption*: the representee must prove inducement (*BV Nederlandse Industrie van Eiprodukten v Rembrandt Enterprises* [2019] EWCA Civ 596). If the statement would have induced a reasonable person, inducement is presumed and the representor must disprove it (*Museprime Properties v Adhill* (1991)). If it would not, the representee must prove actual inducement (*Dadourian Group v Simms* [2009] EWCA Civ 169). *Sharland v Sharland* [2015] UKSC 60 is cited for an objective element, though its scope is debated.
- *The "but for" debate*: McKendrick says a misrepresentation without which the representee would not have contracted clearly qualifies, and one that made no difference clearly does not (*Versloot Dredging v HDI Gerling* [2016] UKSC 45). *Rembrandt* also contains authority that it is enough for the misrepresentation to be "actively present to [the] mind", a lower threshold used especially in fraud cases. McKendrick argues the but-for test should be kept, at least where the remedy is rescission. The Concentrate guide reads *Rembrandt* as requiring but-for causation for non-fraudulent misrepresentation.
- *Fraud*: reliance matters much less. The discussion says fraud is actionable even if the claimant might have contracted anyway. Inducement may be inferred from materiality (*Barton v County NatWest* (1999)), and the materiality requirement does not apply to a fraudulent misrepresentation (*Ross River Ltd v Cambridge City FC* [2007] EWHC 2115 (Ch)).

### 4. Types of misrepresentation (the representor's state of mind)
| Type | Definition | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Fraudulent | Made (1) knowingly, (2) without belief in its truth, or (3) recklessly, careless whether true or false. The essence is the absence of honest belief, judged subjectively. | *Derry v Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337; *Akerhielm v De Mare* [1959] AC 789; recklessness means dishonesty: *Thomas Witter v TBP Industries* [1996] 2 All ER 573 (Jacob J) |
| Negligent | Honestly believed, but made without reasonable care or grounds | *Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465 (common law); MA 1967 s 2(1) (statute) |
| Innocent | Honestly believed on reasonable grounds, but false | MA 1967 s 2(2) (damages in lieu only) |

*Derry v Peek*: directors' prospectus said the company had the right to use steam power, when Board of Trade consent was in fact needed. Because the directors honestly believed the statement, deceit failed. Lord Herschell held that carelessness "falls far short of" fraud, and that motive is irrelevant once fraud is proved. He suggested Parliament might make careless prospectus statements actionable. The discussion also gives the modern formulation from *Ludsin Overseas Ltd v Eco3 Capital Ltd* (the discussion dates it 2003; the Concentrate Q&A cites [2013] EWCA Civ 413):
1. a false representation by D to C;
2. made knowing it was false, or reckless as to its truth;
3. intended to be relied on by C;
4. actually relied on by C, who suffers loss as a result.

An intention to deceive is not a separate requirement.

### 5. Rescission
- **Nature**: rescission sets the contract aside *retrospectively and prospectively*, and each party returns what it received. Termination for breach is different: it discharges the contract only *for the future* (McKendrick). Rescission is the innocent party's choice, so the contract is voidable and remains valid until rescinded. Historically this was the main remedy.
- **Available for every type** of misrepresentation, fraudulent, negligent or innocent (*Redgrave v Hurd*). Under MA 1967 s 1, rescission is also available even though the misrepresentation has become a term, or the contract has been performed, "if otherwise he would be entitled to rescind … without alleging fraud".
- **How to rescind**: as a rule, the decision must be communicated to the other party. In *Car and Universal Finance Co v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525, a seller defrauded by a rogue who paid with a dishonoured cheque and disappeared validly rescinded by telling the police and the AA straight away. Ownership therefore went back to him before the car reached an innocent buyer. Upjohn LJ said that a party who absconds cannot insist on being told of the rescission. The Scottish decision *Macleod v Kerr* (1965) went the other way. Whether a court order is needed is a "highly controversial question" (*SK Shipping v Capital VLCC 3 Corp* [2022] EWCA Civ 231; compare Dyson LJ in *Islington LBC v UCKAC* [2006]). McKendrick argues that no order is needed and that rescission is the party's own act.
- **Bars to rescission** (McKendrick, Concentrate):
  1. **Affirmation** after discovering the truth, by words or by conduct showing an unequivocal intention to continue (*Long v Lloyd* [1958]: a lorry buyer accepted a part-payment for repairs and took the lorry on a second journey; *Re Hop and Malt Exchange, ex p Briggs* (1866)). Affirmation needs knowledge of the facts and of the right to rescind (*Ceviz v Frawley* (2021)). Merely having the means to discover the truth is not knowledge (*SK Shipping* (2020), apparently approved on appeal).
  2. **Lapse of time**. *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] 2 KB 86: a painting innocently said to be a Constable could not be returned five years later. More time is allowed for fraud, where time runs from when the fraud could reasonably have been discovered. *Salt v Stratstone* [2015] EWCA Civ 745 (a "brand new" Cadillac that was in fact two years old and had been in a collision) casts doubt on *Leaf*: lapse of time "on its own" is not a bar, and the question is now absorbed into laches (whether delay makes rescission inequitable). Longmore LJ questioned whether *Leaf* survives s 1 of the 1967 Act.
  3. **Restitutio in integrum impossible**, as where the goods have been destroyed, used up or changed (*Clarke v Dickson* (1858): shares in a mine that had since been worked; *Vigers v Pike* (1842); *Thomas Witter v TBP*: changes to a business that had been bought). Equity allows rescission where *substantial* restitution is possible, with money adjustments (*Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate Co* (1878)). *Halpern v Halpern* [2007] EWCA Civ 291 suggests the requirement is becoming more flexible.
  4. **Third-party rights**: a bona fide purchaser for value who acquires the property before rescission takes good title (*Crystal Palace FC (2000) Ltd v Dowie* [2007] EWHC 1392 (QB); *White v Garden* (1851); SGA 1979 s 23). This is why *Caldwell* turned on the *timing* of the rescission.
  5. **Court's discretion under MA 1967 s 2(2)** to declare the contract subsisting and award damages in lieu (non-fraudulent misrepresentations only).
- **Indemnity**: before damages were widely available, a rescinding party could recover an "indemnity" for obligations *created by the contract*, but not general losses. In *Whittington v Seale-Hayne* (1900) 82 LT 49, the tenant recovered rent and repair costs that the lease required, but not the value of his dead poultry or compensation for his illness. McKendrick treats this as a personal restitutionary claim.

### 6. Damages
**(a) Deceit (fraud).**
- The aim is the tort measure: put the claimant in the position they would have been in had the representation *not been made*.
- The claimant recovers *all direct loss*, whether or not foreseeable (*Doyle v Olby (Ironmongers) Ltd* [1969] 2 QB 158). Lord Denning MR's reasoning was that the defrauded party can say they would not have entered the bargain at all.
- *Smith New Court Securities v Scrimgeour Vickers* [1997] AC 254: the normal measure is price paid minus actual value at the transaction date. But the claimant was "locked into" shares that turned out to be worth only 44p because of a separate fraud, and recovered the difference between 82.25p and 44p.
- Lost profits are recoverable on a *tort* basis: the profit the claimant would probably have made from a different, comparable business (*East v Maurer* [1991] 1 WLR 461, a hair salon; see also *Clef Aquitaine*, *4 Eng Ltd v Harper* [2008] EWHC 915 (Ch), *Parabola Investments v Browallia* [2010] EWCA Civ 486).
- Non-pecuniary loss is recoverable, such as distress (*Archer v Brown* [1985] QB 401) and inconvenience (*Mafo v Adams* [1970]).
- Contributory negligence is no defence to deceit (*Standard Chartered Bank v Pakistan National Shipping* (2002)).
- Mitigation still applies: refusing a reasonable offer can break the chain of causation (*Downs v Chappell* (1997)).
- The summary: the court looks at all the losses flowing from the deceit, without the usual contractual foreseeability limit.

**(b) Negligent misstatement at common law.**
- Based on *Hedley Byrne & Co v Heller & Partners* [1964] AC 465. It needs a special relationship or assumption of responsibility (see *Caparo*, *Henderson v Merrett*, *Steel v NRAM* [2018] UKSC 13) and reliance.
- It applies to pre-contractual statements between contracting parties (*Esso v Mardon*).
- The *claimant* must prove negligence. Unlike s 2(1), no contract with the representor is needed, which is useful where a third party made the statement.
- Remoteness is reasonable foreseeability, and damages may be reduced for contributory negligence (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945).
- The discussion notes that this action covers opinions as well as statements of fact or law.

**(c) Misrepresentation Act 1967, s 2(1).**
- The text: where a person has contracted after a misrepresentation by *another party to the contract* and suffered loss, then if the maker "would be liable to damages … had the misrepresentation been made fraudulently, that person shall be so liable notwithstanding that the misrepresentation was not made fraudulently, unless he proves that he had reasonable ground to believe and did believe up to the time the contract was made that the facts represented were true."
- It has been called a "statutory tort" (*First Tower Trustees v CDS (Superstores International)* [2018] EWCA Civ 1396).
- **Reversed burden**: once a misrepresentation is shown, negligence is presumed and the *defendant* must prove both honest belief and reasonable grounds. This is hard to do. In *Howard Marine & Dredging Co v A Ogden & Sons* [1978] QB 574, a barge owner's representative relied on the Lloyd's Register figure for carrying capacity when the correct figure was in head-office files, and the owners were liable.
- **Measure, the "fiction of fraud"**: *Royscot Trust Ltd v Rogerson* [1991] 2 QB 297 held that "so liable" means liable as if fraudulent, so the *Doyle v Olby* direct-consequence rule applies regardless of foreseeability. There, a dealer innocently misstated the price and deposit to a finance company. The CA awarded £3,625.24 (the sum advanced minus the hirer's payments). The hirer's dishonest sale was foreseeable, so it did not break the chain of causation. See also *Naughton v O'Callaghan* [1990] 3 All ER 191: a colt with a misdescribed pedigree fell in value after racing, and damages ran to the date of judgment.
- **Contributory negligence**: available where a s 2(1) claim is run alongside a concurrent *Hedley Byrne* claim (*Gran Gelato Ltd v Richcliff* [1992] Ch 560: Sir Donald Nicholls V-C said s 2(1) liability is "essentially founded on negligence"). It is arguable whether it applies to a claim brought under s 2(1) alone.
- **Limits**: s 2(1) applies only where the representor is the other contracting party. A third party's statement must be sued on in tort.
- **Consumers**: under s 2(4) (inserted by SI 2014/870), a consumer with a right to redress under Part 4A of the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 cannot recover s 2 damages for the same conduct (Concentrate; McKendrick).

**(d) Misrepresentation Act 1967, s 2(2): damages in lieu of rescission.**
- Where the misrepresentation was not fraudulent and the representee would be entitled to rescind, the court "may declare the contract subsisting and award damages in lieu of rescission". It does so if equitable, having regard to:
  - the nature of the misrepresentation;
  - the loss that would be caused if the contract were upheld;
  - the loss that rescission would cause the other party.
- Section 2(3): damages can be awarded under s 2(2) whether or not the representor is liable under s 2(1), but a s 2(2) award is taken into account under s 2(1).
- It is a *discretion*, not a right. It needs jurisdiction to rescind at the relevant date, so if rescission is barred there are no s 2(2) damages (*Salt v Stratstone*).
- *William Sindall plc v Cambridgeshire CC* [1994] 1 WLR 1016 (obiter): an £18,000 sewer problem on land worth about £5m. Rescission would have shifted a market fall onto the council, so damages in lieu would have been awarded. Section 2(2) damages are narrower than s 2(1) damages and would not cover the fall in market value.
- In *SK Shipping* (2020), Foxton J allowed some consequential losses. The CA (2022) expressly did not endorse that approach.
- Chitty suggests a *SAAMCO*-type limit: the contract price minus the true value, ignoring general market falls (McKendrick).

### 7. Excluding liability for misrepresentation
- **Fraud cannot be excluded** (*S Pearson & Son Ltd v Dublin Corporation* [1907] AC 351).
- Whether an agent's fraud in negotiating the contract can be excluded remains open. Any such exclusion would need "clear and unmistakable terms" (Lord Bingham, *HIH Casualty v Chase Manhattan Bank* [2003] UKHL 6).
- **MA 1967 s 3** controls terms excluding or restricting liability or remedies for pre-contractual misrepresentation:
  - in the 1967 text in official-sources, such a term was of no effect unless the court allowed reliance on it as "fair and reasonable";
  - in the amended version quoted by McKendrick, it must satisfy the UCTA 1977 s 11(1) reasonableness test, and the burden lies on the party relying on it;
  - under the amended s 3(2), consumer contracts are governed instead by CRA 2015 s 62.
- A clause excluding liability for "any representation" may be read as covering fraud and so be unreasonable (*Thomas Witter v TBP*). *Zanzibar v British Aerospace* [2000] held that such words did not cover fraud.
- **Non-reliance and "basis" clauses**: in *First Tower Trustees v CDS* [2018] EWCA Civ 1396, the CA held that a clause stating that the lease was not entered into in reliance on any representation is subject to s 3. Substance matters, not form (Lewison LJ, Leggatt LJ, drawing on Bridge LJ in *Cremdean Properties v Nash* (1977)). Some room may remain after *Taberna* [2016] EWCA Civ 1262, which said s 3 concerns excluding liability "after the event", not whether a representation was made at all. See `exemption-clauses-and-unfair-terms.md`.

## Key authorities
- *Bisset v Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177 (PC): a vendor's statement that New Zealand land could carry 2,000 sheep. Both sides knew the land had never been farmed for sheep, so the statement was honest opinion and not actionable. This is the leading case on opinion, and also hints that an opinion no reasonable person with the maker's knowledge could hold may not be protected.
- *Smith v Land and House Property Corp* (1884) 28 Ch D 7 (CA): a "most desirable tenant" who was in arrears. An opinion from the person who knows the facts best implies that facts exist to justify it.
- *Esso Petroleum v Mardon* [1976] QB 801 (CA): an expert's forecast of petrol throughput gave rise to liability in negligent misstatement and as a collateral warranty.
- *Edgington v Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459 (CA): directors raised loans claiming they would improve buildings, when they really intended to pay off debts. A misstatement of intention is a misstatement of fact, and a misrepresentation need only be one of several inducing causes.
- *Dimmock v Hallett* (1866): a half-truth about tenancies. Partial disclosure can be a misrepresentation.
- *With v O'Flanagan* [1936] Ch 575 (CA): a medical practice's takings collapsed between the statement and the contract. A continuing representation must be corrected.
- *Spice Girls v Aprilia* [2002] EWCA Civ 15: representation by conduct. McKendrick says it shows the strain of having no duty of disclosure or good faith.
- *Redgrave v Hurd* (1881) 20 Ch D 1 (CA): failing to check the accounts does not defeat reliance, and rescission is available for innocent misrepresentation.
- *Attwood v Small* (1838): reliance on one's own experts negates inducement.
- *Horsfall v Thomas* (1862): a misrepresentation the claimant never noticed cannot induce.
- *Derry v Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337 (HL): the definition of fraud.
- *Hedley Byrne v Heller* [1964] AC 465 (HL): liability for negligent misstatement.
- *Doyle v Olby* [1969] 2 QB 158; *Smith New Court v Scrimgeour Vickers* [1997] AC 254 (HL); *East v Maurer* [1991] 1 WLR 461 (CA): the deceit measure.
- *Howard Marine v Ogden* [1978] QB 574 (CA): how hard it is to discharge the s 2(1) burden.
- *Royscot Trust v Rogerson* [1991] 2 QB 297 (CA): the fiction of fraud governs the measure under s 2(1).
- *Gran Gelato v Richcliff* [1992] Ch 560: contributory negligence and s 2(1).
- *William Sindall v Cambridgeshire CC* [1994] 1 WLR 1016 (CA): the s 2(2) discretion and measure.
- *Car and Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525 (CA): rescission without communication where the rogue has absconded.
- *Long v Lloyd* [1958]: affirmation.
- *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] 2 KB 86 and *Salt v Stratstone* [2015] EWCA Civ 745: lapse of time.
- *Clarke v Dickson* (1858) and *Erlanger v New Sombrero* (1878): restitutio in integrum.
- *Whittington v Seale-Hayne* (1900): the indemnity.
- *First Tower Trustees v CDS* [2018] EWCA Civ 1396: non-reliance clauses fall under s 3.
- **Misrepresentation Act 1967**:
  - s 1: removes two bars to rescission (the misrepresentation having become a term; the contract having been performed);
  - s 2(1): damages for non-fraudulent misrepresentation, with a reversed burden;
  - s 2(2): damages in lieu of rescission;
  - s 2(3): the two awards interact;
  - s 2(4): consumers with CPUTR redress are excluded;
  - s 3: exclusion clauses must be reasonable.

## Debate and critique
- **The fiction of fraud.** *Royscot* treats "the foolish but honest man as if he were dishonest" (Hooley, "Damages and the Misrepresentation Act 1967" (1991) 107 LQR 547). In *Smith New Court*, Lord Steyn noted "trenchant academic criticism" but expressed no concluded view. Atiyah and Treitel (1967) 30 MLR 369 first thought the deceit measure applied. Treitel later preferred foreseeability, as Chitty and Furmston did, and *Royscot* rejected those views on the "plain words" of the section. Poole and Devenney [2007] JBL 269 argue the fiction should go only to *liability* and that remoteness under s 2(1) should match *Hedley Byrne*. They identify three faults in *Royscot*:
  1. it fails to distinguish fraud from negligence;
  2. it creates an artificial gap between negligent misstatement and negligent misrepresentation;
  3. it rests on a very literal reading of the section.

  *Gran Gelato* (liability "essentially founded on negligence") pulls the other way. *Hodgson v Creation Consumer Finance* [2021] EWHC 2167 (Comm) notes that the loss in *Royscot* was in fact foreseeable, so its foreseeability remarks are strictly obiter. The discussion records that courts seem uneasy with the doctrine; it cites *Pankhania v Hackney LBC*, though the textbooks cite *Pankhania* for misrepresentations of law.
- **Should there be a duty of disclosure?** English law refuses one, but recognises so many exceptions (conduct, half-truths, continuing representations, concealment) that, as McKendrick shows through *Spice Girls*, courts often have to construct a positive representation in order to reach a fair result. The Concentrate Q&A asks whether England should follow those European systems that impose a pre-contractual duty of good faith. The CPUTRs 2008 already regulate misleading omissions to some extent. The efficiency argument for non-disclosure (parties must be free to profit from their own information) is the counterpoint. See the good faith material in the theory note.
- **Rescission for wholly innocent misrepresentation.** *Redgrave* allows a party to escape a contract even though the representor was blameless when speaking, because Lord Esher said one may not keep a benefit from a statement one "now" admits is false. McKendrick contrasts *Hart v O'Connor* [1985] AC 1000, which focused on what the defendant knew at the time of contracting. He suggests s 2(2) now brings English law close to a sensible middle position.
- **Is rescission a self-help remedy?** O'Sullivan, "Rescission as a Self-Help Remedy: A Critical Analysis" [2000] CLJ 509. McKendrick argues the decision belongs to the party and that requiring a court order is unwarranted. *Islington v UCKAC* and *SK Shipping* show the point is unsettled.
- **Partial rescission and restitution.** Meikle (2003) 19 JCL 40. *Halpern* suggests restitutio in integrum is becoming more flexible.
- **Inducement and the "but for" test.** Venkatesan (2021) 137 LQR 503; McKendrick defends the but-for test at least for rescission; *Rembrandt* has dicta both ways.
- **Choosing misrepresentation or breach.** The Concentrate Q&A argues that a claimant will often do better in misrepresentation because:
  - rescission does not depend on classifying the term (condition, innominate term or warranty);
  - s 2(1) reverses the burden of proof;
  - the deceit measure ignores foreseeability (contrast *Hadley v Baxendale*);
  - non-pecuniary loss can be recovered;
  - *East v Maurer* gives something close to lost profits.

  Against this, contract damages protect the expectation interest (*Robinson v Harman*), which can be larger when the claimant made a good bargain.
- **Other reading**: Trukhtanov (2009) 125 LQR 648 on acknowledgments of non-reliance as a defence, and Brown and Chandler [1992] LMCLQ 40 on deceit and s 2(1). Both are listed only.
- **Consumer reform.** The Law Commission (LCCP 199 (2011); Law Com No 332 (2012)) proposed tiered remedies: unwinding the contract and a discount, then damages. These became the 2014 "rights to redress" in the CPUTRs (unwind within 90 days, discount, damages with a due diligence defence). They cover misleading *actions* only, not omissions. The Concentrate guide noted that a Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Bill would repeal the CPUTRs and re-enact these rights. The 2024 Act appears in official-sources, but its provisions are not discussed in these materials.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify each statement separately.** Several statements may have a cumulative effect (*Loreley Financing v Credit Suisse* (2023)).
2. **Term or representation?** Consider:
   - written-contract silence or an entire agreement clause;
   - the relative expertise of the parties (*Oscar Chess v Williams*; *Dick Bentley v Harold Smith*);
   - importance to the claimant (*Bannerman v White*);
   - whether the claimant was invited to verify (*Ecay v Godfrey*).

   If the statement is a term, deal briefly with breach remedies too (MA 1967 s 1(a)).
3. **Is it false?** Watch for statements that are literally true but are half-truths, and for statements that became false before signing.
4. **Is it a statement of fact or law?** For opinions, ask who knew the facts best and whether the maker was an expert (*Bisset*, *Smith v Land*, *Esso*). For intentions, ask whether the stated intention was really held at the time (*Edgington*). Puffs such as "a steal" or "most trendy in town" are probably not actionable.
5. **Silence?** Go through the five exceptions. Look for concealment, such as painting over damp or keeping a ship afloat.
6. **Addressed to the claimant?** Consider statements made via an agent or an advertisement (*Peek v Gurney*; *Pilmore v Hood*).
7. **Inducement?** Ask:
   - whether the claimant knew of the statement;
   - whether they relied on their own survey or expert (*Attwood*);
   - whether failing to check the accounts matters (*Redgrave*: no, for reliance);
   - whether it was material to a reasonable person (presumption);
   - whether it was corrected in time (*Peekay*).
8. **Classify the misrepresentation.** Look for hints about the representor's state of mind, such as a deliberate lie, a "quick glance" at the accounts, or careful checking of a reliable source.
9. **Rescission first.** Is any bar present?
   - affirmation, such as continuing to use goods after discovering the problem (*Long v Lloyd*);
   - delay (*Leaf*, *Salt*);
   - the business or goods having been altered or used up;
   - a third-party purchaser (*Caldwell* on timing).
10. **Then damages.** Usually the best claim is s 2(1) (a contract with the representor, reversed burden, the *Royscot* measure). Use *Hedley Byrne* if the maker is a third party, and deceit if fraud is clear and contributory negligence would otherwise reduce the award.
11. **Measure.** Under the tort measure, consider:
    - the price paid minus the true value;
    - consequential and wasted expenditure (*Naughton*);
    - a "lock-in" loss down to the date of sale (*Smith New Court*);
    - lost opportunity profits (*East v Maurer*);
    - distress (*Archer v Brown*);
    - mitigation;
    - contributory negligence (*Gran Gelato*; not available for fraud).
12. **Innocent misrepresentation.** Rescission, or s 2(2) damages in lieu if rescission is still available. If rescission is barred, the claimant gets nothing beyond an indemnity.
13. **Exclusion clause?** Fraud cannot be excluded. Non-reliance or entire agreement wording is still subject to s 3 and reasonableness (*First Tower*). In a consumer case, apply the CRA 2015 and the CPUTR redress rules (s 2(4)).

**Typical traps**:
- assuming silence is never actionable;
- treating the opportunity to inspect as defeating reliance;
- saying that s 2(1) "applies to fraud" (it applies to non-fraudulent misrepresentations, measured *as if* fraudulent);
- forgetting that s 2(2) needs rescission still to be available;
- forgetting that s 2(1) needs a contract between the representor and the representee;
- ignoring third-party purchasers.
