# Redgrave v Hurd (1881) 20 Ch D 1 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal, 1881. Authority that a person who has been **induced by a material misrepresentation** to contract may rescind even though they had the means to discover the truth and did not use them, and that rescission is available for **innocent** misrepresentation.

## Facts
Redgrave, a solicitor, wanted to sell his practice (together with his house) and told Hurd, the prospective buyer, that the practice brought in about £300 a year. The papers he showed Hurd indicated only about £200 a year, and he said other office papers would make up the difference; those papers showed little or no business. Hurd did not inspect the papers before signing, and refused to complete once he looked at them. Redgrave sued for specific performance; Hurd counterclaimed for rescission. At first instance Fry J found for Redgrave, reasoning that Hurd, having not inspected the papers, could not have relied on the statement.

## Held
- The Court of Appeal reversed Fry J.
- Fraud was not established (the knowledge of falsity needed for deceit was not made out), so damages for fraud were not available.
- Hurd was nevertheless entitled to **rescission** for innocent misrepresentation: a representee who has relied on a material statement is not defeated by having had the opportunity to check it. There is no duty to inspect.
- Where a material statement is likely to induce a contract, inducement is inferred and it is for the representor to show otherwise.

## Why it matters
- Core authority on **reliance/inducement**: opportunity to verify is not a defence to rescission for non-fraudulent misrepresentation.
- Establishes that rescission is available without fraud, which later statute (Misrepresentation Act 1967) builds on.
- Often cited with *Edgington v Fitzmaurice* for the proposition that the statement need only be one cause of the claimant's decision.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was the false statement a material statement of fact that would naturally induce the contract?
2. If so, expect inducement to be inferred; the other party's argument that the claimant could have checked will usually fail where fraud is not in issue.
3. Consider the bars to rescission (see *Leaf v International Galleries*, *Clarke v Dickson*) and statutory remedies in misrepresentation.md.
