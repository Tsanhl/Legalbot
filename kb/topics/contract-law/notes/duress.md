# Duress

## Overview
Duress is a common law vitiating factor (English law). It applies where one party gets the other to enter a contract by using illegitimate pressure. Contracting always involves some pressure, so the hard question is where the law should draw the line between ordinary hard bargaining and pressure it will not accept. A contract made under duress is **voidable**: the victim may rescind it, subject to the usual bars. Duress sits next to the equitable doctrine of undue influence (see `undue-influence.md`). The two share a philosophy and work in similar ways but are distinct. In the commercial setting, economic duress also controls variations of existing contracts, which links it closely to consideration (*Williams v Roffey*; see `consideration.md`).

## The law

### 1. Basis of the doctrine
- McKendrick (*Contract Law: Text, Cases and Materials*) places the basis of duress in two things together: the victim's lack of consent, and the wrongful or illegitimate nature of the threats used to induce entry into the contract. Both are present in all three types of duress.
- Duress is a form of **procedural** unfairness. The fact that the terms are unfair is neither necessary nor sufficient. A robber who forces me at gunpoint to sell my house at a fair price still commits duress. A seller forced by circumstances to sell cheaply has no claim. Authority for this is *The Law Debenture Trust Corporation plc v Ukraine* [2023] UKSC 11, [182].
- The older "coercion of the will" language (*Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614: pressure amounting to "a coercion of will that vitiates consent") has been criticised. Lord Goff in *The Evia Luck* doubted that it helps to talk of the will being coerced, citing criticism by Beatson, Atiyah and Birks. Lord Scarman in *The Universe Sentinel* [1983] 1 AC 366 said the classic case of duress is not lack of will to submit but the victim's **intentional submission**, made because they realise they have no other practical choice.

### 2. The elements
the materials builds the doctrine around Dyson J's formulation in *DSND Subsea Ltd v Petroleum Geo-Services ASA* [2000] BLR 530 [131]. There must be pressure (a) whose practical effect is compulsion on, or lack of practical choice for, the victim, (b) which is illegitimate, and (c) which is a significant cause inducing the claimant to enter the contract.

The current authoritative statement is Lord Burrows in *Pakistan International Airline Corporation v Times Travel (UK) Ltd* [2021] UKSC 40, [2023] AC 101, [78]–[79], on a point where the whole court agreed:
1. **An illegitimate threat or pressure** exerted by the defendant;
2. **Causation**: that pressure caused the claimant to enter the contract;
3. **For economic duress only**: the claimant had **no reasonable alternative** to giving in (*DSND*; *Borrelli v Ting* [2010] UKPC 21, [35]).

In problem answers, structure your analysis as follows:

**(a) Identify the pressure and its type.** Whether there was pressure is a question of fact. Say what kind of pressure it was and explain why the facts amount to pressure. There are three categories.
- **Duress of the person**: threats of violence to the claimant (*Barton v Armstrong* [1976] AC 104; *Sheikh Tahnoon Bin Saeed Bin Shakhboot Al Nehayan v Kent* [2018] EWHC 333 (Comm)). The threat can be aimed at a family member or someone in a contractual relationship with the claimant, such as an employee. A threat by the claimant to kill himself also counts. In the case of a state, threats to the safety of its citizens or armed forces count too (*Law Debenture v Ukraine*, [176]–[179]). The common element is a threat to physical well-being. A threat of financial ruin is economic duress, not duress of the person (*Holyoake v Candy* [2017] EWHC 3397 (Ch), [233]).
- **Duress of goods**: threats to detain or damage property. *Skeate v Beale* (1841) 11 Ad & E 983 held that wrongful detention of goods could not vitiate an agreement. It is no longer good law: Lord Goff in *Dimskal Shipping v ITWF (The Evia Luck)* [1992] 2 AC 152. A threat to invade a country, which would almost inevitably damage property, could amount to duress of goods (*Law Debenture v Ukraine*, [183]).
- **Economic duress**: illegitimate use of economic power, most often a threatened breach of contract. It was first formally recognised by Kerr J in *Occidental Worldwide Investment Corp v Skibs A/S Avanti (The Siboen and The Sibotre)* [1976] 1 Lloyd's Rep 293. It was developed in *North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai (The Atlantic Baron)* [1979] QB 705, *Pao On*, *Atlas Express v Kafco* [1989] QB 833 and *The Evia Luck*.

**(b) Is the pressure illegitimate?** Lord Hoffmann in *R v Attorney-General for England and Wales* [2003] UKPC 22, [16], following Lord Scarman in *The Universe Sentinel*, said legitimacy is judged from two angles: the nature of the pressure and the nature of the demand it supports. Generally, a threat of any unlawful action is illegitimate, but a lawful threat is not necessarily legitimate. The blackmail analogy is Lord Atkin's in *Thorne v Motor Trade Association*: the blackmailer must justify not the threat but the demand.
- *Threats of crimes or torts* are readily treated as illegitimate (*The Universe Sentinel*).
- *Threatened breach of contract.* Such a threat "will generally be regarded as illegitimate, particularly where the defendant must know that it would be in breach of contract if the threat were implemented" (Christopher Clarke J in *Kolmar Group AG v Traxpo Enterprises* [2010] EWHC 113 (Comm), [92]). The open question is the party who genuinely but wrongly believes it is entitled to make the demand. McKendrick suggests good faith should not normally prevent a finding of illegitimacy, because English law does not usually distinguish good-faith from bad-faith breaches (and see *The Atlantic Baron*).
- *Dyson J's factors* (*DSND*) are: (1) whether there was an actual or threatened breach of contract; (2) whether the party exerting pressure acted in good or bad faith; (3) whether the victim had any realistic practical alternative; (4) whether the victim protested at the time; (5) whether the victim affirmed and sought to rely on the contract. Illegitimate pressure must be distinguished from "the rough and tumble of the pressures of normal commercial bargaining".
- *Lawful act duress* is covered in section 3 below.

**(c) Compulsion: no reasonable practical alternative (economic duress).** The victim must have had "no realistic choice" or "no reasonable practicable alternative" (*DSND*; *Atlas v Kafco*; *The Evia Luck*). Kerr LJ in *B & S Contracts and Design v Victor Green Publications* [1984] ICR 419 said a threat to break a contract will only be duress if the consequences of refusing would be serious and immediate, so that no reasonable alternative was open, "such as by legal redress, obtaining an injunction, etc". This requirement matters most where the claimant *had* a reasonable alternative, such as going to court, terminating and buying elsewhere, or arbitration with security, and chose not to take it. Lord Burrows (*Times Travel*, [79]) said this element applies to economic duress but apparently not to other forms. Citing *Astley v Reynolds* (1731), the Supreme Court in *Law Debenture v Ukraine*, [144], confirmed that it is not a requirement for duress by threats to property.

**(d) Causation.** The pressure must have induced the contract (*Huyton SA v Peter Cremer* [1999] 1 Lloyd's Rep 620). The required test **differs by type of duress**:
- *Duress of the person*: it is enough that the threat was **"a" reason**, even if the claimant might well have entered the contract anyway. Once threats are shown, the onus is on the threatener to prove they "contributed nothing" to the decision (*Barton v Armstrong*, by analogy with fraudulent misrepresentation; affirmed in *Law Debenture v Ukraine*, [143]).
- *Economic duress*: the pressure must be a **"significant cause"** (Lord Goff, *The Evia Luck*). The case law treats this as the ordinary **"but for"** test (*Kolmar*, [92]). In *Al Nehayan v Kent* the judge stated the split clearly: for threats of violence "a" reason suffices, but for economic duress the claimant generally must show they would not have contracted "but for" the defendant's act. Mance J in *Huyton* warned that a simple but-for test "could lead too readily to relief being granted", though he relied on the other elements, such as alternative redress, to keep the doctrine in check.
- *Duress of goods*: the test is undecided. The Supreme Court in *Law Debenture* seemed at times to group it with duress of the person. McKendrick argues the claimant should have to prove but-for causation.
- The Supreme Court explained the difference this way: duress of the person and of goods have "always been considered illegitimate", whereas economic duress is illegitimate "only in limited circumstances" (*Law Debenture*, [148]). McKendrick notes a possible "sliding scale" approach, under which the causal threshold falls as the illegitimacy rises, but rejects it as too uncertain.

**(e) Remedy: rescission, unless barred.** Duress makes the contract **voidable, not void**. *Barton* accepted counsel's submission that the deed was "void", but this conflicts with later authority. *The Universe Sentinel* and *The Evia Luck* implicitly reject the idea that consent is absent, and *The Atlantic Baron* treated the contract as voidable. The bars to rescission are the same as for misrepresentation (see `misrepresentation.md`):
- **Impossibility** of restitution (*Clarke v Dickson* (1858) 120 ER 463);
- **Affirmation** (*Peyman v Lanjani* [1984] 3 All ER 70). In duress, the danger is affirming once the pressure has lifted. In *The Atlantic Baron* the owners paid the extra 10% without protest and waited until July 1975 to claim although the ship was delivered in November 1974 (the Concentrate guide puts the gap at nine months), so they were held to have affirmed. Kerr J in *The Siboen* stressed asking whether the victim protested at the time or shortly afterwards;
- **Lapse of time** (*Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] 2 KB 86);
- **Third-party rights** (*Car and Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525).

Where money was paid under duress, it can be recovered once the agreement is avoided (*Atlantic Baron*; *B & S Contracts*, where the payer recovered by deducting the sum from the contract price).

### 3. Lawful act duress (*Times Travel*)
- **Facts.** PIAC was the only airline offering direct UK–Pakistan flights. Times Travel was a small travel agency that depended almost entirely on selling those tickets. PIAC lawfully gave notice ending its agency contracts and lawfully cut Times Travel's ticket allocation from 300 to 60. It then offered new contracts on condition that agents waived existing claims for unpaid commission. Times Travel signed, because otherwise it would almost certainly have gone out of business. It later sought to avoid the agreement.
- **Unanimous holdings.**
  - Lawful act duress **exists** in English law. Lord Burrows gave three reasons: the leading cases use the word "illegitimate", not "unlawful"; the crime of blackmail (Theft Act 1968 s 21) covers threats of lawful action; and older cases set aside contracts procured by threats to prosecute (*Williams v Bayley* (1866) LR 1 HL 200; *Mutual Finance v John Wetton* [1937] 2 KB 389; *Kaufman v Gerson* [1904] 1 KB 591). Those cases were decided as "undue influence" but are better seen today as duress.
  - Lawful act duress must be kept within very narrow limits, because English commercial law values certainty. Because the threat is lawful, the focus is on the **demand**, not the threat. A demand motivated by commercial self-interest is in general justified. A multi-factor approach is inappropriate, and so is reliance on a general principle of good faith. There is no doctrine of inequality of bargaining power (see also *National Westminster Bank v Morgan* [1985] AC 686).
- **Majority (Lord Hodge, with Lords Reed, Lloyd-Jones and Kitchin).** Lawful act duress draws on equity's treatment of morally reprehensible conduct (undue influence and unconscionable bargains). So far the courts have recognised it in two circumstances:
  1. the defendant uses knowledge of criminal activity by the claimant or a close family member to obtain a personal benefit, by an express or implied threat to report it or prosecute; and
  2. the defendant, having exposed itself to a civil claim by the claimant, **deliberately manoeuvres the claimant into a position of vulnerability by illegitimate means** and so forces the claimant to waive the claim. Examples are *Borrelli v Ting* (opposition to a scheme backed by forgery and false evidence) and *Progress Bulk Carriers v Tube City IMS (The Cenk Kaptaboglu, also called The Cenk K)* [2012] EWHC 273 (Comm). In the latter, a shipowner in repudiatory breach first gave assurances of compensation, then at a late stage demanded a waiver as the price of a substitute vessel.

  The boundaries "are not fixed", but courts should approach any extension with caution, especially between commercial parties. PIAC's conduct was "hard-nosed" exploitation of a monopoly, not reprehensible means, and there was no finding of bad faith. So there was no duress.
- **Lord Burrows (concurring in the result, dissenting on reasoning).** In this context, a necessary requirement is a **"bad faith demand"**: the threatening party does not genuinely believe it is owed what it claims, or does not genuinely believe it has a defence to the claim being waived. A demand for a waiver is then illegitimate if the threatener deliberately created or increased the victim's vulnerability **and** the bad faith demand requirement is met. The claim failed because Times Travel did not prove that PIAC lacked a genuine belief in its entitlement.
- **Lord Hodge's objections.** He gave four. A demand for a waiver is no different in principle from a demand for money as the price of contracting, which Burrows accepts is not duress. Burrows's line would increase uncertainty. Bad faith matters to the *context* of the demand as well as its content. And the mere assertion of bargaining power, such as cutting the ticket allocation, cannot be enough without something more.
- **Later confirmation.** *Law Debenture v Ukraine* affirmed the majority approach. It said it was "difficult to envisage circumstances" in which exercising pre-existing contractual rights could be duress ([155]). See also *Heritage Travel and Tourism v Windhorst* [2021] EWHC 2380 (Comm): lawful act duress in commercial negotiation will be rare.
- **Where the two views differ: *CTN Cash and Carry v Gallaher* [1994] 4 All ER 714 (CA).** Gallaher mis-delivered cigarettes, which were then stolen. Gallaher believed in good faith, though wrongly, that the goods were at CTN's risk, and threatened to withdraw credit facilities it was not obliged to give unless CTN paid. CTN paid. Its duress claim failed: the demand was made in good faith, and extending duress would create commercial uncertainty. On hypothetical facts where Gallaher knew it had no claim, Lord Burrows would find duress, but Lord Hodge would not, absent reprehensible manoeuvring or an unconscionable transaction.

## Key authorities
- *Barton v Armstrong* [1976] AC 104 (PC): Armstrong threatened to have Barton killed during a boardroom struggle, and Barton executed a deed buying Armstrong out. The threats need only be "a" reason, not the predominant or clinching one, and the onus is on the threatener to show they contributed nothing. This is the leading case on duress of the person and its relaxed causation test.
- *Skeate v Beale* (1841): wrongful detention of goods was held not to be duress. It has since been discarded (*The Evia Luck*).
- *Occidental v Skibs (The Siboen and The Sibotre)* [1976] 1 Lloyd's Rep 293: first formal recognition of economic duress. Kerr J stressed protest at the time.
- *North Ocean Shipping v Hyundai (The Atlantic Baron)* [1979] QB 705: a shipbuilder demanded 10% more after dollar devaluation, with no contractual basis, refused arbitration and threatened to stop work. The owners needed delivery for a lucrative charter and paid "without prejudice". Held: there was consideration for the promise, but a threatened breach can be economic duress making the contract voidable, but the owners had affirmed by delay and paying without protest. The case is authority on threatened breach as duress and on the affirmation trap.
- *Pao On v Lau Yiu Long* [1980] AC 614 (PC): an early statement of illegitimate pressure plus "coercion of will". There was no duress because the promise was a commercial decision.
- *Universe Tankships v ITWF (The Universe Sentinel)* [1983] 1 AC 366 (HL): a union "blacked" a ship until a payment was made to its welfare fund. This was illegitimate pressure not protected by trade dispute legislation. Lord Scarman set out the two aspects of illegitimacy (pressure and demand) and the "intentional submission" analysis.
- *B & S Contracts and Design v Victor Green Publications* [1984] ICR 419 (CA): a stand erector's workforce refused to work, and the customer paid £4,500 extra to avoid claims from exhibitors ("you have me over a barrel"). Held to be duress: the consequences were serious and immediate and there was no practical alternative.
- *Atlas Express v Kafco* [1989] QB 833: listed in the materials as a core economic duress case. The Concentrate Q&A stresses the *manner* of the pressure: renegotiation forced purely for the carrier's benefit, the threat relayed through an innocent third party and timed for the threatener's absence, and the moment chosen when the victim realised only the threatener could meet its needs.
- *Williams v Roffey Bros* [1991] 1 QB 1: there was no duress because the main contractor itself offered the extra payment. It shows that duress is now the preferred control on contract modifications (see `consideration.md`).
- *Dimskal Shipping v ITWF (The Evia Luck)* [1992] 2 AC 152 (HL): economic pressure can be duress if illegitimate and a "significant cause". Lord Goff doubted the "coerced will" theory and treated *Skeate v Beale* as no longer good law.
- *CTN Cash and Carry v Gallaher* [1994] 4 All ER 714 (CA): a good-faith lawful threat to withdraw credit was not duress (facts above).
- *Huyton SA v Peter Cremer* [1999] 1 Lloyd's Rep 620: on inducement and causation. Mance J warned that a bare but-for test is too generous.
- *DSND Subsea v Petroleum Geo-Services* [2000] BLR 530: Dyson J's elements and five factors for illegitimacy. There was no duress, which McKendrick explains by the lack of contemporaneous evidence of causation.
- *Carillion Construction v Felix (UK)* [2001] BLR 1: cited as a threatened-breach duress case.
- *R v Attorney-General for England and Wales* [2003] UKPC 22: an SAS soldier signed a confidentiality contract. Lord Hoffmann set out the two-aspect test of legitimacy. Requiring the contract as a condition of continued SAS service was not illegitimate.
- *Kolmar Group v Traxpo* [2010] EWHC 113 (Comm): a threatened breach is "generally" illegitimate, "particularly" if the defendant knows it would be a breach. A but-for approach to causation.
- *Borrelli v Ting* [2010] UKPC 21: forgery and false evidence used to block liquidators left them with no reasonable alternative but to settle. Duress.
- *Progress Bulk Carriers v Tube City (The Cenk Kaptaboglu, also called The Cenk K)* [2012] EWHC 273 (Comm): illegitimate pressure through conduct not in itself unlawful. A waiver demanded as the price of a substitute vessel was voidable.
- *Al Nehayan v Kent* [2018] EWHC 333 (Comm): causation differs by type of duress. the materials attributes the passage to "Leggat LJ"; the citation is a High Court (Comm) decision, so check the judge's title in the report.
- *Pakistan International Airline Corp v Times Travel (UK) Ltd* [2021] UKSC 40, [2023] AC 101: lawful act duress exists but is narrow. Majority (Lord Hodge): two categories of reprehensible conduct. Lord Burrows: the "bad faith demand" requirement. the materials cites the earlier Court of Appeal decision ([2019] EWCA Civ 828); the Supreme Court decision is now the leading authority.
- *The Law Debenture Trust Corporation plc v Ukraine* [2023] UKSC 11: threats to a state's citizens or armed forces can be duress of the person. Threats of invasion can be duress of goods. Confirms the causation split and the *Times Travel* majority.

## Debate and critique
- **What is the doctrine for?** The "overborne will" theory is largely discredited (Lord Goff in *The Evia Luck*, endorsing criticism by Atiyah (1982) 98 LQR 197, Birks [1990] LMCLQ 342 and Beatson). The victim of duress knows exactly what they are doing; their choice is real but constrained. Duress therefore rests on the combination of impaired consent and the wrongful or illegitimate nature of the pressure (McKendrick). Optional reading on the theory: Stephen Smith, "Contracting Under Pressure: A Theory of Duress" (1997) 56 CLJ 343. McKendrick also lists Beatson ("Duress, Restitution and Contract Modification"), Birks ("The Travails of Duress") and his own "The Further Travails of Duress".
- **Illegitimacy and good faith.** Should a good-faith threatened breach be illegitimate? *The Atlantic Baron* did not ask about bad faith, and McKendrick argues good faith should rarely matter because a breach is a breach. *DSND* and *CTN* treat good faith as relevant. After *Times Travel*, the relevance of good or bad faith is unclear: Lord Burrows makes bad faith central to one category of lawful act duress, while Lord Hodge treats it as part of a wider assessment of reprehensible conduct.
- **Should lawful act duress exist at all?** Birks (quoted by Lord Burrows) warned that once lawful pressures count, the only basis for drawing lines is "social morality", which makes judges the arbiters of social evaluation. Some commentators argue a firm rule of non-existence would be more certain. Lawful act duress was rejected by the NSW Court of Appeal in *ANZ Banking Group v Karam* (2005). Virgo would allow restitution for lawful threats only under a stricter unconscionability test, but McKendrick argues that this simply moves the uncertainty elsewhere. McKendrick supports the Supreme Court's approach: openly recognising narrow exceptions is preferable, and lower courts will probably resist expanding them.
- **Hodge v Burrows on certainty.** Each claims his test is more certain. Burrows says "reprehensible" and "illegitimate means" are vague and invite uncertainty in commercial contracts, while a bad faith demand tied to an existing legal right offers a workable standard of dishonesty. Hodge says Burrows's line between a demand for a waiver and a demand for a price is unprincipled. The Q&A guide asks whether *Times Travel*'s focus on "highly reprehensible" behaviour has reduced the uncertainty caused by inconsistent judicial language.
- **No doctrine of inequality of bargaining power.** Lord Denning's attempt in *Lloyds Bank v Bundy* to unify duress, undue influence and unconscionable bargains under "inequality of bargaining power" was rejected in *National Westminster Bank v Morgan* [1985] AC 686. *Times Travel* (at [26]) confirmed that position. Collins (quoted in McKendrick ch 20) explains the classical position: freedom of contract permits scrutiny of the *procedure* (pressure, fraud, abuse of trust) but not of the substantive fairness of the bargain, so courts tend to frame interventions as procedural defects. In *Times Travel* a small agency facing a monopolist had no remedy, which shows how hard-edged that choice is.
- **Further optional reading.** The optional reading includes Orit Gan, "Contractual Duress and Relations of Power" (2013) 36 Harvard Journal of Law & Gender 171. The sources give only the title and citation, so its argument is not summarised here. Phang, "Whiter Economic Duress? Reflections on Two Recent Cases" (1990) 53 MLR 107 (title as printed in the handout) is also listed without discussion.
- **Duress v consideration as regulators of variations.** Some judges and authors explain *Stilk v Myrick* as a duress case decided under the label of consideration. McKendrick doubts this, since the ship was in port, but accepts the modern trend of making duress the main control on contract modifications (*The Alev*; *Roffey*; *The Atlantic Baron*'s finding of consideration but duress). He questions whether this development is desirable.
- **Should duress and undue influence merge?** In *Times Travel* (at [19]) Lord Hodge noted equity's influence on common law duress. Threat-based "undue influence" cases are now treated as duress (*Times Travel* [9], [90]; *Nature Resorts v First Citizens Bank* [2022] UKPC 10, [10]). The Concentrate guide poses merger as a key debate.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the transaction to be avoided.** It might be the original contract, a variation (for example, a promise to pay more) or a waiver or settlement. If it is a variation, first ask whether there was consideration (*Roffey* practical benefit). Then ask whether it was procured by duress; a variation can be supported by consideration and still be voidable for duress (*Atlantic Baron*).
2. **Was there a threat or pressure?** If the promisor volunteered the extra payment, there may be no threat at all (*Roffey*). Consider whether a bare statement of financial difficulty counts as pressure (the Concentrate guide flags this as a question to discuss).
3. **Classify the threat** as person, goods or economic. Classification affects causation and whether "no reasonable alternative" applies. A threat of financial ruin is economic (*Holyoake v Candy*).
4. **Illegitimacy.**
   - Unlawful threats (crime, tort, breach of contract) are generally illegitimate (*Kolmar*). Ask whether the threatener knew it had no right to the demand.
   - Lawful threats (to terminate lawfully, refuse to deal, call in a loan repayable on demand, withdraw discretionary credit) are almost never duress. Check whether the facts fit Lord Hodge's two categories (threats to report a crime; deliberate manoeuvring into vulnerability by reprehensible means to force a waiver). Also apply Lord Burrows's bad faith demand test and say whether the outcome differs.
   - Use the *DSND* factors: breach, good or bad faith, alternatives, protest, affirmation.
5. **No reasonable alternative (economic duress).** Could the victim have sued, sought an injunction, gone to arbitration or bought elsewhere in time? Look for timing facts, such as deadlines, a sub-contract or charter at stake, or customers waiting (*B & S Contracts*; *Atlantic Baron* and the Shell charter; *Atlas*).
6. **Causation.** For threats to the person, it is enough that the threat was "a" reason, and the burden then shifts. For economic duress, the claimant must show a significant or but-for cause. Look for evidence that the victim would have agreed anyway, such as a commercial decision taken for its own reasons (as in *Pao On*).
7. **Remedy and bars.** Rescission (with restitution of money paid). Check for affirmation, which is the classic trap. Did the victim protest at the time? Did they act promptly once the pressure was lifted, for example on delivery of the ship? Delay and continued performance without protest suggest affirmation (*Atlantic Baron*). Also check for third-party rights, impossibility of restitution and lapse of time.
8. **Alternatives.** Consider undue influence (if there is a relationship of trust), misrepresentation (for example, false statements made alongside the pressure), and in a consumer context the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008. The Q&A guide flags these regulations but tells candidates to ignore them in its questions.

**Common traps:**
- Saying duress makes a contract void (*Barton*'s wording). It makes it voidable.
- Applying the "a reason" test to economic duress.
- Forgetting the no-reasonable-alternative element.
- Treating hard commercial bargaining or a monopolist's lawful demands as duress (*Times Travel*).
- Ignoring affirmation.
