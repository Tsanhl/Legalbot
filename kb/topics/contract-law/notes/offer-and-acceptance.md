# Offer and Acceptance (Agreement)

## Overview
English law (England & Wales) normally finds the agreement at the core of a contract by locating an **offer** and a matching **acceptance**. Agreement is only one ingredient: certainty, intention to create legal relations, consideration and privity are also needed (see Links). Whether a statement is an offer, an acceptance, a counter-offer or a revocation, and when each takes effect, is judged **objectively**: the question is what the words and conduct would mean to a reasonable person, not what either party privately intended. This is one of the topics examiners set most often, nearly always as a problem question in which a series of messages has to be analysed in date order. Its essay side covers objectivity, the postal rule, silence, unilateral contracts and whether the offer/acceptance model fits real transactions.

## The law

### 1. The objective approach to intention
1. **General rule.** Intention is judged by the outward meaning of words and conduct. *Smith v Hughes* (1871) LR 6 QB 597 (Blackburn J): if a person behaves so that a reasonable person would believe he assents to the proposed terms, and the other party contracts on that belief, he is bound as if he had really intended to agree. Supporting statements:
   - an early (1478) dictum that the intent of a man cannot be tried, since even the Devil does not know it;
   - *Kennedy v Lee* (1817) (Lord Eldon): the court asks whether both parties assented to the proposition in their correspondence, not whether they meant the same thing;
   - *Storer v Manchester CC* [1974] 1 WLR 1403 (Lord Denning MR): look at what was said and done; a contract exists if to all outward appearance there is one;
   - *RTS Flexible Systems Ltd v Molkerei Alois Müller* [2010] UKSC 14 at [45] (Lord Clarke): whether a contract exists, and on what terms, depends on what was communicated by words or conduct, objectively assessed, not on subjective states of mind;
   - on interpretation, *Rainy Sky SA v Kookmin Bank* [2011] UKSC 50 at [14] and *A-G of Belize v Belize Telecom* [2009] UKPC 10 at [16].
2. **Why objectivity?** the materials (citing Ashby) says a subjective test would require courts to reconstruct two competing states of mind, and possibly add the judge's own. An objective test gives a "detached" view and makes outcomes more certain. Lord Steyn (extrajudicially, (1997) 113 LQR 433) says the objective theory promotes certainty and predictability. Stevens ((2020) 136 LQR 599) argues that the real justification is that agreements are acts of communication, so they carry an objective meaning, and that certainty and reliance are only incidental benefits.
3. **Subjective intention is generally irrelevant.** *Tamplin v James* (1880) 15 Ch D 215: a buyer who believed he was buying more land than the lot described was still held to the contract (specific performance). The same goes for signed documents: *L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394 (treated further under exemption clauses).
4. **Limited relevance of subjective intention** (lecture list):
   - (i) *Knowledge.* A party who knows, or ought to know, that the other's words do not express their real intention cannot hold them to those words: *Hartog v Colin & Shields* [1939] 3 All ER 566 (hare skins offered at a price per pound when negotiations had been on a price per piece).
   - (ii) *Ambiguous facts.* Even a reasonable observer cannot tell what was agreed: *Falck v Williams* [1900] AC 176; *Raffles v Wichelhaus* (1864) 2 H & C 906 (two ships named *Peerless*).
   - (iii) *Mistake as to terms*: *Smith v Hughes* (a case that is read in different ways).
5. **Consensus ad idem.** There must be a "meeting of minds", but this is assessed from external evidence: would the reasonable person say the parties had agreed? In *OT Africa Line v Vickers plc* [1996] 1 Lloyd's Rep 700, Mance J described *Raffles* as a case where offer and acceptance were identical in words but, objectively, referred to different things.

### 2. Bilateral and unilateral contracts
- **Bilateral**: a promise is exchanged for a promise, and both parties are bound from the exchange (e.g. a sale of a car).
- **Unilateral**: a promise is exchanged for an act. Only the promisor is bound, and the other side accepts by performing (e.g. a reward for returning a lost cat; "if you paint my kitchen I will pay you £200").
- Trap (Concentrate): an ordinary sale is not unilateral just because payment is an act. If the offer can be accepted by a promise to buy, it is bilateral.

### 3. Offer
1. **Definition.** An offer is an expression of willingness to contract on certain terms, made with the (actual or apparent) intention of being bound as soon as it is accepted. It may be made to one person, a group or the world (Treitel, cited in the materials). Concentrate's version is "a definite promise to be bound, without more, if the offeree agrees".
2. **Test.** Would a reasonable person think the offeror was promising to be bound? With no promise there is no offer. The offer must show a *present* willingness to be bound.
   - *Storer v Manchester CC*: the council's letter enclosing an agreement for sale ("if you will sign the agreement and return it … I will send you the agreement signed on behalf of the corporation") showed an intention to be bound once the tenant signed. A contract was formed, and the new council could not stop the sale.
   - *Gibson v Manchester CC* [1979] 1 WLR 294 (HL): "may be prepared to sell", with an invitation to "make formal application", was not an offer. The House of Lords reversed the majority of the Court of Appeal ([1978] 1 WLR 520).
   - Labels are not conclusive (*Evergreen Timber Frames Ltd v Harrington* (2021), EAT: a car described as a "gift" was a contractual offer on the facts). Where parties have been negotiating, the point at which a firm offer was made is assessed objectively (*Dana UK Axle Ltd v Freudenberg FST GmbH* (2021)).
3. **Invitation to treat.** This is a statement that shows willingness to *receive* offers. Answering an invitation to treat can produce, at most, an offer. Concentrate suggests asking which party the law thinks should *control* whether a contract is made.

#### 3.1 Recurring situations
| Situation | General rule | Exceptions / qualifications |
|---|---|---|
| **Advertisements** | Invitation to treat: *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 2 All ER 421, explained by the limited-stocks argument (Lord Herschell, *Grainger & Son v Gough* [1896] AC 325) | (1) A reward or unilateral advertisement that is certain enough is an offer: *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 (CA); also *Bowerman v ABTA* [1996] CLC 451 and *O'Brien v MGN* [2001] EWCA Civ 1279. (2) Wording such as "first come first served", "highest bidder" or a guaranteed sale can turn an advertisement into an offer: *Warwickshire CC v Johnson* [1993] 1 All ER 299; *Lefkowitz v Great Minneapolis Surplus Stores* (1957) 86 NW 2d 689 (a US case, persuasive only) |
| **Shop displays / shelves** | Invitation to treat: *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394 (flick knife in a shop window); *Pharmaceutical Society of GB v Boots Cash Chemists* [1953] 1 QB 401 (self-service shop). The customer offers at the till and the cashier accepts | Rationale: no reasonable shopper thinks they have bought an item just by putting it in a basket |
| **Retail websites** | Probably invitations to treat; no definitive authority. Reg 12 of the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 suggests the customer's order is the offer (Concentrate) | McKendrick notes that an electronic equivalent of *Carlill* or *Lefkowitz* is a real possibility |
| **Auctions** | A request for bids, or an advertisement that an auction will be held, is an invitation to treat: *Harris v Nickerson* (1873) LR 8 QB 286. Each bid is an offer. Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 57(2): the sale is complete at the fall of the hammer (or other customary manner), and until then a bidder may retract | "Without reserve": a collateral unilateral contract between the auctioneer and the highest bona fide bidder, separate from the main sale contract with the owner: *Warlow v Harrison* (1859) 1 E & E 309 (obiter); applied in *Barry v Davies* [2001] 1 WLR 1962 (auctioneer refused a £400 bid on machines said to be worth £28,000; about £26,000 damages, i.e. replacement cost minus the bid) |
| **Tenders** | A request for tenders is an invitation to treat and each tender is an offer, so the requester may accept any or none: *Spencer v Harding* (1870) LR 5 CP 561 | An express undertaking to accept the highest or lowest bid is a unilateral offer, accepted by submitting that bid (*Harvela Investments Ltd v Royal Trust Co of Canada* (HL, Lord Diplock)). An express or implied undertaking to *consider* conforming tenders binds the requester: *Blackpool & Fylde Aero Club v Blackpool BC* [1990] 1 WLR 1195 (implied obligation where a small number of parties were invited to tender under a set procedure). Lord Toulson in *Central Tenders Board v White* (2015) suggested this would not necessarily extend to a private seller who sets a deadline for offers. Standing offers: *Great Northern Railway v Witham* (1873) LR 9 CP 16 |

**The two-contract analysis** (Concentrate). *Harvela*, *Blackpool* and *Warlow*/*Barry v Davies* all set a *unilateral* contract (e.g. a promise to accept the lowest tender, to consider conforming tenders, or to sell without reserve) alongside the *bilateral* main contract. The unilateral contract is how the law imposes pre-contractual liability. A claimant who loses out usually gets damages for breach of the unilateral contract, but does not get the goods or the work.

### 4. Termination of the offer
1. **Revocation before acceptance.** An offer can be revoked at any time before it is accepted (*Offord v Davies* (1862); *Payne v Cave* (1789)), even if it was stated to be open for a fixed period, unless something was given for keeping it open (*Routledge v Grant* (1828)). Once accepted, it can no longer be revoked (*Great Northern Railway v Witham*).
2. **Revocation must be communicated.** No express words are needed; conduct showing the offeror is no longer willing is enough (*Financings Ltd v Stimson* [1962] 1 WLR 1184). But the revocation must reach the offeree. *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876) 2 Ch D 463: communication through a reliable third party, or the offeree's knowledge of conduct inconsistent with the offer (e.g. a sale to someone else), is enough. Concentrate notes this leaves the offeree to judge whether the third party can be trusted.
3. **The postal rule does not apply to revocation.** *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880) 5 CPD 344: an offer posted on 1 Oct was received 11 Oct and accepted by telegram that day. A revocation posted on 8 Oct arrived on 20 Oct. The contract was formed on 11 Oct because the revocation only counted when it arrived. Lindley J stressed that an offeree must be able to act safely on an acceptance of an offer they do not know has been withdrawn. *Henthorn v Fraser* [1892] 2 Ch 27: a revocation posted at noon arrived at 5 pm, and a posted acceptance at 3.50 pm won. Lord Herschell said a revocation must be "brought to the mind" of the offeree. For businesses, McKendrick suggests a revocation received in office hours takes effect when, in normal practice, it would be read.
4. **Unilateral offers.** Revocation is generally impossible once the offeree has begun to perform (see 7 below). For offers to the whole world, revocation through the same channel with "the same notoriety" is enough, even if a particular offeree never sees it: *Shuey v US* (1875) (a US case, cited in Concentrate).
5. **Rejection or counter-offer** ends the offer: *Hyde v Wrench* (1840) 3 Beav 334.
6. **Lapse.** The offer ends at any stated deadline (*Routledge v Grant*). If there is none, it ends after a reasonable time (*Ramsgate Victoria Hotel v Montefiore* (1866) LR 1 Ex 109).
7. **Death.** Mellish LJ (obiter) in *Dickinson v Dodds* said an offer cannot be accepted after the offeror dies. the materials's position: the offeror's death ends offers of personal services, but not necessarily an offer that can still be performed when the offeree does not know of the death (*Bradbury v Morgan* (1862) 1 H & C 249).

### 5. Acceptance: what counts
1. **Definition.** A "final and unqualified expression of assent to the terms of an offer" (Treitel). A bare acknowledgement, or a statement of "intention to place an order", is not enough. Test: would a reasonable person in the offeror's position think the offeree was accepting?
2. **Who may accept.** Anyone to whom the offer is addressed, which may be the world at large. Communication may come through an *authorised* agent (*Powell v Lee* (1908) 99 LT 284).
3. **Mirror-image rule.** The acceptance must match the offer exactly and be unconditional (Farnsworth, cited in the materials).
4. **Counter-offer.** A purported acceptance that changes the terms or adds a new term is a counter-offer. It (1) destroys the original offer and (2) is itself a new offer that the original offeror can accept. *Hyde v Wrench*: an offer of a farm at £1,000 was met with £950. A later attempt to accept £1,000 failed because the original offer was dead.
5. **Request for information.** This is not a counter-offer. *Stevenson, Jacques & Co v McLean* (1880) 5 QBD 346: iron was offered at "40s net cash per ton, open till Monday". A telegram asking whether delivery over two months would be accepted was only an inquiry. The later acceptance at 1.34 pm was valid, even though the offeror had already sold elsewhere and wired at 1.25 pm (a telegram that crossed with the acceptance). The question to ask is whether the response contradicts an express term of the offer.
6. **Battle of the forms** (textbook material, not developed in the materials). *Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O* [1979] (CA): the majority applied the counter-offer analysis. The buyer's order on its own terms was a counter-offer, which the seller accepted by returning the tear-off slip, so the buyer's terms governed. Acceptance can also be by conduct, e.g. delivering the goods (*Brogden v Metropolitan Railway* (1877); *Tekdata Interconnections v Amphenol* [2009] EWCA Civ 1209, where Longmore LJ said the traditional analysis applies unless the documents and conduct show a different common intention). Lord Denning took a different route: he asked whether the parties were "broadly in agreement" and then chose whose terms governed. Concentrate objects that this effectively abandons the mirror-image rule.
7. **Knowledge of the offer.** An acceptance must be a response to the offer, so **cross-offers** do not make a contract: *Tinn v Hoffman* (1873) 29 LT 271 (Blackburn J: exchanging identical offers is not an exchange of promises).
8. **Mistakenly accepted offer.** Whether a contract exists still turns on objectively assessed consensus ad idem, not simply on whether enforcement would be oppressive: *Scriven Bros v Hindley* [1913] 3 KB 564 (hemp and tow at auction, where the auctioneer's own conduct induced the bidder's mistake); *Tamplin v James*.

### 6. Silence
1. **Bilateral contracts.** The general rule is that silence is not acceptance. This stops an offeror from imposing a contract simply by announcing that silence will count, and recognises that silence is usually equivocal. *Felthouse v Bindley* (1862) 142 ER 103: the uncle wrote "if I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine". The nephew told the auctioneer the horse was reserved, but there was no contract, so the uncle could not sue the auctioneer in conversion. See also *Re Selectmove Ltd* [1995] 2 All ER 531.
2. **Exceptions** (lecture and Concentrate):
   - the *offeree* proposed that their own silence would amount to acceptance (*Re Selectmove*);
   - the *offeree* holds the offeror to the offeror's own silence stipulation (the *Felthouse* scenario reversed);
   - conduct clearly shows acceptance (*Nissan UK v Nissan Motor Manufacturing* (1994, unreported); *Brogden*);
   - previous dealings combined with a long period of inaction (*Rust v Abbey Life* [1979] 2 Lloyd's Rep 334: the insured kept the policy for seven months without objecting). Lord Steyn approved this in *Vitol SA v Norelf* [1996] AC 800: silence can be acceptance in exceptional cases. See also *The Leonidas D* [1985] 1 WLR 925.
3. **Unilateral contracts.** No communication of acceptance is needed where, read reasonably, the offer asks only for performance. In *Carlill*, Bowen LJ said it follows from the nature of the transaction that performing the condition is enough without notice (the lost-dog example). See also *Errington v Errington and Woods* [1952] 1 KB 290.

### 7. Unilateral contracts: special rules
1. A unilateral advertisement is an offer (*Carlill*; *Bowerman*).
2. Acceptance is **performance of the requested act**, and the offeror is taken to have waived communication.
3. **Knowledge.** A person who acts without knowing of the offer cannot accept it. *R v Clarke* (1927) 40 CLR 227 (Australia): information given "exclusively" to clear himself of a murder charge, without relying on the reward offer, did not earn the reward. *Gibbons v Proctor* (1891): the claimant knew of the offer when the information reached the named person. **Motive is irrelevant** if the person knew of the offer: *Williams v Carwardine* (1833).
4. **Revocation after performance has begun.**
   - *Errington v Errington*: the father promised the house if the couple paid off the mortgage. That promise could not be revoked once they had started paying, but it would stop binding him if they left the payments unfinished.
   - *Daulia v Four Millbank Nominees* [1978] Ch 231 (Goff LJ): the offeror may insist on full performance, but there is an implied obligation not to prevent the condition being satisfied, which arises once the offeree starts to perform.
   - *Luxor (Eastbourne) Ltd v Cooper* [1941] AC 108: an estate agent's commission was payable only on completion. The House of Lords would not imply a promise not to revoke before completion, because the offer's terms showed the owner kept the freedom to withdraw.
   - Theories: (a) an implied collateral promise not to revoke (McGovney), which gives only damages, not the reward; or (b) acceptance takes place when performance begins and the reward is payable on completion (Law Revision Committee, 6th Interim Report 1937), which would wrongly bind the offeree to finish.
   - the materials's statement: revocation is generally not possible once performance has begun, because of an implied promise not to revoke.

### 8. Communication of acceptance
1. **General rule.** Acceptance must be communicated to the offeror: *Entores Ltd v Miles Far East Corp* [1955] 2 QB 327 (Denning LJ: an acceptance shouted across a river and drowned out by an aircraft does not count). *Robophone Facilities v Blank* [1966] (Lord Denning): signing a form without telling the other side is not enough. Mental or internal acceptance does not count.
2. **Prescribed method.** First ask whether the offer prescribes a method.
   - If the offer insists that *only* that method will do, it must be followed.
   - Otherwise any method that is no less advantageous to the offeror is enough: *Tinn v Hoffman* ("reply by return of post" allows a telegram or any reply that arrives as fast); *Manchester Diocesan Council for Education v Commercial & General Investments* [1970] 1 WLR 241 (a clause requiring notice by post to the tenderer's address was not the only permitted mode, and was in any case for the offeree's benefit, so it could be waived). See also *Yates Building v Pulleyn* (1975).
3. **Instantaneous communication: the receipt rule.** Acceptance takes effect when and where it reaches the offeror. This applies to face-to-face speech, telephone, telex (*Entores*, so the contract was made where the acceptance was received), fax (*JSC Zestafoni Nikoladze* [2004] EWHC 245 (Comm)) and, probably, email.
   - **Denning LJ's fault principles in *Entores*:**
     1. the onus is on the sender to get the message through;
     2. if the line goes dead and the sender knows, they must repeat the message;
     3. if the recipient knows a message was sent but not received, and the sender believes it got through, the recipient must ask for it to be repeated, or cannot deny receiving it;
     4. if neither is at fault (the sender believes it got through and the recipient knows nothing of it), there is no communication and no contract.
   - **Lord Wilberforce in *Brinkibon v Stahag Stahl* [1983] 2 AC 34:** "No universal rule can cover such cases"; they depend on the parties' intentions, sound business practice and, sometimes, a judgment about where the risk should lie.
   - **Business hours.** A message to a business during business hours counts on arrival at the machine (*The Brimnes* (Tenax Steamship v Brimnes) [1975] QB 929, sent between 17.30 and 18.00). A message sent out of hours takes effect when it would reasonably be read, normally the next working day (*Mondial Shipping v Astarte* (1995): 23.41 on a Friday took effect on Monday). *Thomas v BPE Solicitors* [2010] EWHC 306 (Ch) (Blair J, obiter): the receipt rule applies to email, and 18.00 was not outside business hours. *Lehman Brothers v Exxonmobil* (2016): business hours ran to 19.00 on those facts. *Greenclose v National Westminster Bank* (2014) also applies the receipt rule to email. The receipt rule has been applied to WhatsApp too (*Eurasia Sports v Tsai* [2016] EWHC 2207 (QB)).
   - **Answering machines.** There is no authority. Concentrate thinks *Entores* principles apply, so the sender bears the risk. Coote argued that the postal rule should apply instead.
   - **Websites.** The 2002 Regulations (reg 11) treat an order and its acknowledgement as received when the addressee is able to access them. Email contracts are excluded (regs 9(4), 11(3)). Rajah JC discussed both in *Chwee Kin Keong v Digilandmall.com* [2004] 2 SLR(R) 594 at [97]–[103].
4. **Non-instantaneous communication: the postal rule.**
   - Acceptance is complete when the letter is posted: *Adams v Lindsell* (1818) 106 ER 250 (wool; otherwise, the court said, confirmation would have to go on "ad infinitum").
   - It applies even if the letter is delayed or never arrives: *Household Fire Insurance v Grant* (1879) 4 Ex D 216 (shares allotted by a letter that was lost). The letter must be properly addressed and posted: *LJ Korbetis v Transgrain Shipping* [2005] EWHC 1345; and see *Contimar* [1953] 1 WLR 793 on wrongly addressed letters.
   - It has been applied to telegrams (*Cowans v O'Conner*, cited in the materials as (1880) 20 QBD 640).
   - Once the acceptance is posted, the offeror can no longer revoke (*Byrne*). The rule does not apply to revocations (*Henthorn v Fraser*).
   - Post must be a reasonable method: in *Henthorn*, post was reasonable because the parties were at a distance.
   - It does not apply to instantaneous methods (*Brinkibon*).
   - **Ousting the rule:**
     1. prescribe a different, exclusive mode;
     2. state that any mode other than post will do;
     3. post would be unreasonable or absurd (e.g. "get back to me by 1 pm today"): *British & American Telegraph v Colson* (1871) (Bramwell B; McKendrick notes it was overruled in *Household Fire*);
     4. require actual "notice" of acceptance: *Holwell Securities v Hughes* [1974] 1 WLR 155 (notice "in writing to the intending vendor" meant actual receipt; Lawton LJ also said the rule does not apply where it would produce "manifest inconvenience and absurdity");
     5. the letter is wrongly addressed.
   - **Overtaking a posted acceptance** (e.g. a phone call withdrawing it before the letter arrives) is unsettled. *Countess of Dunmore v Alexander* (1830, Scotland) may really concern revocation of an offer. *Wenkheim v Arndt* (1873, New Zealand) and *A to Z Bazaars v Minister of Agriculture* (1974, South Africa, first instance) suggest it is not possible. Hudson ((1966) 82 LQR 169) argues it should be allowed where the offeror is not disadvantaged.

## Key authorities
- *Smith v Hughes* (1871) LR 6 QB 597: oats sold by sample; the classic statement of the objective test (Blackburn J).
- *Storer v Manchester CC* [1974] 1 WLR 1403 (CA): the council's letter was an offer; outward appearance governs.
- *Gibson v Manchester CC* [1979] 1 WLR 294 (HL): "may be prepared to sell" was not an offer; Lord Diplock rejected Lord Denning's broad "look at the correspondence as a whole" approach.
- *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 (CA): an advertised £100 reward, backed by a £1,000 bank deposit, was a unilateral offer to the world, accepted by using the smoke ball as directed; no notice of acceptance needed. Simpson ("Quackery and Contract Law") places the case in the context of the influenza epidemic and reads it as recognising intention to create legal relations and making unilateral contract analysis canonical.
- *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 2 All ER 421: an advertisement is an invitation to treat.
- *Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394; *Boots* [1953] 1 QB 401: goods on display are an invitation to treat.
- *Harris v Nickerson* (1873); *Warlow v Harrison* (1859); *Barry v Davies* [2001]: auctions, and the collateral contract in a sale without reserve.
- *Spencer v Harding* (1870); *Harvela* (HL); *Blackpool & Fylde Aero Club* [1990]: tenders.
- *Hyde v Wrench* (1840): a counter-offer destroys the offer.
- *Stevenson v McLean* (1880): a request for information is not a counter-offer.
- *Butler Machine Tool v Ex-Cell-O* [1979] (CA): battle of the forms decided by counter-offer analysis.
- *Felthouse v Bindley* (1862): silence is not acceptance.
- *Entores v Miles Far East* [1955]; *Brinkibon v Stahag Stahl* [1983] (HL): instantaneous communications.
- *Adams v Lindsell* (1818); *Household Fire v Grant* (1879); *Holwell Securities v Hughes* [1974]; *Henthorn v Fraser* [1892]: the postal rule and its limits.
- *Byrne v Van Tienhoven* (1880); *Dickinson v Dodds* (1876): revocation must be communicated, and may come through a third party.
- *Errington v Errington* [1952]; *Daulia* [1978]; *Luxor v Cooper* [1941]: revoking unilateral offers.
- *R v Clarke* (1927); *Williams v Carwardine* (1833): knowledge and motive in unilateral acceptance.
- *Tinn v Hoffman* (1873): cross-offers.
- Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 57(2): when an auction sale is complete.
- Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002, regs 11–12: online orders and acknowledgements.

## Debate and critique
- **Is offer and acceptance a universal model?** Lord Denning in *Gibson* (CA) said not every contract can be analysed this way. Lord Diplock answered (HL) that such cases are "exceptional" and that correspondence in reply to correspondence must be analysed conventionally. Lord Wilberforce in *The Eurymedon* [1975] AC 154 said English law has "a rather technical and schematic doctrine" but takes a practical approach, often forcing facts into the slots of offer, acceptance and consideration (examples: auctions, supermarkets, buses, tenders, rewards, acceptance by post). *The Satanita* / *Clarke v Dunraven* [1897] AC 59 (yacht race competitors bound to each other by the race rules) is the classic case that resists the analysis. Treitel says the competitors agreed, but not by any process that can be analysed as offer and acceptance. Collins calls the rules formalist: "detailed, technical, and mysterious". McKendrick replies that alternatives such as Lord Denning's "agreement on everything material" create uncertainty, and that courts keep the model but apply it flexibly (*Tekdata*; *Butler*; *Blackpool*). Concentrate cites *LNT Aviation v Airbus Helicopters* (2022) on the importance of the conventional approach, *GHSP Inc v AB Electronic* (2010) for a non-traditional approach, and *Trentham v Archital Luxfer* (1993) (Steyn LJ) for finding agreement more easily once there has been performance.
- **History and will theory.** Simpson ((1975) 91 LQR 247) traces how the law moved from a one-sided "promise broken" to a two-sided "contract broken" under the influence of the will theory (Pothier, Savigny). Ibbetson says this coherence was imposed on the common law from outside, and the result "was a mess". The objective theory, borrowed from Paley, fits awkwardly with a meeting-of-minds theory. Unilateral contracts became anomalous once the bilateral model became the paradigm. Offer and acceptance analysis was first applied to rewards in *Williams v Carwardine* and became canonical only with *Carlill*.
- **Which objectivity?** Howarth ((1984) 100 LQR 265) distinguishes promisor, promisee and detached objectivity, and himself favours detached objectivity. Vorster ((1987) 103 LQR 274) criticises this, pointing out that in a bilateral contract each party is both promisor and promisee. McKendrick's view is that courts start from the reasonable person in the *promisee's* position for the disputed promise and then check the position of the other party. They do not take a detached observer's view. Endicott argues that *Hartog* and *Scriven* can be explained objectively: a reasonable person who knew of the blunder would not treat the other side as having agreed.
- **Snapping up and Centrovincial.** In *Centrovincial Estates v Merchant Investors Assurance* [1983] Com LR 158, an offeror who made a mistaken offer was bound once it was accepted, even though the acceptor had not relied on it. Atiyah ((1986) 102 LQR 363) asks why a bare, unrelied-upon acceptance should create rights. The Court of Appeal's answer was security of transactions: a promise binds on acceptance. *Chwee Kin Keong* (Singapore; printers mistakenly priced at S$66 instead of S$3,854) held that a party with actual knowledge of the mistake cannot enforce. *Longley v PPB Entertainment* takes actual knowledge as the English test. Stevens suggests the mistaken party could still enforce the objective bargain if it wished, which would be a "one-way" escape route.
- **The postal rule.** Treitel lists the suggested justifications: the offer continues while the letter is in the post; the Post Office is a common agent; posting is easier to prove; the offeror chose the post. He finds each weak and calls the rule "arbitrary". Its strongest justification is that it limits the offeror's power to revoke. McKendrick argues the law goes too far. It would be enough to bar revocation once the acceptance is posted and to treat acceptance as effective on receipt, which is the approach of the CISG (Art 23), the Unidroit Principles (Art 2.1.6(2)) and PECL (Arts 2:202, 2:205). Gardner ("Trashing with Trollope" (1992) 12 OJLS 170) explains the rule historically: the 1840 postal reforms (penny post, prepayment, stamps, letter boxes) made posting seem equivalent to delivery, and the courts lost enthusiasm for the rule as instantaneous methods arrived. The academic majority (e.g. Nolan, "Offer and Acceptance in the Electronic Age") is against extending the rule to email. Lord Sumption in *Brownlie v Four Seasons* (2017) put the rule down to "pragmatic convenience", and Leggatt (2017) 133 LQR 521 suggests it is becoming less relevant. Adams and Brownsword (in McKendrick ch 1) present the postal rule as a model of market-individualist clarity.
- **Felthouse.** Miller ((1972) 35 MLR 489) argues the decision was wrong in principle, because the nephew had clearly shown acceptance to the auctioneer, and that the result can only be supported on Statute of Frauds grounds. the materials asks whether the case was wrongly decided.
- **Unilateral contracts.** McKendrick argues that letting an offeror revoke just before the walker reaches York would allow opportunism. He prefers Goff LJ's view in *Daulia*: no revocation once performance starts, but no payment until it is complete. On acceptance in ignorance, he suggests an exception allowing someone who performs without knowing of the offer to claim, at least where the performance cost them nothing. He also argues the offeror should bear the burden of proving that the offeree did not act with reference to the offer (Mitchell and Phillips (2002) 22 OJLS 115; Isaacs ACJ's example in *Clarke* of a swimmer thrown overboard).
- **Electronic contracting.** Rajah JC's analysis in *Chwee* sets out arguments both for a postal rule and for a receipt rule for email. A receipt rule for websites commands general assent.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Work in date order.** For each message or act, ask (a) is it, in law, capable of being an offer, an invitation to treat, an acceptance, a counter-offer, a request for information or a revocation? and (b) was it communicated, and *when*, under the correct rule? (Concentrate's structure.)
2. **Classify the deal.** Is it bilateral (promise for promise) or unilateral (promise for act)? Is there a two-contract structure (tender, or auction without reserve)?
3. **Offer or invitation to treat?** Use the categories above. Look for wording of commitment ("will sell", "first come first served", deposits of money as in *Carlill*) and wording of negotiation ("may be prepared to", "please apply").
4. **Acceptance.** Does it mirror the offer? Is a new term a counter-offer (*Hyde v Wrench*) or a mere inquiry (*Stevenson v McLean*)? Was the person aware of the offer? Is there a prescribed method, and is it mandatory? Is there an issue of silence or conduct?
5. **Which communication rule?**
   - Post: did the postal rule apply, or was it ousted (*Holwell*, unreasonable mode, misaddressed letter)?
   - Telephone, fax, email: apply *Entores* fault principles and *Brinkibon*, and check business hours. Friday afternoons, early closing and bank holidays are favourite traps.
   - Answering machines: there is no authority, so argue both ways.
6. **Revocation.** Was it actually communicated before acceptance took effect? Never apply the postal rule to a revocation. Was it communicated through a reliable third party (*Dickinson v Dodds*)? For a unilateral offer, had performance begun (*Errington*/*Daulia* v *Luxor*), and for an offer to the world, was the same notoriety given (*Shuey*)?
7. **Other terminating events.** Lapse (stated deadline or reasonable time), rejection, death (personal services, *Bradbury v Morgan*).
8. **Objective meaning and mistake.** Did one party know of the other's error (*Hartog*)? Are the facts hopelessly ambiguous (*Raffles*)?
9. **Remember the other elements.** Even if agreement exists, briefly flag certainty, ICLR and consideration if the facts raise them.

*Worked example (lecture scenario).* A company advertises in a trade periodical that it will award a £100,000 courier contract to "the first vessel to arrive" at its laboratory. A driver reads it and sets off. Before she arrives, the company announces on local radio that it no longer needs a courier, and she hears the broadcast. Points to argue:
- whether the advertisement is a unilateral offer to the world (*Carlill*), made more certain by "first to arrive" wording (*Lefkowitz*);
- whether setting off counts as beginning performance, so that revocation is barred (*Errington*; *Daulia*) or remains possible (*Luxor*);
- whether revocation through a *different* channel (radio, not the periodical) is effective. It was actually communicated to her, which is enough under *Dickinson v Dodds*; the *Shuey* "same notoriety" point matters only for offerees who did not hear it;
- what the company could have done differently, e.g. reserving a right to withdraw, or using the same channel.

*Typical traps:* applying the postal rule to a revocation; calling an ordinary sale unilateral; discussing communication before checking the mirror-image rule (the materials warns against this unless you argue in the alternative); forgetting that a counter-offer is itself an offer.
