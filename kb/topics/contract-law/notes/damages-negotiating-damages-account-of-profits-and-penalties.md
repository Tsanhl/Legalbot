# Negotiating Damages, Account of Profits and Agreed Damages (Penalties)

## Overview
This note covers two further topics. Both sit at the edge of the ordinary rule that contract damages compensate for loss.

1. **Negotiating damages** (also called *Wrotham Park* or hypothetical release damages) and the **account of profits**. The question is whether a claimant can recover by reference to what it could have charged to release the defendant, or by reference to the profit the defendant made. In *Morris-Garner v One Step* [2018] UKSC 20 the Supreme Court classed negotiating damages as compensatory and confined them. *Attorney-General v Blake* [2001] confined the account of profits to exceptional cases.
2. **Agreed damages clauses and the penalty rule.** Parties may fix in advance the sum payable on breach, but the court will not enforce a penalty. *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi; ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67 recast the test as one of "legitimate interest" and proportionality.

All of this is English law (England and Wales). Foreign comparisons (Australia, Singapore) are noted where the sources make them.

## The law

### A. Negotiating damages
1. **What they are.** The price the claimant could reasonably have charged the defendant to release it from the obligation, had release been sought before the breach.
2. **Origin.** *Wrotham Park Estate Co Ltd v Parkside Homes Ltd* [1974] 1 WLR 798: damages were awarded in lieu of an injunction for breach of a restrictive covenant over land. Some read it as gain-based ("restitutionary"). In *Jaggard v Sawyer* [1995] 1 WLR 269, Sir Thomas Bingham MR read the reference to the defendant's profits as context for what the defendant would have paid for a release, not as profit-stripping (Concentrate).
3. **Facts of *One Step (Support) Ltd v Morris-Garner*** [2018] UKSC 20, [2019] AC 649:
   - Two former employees broke three-year covenants not to compete, not to solicit clients and not to use confidential information.
   - Their new company did well and they sold it for £12.8m. The claimant's own business declined.
   - The claimant's expert put its loss at £3.4m to £4.6m, but the release fee at £5.6m to £6.3m.
   - The claimant won at first instance and in the Court of Appeal. The Supreme Court allowed the appeal: the claimant must prove its actual financial loss, and the release fee is at most evidence of that loss.
4. **Lord Reed's propositions** (with Lady Hale, Lord Wilson and Lord Carnwath; 12-point summary at [95], as summarised by McKendrick):
   - Damages for breach of contract are awarded as of right. They compensate for loss, and are not awarded at the court's discretion.
   - Negotiating damages are **compensatory**, not restitutionary. They compensate for a loss "not of a conventional kind": the loss of the economic value of a right to control the use of an asset.
   - They are mainly available in two settings:
     - "user damages", where the defendant uses the claimant's property without consent;
     - damages in lieu of specific performance or an injunction under s 50 of the Senior Courts Act 1981 (formerly Lord Cairns' Act).
   - At common law they may be awarded where the breach causes the loss of "a valuable asset created or protected by the right which was infringed". Lord Reed's examples at [92] are a restrictive covenant over land, an intellectual property agreement and a confidentiality agreement. In those cases the imaginary negotiation is just a tool for valuing the asset.
   - Not every contractual right is an "asset" in this sense ([93]). Lord Reed said it is "not easy" to see other applications.
   - On the facts the covenants protected a purely commercial interest, and the loss (competition and lost profits) was of a familiar, quantifiable type. So conventional loss had to be assessed.
5. **Lord Sumption** concurred with a broader, three-category analysis:
   - cases where the claimant's interest goes beyond financial reparation, such as invasion of property rights;
   - cases where the obligation was specifically enforceable and the release fee is the price of non-enforcement;
   - cases where the release fee is evidence of pecuniary loss.
   Lord Reed's judgment had majority support and so prevails.
6. **After *Morris-Garner*.**
   - *Priyanka Shipping Ltd v Glory Bulk Carriers* [2019] EWHC 2804 (Comm): breach of a negative covenant restricting a ship's use to demolition fell outside Lord Reed's categories, so there were no negotiating damages for the past use. Only nominal damages were available, plus an injunction for the future.
   - *Experience Hendrix LLC v PPX Enterprises Inc* [2003] EWCA Civ 323: the court combined an injunction against future breaches with damages for past breaches (Senior Courts Act 1981 s 49). Lord Reed disagreed with its wider "practical justice" statements, but accepted the decision as an intellectual property case.
   - *Vercoe v Rutland Fund Management* [2010] EWHC 424 (Ch) is listed without detail.

### B. Account of profits
1. ***Attorney-General v Blake*** [2001] 1 AC 268 (HL):
   - **Facts:** George Blake, a former intelligence officer and spy who had defected, published an autobiography in breach of his undertaking not to divulge information.
   - **Held:** the Crown recovered the whole of his profits, even though the information was no longer confidential and the Crown had suffered no conventional financial loss.
2. **Lord Nicholls's guidance:**
   - An account is appropriate "only in exceptional circumstances", where damages, specific performance and injunction are inadequate.
   - The court looks at all the circumstances: the subject matter of the contract, the purpose of the provision broken, how and why the breach occurred, its consequences, and the circumstances in which relief is sought.
   - A "useful general guide": did the claimant have a **legitimate interest** in preventing the defendant's profit-making activity, and so in depriving it of the profit?
   - Skimped performance, or profiting from doing the very thing the defendant promised not to do, is not enough by itself (Concentrate).
3. **Why *Blake* was exceptional** (Concentrate). The quasi-fiduciary employment relationship, and the harm to the morale, trust and effectiveness of the intelligence services.
4. ***Esso Petroleum v Niad*** [2001] EWHC 6 (Ch). A petrol station broke a pricing agreement, and *Blake*-type relief was held potentially available. The Concentrate author thinks it is unlikely to be followed.
5. **Status after *Morris-Garner*.**
   - Lord Reed saw the *Blake* award as not compensatory, and found parts of Lord Nicholls's speech "not altogether easy to interpret". But *Blake*'s soundness was not in issue. He said only that *Blake* decided that "in exceptional circumstances an account of profits can be ordered".
   - McKendrick thinks an account is unlikely between commercial parties in an ordinary business deal.
6. **Account v negotiating damages v punitive damages:**
   - An account strips the whole profit. Negotiating damages take only a share, fixed by the hypothetical bargain.
   - An account is capped at the defendant's profit, whereas punitive damages have no ceiling.
   - Punitive damages remain barred in contract (*Addis*).

### C. Agreed damages clauses and the penalty rule
1. **Function.** A liquidated damages clause fixes the sum payable for a breach "irrespective of what loss may actually be suffered" (Lord Leggatt, *Triple Point Technology v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29, [74]). It avoids costly disputes over quantum and caps the contractor's exposure. If the clause is valid, the claimant recovers the stated sum and the rules on remoteness and mitigation fall away. If it is a penalty, it is unenforceable, and the claimant is left to prove its loss in the ordinary way.
2. **The old law:** *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v New Garage and Motor Co Ltd* [1915] AC 79, Lord Dunedin's propositions:
   - The label "penalty" or "liquidated damages" is not conclusive.
   - A penalty is a payment stipulated *in terrorem* of the offending party. Liquidated damages are a "genuine covenanted pre-estimate of damage" (from *Clydebank Engineering v Don Jose Yzquierdo y Castaneda* [1905] AC 6).
   - The question is one of construction, judged at the time the contract is made, not at the time of breach.
   - The tests:
     - (a) a sum that is "extravagant and unconscionable" compared with the greatest loss that could conceivably follow is a penalty;
     - (b) where the breach is failure to pay money, a larger sum is a penalty;
     - (c) a single lump sum payable on several events, some serious and some trifling, raises a presumption (no more) of a penalty;
     - (d) it is no obstacle that precise pre-estimation is almost impossible. That is when a pre-estimate is most likely to be the true bargain.
3. **Problems with *Dunlop*.** In *Makdessi* (at [22]), Lords Neuberger and Sumption said Lord Dunedin's speech had wrongly acquired the status of a "quasi-statutory code". "In terrorem" is unhelpful, because commercial parties are not terrified. A clause can be neither a pre-estimate nor a deterrent. Some courts had developed a "commercially justifiable" test (*Lordsvale Finance v Bank of Zambia* [1996] QB 752; *Murray v Leisureplay plc* [2005] EWCA Civ 963), which made the law less certain.
4. ***Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi; ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis*** [2015] UKSC 67, [2016] AC 1172. This was the first full consideration of the penalty rule at the highest level in a century. The rule was neither abolished nor extended, but recast.
   - **The test (Lords Neuberger and Sumption at [32]):** whether the clause "is a secondary obligation which imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in the enforcement of the primary obligation". The innocent party has no legitimate interest in punishment. For a straightforward damages clause, the interest will rarely go beyond compensation, so *Dunlop*'s tests will usually suffice.
   - **Lord Mance (at [152]):** first ask whether a legitimate business interest is served. Then ask whether the provision is nevertheless extravagant, exorbitant or unconscionable. Arm's-length negotiation with legal advice is relevant.
   - **Lord Hodge (at [255]):** whether the sum or remedy is "exorbitant or unconscionable" having regard to the innocent party's interest in performance.
   - **Bargaining context:** where properly advised parties of comparable power negotiate, there is a "strong initial presumption" that they are the best judges of what is legitimate ([35]).
5. **The two cases:**
   - ***Makdessi*.** A seller of shares in a marketing company broke a non-compete covenant. The clauses provided that he would lose further instalments of the price (cl 5.1) and could be made to sell his remaining shares at a price excluding goodwill (cl 5.6). Held not penal: the buyer had a legitimate interest in protecting the goodwill, and the parties were experienced and advised. (The Concentrate guide notes the court was uneasy about whether one clause was primary or secondary.)
   - ***ParkingEye v Beavis*.** Two hours' free parking, then an £85 charge. Mr Beavis overstayed by about an hour. Held not penal: although £85 did not represent the operator's loss from the overstay, it had a legitimate interest in the efficient functioning of the car park, and the charge was comparable to others in the locality (Concentrate). The term was also held not unfair under the consumer unfair terms regime. The imbalance did not arise contrary to good faith, because the business model gave motorists two hours free, and a reasonable motorist would have agreed to the charge (McKendrick ch 14). Lord Toulson dissented: £85 is a lot for many people, and it was payable even for a one-minute overstay.
6. **Scope of the rule.**
   - It applies only to obligations triggered by **breach** (*Export Credit Guarantee Department v Universe Oil Products* [1983] 1 WLR 399). It does not apply to sums payable on other events: *Alder v Moore* (1961), a footballer's £500 repayable if he played again.
   - It covers payment obligations, obligations to transfer property on breach, rights to withhold sums, and possibly extravagant non-refundable deposits.
   - It does not reach primary obligations. That leaves the rule open to clever drafting, but Lord Hodge (at [258]) left room for a "disguised penalty".
   - A forfeiture clause can engage both the penalty rule and equitable relief against forfeiture (Lord Hodge at [227]; Lords Mance, Clarke and Toulson agreeing).
   - Australia has abandoned the breach limitation (*Andrews v ANZ Banking Group* (2012) 247 CLR 205). The Supreme Court declined to follow.
7. **Why the rule was kept** (*Makdessi*):
   - it is long-standing;
   - it is common to most Western systems;
   - statutory regulation of unfair terms is incomplete;
   - commercial bargaining power is often unequal;
   - its narrow scope does not stop sensible agreements;
   - anomalies are better handled by a realistic look at substance.
   The suggestion of an exemption for commercial parties with equal power and legal advice was rejected as unworkable.
8. **Later examples** (Concentrate):
   - *Ahuja Investments v Victorygame* (2021): a 400% increase in the interest rate on default was a penalty.
   - *Britannia Parking v Semark-Jullien* (2020): *ParkingEye* distinguished because of an added debt-recovery charge, and remitted.
   - *CFL Finance v Bass* [2019] EWHC 1839: there is little guidance on what a "legitimate interest" is.

## Key authorities
- *Wrotham Park Estate v Parkside Homes* [1974] 1 WLR 798: release-fee damages for breach of a restrictive covenant, now regarded as compensatory.
- *Jaggard v Sawyer* [1995] 1 WLR 269: the defendant's profits used only to set the hypothetical fee.
- *Experience Hendrix v PPX* [2003] EWCA Civ 323: an injunction combined with damages for past breaches. Lord Reed later accepted it as an intellectual property case.
- *Morris-Garner v One Step* [2018] UKSC 20: negotiating damages are compensatory and confined; damages are a matter of right.
- *Attorney-General v Blake* [2001] 1 AC 268: account of profits in exceptional circumstances; the legitimate interest guide.
- *Dunlop v New Garage* [1915] AC 79: the classic penalty tests.
- *Makdessi; ParkingEye* [2015] UKSC 67: the legitimate interest and "out of all proportion" test.
- *Triple Point v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29: the function of liquidated damages clauses.
- Senior Courts Act 1981 s 50: damages in lieu of specific performance or injunction; s 49: combined claims.

## Debate and critique
- **Nature of negotiating damages.** Compensatory, gain-based, or neither?
  - The Law Commission (Law Com No 247, 1997) called *Wrotham Park* restitutionary.
  - *Morris-Garner* says compensatory. Critics noted there was often no real loss, and that the "willing seller" is a fiction because the claimant would never have agreed to release. Lord Reed answered with the idea of an "imaginary negotiation" and loss of a valuable asset.
  - The hard part is knowing which contractual rights count as "assets".
  - Further reading: Burrows (2008), Cunnington (2008) 71 MLR 559 on gain-based measures, Senu (2020) 40 OJLS 110, Sharpe and Waddams (1982) 2 OJLS 290.
- **Disgorgement for breach.**
  - Fox (2001) 60 CLJ 33 argues that gain-based awards remove the incentive for deliberate breach.
  - Against that, the Concentrate author asks how *Blake* can be compensatory when no bargain would ever have been struck.
  - Chen-Wishart (1998) 114 LQR 363, McKendrick (2003), and Pearce and Halson (2008) 28 OJLS 73.
- **Punishment.** Whether punitive damages belong in contract: Cunnington (2006); Rowan (2010) 30 OJLS 495. McKendrick is doubtful.
- **Penalties.**
  - The rule interferes with freedom of contract, so it must be kept narrow (*Makdessi* at [33]).
  - Critics ask why a party who breaks is protected but one who exercises a termination right is not (Lord Denning's "absurd paradox" in *Campbell Discount v Bridge* [1962] AC 600). They also ask whether a form-based primary/secondary test can coexist with a substance-based "disguised penalty" doctrine (McKendrick).
  - "Legitimate interest" is vague (*CFL Finance*). It is the same concept as in *White & Carter*.
  - Goetz and Scott (1977) argue from economics for enforcing agreed sums. Rowan [2019] CLJ 148 examines the legitimate interest in performance. Peel (2020) and Summers [2017] LMCLQ 95 address unresolved issues.
- **Essay link.** Both topics test the boundaries of the compensatory principle in *Robinson v Harman* and *Addis*. Compare them with the performance-interest debate in `damages-compensatory-measure.md`.

## Applying it to a problem question
**Agreed damages clause:**
1. Is there a clause fixing the consequences of breach? If so, deal with it **first**, because a valid clause displaces unliquidated damages.
2. Is it triggered by breach? If it is a primary obligation, a price, or a payment on some non-breach event, the penalty rule does not apply (*Alder v Moore*). Watch for a "disguised penalty".
3. What legitimate interest does the innocent party have? Is it only compensation, or something wider, such as goodwill, the management of a business system, or a pricing structure?
4. Is the sum "out of all proportion", extravagant or unconscionable, compared with that interest, judged at the date of contracting?
   - For a pure damages clause, use the *Dunlop* tests: greatest conceivable loss; a single lump sum for breaches of very different seriousness (a clause that does not grade the sum by length of delay is vulnerable); difficulty of pre-estimating loss.
5. Who were the parties? Advised commercial parties of equal power get a strong presumption of validity. Where a consumer is involved, consider the Consumer Rights Act 2015 fairness test too (see `exemption-clauses-and-unfair-terms.md`).
6. Outcome: if valid, the stated sum is recoverable whatever the actual loss. If penal, it is unenforceable, so assess ordinary damages, applying remoteness and mitigation.

**Gain-based claims:**
7. Did the claimant suffer little or no provable loss while the defendant profited?
   - First try conventional loss (*Morris-Garner*).
   - Was a valuable asset taken: land covenant, intellectual property, confidential information? If so, consider negotiating damages.
   - Is the case truly exceptional, with a fiduciary-like relationship, inadequate other remedies, and a legitimate interest in stopping the profit? If so, consider an account (*Blake*).
   - Is an injunction still possible? Consider damages in lieu under s 50 of the Senior Courts Act 1981.
8. **Traps:**
   - calling a clause a penalty because it is labelled one; the label is not decisive;
   - applying the *Dunlop* tests mechanically to complex commercial clauses;
   - treating *Blake* as available whenever a breach is deliberate and profitable.
