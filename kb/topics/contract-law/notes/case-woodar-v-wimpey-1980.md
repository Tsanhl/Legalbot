# Woodar Investment Development Ltd v Wimpey Construction UK Ltd [1980] 1 WLR 277 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1980] 1 WLR 277 (also [1980] 1 All ER 571;
> [1980] UKHL 11) before citing. The privity comments were not the basis of the decision.

## Overview
House of Lords, 14 February 1980. Panel: Lord Wilberforce, Lord Salmon, Lord Russell of Killowen, Lord Keith of Kinkel
and Lord Scarman. By a majority of 3 to 2 (Lords Salmon and Russell dissenting) the House held that the purchaser had
not repudiated the contract. The case is mainly cited for its (obiter) remarks on whether a promisee can recover
damages for a third party's loss, and for the limited view it took of *Jackson v Horizon Holidays*.

## Facts
Wimpey agreed to buy land from Woodar for £850,000. A special term also required Wimpey to pay £150,000 to a third
party, Transworld Trade Ltd. A condition allowed the purchaser to rescind if a public authority began compulsory
acquisition of any of the land. In 1974 (when land prices had fallen) Wimpey purported to rescind, relying on
compulsory acquisition procedures started by a government department. Those procedures had begun before the contract
was made, so the condition did not cover them, and that finding was not appealed. Woodar claimed that Wimpey's
wrongful rescission was a repudiation and sued for damages, including the £150,000 that would have gone to Transworld.
The Court of Appeal felt bound by *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* to allow recovery of the £150,000.

## Held
- **Repudiation.** The House allowed Wimpey's appeal. Judged objectively, Wimpey's conduct as a whole did not show an
  intention to refuse to perform its contractual obligations whatever the position; it was relying, wrongly, on a
  contractual right. So there was no repudiation, and Woodar's damages claim failed.
- **Privity (obiter).** The House did not need to decide whether Woodar could recover the £150,000. Lord Wilberforce
  doubted that it could, and said *Jackson* could be supported only on its special facts (a holiday for a family,
  where the promisee recovers for the group). He reserved his opinion on the point. The speeches disapproved of using
  *Jackson* as authority for a general right to recover another person's loss.

## Effect
- A wrongful but honest attempt to rely on a contractual term is not necessarily repudiation; the question is
  objective, whether the party has shown that it will not perform.
- The case is the usual source for treating *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* as confined to a narrow category (ordinary
  consumer or family contracts), not a general principle.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. If a party serves an invalid notice or wrongly relies on a clause, ask objectively whether its conduct shows it will
   not perform at all, or that it is acting on a mistaken view of its rights. Look at what else the parties said and
   did.
2. If the claimant seeks damages for the loss of a third party (here, a payment due to Transworld), explain the
   general rule that a claimant recovers only its own loss and the doubts about *Jackson*.
3. Consider the 1999 Act: a payment term that purports to confer a benefit on the third party may now give them a
   direct right.
