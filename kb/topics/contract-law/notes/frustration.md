# Frustration

## Overview
Frustration is a common law doctrine (England and Wales) that discharges a contract automatically when, after formation, an outside event makes performance illegal, impossible or radically different from what was undertaken. It is a defence to a claim for breach, and because it kills the contract the courts keep it narrow. The money consequences are governed by the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943. It is one of the several ways a contract ends: full performance, agreement, operation of a term, frustration (by operation of law) and termination for breach.

## The law
1. **Definition.** Lord Simon in *National Carriers v Panalpina (Northern) Ltd* [1981] AC 675: frustration occurs when a supervening event, without default of either party and for which the contract makes insufficient provision, so changes the *nature* (not merely the expense or onerousness) of the outstanding obligations that it would be unjust to hold the parties to the literal terms; both are then discharged.
2. **Narrow doctrine.** Bingham LJ in *The Super Servant Two* [1990] 1 Lloyd's Rep 1: the doctrine mitigates the rigour of literal performance of absolute promises, is not to be lightly invoked, must be kept within narrow limits, discharges the contract forthwith and automatically, and requires an outside event arising without blame on the party relying on it.
3. **Radical change, not hardship.** *Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC* [1956] AC 696 (Lord Radcliffe): hardship, inconvenience or loss alone is not enough; the thing undertaken would, if performed, have to be a different thing from that contracted for. In that case delay in a building job made it more onerous but never a different kind of job (Lord Reid). Whether an event frustrates is "a question of degree" (*National Carriers*).
4. **Historical background.** Before 1863 liability was absolute: *Paradine v Jane* (1647) held a party bound by his own undertaking even if the property was burnt or destroyed, because he could have provided against it. *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) changed this: a concert hall destroyed by fire before the first concert; Blackburn J implied a condition that perishing of the thing on which performance depends excuses performance. The doctrine then moved beyond physical impossibility to "frustration of adventure" (*Jackson v Union Marine Insurance* (1879): a voyage after repair would have been a different adventure).
5. **Relation to mistake.** Both deal with wrong assumptions, but mistake concerns assumptions wrong at formation, whereas frustration concerns assumptions correct at formation that later fail (the materials cites *Krell v Henry* and *Griffith v Brymer* in this context).
6. **Allocation of risk.** Frustration applies only to events the contract does not adequately provide for.
   - *Express allocation*: force majeure, hardship or intervener clauses. The court construes the clause (restrictively, like exemption clauses) and asks whether it fully covers the new situation: *Metropolitan Water Board v Dick Kerr* [1918] AC 119; also cited: *The Playa Larga*, *Staffordshire AHA v South Staffordshire Waterworks*, *The Saldanha*.
   - *Implied allocation*: from the nature of the transaction (building contracts in *Davis*, leases, currency and price fluctuations) and foreseeability. Lord Denning in *The Eugenia* [1964] 2 QB 226 said foreseeability is not essential: the only essential is that the parties made no provision; foreseeability merely helps infer whether provision was made. the materials contrasts *The Eugenia* with *Walton Harvey v Walker & Homfrays*.
   - Multi-factor approach in *The Sea Angel* (2007): contract terms; matrix; parties' objectively assessed knowledge and assumptions about risk at formation; nature of the supervening event; and their reasonable calculations as to possible performance in the new circumstances.
7. **Fault (self-induced frustration).** A party cannot rely on frustration it caused. The burden is on the party alleging self-induction. *The Super Servant Two* (Hobhouse J) identified categories: breach of contract, anticipatory breach, and the power to elect (*Maritime National Fish v Ocean Trawlers* [1935] AC 524: the owner chose which vessel to allocate its limited number of licences to, so the resulting non-performance was its election). Lord Russell in *Joseph Constantine Steamship v Imperial Smelting* [1942] AC 154 warned that not every degree of responsibility makes frustration self-induced: the range runs from the scuttler to the prima donna who sits in a draught and loses her voice.
8. **Categories of frustrating event** (overlapping):
   - *Legal impossibility*: supervening legislation or government intervention. *Metropolitan Water Board* (reservoir construction halted by a ministry order in WWI); *Fibrosa* [1943] AC 32 (trading with the enemy made illegal); *Denny, Mott & Dickson v Fraser* (1944); *Amalgamated Investment v John Walker* [1976]; *BP Exploration v Hunt (No 2)* [1982]. Illegality arising after formation frustrates the contract.
   - *Physical impossibility*: death or incapacity in personal service contracts (*Stubbs v Holywell Railway*; *Marshall v Harland & Wolff* [1972]; *Notcutt v Universal Equipment* [1986]), whereas non-personal performance is not necessarily frustrated (*Phillips v Alhambra Palace* [1901]); destruction of the subject matter (*Taylor v Caldwell*; *Appleby v Myers*; *National Carriers*); failure of supplies; delay.
   - *Impossibility of purpose*: the purpose must be common to both parties and be the basis of the contract, and the event must radically alter obligations (*Krell v Henry* [1903] 2 KB 740, coronation procession cancelled; contrast *Herne Bay Steam Boat v Hutton* [1903] 2 KB 683).
9. **Supplies.** For specific goods, Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 7 avoids the agreement where the goods perish without fault before risk passes. For unascertained goods the source of supply is normally the supplier's risk; frustration is possible only where a particular source intended by *both* parties fails without fault (*Healy v Howlett* [1917] 1 KB 337, on appropriation of mackerel, is cited here).
10. **Delay.** Lord Roskill in *The Nema* [1982] AC 724: one may need to wait on events, but businessmen are "entitled to know where they stand"; decide by informed judgment of what has occurred and what is likely, as a question of degree. Lord Parmoor in *Dick Kerr*: an indefinite, indeterminate burden would mean performance under a "different contract". In *The Eugenia* the cargo was unaffected by delay, so no frustration.
11. **Leases and land.** Rarely frustrated; possible if a lease is short and its purpose known to both. *National Carriers*: a ten-year warehouse lease was not frustrated by a two-year closure of the only access road. Also cited: *Canary Wharf (BP4) T1 v European Medicines Agency* [2019] EWHC 335 (Ch); *Bank of New York Mellon v Cine-UK* [2021] EWHC 1013 (QB).

## Effect of frustration
- Automatic discharge: both parties are released from future performance and from liability for non-performance. Neither can elect to continue and the court cannot keep the contract alive by adjusting it.
- *Common law position (superseded by the 1943 Act)*: money paid or payable: loss lay where it fell (*Chandler v Webster* [1904] 1 KB 493), softened by recovery for total failure of consideration (*Fibrosa*); non-money benefits: *Appleby v Myers* (1867).
- *Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943*:
  - **s 1(2)**: sums paid before discharge are recoverable and sums payable cease to be payable; but the court may, if just, let the payee retain or recover sums up to the expenses incurred in or for performance. See *Gamerco v ICM/Fair Warning* [1995] 1 WLR 1226.
  - **s 1(3)**: where a party obtained a "valuable benefit" (other than money under s 1(2)) by reason of the other's performance, the other may recover such sum as the court considers just, not exceeding the value of the benefit, having regard to the benefited party's expenses and the effect of the frustrating event on the benefit. See *BP Exploration v Hunt (No 2)*.

## Key authorities
- *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863): origin of the doctrine; implied condition on perishing of the subject matter.
- *Krell v Henry* [1903]: common purpose frustrated (room hired to view coronation).
- *Davis Contractors* [1956]: hardship is not frustration.
- *National Carriers* [1981]: Lord Simon's definition; lease not frustrated.
- *The Super Servant Two* [1990]: narrowness; self-induced frustration.
- *Fibrosa* [1943] and the 1943 Act: restitution after discharge.

## Debate and critique
a further reading shows the live questions: whether discharge should be automatic for both parties (McKendrick, "Frustration: Automatic Discharge of Both Parties?"), the relationship between force majeure clauses and frustration (McKendrick; Bridge on frustration clauses), the "coronation cases" (McElroy and Williams; Brownsword), economic analyses of excuse (Posner and Rosenfield), increased expense (Beatson), and recent work on frustration and COVID-19 and Brexit (Morgan, MacMillan, Schwartz, McKendrick). The sources cited give the titles only, so no argument is attributed beyond those headings. A recurring tension is between certainty (the narrowness and the "different thing" test) and fairness, since the all-or-nothing discharge gives courts no power to adjust the contract.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an express clause (force majeure, hardship)? Construe it and see whether it covers the event. If so the clause governs.
2. Was the event after formation and outside both parties' control? If self-induced (breach, election), frustration is barred.
3. Which category: legal, physical or purpose impossibility? Is it merely more expensive or onerous (no) or radically different (possible)?
4. Foreseeability and implied risk allocation (building, price, currency risks sit with the contractor or party).
5. Delay: how long, and how much longer is likely; the *Nema* test.
6. Leases or land: very high bar.
7. If frustrated: apply s 1(2) for money (expenses allowance), s 1(3) for valuable benefits; for goods check SGA s 7.
