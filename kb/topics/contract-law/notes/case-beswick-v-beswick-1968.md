# Beswick v Beswick [1968] AC 58 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the House of Lords judgment itself was not read. Check the report ([1968] AC 58;
> also cited [1967] UKHL 2) before citing any speech or paragraph. The full bench is not confirmed (see Overview).

## Overview
House of Lords, judgment 29 June 1967, reported [1968] AC 58. Lord Reid gave the leading speech (per one source). The
House refused to accept that a third party can generally sue on a contract made for their benefit, but upheld an
order of specific performance obtained by the widow as administratrix of her husband's estate. Sources differ on the
exact membership of the bench, so only the leading speech is attributed here.

## Facts
Peter Beswick, an elderly coal merchant in poor health, agreed to transfer his business to his nephew. In return the
nephew promised to pay Peter (for his lifetime) and, after Peter's death, to pay an annuity to Peter's widow. Peter
died; the nephew made one payment to the widow and then stopped. The widow sued both in her personal capacity and as
administratrix of Peter's estate, seeking specific performance. The Court of Appeal, in a judgment led by Lord
Denning MR, had been willing to let a third party enforce a contract made for their benefit.

## Held
- **Personal claim failed.** Mrs Beswick, not being a party to the contract, could not enforce the promise in her own
  right. Section 56(1) of the Law of Property Act 1925 did not assist her: it lets a person who is not named take the
  benefit of a covenant in appropriate cases, but it does not make a non-party able to enforce a contract.
- **Representative claim succeeded.** As administratrix she stood in the shoes of the contracting promisee (Peter's
  estate) and could enforce the nephew's promise. The House upheld **specific performance** of the annuity promise.
- **Why specific performance.** Damages would have been inadequate: the estate's own loss was only nominal, whereas the
  nephew had received the business, so an award of damages would have left the promise unenforced. (Sources frame this
  as the remedy that produces a just result for the intended beneficiary.)
- The nephew's appeal was dismissed, so the result of the Court of Appeal was upheld although the House disagreed with
  the broad reasoning that third parties can sue.

## Effect
- The case is a leading statement of the common law privity rule: a non-party cannot enforce a contract even though
  it was made for their benefit.
- It also shows a route around privity: the **promisee** (or their personal representative) can enforce the promise,
  and specific performance can give the third party the practical benefit.
- The Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 later gave third parties statutory rights to enforce in defined
  circumstances.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the third party who is to receive the benefit and whether they are named or identified in the contract.
2. Start with the 1999 Act (s 1: express term, or term purporting to confer a benefit, unless the parties did not
   intend it to be enforceable). If it applies, the third party can sue directly.
3. If the Act is excluded or does not apply, apply the common law: the third party cannot sue in their own right, but
   the promisee may sue (and a deceased promisee's estate through the personal representative).
4. Consider whether damages are an adequate remedy for the promisee. Where the promisee has little or no loss, argue
   for specific performance as in *Beswick* rather than nominal damages.
5. Do not rely on s 56 LPA 1925 for a non-party's contractual claim.
