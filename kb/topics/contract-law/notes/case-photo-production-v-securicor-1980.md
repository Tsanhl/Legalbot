# Photo Production Ltd v Securicor Transport Ltd [1980] AC 827 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1980] AC 827 (also [1980] 1 All ER 556;
> [1980] UKHL 2) before citing. The sources disagree on how the fire started and on the amount of the loss, so those
> details are left out.

## Overview
House of Lords, 14 February 1980. Panel: Lord Wilberforce, Lord Diplock, Lord Salmon, Lord Keith of Kinkel and Lord
Scarman; Lord Wilberforce delivered the leading speech and Lord Diplock a separate speech. The appeal was allowed
unanimously (per one source). The House held that an exclusion clause is not struck down by a rule of law merely because the breach is
"fundamental". Whether the clause covers the breach is a question of construction, and it overruled *Harbutt's
"Plasticine" v Wayne Tank*.

## Facts
Photo Production engaged Securicor under a 1968 agreement to make night-time visits to its factory to check the
premises, for a small weekly charge. During a visit, Securicor's employee (a patrolman named Musgrove) started a fire
that spread and destroyed the factory, causing a loss of several hundred thousand pounds. The trial judge could not
determine the employee's intention. The contract contained a clause under which Securicor was not to be responsible
for an injurious act or default of its employees unless it could have foreseen and avoided it through due diligence.
Photo Production argued that the breach was so serious ("fundamental") that the clause could not protect Securicor.
The Court of Appeal (Lord Denning MR, Shaw and Waller LJJ) had accepted that argument, relying on *Harbutt's
Plasticine*.

## Held
- The House of Lords allowed Securicor's appeal and restored the trial judge's decision: the exclusion clause covered
  the loss.
- There is no rule of law that a fundamental breach automatically deprives a party of an exclusion clause. Whether the
  clause applies depends on the true construction of the contract. Lord Wilberforce rejected the doctrine of
  fundamental breach as a rule of law; Lord Diplock treated the question as one of contractual construction. The House
  overruled *Harbutt's Plasticine*, on which the Court of Appeal had relied.
- Where parties negotiate at arm's length and set out who should bear the risks, the courts should be unwilling to
  interfere (a principle one source draws from the case).

## Effect
- It replaced the "rule of law" version of fundamental breach with a construction approach: look at the wording, in
  context.
- Statutory control now matters more: the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 apply to
  limit exclusion clauses, so a clause that passes construction may still be tested for reasonableness or fairness.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Ask whether the clause, properly construed, covers the breach that happened, however serious it is.
2. Do not argue that a "fundamental breach" automatically defeats the clause.
3. Consider whether the clause is subject to UCTA 1977 (s 2, s 3, reasonableness under s 11) or, in a consumer
   contract, the Consumer Rights Act 2015.
4. Note the commercial context: parties dealing at arm's length who have allocated risk will find the clause harder
   to attack.
