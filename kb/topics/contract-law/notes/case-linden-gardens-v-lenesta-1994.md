# Linden Gardens Trust Ltd v Lenesta Sludge Disposals Ltd; St Martin's Property Corp Ltd v Sir Robert McAlpine & Sons Ltd [1994] 1 AC 85 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1994] 1 AC 85 (also [1993] 3 WLR 408;
> [1993] 3 All ER 417; [1993] UKHL 4) before citing paragraphs.

## Overview
House of Lords, conjoined appeals, judgment 22 July 1993. Panel (as named by the sources): Lord Keith, Lord Bridge, Lord
Griffiths, Lord Ackner and Lord Browne-Wilkinson; Lord Browne-Wilkinson gave the principal speech. Two points: (1) a
contractual prohibition on assignment is effective and not contrary to public policy, and (2) in a building contract
the original employer can, in the circumstances of *St Martin's*, recover substantial damages even though it had
transferred the property to others.

## Facts
- **Linden Gardens.** Stock Conversion, a lessee, employed contractors in 1979 to remove asbestos from its building.
  The building contract (a JCT form) contained clause 17, barring the employer from assigning the contract without the
  contractor's written consent. Stock Conversion transferred its leases to Linden Gardens in 1985 to 1986 and in
  January 1987 purported to assign its rights of action against the contractors, without their consent. Linden Gardens
  was substituted as claimant. The Court of Appeal had held the assignment effective.
- **St Martin's.** St Martin's commissioned a shop development under a JCT contract with Sir Robert McAlpine (dated
  October 1974, with the same clause 17). It transferred its interest and the benefit of the contract to a related
  company in 1976 without consent. Defects were found after completion; remedial cost was put at about £800,000.

## Held
- **Assignment (Linden Gardens).** The House allowed the contractors' appeal. Clause 17 covered both rights to future
  performance and accrued causes of action, and was valid: a party can have a genuine commercial interest in
  choosing with whom it deals, so the prohibition was not contrary to public policy. The purported assignment did not
  vest the contractual rights in Linden Gardens.
- **Third-party loss (St Martin's).** McAlpine's appeal was dismissed: the original employer could recover
  substantial damages. The leading approach, in Lord Browne-Wilkinson's speech, adapted the exception recognised in
  *The Albazero* (carriage of goods) to building contracts where the parties contemplated that the property would be
  occupied or acquired by others who could not themselves sue. Lord Keith agreed with that reasoning. Lord Griffiths
  took a broader view, that the employer's own loss (the cost of putting the work right) does not depend on holding a
  proprietary interest at the date of breach.

## Effect
- Anti-assignment clauses are enforced; check the clause wording before concluding an assignment works.
- The "narrow ground" (an extension of the *Albazero* exception) and the "broad ground" (Lord Griffiths' view) are
  the two routes later discussed in *Alfred McAlpine v Panatown*.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. If a contract right has been assigned, check for an anti-assignment term and ask whether it is clear enough to
   cover accrued claims; if so the purported assignment gives the assignee nothing against the contractor.
2. Where the employer has parted with the property but the contractor knew others would own or occupy it, ask
   whether the employer can recover substantial damages for the defective work (*St Martin's*).
3. Check whether the third party has its own direct remedy, which may displace this route (see *Panatown*).
