# Terms of the Contract (express terms, incorporation, implied terms, classification)

## Overview
Once a contract exists, the next questions are what it actually contains and what each obligation is worth. The module frames this as three linked questions: (1) is a given statement a term at all (rather than a mere representation or a "puff")? (2) what does the term mean, and so has it been broken? and (3) what kind of term is it (condition, warranty or innominate term), which fixes the consequence of breach. Terms may be **express** (actually agreed) or **implied** (by custom, by the courts in fact or in law, or by statute). This note covers identification, incorporation, parol evidence and collateral contracts, implied terms and an outline of classification. Interpretation has its own note (`interpretation-of-contracts.md`), and the consequences of classification are covered in `breach-and-termination.md`. The law stated is that of England and Wales.

## The law

### 1. Term or mere representation?
1. **The test is the parties' intention, objectively assessed.** Whether a pre-contractual statement became a term depends on whether the parties intended it to form part of the contract (*Bannerman v White* (1861)). Lord Moulton in *Heilbut, Symons & Co v Buckleton* [1913] AC 30 said intention "can only be deduced from the totality of the evidence". Statements that are not terms may still be actionable misrepresentations (see `misrepresentation.md`), and some statements are commendatory "puffs" with no legal effect.
2. **Guiding factors** (no fixed order, and they may point in different directions):
   - *Importance / reliance.* The more important the statement was to the recipient, and the more clearly that importance was made known to the maker, the likelier it is a term: *Bannerman v White* (buyer of hops said he would not even ask the price if sulphur had been used); *Couchman v Hill* [1947] KB 554 (oral assurance prevailed over a written sale catalogue; the materials dates this case 1957); *Schawel v Reade* [1913] 2 IR 64 (buyer stopped examining a horse when told it was sound).
   - *Timing.* A long gap between statement and contract suggests a representation: *Routledge v McKay* [1954] 1 WLR 615 (a week's gap in the sale of a motorcycle was enough to deny contractual intent). How long is "long" depends on the complexity of the deal.
   - *Special skill or knowledge.* A statement by someone with special knowledge, or the better means of verifying it, is more likely a term: *Dick Bentley Productions Ltd v Harold Smith (Motors) Ltd* [1965] 2 All ER 65 (dealers' statement about a Bentley's mileage was a term); contrast *Oscar Chess v Williams* [1957] 1 All ER 325 (a private owner's statement of a car's age was not a term because the buyer was the motor dealer who could have checked); also *De Lassalle v Guildford* [1901] 2 KB 215, *Esso Petroleum Co Ltd v Mardon* [1976] QB 801 (statement about petrol "throughput" from the party with expertise held a term), *Harling v Eddy* [1951] 2 KB 739 (owner who "absolutely guaranteed" a heifer).
   - *Reduction to writing.* If a statement is left out of a later written contract, that suggests it was not meant to be a term (*Heilbut v Buckleton*). But a contract may be partly written and partly oral: *J Evans & Son (Portsmouth) Ltd v Andrea Merzario Ltd* [1976] 1 WLR 1078 (oral promise that containers would be shipped below deck overrode the written conditions). In *Evans*, Lord Denning said much of *Heilbut* was "entirely out of date".
3. **Why it matters.** Proving a term can be better than suing for misrepresentation. Inducement need not be shown, the statement need not be one of fact, and a written term may be easier to prove. But s 2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967 is an attractive alternative because it reverses the burden of proof on negligence (Devenney, Concentrate Q&A).

### 2. Incorporation of express terms
4. **By signature.** A party who signs a contractual document is bound by its terms whether or not they read it, unless there was fraud or misrepresentation: *L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394 (Scrutton LJ). See also *Gallie v Lee* [1971] AC 1004, which the textbook cites as *Saunders v Anglia Building Society* [1971] AC 1004 in discussing non est factum. Exceptions in the textbook:
   - *Misrepresentation of the document's effect:* *Curtis v Chemical Cleaning and Dyeing Co Ltd* [1951] 1 KB 805 (customer told the clause only covered beads and sequins; the cleaners could not rely on the wider clause for a stain).
   - *Non est factum*, for a signer who could not understand the document through no carelessness of their own.
5. **By reasonable notice** (unsigned documents), three requirements (McKendrick):
   - (a) *Timing.* Notice must come at or before the time of contracting: *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532 (notice in a hotel bedroom came after the contract was made at reception); *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163 (the contract was concluded when the motorist drove up to the automatic machine, so terms on the ticket and inside the car park came too late).
   - (b) *A contractual document.* The terms must be in, or referred to in, a document a reasonable person would expect to carry contractual terms: *Chapelton v Barry UDC* [1940] 1 KB 532 (a deckchair ticket was a mere receipt).
   - (c) *Reasonable steps.* The party relying on the term must have taken reasonable steps to bring it to the other's attention: *Parker v South Eastern Railway* (1877) 2 CPD 416; *Thompson v London Midland & Scottish Railway Co* [1930] 1 KB 41 (a reference on the front of the ticket was enough). The focus is on what the party relying on the term did, not on whether the recipient read it.
6. **Onerous or unusual terms need more.** The more onerous or unusual the term, the more must be done to draw it to the other party's attention: *Thornton* (Lord Denning's "red hand" remark, first made in *J Spurling Ltd v Bradshaw* [1956] 1 WLR 461); *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433 (a £5 per day holding fee per transparency, totalling £3,783.50, was not incorporated because it had not been fairly brought to the other party's attention; a quantum meruit was awarded instead). The principle applies to commercial and consumer contracts alike (*Kaye v Nu Skin UK Ltd* [2009] EWHC 3509 (Ch)). The hurdle for showing that a term is onerous or unusual is high, and the authorities "do not always agree" (*Goodlife Foods Ltd v Hall Fire Protection Ltd* [2018] EWCA Civ 1371).
7. **By course of dealing.** Terms may be incorporated where there has been a regular and consistent course of dealing such that a reasonable person would know one party only contracts on those terms:
   - *Henry Kendall & Sons v William Lillico & Sons Ltd* [1969] 2 AC 31 (the materials calls this *Hardwick Game Farm v Suffolk Agricultural Poultry Producers Association*): three or four transactions a month over three years was enough.
   - *Hollier v Rambler Motors (AMC) Ltd* [1972] 2 QB 71: three or four transactions over five years ("not quite one dealing a year") between a consumer and a garage was not enough.
   - *McCutcheon v David MacBrayne Ltd* [1964] 1 WLR 125: failed because past practice was inconsistent (sometimes a risk note was signed, sometimes not).
   - *British Crane Hire Corp Ltd v Ipswich Plant Hire Ltd* [1975] QB 303: between parties in the same trade and of equal bargaining power, terms were incorporated on the basis of their common understanding of standard trade conditions, even though there were only two previous dealings.

### 3. The parol evidence rule and collateral contracts
8. **Parol evidence rule.** Where the parties have recorded their whole agreement in writing, there is a presumption that the document is the whole contract and extrinsic evidence cannot add to, vary or contradict it. Lord Hobhouse in *Shogun Finance Ltd v Hudson* [2004] 1 AC 919 called this rule "fundamental to the mercantile law"; in his words, "the bargain is the document". See also *City and Westminster Properties (1934) Ltd v Mudd* [1959] Ch 129.
9. **Exceptions** listed in the materials. Evidence may be admitted:
   - to establish implied terms;
   - where the writing was not intended to be the entire agreement;
   - to show that the contract is not operative;
   - to challenge validity for fraud, misrepresentation, mistake, duress or illegality;
   - to prove a collateral contract.
   The Concentrate Q&A describes the rule as "surrounded by countless exceptions".
10. **Collateral contracts.** A promise not incorporated into the main contract may still be enforceable as a separate contract, with the consideration being entry into the main contract (Lord Moulton in *Heilbut v Buckleton*; *Andrews v Hopkinson* [1957] 1 QB 229, where a dealer's assurance that a car was "a good little bus" was a collateral contract alongside a hire-purchase agreement with a finance company). Lord Moulton warned that such contracts "must from their very nature be rare", are "viewed with suspicion", and must be proved strictly, including a clear intention to contract. Otherwise oral side agreements would undermine written contracts.

### 4. Implied terms
11. **General approach.** Freedom of contract means the parties set their own terms, but terms may be implied in specific circumstances (the materials cites *Ivey v Genting Casinos UK Ltd* [2017] UKSC 67). A term can never be implied if it contradicts an express term (*Hutton v Warren* (1836) 1 M&W 466; also Lord Simon's fifth condition in *BP Refinery*).
12. **Implied by custom.** A term may be implied from the custom or usage of a trade or market if the custom is "notorious, certain and reasonable" and not excluded by the contract (*Hutton v Warren*; *Cunliffe-Owen v Teather and Greenwood* [1967] 1 WLR 1421).
13. **Implied in fact** (a term in a particular contract, based on the parties' presumed intention, objectively assessed):
   - *Business efficacy:* *The Moorcock* (1889) 14 PD 64. The wharf owners were held to have impliedly undertaken to take reasonable care to check that the river bed alongside the jetty would not damage a vessel moored there. See also *Reigate v Union Manufacturing Co (Ramsbottom) Ltd* [1918] 1 KB 592.
   - *Officious bystander:* MacKinnon LJ in *Shirlaw v Southern Foundries (1926) Ltd* [1939] 2 KB 206. The term must be so obvious that if a bystander suggested it, the parties would "testily suppress him with a common 'Oh, of course!'"
   - *BP Refinery (Westernport) Pty Ltd v Shire of Hastings* (Lord Simon) gave five conditions. The term must be (1) reasonable and equitable, (2) necessary for business efficacy, (3) so obvious it goes without saying, (4) capable of clear expression, and (5) consistent with the express terms.
   - *AG of Belize v Belize Telecom Ltd* [2009] UKPC 10: Lord Hoffmann treated implication as part of interpretation, asking only whether the term "would spell out in express words what the instrument, read against the relevant background, would reasonably be understood to mean".
   - *Marks & Spencer plc v BNP Paribas Securities Services Trust Co (Jersey) Ltd* [2015] UKSC 72 (now the leading case). Lord Neuberger for the majority restated the traditional, strict approach:
     - The test is **necessity, not reasonableness**. A term is not implied just because it seems fair or because the parties would have agreed to it if asked.
     - The question is judged by reference to notional reasonable people in the parties' position at the time of contracting, not with hindsight.
     - Business necessity and obviousness are **alternatives**: only one need be satisfied, though it would be rare for only one to be met.
     - The officious bystander's question must be framed "with the utmost care".
     - Necessity is not "absolute necessity". A helpful formulation is whether, without the term, the contract would lack "commercial or practical coherence".
     - Interpretation and implication are **different processes** governed by different rules, and express terms must be interpreted first. *Belize* was a "characteristically inspired discussion", not "authoritative guidance". Lord Carnwath remained more sympathetic to *Belize*.
   - *Errington v Errington* [1952] 1 KB 290 is listed in the materials under business efficacy, without further detail.
14. **Implied in law** (a term attached to every contract of a defined type as a "necessary incident" of the relationship, unless excluded):
   - *Liverpool City Council v Irwin* [1977] AC 239: a landlord of a council tower block was under an implied obligation to take reasonable care to keep the common parts (lifts, lighting, rubbish chutes) in reasonable repair. The obligation was qualified, not absolute, and the council was not in breach. The majority used "necessity"; Lord Cross used reasonableness.
   - *Scally v Southern Health and Social Services Board* [1992] 1 AC 294: an employer was under an implied obligation to inform employees of a valuable contractual right. Lord Bridge distinguished business-efficacy implication from implication based on "wider considerations" for a "definable category of contractual relationship". The term was confined to limited circumstances.
   - *Crossley v Faithful & Gould Holdings Ltd* [2004] EWCA Civ 293: Dyson LJ called necessity "elusive" and preferred to recognise that such terms raise "questions of reasonableness, fairness and balancing competing policy considerations". He refused to imply a duty on employers to take care of employees' economic well-being.
   - *Geys v Société Générale* [2012] UKSC 63 (Lady Hale, cited in *M&S*) distinguished the two types of implied term.
15. **Implied by statute.**
   - *Sale of Goods Act 1979, ss 12–15* (title, description, satisfactory quality and fitness for purpose, sample), as amended by the Sale and Supply of Goods Act 1994.
   - *Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982*, e.g. s 13, reasonable care and skill in a business supply of services.
   - *Consumer Rights Act 2015* for trader–consumer contracts (e.g. s 9 satisfactory quality; s 49 reasonable care and skill). The CRA does not use the condition/warranty labels and instead gives a menu of remedies.
   On a sale of goods, the courts have read s 13 narrowly, as covering words that identify the goods (*Ashington Piggeries Ltd v Christopher Hill Ltd* [1972] AC 441), and a forged painting was held of merchantable quality in *Harlingdon and Leinster Enterprises Ltd v Christopher Hull Fine Art Ltd* [1991] 1 QB 564.

### 5. Classification of terms (outline)
16. **Contingent conditions vs promissory terms.** Some "conditions" are events on which obligations depend rather than promises (*Behn v Burness* (1862) 1 B&S 877; Lord Denning in *Trans Trust SPRL v Danubian Trading Co Ltd* [1952] 2 QB 297: a stipulation for a credit may be a condition precedent to formation of the contract, or a condition precedent to the seller's obligation to deliver).
17. **Conditions and warranties.** A condition is an essential term whose breach allows termination. A warranty is subsidiary and gives damages only. the materials contrasts *Poussard v Spiers* (1876) 1 QB 351 with *Bettini v Gye* (1876) 1 QBD 183 as the classic pair on distinguishing a condition from a warranty. the materials gives no facts for either case.
18. **Innominate terms.** For many terms, the remedy depends on how serious the breach is: does it "deprive the party who has further undertakings still to perform of substantially the whole benefit" of the contract? (Diplock LJ, *Hong Kong Fir Shipping v Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha* [1962] 2 QB 26).
19. **The modern default.** Anson's Law of Contract (cited in the materials) says any term is probably intermediate unless it is a condition:
   - by statute (SGA 1979; *The Hansa Nord* [1976] QB 44);
   - by judicial decision (*The Mihalis Angelos* [1971] 1 QB 164);
   - by express designation (*Lombard North Central plc v Butterworth* [1987] QB 527, subject to *Schuler AG v Wickman* [1974] AC 235); or
   - by implication from the nature of the contract (*Bunge Corp v Tradax* [1981] 1 WLR 711).
   Full treatment is in `breach-and-termination.md`.

## Key authorities
- *Heilbut, Symons & Co v Buckleton* [1913] AC 30 (HL): intention, deduced from all the evidence, decides whether a statement is a term; collateral contracts must be strictly proved.
- *Bannerman v White* (1861): buyer made clear that sulphur treatment was decisive; the seller's assurance was a term.
- *Routledge v McKay* [1954] 1 WLR 615: a week's interval meant the statement was not a term.
- *Oscar Chess v Williams* [1957] (CA): a non-expert seller's statement to an expert dealer was not a term. Contrast *Dick Bentley* [1965] (CA): an expert dealer's mileage statement was a term.
- *J Evans & Son v Andrea Merzario* [1976] (CA): an oral promise was part of a partly written contract and overrode the printed terms.
- *L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394: a signer is bound whether or not they read the document. Criticised by J R Spencer (below).
- *Curtis v Chemical Cleaning* [1951] (CA): a misrepresentation about a clause's scope prevents reliance on it.
- *Olley v Marlborough Court* [1949]; *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* [1971] (CA): notice that comes after formation is too late; the contract is made at the automatic machine.
- *Chapelton v Barry UDC* [1940] (CA): a ticket that is a mere receipt is not a contractual document.
- *Parker v South Eastern Railway* (1877) (CA): the reasonable-notice test for ticket cases.
- *Interfoto v Stiletto* [1989] QB 433 (CA): onerous or unusual terms must be fairly and reasonably brought to the other party's attention.
- *Hollier v Rambler Motors* [1972]; *McCutcheon v MacBrayne* [1964] (HL); *Henry Kendall v Lillico* [1969] (HL); *British Crane Hire* [1975] (CA): when a course of dealing or trade understanding incorporates terms.
- *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2004] 1 AC 919 (HL): the parol evidence rule restated (Lord Hobhouse).
- *Andrews v Hopkinson* [1957]: a dealer's assurance was a collateral contract.
- *Hutton v Warren* (1836): terms implied by custom; no implication against an express term.
- *The Moorcock* (1889) (CA); *Shirlaw v Southern Foundries* [1939] (CA): the business efficacy and officious bystander tests.
- *AG of Belize v Belize Telecom* [2009] UKPC 10: implication treated as interpretation. Now confined by *M&S*.
- *Marks & Spencer v BNP Paribas* [2015] UKSC 72: strict necessity; the two tests are alternatives; implication is distinct from interpretation.
- *Liverpool CC v Irwin* [1977] AC 239 (HL); *Scally* [1992] 1 AC 294 (HL); *Crossley v Faithful & Gould* [2004] (CA): terms implied in law and the policy basis for them.
- Sale of Goods Act 1979, ss 12–15; Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982, s 13; Consumer Rights Act 2015 (e.g. ss 9, 49): statutory implied terms.

## Debate and critique
- **Signature rule.** J R Spencer, 'Signature, Consent, and the Rule in L'Estrange v Graucob' [1973] CLJ 104, argues the case could have gone the other way on ordinary objective-agreement principles. The clause was in tiny print on brown paper, in a confusing form. A party is not bound by apparent consent where the other party caused the misunderstanding (compare *Scriven v Hindley*). A similar line was taken in Ontario in *Tilden Rent-A-Car Co v Clendinning* (1978) 83 DLR (3d) 400, where reasonable steps were needed to alert the signer to onerous terms. Others say the answer to unfair signed terms lies in statutory control rather than in weakening the signature rule.
- **Incorporation as covert control of content.** McKendrick observes that the *Interfoto* "onerous or unusual" rule is in effect a "second-best alternative" to a direct power to strike down unfair terms. UCTA 1977 did not cover the clause in *Interfoto*, so the court regulated it through incorporation. That invites the question why incorporation should depend on the content of a term.
- **Practical limits of "red hands".** Commercial documents do not use red hands. The courts have struggled to say which terms are onerous or unusual (*Goodlife Foods*), and the hurdle is high.
- **Basis of implication in fact.** Lord Hoffmann's *Belize* approach (implication as interpretation) caused academic controversy and judicial uncertainty (e.g. Arden LJ in *Stena Line*).
  - Hooley ('Implied Terms After Belize Telecom' (2014) 73 CLJ 315) welcomed it, arguing that the traditional tests are no more certain.
  - Kramer (2004) 63 CLJ 384 and McLauchlan [2014] LMCLQ 203 defend the link between implication and construction. Courtney and Carter (2014) 31 JCL 151 critically consider it.
  - *M&S* reasserted necessity and separated the two processes. Peel, 'Terms Implied in Fact' (2016) 132 LQR 531, examines the developments, as do the optional readings: O'Sullivan, 'Silence is Golden: Implied Terms in the Supreme Court' (2016) 75 CLJ 199; McCunn, 'Belize it or not' (2016) 79 MLR 1090.
  - The essay question in the Q&A text is whether the distinction between implication in fact and in law is clear while each process is uncertain in operation.
- **Basis of implication in law.** Terms implied in law rest on policy rather than intention.
  - Peden, 'Policy Concerns Behind Implication of Terms in Law' (2001) 117 LQR 459, lists the policy factors: who can better bear the loss, bargaining positions, the size of the burden, and the effect on society. The list is not closed and the weights are not fixed.
  - Dyson LJ in *Crossley* openly accepted policy reasoning.
  - Phang ([1993] JBL 242) argues that *Scally*'s narrowly defined category blurs the line between implication in fact and in law. Phang ([1998] JBL 1) traces the history of the two tests.
- **Parol evidence rule.** The rule is now only a presumption, and its exceptions are so wide that the Concentrate Q&A describes it as "surrounded by countless exceptions". That raises the question of how much the rule still does. Optional reading: Marston, 'The Parol Evidence Rule: The Law Commission Speaks' (1986) 45 CLJ 192.
- **Collateral contracts** (optional reading: Wedderburn, 'Collateral Contracts' (1959) 17 CLJ 58) can be used to get round the parol evidence rule. This is why Lord Moulton in *Heilbut* insisted that they be strictly proved: otherwise written contracts would lose their authority.
- **Certainty vs justice in classification.**
  - The condition/warranty split gives certainty but lets a party escape a bad bargain for a trivial breach. *Arcos v Ronaasen* [1933] AC 470 was a market-motivated rejection of staves that were only slightly too thick. It was recently applied in *Local Boy'z Ltd v Malu NV* [2021] EWHC 2439 (Comm).
  - Innominate terms give proportionate results but at the cost of predictability.
  - Carter, 'Classification of Contractual Terms: The New Orthodoxy' (1981) 40 CLJ 219, is optional reading. See `breach-and-termination.md` for the fuller debate.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Find the contract.** Is it oral, written, or partly both? Check for an entire agreement clause or a written document that might exclude prior statements (parol evidence rule). Can a collateral contract be argued instead?
2. **For each pre-contractual statement, is it a term?**
   - Importance stressed to the maker?
   - Time gap?
   - Which party had the expertise or means of checking? Watch for the expert *buyer* (*Oscar Chess*), which can cut against the claimant.
   - Left out of the later writing?
   - If not a term, flag misrepresentation briefly and cross-refer.
3. **Was a written term incorporated?**
   - Signed? Then *L'Estrange*, subject to misrepresentation (*Curtis*) or non est factum.
   - Unsigned? Check (a) timing against the offer and acceptance analysis (*Olley*, *Thornton*); (b) whether the document was contractual (*Chapelton*); (c) reasonable steps (*Parker*); (d) whether the term was onerous or unusual (*Interfoto*, *Thornton*).
   - Course of dealing? Check regularity and consistency (*Hollier* for consumers; *Kendall v Lillico* and *British Crane Hire* for traders).
4. **Can a term be implied?**
   - By statute: SGA 1979 ss 12–15 between businesses; CRA 2015 for consumers; SGSA 1982 for services.
   - By custom: notorious, certain and reasonable?
   - In fact (*M&S*): is it necessary for business efficacy *or* obvious? Is it capable of clear expression and consistent with the express terms? Fairness alone is not enough.
   - In law: is this a recognised category of relationship, with *Liverpool v Irwin*-style necessity or policy?
5. **What does the term mean?** See `interpretation-of-contracts.md`.
6. **Classify the term and state the consequence of breach.** Is it a condition by statute, precedent, express label (subject to *Schuler*) or commercial nature (*Bunge*)? Otherwise it is innominate (*Hong Kong Fir*). Then apply the remedies analysis in `breach-and-termination.md`.
7. **Typical traps:**
   - Treating every pre-contract statement as a term.
   - Ignoring the timing of formation in automatic-machine and hotel cases.
   - Forgetting that statutory implied terms may not help, e.g. *Harlingdon* (forged painting) and the narrow reading of s 13.
   - Treating *Belize* as still stating the law.
   - Calling a term a "condition" just because the contract uses that word.
8. **Facts that change the outcome:**
   - Who had the expertise.
   - Whether the signer was misled about the clause.
   - Whether the notice was given before acceptance.
   - Whether the term was unusual for that trade.
   - How regular the previous dealings were.
   - Whether the contract works without the proposed implied term.
