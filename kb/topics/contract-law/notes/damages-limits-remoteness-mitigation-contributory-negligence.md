# Limits on Contract Damages - Remoteness, Mitigation and Contributory Negligence

## Overview
A claimant whose loss was caused by a breach still may not recover all of it. English law (England and Wales) has three main limits on compensatory damages, covered in the second part of Lectures 7 and 8:
- **Remoteness:** the loss must have been within the parties' reasonable contemplation when they made the contract.
- **Mitigation:** the claimant cannot recover loss it should reasonably have avoided.
- **Contributory negligence:** apportionment under the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945, available only in a narrow class of contract claims.

McKendrick presents all three as showing that the law is not fully committed to protecting the performance interest. Remoteness is the most examined of the three. The leading House of Lords case, *The Achilleas* (2008), left the test uncertain, and later authorities have taken different lines on it.

## The law

### A. Causation (a preliminary step)
- The claimant must show that the breach caused the loss. A breach that is one of two concurrent causes can still ground damages.
- In *Smith, Hogg & Co v Black Sea Insurance* [1940] AC 997 (as discussed in the Concentrate guide), cargo was lost through both bad weather and the ship's unseaworthiness, which was a breach. The breach was enough to found a claim. Had the weather been so extreme that no ship could have survived, damages might not have been recoverable.
- the materials lists two cases on causation and intervening conduct without giving any detail: *Quinn v Buch Bros (Builders) Ltd* [1996] 2 QB 370, cited exactly as the materials gives it, and *Galoo Ltd v Bright Grahame Murray* [1994] 1 WLR 1360.

### B. Remoteness

#### 1. *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341: the two limbs
**Facts:** a carrier delayed taking a mill's broken crankshaft to the engineer for repair. The mill was idle for five extra days. The lost profits were held too remote, because the carrier had not been told that the mill had no spare shaft.

**The rule (Alderson B):** damages are recoverable for loss that either:
- (limb 1) arises "naturally, i.e. according to the usual course of things" from the breach; or
- (limb 2) "may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties at the time they made the contract, as the probable result of the breach of it".

**Special circumstances:**
- They count only if they were communicated to the defendant and so were known to both parties.
- If they were "wholly unknown" to the defendant, he is liable only for the loss that would arise generally.
- The stated reason: if the defendant had known, he could have bargained for special terms about damages.

#### 2. *Victoria Laundry (Windsor) Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd* [1949] 2 KB 528 (CA)
**Facts:** the sellers delivered a boiler for a laundry five months late.

**Held:** the laundry recovered its ordinary lost profits. It could not recover the exceptional profits on lucrative contracts with the Ministry of Supply, which the sellers knew nothing about.

**Asquith LJ's propositions:**
- The recoverable loss is the part of the loss "actually resulting" that was, at the time of the contract, reasonably foreseeable as liable to result from the breach.
- What was foreseeable depends on the knowledge the parties had, or at least the knowledge of the party who later broke the contract.
- That knowledge is of two kinds:
  - imputed knowledge of the "ordinary course of things", which is limb 1;
  - actual knowledge of special circumstances, which is limb 2.
- The defendant need not actually have asked himself what loss would follow. It is enough that, if he had considered the question, he would as a reasonable person have concluded that the loss was liable to result.
- The loss need not be certain, only "likely". A "serious possibility" or a "real danger" is enough.

#### 3. *Koufos v C Czarnikow Ltd (The Heron II)* [1969] 1 AC 350 (HL)
**Facts:** a ship carrying sugar to Basrah arrived nine days late, and in the meantime the market price had fallen. The shipowner knew there was a market for sugar at Basrah.

**Held:** the loss from the fall in price was not too remote.

**The test:**
- Contract requires a higher degree of probability than tort. Burrows' summary, quoted by McKendrick: tort needs a slight possibility of the loss, contract a **serious** possibility.
- Lord Reid used "not unlikely" to mean a degree of probability considerably less than an even chance, but "nevertheless not very unusual and easily foreseeable". Lords Pearce and Upjohn used "a real danger" or "a serious possibility". Lord Morris used "not unlikely to occur" or "liable to result", and Lord Hodson accepted "liable to result".
- Four of their Lordships agreed that the colloquialism "on the cards" should not be used.
- Lord Reid disagreed with *Victoria Laundry* in so far as it went beyond the older authorities.
- Writing extrajudicially, Lord Hoffmann later said *The Heron II* contains "a thesaurus of expressions".

#### 4. *Parsons (Livestock) Ltd v Uttley Ingham & Co Ltd* [1978] QB 791 (CA)
**Facts:** a hopper for storing pig food was installed with its ventilator closed. The nuts went mouldy, and 254 pigs died of E coli.

**Held:** the defendant was liable for the pigs' illness and deaths, but the judges gave different reasons.
- **Lord Denning MR** distinguished physical damage, where he applied a tort-style foreseeability test, from economic loss, where the stricter contract test applied.
- **Scarman and Orr LJJ** rejected that distinction. They framed the question generally: was illness of the pigs a serious possibility if the hopper was unfit for storing their food? On this approach the **type** of loss must be within contemplation, not its extent or the precise way it came about.

**Commentary:**
- Burrows, as quoted by McKendrick: defining the breach more generally makes it less likely that a loss will be judged too remote.
- McKendrick: the broad approach in *Parsons* is hard to reconcile with the line *Victoria Laundry* drew between ordinary and exceptional profits.

#### 5. *Transfield Shipping Inc v Mercator Shipping Inc (The Achilleas)* [2008] UKHL 48, [2009] 1 AC 61
**Facts:**
- The charterers redelivered a ship nine days late.
- The market then fell sharply, so the owners had to accept a lower rate on their next charter. That cost them $8,000 a day over its 191 days, about $1.36m.
- The charterers argued that their liability was only the difference between the market rate and the charter rate for the nine-day overrun, about $158,000.
- It was found that the general understanding in the shipping market was that liability was limited to the overrun.

**Held:** liability was limited to the overrun figure. The reasoning was divided.
- **Lord Hoffmann (with Lord Hope):**
  - Foreseeability is only a prima facie assumption about what the parties intended.
  - That assumption can be displaced where the context, the surrounding circumstances or the general understanding in the market show that a party would not reasonably be regarded as **assuming responsibility** for the loss.
  - Liability rests on interpreting the contract as a whole in its commercial setting. He drew an analogy with *South Australia Asset Management Corp v York Montague Ltd* [1997] AC 191 (SAAMCO).
  - Lord Hope added that assumption of responsibility is "determined by more than" foreseeability. The charterers could neither control nor quantify the loss on the next charter.
- **Lord Rodger and Baroness Hale:**
  - They applied the orthodox test.
  - The loss arose from "extremely volatile" and unusual market conditions, so it was not within the parties' contemplation.
  - McKendrick's objection: what was unforeseen was the **extent** of the loss, not its **kind**.
- **Lord Walker:**
  - He agreed with the reasons of Lords Hoffmann, Hope and Rodger.
  - He found the SAAMCO analogy "helpful" without fully endorsing it.

**Factors that drove the result (McKendrick):**
- reluctance to make the charterers liable for a potentially huge loss which, when they contracted, they could neither know about nor control;
- the market's own understanding of where liability stopped.

#### 6. After *The Achilleas*
- ***Sylvia Shipping Co Ltd v Progress Bulk Carriers Ltd (The Sylvia)*** [2010] EWHC 542 (Comm), Hamblen J:
  - Assumption of responsibility had majority support, but the result is "an amalgam".
  - The orthodox test still governs the great majority of cases.
  - Assumption of responsibility needs specific attention only in "unusual" cases, mainly where the general test would lead to an unquantifiable, unpredictable, uncontrollable or disproportionate liability, or to one contrary to market understanding.
  - McKendrick doubts that this is what Lord Hoffmann intended.
- ***Supershield Ltd v Siemens Building Technologies FE Ltd*** [2010] EWCA Civ 7, [2010] 1 Lloyd's Rep 349:
  - Hadley remains the standard rule, explained as the expectation to be imputed to the parties in the ordinary case.
  - *SAAMCO* and *The Achilleas* show that the contract and its commercial background can sometimes **exclude** liability for loss of a foreseeable kind.
  - The same principle can also be **inclusionary**. If a loss falls within the scope of the duty, it is not too remote even though it would not occur in ordinary circumstances.
- ***John Grimes Partnership Ltd v Gubbins*** [2013] EWCA Civ 37, Sir David Keene:
  - Where the contract is silent, the law in effect implies a term accepting responsibility for losses that are foreseeable as not unlikely.
  - That implied assumption gives way where the nature of the contract or its commercial background makes it inappropriate, as in *The Achilleas*.
  - McKendrick reads this as treating Hadley as a default rule that the parties can contract out of in either direction.
- ***Attorney General of the Virgin Islands v Global Water Associates Ltd*** [2020] UKPC 18, [2021] AC 23 (Privy Council):
  - **Facts:** the government broke a design-and-build agreement by failing to provide a site. The contractor lost the profits it would have earned under a related contract to operate and maintain the plant.
  - **Held:** those losses were not too remote. Both contracts were made on the same day between the same parties for the same plant, and the government knew that performing one would lead into the other.
  - Lord Hodge treated assumption of responsibility as a "further limitation" or "further restriction", relevant to unusual market volatility. He relied mainly on *The Heron II* and *Victoria Laundry*, pushing *The Achilleas* to the margins.
  - At [32]: the type of loss must have been "reasonably contemplated as a serious possibility".
- Singapore's Court of Appeal has taken a different route. McKendrick notes that English courts below the Supreme Court cannot follow it.

### C. Mitigation
1. *British Westinghouse Electric v Underground Electric Railways Co of London* [1912] AC 673. Viscount Haldane LC qualified the compensatory principle with a "duty" on the claimant "of taking all reasonable steps to mitigate the loss consequent on the breach". The claimant cannot claim any loss that is "due to his neglect to take such steps".
2. **"Duty" is a misnomer.** Failing to mitigate is not a wrong, and the claimant cannot be sued for it. The only consequence is that avoidable loss cannot be recovered (Bridge (1989) 105 LQR 398; lecture).
3. **Two aspects** (McKendrick):
   - the claimant must take reasonable steps to minimise its loss;
   - it must not unreasonably increase its loss (*Banco de Portugal v Waterlow* [1932] AC 452).
4. **The standard is not demanding.**
   - Only reasonable steps are required, not the most reasonable course (*Pilkington v Wood* [1953] Ch 770).
   - The contract-breaker cannot insist on onerous steps. Tomlinson J in *Britvic Soft Drinks v Messer* [2002] spoke of a "tender approach" to those placed in a predicament by a breach.
   - The claimant need not take steps it cannot afford (lecture).
   - Whether steps were reasonable is a question of fact.
5. **Typical example.** If a seller fails to deliver, the buyer should buy substitute goods on the market. It cannot claim loss caused by failing to do so. The market rules in the sale of goods reflect this.
6. **Benefits of mitigation are brought into account.**
   - If the claimant's mitigating steps reduce the loss, the defendant gets the benefit.
   - An independent act that does not reduce the particular loss claimed need not be credited. In *Globalia Business Travel v Fulton Shipping* [2017] UKSC 43, the owner did not have to credit the capital gain from selling the ship against its lost hire.
7. **Accepting the contract-breaker's offer.**
   - In *Payzu Ltd v Saunders* [1919] 2 KB 581, the buyers unreasonably rejected the seller's offer to keep supplying silk for cash on delivery. They could not recover the price difference in a rising market. Scrutton LJ said it is generally reasonable, in a commercial contract, to consider and accept a reasonable offer from the party in breach. The position differs for personal service contracts (*Clayton-Greene v De Courville* (1920)).
   - *The Solholt* [1983] 1 Lloyd's Rep 605 went further: buyers who lawfully rejected a late ship should have offered to buy it at the contract price. McKendrick criticises the case on three grounds: it makes the right to reject illusory; it lets the seller keep the market rise; and the loss had already occurred at the date of breach.
   - Later cases put the onus on the contract-breaker to make a properly formulated proposal that the claimant could not reasonably refuse (*Manton Hire v Ash Manor Cheese* [2013] EWCA Civ 548).
8. **Personal service contracts.** A wrongfully dismissed employee must look for comparable work. He need not accept re-engagement after a humiliating dismissal (compare *Payzu* with *Brace v Calder* [1895] 2 QB 253, from the Concentrate guide).
9. **Wider reasonableness principle.** Mitigation can be seen as part of a broader principle that the claimant must act reasonably. Lord Lloyd in *Ruxley* rejected the idea that reasonableness is confined to mitigation.
10. **Limit:** mitigation does not apply where the innocent party affirms and sues for the agreed sum (*White & Carter v McGregor* [1962] AC 413; see `specific-remedies.md`).

### D. Contributory negligence
1. **Statute.** Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945:
   - Section 1(1): where damage results "partly of his own fault and partly of the fault of any other person", the claim is not defeated, but damages are reduced "to such extent as the court thinks just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility for the damage".
   - Proviso (a) preserves any contractual defence. Proviso (b) keeps any contractual limitation of liability as the cap.
   - Section 4 defines "fault" as "negligence, breach of statutory duty or other act or omission which gives rise to a liability in tort or would, apart from this Act, give rise to the defence of contributory negligence".
   - The Act was drafted with tort in mind, so "fault" does not fit contract easily (McKendrick).
2. ***Forsikringsaktieselskapet Vesta v Butcher*** [1988] 3 WLR 565 (CA; affirmed in the HL on a different point). Three categories of claim:
   - **Category 1:** breach of a **strict** contractual duty. The Act does not apply.
   - **Category 2:** breach of a contractual duty of care, with **no** concurrent duty in tort. The Act does not apply.
   - **Category 3:** breach of a contractual duty of care that is **co-extensive with a concurrent duty in negligence**. The Act applies.
3. ***Barclays Bank plc v Fairclough Building Ltd*** [1995] QB 214 (CA):
   - **Facts:** contractors cleaned asbestos roofs with high-pressure hoses and contaminated the building, causing about £4m of remedial work.
   - **Held:** their breaches were of strict obligations (to work to specification and to comply with the Asbestos Regulations). So this was category 1, and the bank's alleged carelessness in failing to warn could not reduce damages.
4. **Reform.** The Law Commission (Law Com No 219, 1993) recommended extending apportionment to category 2. After *Henderson v Merrett Syndicates* [1995] 2 AC 145 accepted concurrent liability in contract and tort, category 2 cases will be rare (lecture).

## Key authorities
- *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341: the two limbs of remoteness.
- *Victoria Laundry v Newman* [1949] 2 KB 528: ordinary profits recoverable, exceptional profits not; Asquith LJ's propositions.
- *The Heron II* [1969] 1 AC 350: "not unlikely" or "serious possibility"; a stricter test than tort.
- *Parsons v Uttley Ingham* [1978] QB 791: the type of loss, not its extent, must be contemplated.
- *The Achilleas* [2008] UKHL 48: assumption of responsibility (Lords Hoffmann and Hope) against the orthodox test (Lord Rodger, Baroness Hale).
- *The Sylvia* [2010] EWHC 542 (Comm): the orthodox test applies in most cases; assumption of responsibility only in unusual ones.
- *Supershield v Siemens* [2010] EWCA Civ 7: scope of duty can include as well as exclude liability.
- *John Grimes Partnership v Gubbins* [2013] EWCA Civ 37: an implied-term rationalisation.
- *AG of the Virgin Islands v Global Water Associates* [2020] UKPC 18: the traditional tests preferred; *The Achilleas* marginalised.
- *SAAMCO* [1997] AC 191: the scope-of-duty analogy.
- Listed in the materials only: *Brown v KMR Services* [1995] 4 All ER 598; *Jackson v Royal Bank of Scotland* [2005] UKHL 3; *Wellesley Partners v Withers* [2015] EWCA Civ 1146.
- *British Westinghouse* [1912] AC 673: the "duty" to mitigate.
- *Payzu v Saunders* [1919] 2 KB 581; *The Solholt* [1983] 1 Lloyd's Rep 605: offers from the contract-breaker.
- *Pilkington v Wood* [1953] Ch 770: only reasonable steps are required.
- Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 ss 1(1) and 4.
- *Vesta v Butcher* [1988] 3 WLR 565: the three categories.
- *Barclays Bank v Fairclough* [1995] QB 214: no apportionment for breach of a strict obligation.

## Debate and critique
- **Why limit recovery at all?** Remoteness allocates risk. A party should be liable only for risks it could price or insure against when it contracted, which is the reasoning in Hadley itself. The Concentrate guide contrasts the layperson's assumption that all loss should be compensated with the economist's view of contract as a tool for allocating risk.
- **Foreseeability or agreement?** Lord Hoffmann, citing Kramer, Tettenborn and Robertson, grounds remoteness in the interpretation of the contract.
  - Robertson, (2008) 28 LS 172, thinks this artificial, because there is seldom evidence of what risks the parties thought they were accepting.
  - Wee, [2010] LMCLQ 150, argues the assumption of responsibility test is at least as uncertain as the test it criticises. He applies it to the facts of Hadley to show this.
  - Kramer, (2009) 125 LQR 408, and Peel, (2009) 125 LQR 6, analyse the "new test". Hunter, (2014) 31 JCL 120, asks "Has The Achilleas Sunk?".
  - Lord Hoffmann's own article, (2010) Edinburgh LR 47, attacks the "high degree of indeterminacy" of the orthodox test.
- **Contract v tort.** Remoteness is stricter in contract ("not unlikely" or "serious possibility") than in tort (reasonable foreseeability). The reason given in Hadley itself is that contracting parties can protect themselves by giving notice and agreeing special terms. Cartwright, "Remoteness of Damage in Contract and Tort: a Reconsideration" [1996] CLJ 488, is on the materials reading list, but the sources do not summarise it. Lord Denning in *Parsons* tried to apply the tort test to physical harm. The majority did not accept that.
- **Level of generality.** Judges' freedom to describe the type of loss broadly (*Parsons*) or narrowly (*Victoria Laundry*, and Lord Rodger in *The Achilleas*) is a large source of unpredictability.
- **Mitigation.** It is a major limit on the performance interest, because the claimant must buy substitute performance rather than wait (McKendrick). On whether it is justified: either the claimant's inaction, not the breach, causes the extra loss, or the defendant reasonably expected the claimant to minimise loss (Concentrate). Bridge says the case-by-case, fact-based approach of *Payzu* and *The Solholt* has created an "intellectual vacuum".
- **Contributory negligence.** It is arguably anomalous that a careless claimant recovers in full against a strict-duty defendant (*Fairclough*), but suffers a reduction where the defendant was only careless. The Law Commission's reform was never implemented.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **List each head of loss separately:** ordinary lost profit, exceptional lost profit, wasted costs, payments to third parties, physical damage, and so on.
2. **Remoteness, for each head:**
   - Would it arise in the ordinary course of things (limb 1)? Think about the kind of business and what any reasonable person in the defendant's position would know (*Heron II*: a carrier knows that markets fluctuate).
   - If not, was the defendant told of the special circumstances **at the time of contracting** (limb 2)? Notice given after the contract is made is irrelevant.
   - Was the **type** of loss a serious possibility (*Parsons*)? Separate ordinary from exceptional profits (*Victoria Laundry*).
   - Is the case unusual, so that liability would be unpredictable, uncontrollable or out of line with market practice? If so, discuss assumption of responsibility (*The Achilleas*, *The Sylvia*, *Global Water*). Remember it can work inclusively too (*Supershield*).
3. **Mitigation:**
   - Did the claimant act reasonably? Could it have bought substitutes, rescheduled deliveries, stored goods, or accepted a reasonable offer from the defendant?
   - Remember that only reasonable steps are required. Expensive or risky steps, and steps the claimant could not afford, are not.
   - Did the mitigating steps reduce the loss? If so, give the defendant credit.
   - Reasonable costs of mitigating are themselves recoverable. For example, a payment to a third contractor to speed up work may be recoverable if it was a reasonable settlement (Concentrate, citing *John Hunt Demolition v ASME* [2007] EWHC 1507 (TCC)).
4. **Contributory negligence:**
   - Classify the broken term. A strict obligation means no reduction. A duty of care with no concurrent tort duty means no reduction. A duty of care co-extensive with a duty in negligence means apportionment under s 1.
   - Professional negligence (surveyors, solicitors, accountants) is the usual category 3 case.
5. **Common traps:**
   - judging remoteness at the date of breach instead of the date of contract;
   - requiring the extent of the loss, rather than its type, to be foreseeable;
   - treating *The Achilleas* as the general test;
   - calling mitigation a duty whose breach can be sued on;
   - applying the 1945 Act to a strict duty.
