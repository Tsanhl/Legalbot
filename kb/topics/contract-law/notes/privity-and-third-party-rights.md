# Privity and Third Party Rights

## Overview
Privity of contract decides who can sue and be sued on a contract. At common law (England & Wales) it has two parts. (i) A third party cannot enforce a contract even if the parties meant to benefit her. (ii) The parties cannot impose a burden on a third party by their contract. The benefit side caused real injustice: it defeated the parties' intentions and left a "black hole" where the person who suffered loss could not sue and the person who could sue had no loss. It was therefore reformed by the **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999**, based on Law Commission Report No 242 (1996). The Act does not abolish privity. It adds a statutory route alongside the old common law devices and promisee remedies, so the topic needs the rule, the Act (especially s 1), the surviving exceptions, and the promisee's remedies for third-party loss. Note: the text of the 1999 Act is not among the shared official-sources files; the statutory wording below is taken from the textbook extracts.

## The law

### 1. The common law rule
1. **Statement of the doctrine** (lecture): (i) a third party cannot sue for a benefit under a contract even if the parties intended to benefit her; (ii) the parties cannot impose a burden on a third party.
2. **Link with consideration.** Rule (i) follows from the traditional requirement that the claimant must be the promisee and must have given consideration ("consideration must move from the promisee"; see `consideration.md`).
3. **Justifications** given for the bar: the exclusivity of contracts, the priority of buyers, and floodgates concerns (lecture).
4. *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861) 1 B & S 393. The fathers of a bride and groom each promised the other to pay the groom; the bride's father died without paying. The groom could not sue: he was not a party and gave no consideration. Wightman J: no stranger to the consideration can take advantage of a contract, although it was made for his benefit.
5. *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co Ltd v Selfridge & Co Ltd* [1915] AC 847 (HL), the leading case before 1999. Dunlop sold to Dew & Co on terms that Dew would get retailers' undertakings not to sell below list price. Selfridge gave Dew the undertaking, then undercut. Dunlop's action failed. It had given no consideration for Selfridge's promise (the discount went to Dew), and its argument that Dew was its agent failed. Viscount Haldane: independently of consideration, it is a fundamental principle that only a party to a contract can sue on it.
6. Further leading authorities listed: *Scruttons Ltd v Midland Silicones Ltd* [1962] AC 446 (stevedores could not rely on a limitation clause in the carriage contract; Lord Reid set out conditions for an agency route); *Beswick v Beswick* [1968] AC 58.

### 2. The Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999
**Section 1: the test of enforceability.**
- s 1(1): a non-party ("third party") may in his own right enforce a term if
  - (a) the contract **expressly provides** that he may; or
  - (b) subject to s 1(2), the term **purports to confer a benefit** on him.
- s 1(2): s 1(1)(b) does not apply if, on a proper construction of the contract, it appears the parties **did not intend** the term to be enforceable by the third party. Trap: the s 1(2) proviso applies only to limb (b), not limb (a) (Concentrate).
- s 1(3): the third party must be **expressly identified** in the contract by name, as a member of a class, or as answering a particular description, but **need not exist** when the contract is made (e.g. an unborn child or a company not yet formed).
- s 1(5): the third party has any remedy that would have been available had he been a party (damages, injunction, specific performance, etc.).
- s 1(6): where a term excludes or limits liability, "enforcing" it means relying on the exclusion or limitation. Third parties can therefore take the benefit of exemption clauses (e.g. Himalaya clauses).
- The Act applies to oral as well as written contracts: *Broadcasting Investment Group Ltd v Smith* (2020) (point not challenged on appeal, 2021).
- **No consideration needed from the third party.** The Act says nothing express, but s 1 would be pointless otherwise. The promisee must still have provided consideration for the promise (Concentrate; McKendrick).

**Interpreting s 1:**
1. "Purports to confer a benefit" (s 1(1)(b)). *Dolphin Maritime & Aviation Services Ltd v Sveriges Angfartygs Assurans Forening* [2009] EWHC 716 (Comm): a recovery agent that would receive payments to pass on to underwriters was not a beneficiary. Christopher Clarke J (at [74]): a contract does not purport to confer a benefit just because the third party will be better off if it is performed; benefiting the third party must be one of the purposes of the bargain, not an incidental effect. *Broadcasting Investment Group v Smith* (2020) agreed with the result but rejected any distinction between major and minor benefits.
2. The s 1(2) proviso. *Nisshin Shipping Co Ltd v Cleaves & Co Ltd* [2003] EWHC 2602 (Comm), [2004] 1 Lloyd's Rep 38: charterparties provided for commission to the broker. The term purported to benefit the broker, and the broker did not have to prove the parties intended it to be enforceable. The contract was neutral, so s 1(1)(b) applied. The burden is usually understood to lie on the party denying enforcement. *Secretary of State for the Home Department v James Cox* (2023) took a more integrated, construction-based approach to s 1(1)(b) and (2) (Concentrate).
3. *The Laemthong Glory (No 2)* [2005] EWCA Civ 519, [2005] 1 Lloyd's Rep 688: receivers argued that a "chain" of letters of indemnity showed no intention that the shipowners could enforce the receivers' indemnity given to the charterers. The CA rejected this. Without an established practice (like the Law Commission's construction-industry example of chains of contracts understood to keep remedies within each contract), linked contracts do not by themselves negate a third-party right. See also *The Jag Ravi* [2012] EWCA Civ 180 (McKendrick).
4. s 1(3) express identification. *Avraamides v Colwill* (2006) (CA): a buyer of a business assumed the seller's liabilities to customers, but the customer was not expressly identified and so could not sue. *Starlight Shipping v Allianz Marine* (2014) took a broader approach ("underwriters" included servants and agents), with some support in *Chudley v Clydesdale Bank* (2019).

**Section 2: variation and rescission.** Once the third party has a s 1 right, the parties cannot, without his consent, rescind or vary the contract so as to extinguish or alter his entitlement if:
- (a) he has communicated his assent to the term to the promisor (s 2(2): the postal rule is excluded for assent by post);
- (b) the promisor is aware that he has relied on the term; or
- (c) the promisor could reasonably have foreseen that he would rely on it, and he has in fact relied.
The parties may contract out (s 2(3)), e.g. by reserving a right to vary or cancel, or by setting a different crystallisation test. The court has a discretion to dispense with consent in some circumstances (s 2(4)-(7)).

**Section 3: defences available to the promisor.**
- s 3(2): any defence or set-off that arises from or in connection with the contract, is relevant to the term, and would have been available against the promisee. Counterclaims against the promisee are excluded, so the third party can be reduced to zero but never left owing money.
- s 3(3): matters made available by an express term.
- s 3(4): defences, set-offs and counterclaims (not arising from the contract) that the promisor would have had against the third party had he been a party.
- s 3(5): the parties can contract out. s 3(6): a third party relying on an exclusion clause cannot do so if he could not have done so had he been a party.

**Section 4:** the promisee's own right to enforce any term is preserved.

**Section 5: double liability.** Where the promisee has already recovered a sum for the third party's loss (or for the cost of making good the default to the third party), the court reduces any award to the third party as it thinks appropriate. The section does not cover the reverse order. If the third party sues first, the promisee is confined to its own (usually nominal) loss (McKendrick). the materials that the third party's claim seems unaffected by sums the promisee recovers for its *own* loss.

**Section 6: exclusions.** No s 1 rights in: negotiable instruments; the contract between a company and its members under s 33 Companies Act 2006; LLP constitutional agreements; terms of employment, worker or agency-worker contracts enforced *against* the employee/worker; and contracts for carriage of goods by sea (bill of lading etc.) or by international rail, road or air conventions. In those carriage contracts a third party *can* still rely on an exclusion or limitation of liability (s 6(5)). McKendrick suggests that an exclusive jurisdiction clause may therefore fall outside the Act in carriage-by-sea cases, since *The Mahkutai* held such a clause is not an exception or limitation clause.

**Section 7: supplementary.**
- s 7(1): any right or remedy a third party has apart from the Act is preserved, so the old exceptions survive and judges may develop new ones (Law Com para 5.10).
- s 7(2): s 2(2) UCTA 1977 (reasonableness control of exclusion of negligence liability for non-death/injury loss) does **not** apply where the third party relies on s 1. The third party is in a weaker position than the promisee, though s 2(1) UCTA (death and personal injury) still protects him. the materials describes the third party's UCTA protection as "limited"; McKendrick calls s 7(2) controversial.
- s 7(3): limitation periods as for simple contract or specialty. s 7(4): the third party is not treated as a party for other Acts.

**Section 8:** a third party enforcing a term subject to a written arbitration agreement is treated as a party to it under the Arbitration Act 1996.

**Differences from the promisee's rights** (lecture): unless otherwise agreed, the third party's right mirrors the promisee's (s 1(5)), with variations. The third party cannot terminate, the promisor cannot use counterclaims against the promisee, and UCTA protection is limited.

### 3. Promisee's remedies in a contract for the benefit of a third party
The Act preserves these (s 4). They matter where the Act is excluded or s 1 is not satisfied.
1. **Specific performance.** *Beswick v Beswick* [1968] AC 58 (HL): a nephew bought his uncle's coal business and promised to pay an annuity to the uncle's widow. In her personal capacity she was a third party and could not sue. As administratrix of the estate she obtained specific performance, because damages to the estate (which had no loss) would be inadequate.
2. **Damages for the third party's loss: general rule against.** A party recovers only its own loss. *Woodar Investment Development Ltd v Wimpey Construction UK Ltd* [1980] 1 WLR 277 (HL) disapproved Lord Denning's broad reasoning in *Jackson*. (There was in fact no breach; £150,000 of the price was payable to a third party.) Lord Millett in *Panatown*: compensation for a third party's loss is "a contradiction in terms" unless the claimant is accountable to the person who suffered it.
3. **Exception 1: "party convenience" / group contracts.** *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* [1975] 1 WLR 1468 (CA): a father booked a substandard family holiday and was awarded £1,100, including £500 for distress. The ratio is unclear. James LJ treated £500 as compensation for Mr Jackson's own loss; Lord Denning would have allowed recovery for the family's loss, held for them. In *Woodar* Lord Wilberforce left room for contracts "calling for special treatment" (family holidays, restaurant meals for a party, a taxi hired for a group), but no appellate court has applied this. Both the materials and the Law Commission (Report paras 7.40, 7.49) say the family members would now probably have their own right under s 1(1)(b).
4. **Exception 2: the *Albazero* / St Martins "narrow ground".** *Linden Gardens Trust Ltd v Lenesta Sludge Disposals Ltd* [1994] 1 AC 85 (the St Martins appeal). Where A contracts with a builder B for work on property that A transfers to C, and C cannot sue (e.g. assignment is prohibited), A may recover substantial damages for C's loss. Otherwise the claim falls into a "black hole". The basis has moved from the parties' contemplation to operation of law. It was applied after an assignment in *Darlington BC v Wiltshier Northern Ltd* (1995) (CA): a leisure centre was built on council land via a finance company, and the company's right to substantial damages passed to the council. *Lowick Rose LLP v Swynson Ltd* (2017): Lord Sumption said the exception could extend beyond construction and carriage contracts but did not apply where benefiting the third party was not an object of the contract. The scope of the *Albazero* exception remains uncertain (lecture).
5. **Limit: the third party has its own remedy.** *Alfred McAlpine Construction Ltd v Panatown Ltd* [2001] 1 AC 518 (HL). Panatown employed McAlpine to build on land owned by UIPL, an associated company. McAlpine also gave UIPL a direct duty of care deed. A majority held Panatown could recover only nominal damages, because UIPL had its own claim; if the third party has a claim, it should decide whether to enforce it.
6. **The "broad ground" / performance interest.** Lord Griffiths in *St Martins*: a promisee who does not get the performance it paid for suffers its own loss of bargain, whether or not it owns the property, and so can recover substantial damages (e.g. paying for a kitchen installed in a daughter's house). It has support from the *Panatown* dissenters (Lords Goff and Millett) and from Lord Sumption in *Lowick Rose*. It is still debated (Lord Mance in *Lowick Rose*). In *Dr Jones Yeovil Ltd v Stepping Stone Group* (2020) the judge accepted the broad ground but doubted it applied to pure economic loss.

### 4. Third-party enforcement outside the Act (preserved by s 7(1))
1. **Agency / The Eurymedon.** *New Zealand Shipping Co v AM Satterthwaite (The Eurymedon)* [1975] AC 154 (PC). A bill of lading extended the carrier's exemption to its servants, agents and independent contractors. The shipper's offer of immunity, made through the carrier as agent, was accepted by the stevedores when they unloaded. Their performance of a duty owed to the carrier was consideration (see `consideration.md`). This followed Lord Reid's four conditions in *Scruttons*: (1) the contract makes clear the third party is to be protected; (2) it makes clear the carrier also contracts as agent for the third party; (3) the carrier has authority (or ratification); (4) the third party provides consideration. the materials calls this creative but expensive and overly technical. Under the 1999 Act the stevedores would now likely succeed directly (identified as a class; benefit purported; s 6(5) allows reliance on exclusions). Compare *London Drugs Ltd v Kuehne & Nagel International Ltd* (1993) (Canada): employees not expressly named would struggle under *Scruttons* and s 1(3).
2. **Collateral contracts.** *Shanklin Pier Ltd v Detel Products Ltd* (1951): the pier owner told its painters to buy the defendant's paint on the strength of the defendant's assurance about durability. A collateral contract arose, with the consideration being the instruction to the painters to buy.
3. **Trust of a contractual promise.** *Les Affréteurs Réunis SA v Leopold Walford (London) Ltd* (1919). There must be an intention to create a trust; it will not be implied merely because the contract benefits a third party (*Re Schebsman* (1944)). A trust was found in *Nisshin Shipping*, alongside the Act.
4. **Undisclosed principal:** the "third party" was really the principal for whom the promisee contracted (but *Dunlop* failed on this).
5. **Tort:** a third party may sue the negligent promisor for breach of a duty of care, or be shielded from a contract party's negligence claim.
6. **Assignment, negotiable instruments, and statutory exceptions** (notably insurance).

### 5. The burden side
Contracting parties generally cannot impose obligations on a third party; the 1999 Act does not change this (Law Com para 2.1). A third party may be bound or affected through:
- the tort of **inducing breach of contract** (*Lumley v Gye* (1853) 2 El & Bl 216), giving damages at common law and an injunction in equity while performance is still possible;
- **sub-bailment** terms binding the bailor;
- **unconscionable conduct** in exercising rights over property acquired subject to an undertaking to honour the claimant's right.

## Key authorities
- *Tweddle v Atkinson* (1861): no stranger to the consideration may enforce.
- *Dunlop v Selfridge* [1915] AC 847 (HL): only a party can sue; agency argument failed.
- *Scruttons v Midland Silicones* [1962] AC 446 (HL): stevedores unprotected; Lord Reid's agency conditions.
- *The Eurymedon* [1975] AC 154 (PC): agency/unilateral contract route for Himalaya clauses.
- *Beswick v Beswick* [1968] AC 58 (HL): specific performance by the promisee's estate.
- *Jackson v Horizon Holidays* [1975] 1 WLR 1468 (CA) and *Woodar v Wimpey* [1980] 1 WLR 277 (HL): recovery for others' loss; Denning's view disapproved; "special treatment" category left open.
- *Linden Gardens / St Martins* [1994] 1 AC 85 (HL): narrow and broad grounds.
- *Alfred McAlpine v Panatown* [2001] 1 AC 518 (HL): no substantial damages where the third party has a direct remedy (duty of care deed).
- *Nisshin Shipping v Cleaves* [2004] 1 Lloyd's Rep 38: s 1(1)(b) and s 1(2), burden on party denying enforcement.
- *The Laemthong Glory (No 2)* [2005] EWCA Civ 519: chain of contracts does not of itself rebut s 1(2).
- *Dolphin Maritime v Sveriges Angfartygs Assurans Forening* [2009] EWHC 716 (Comm): benefit must be a purpose, not an incidental effect.
- *Avraamides v Colwill* (2006) (CA): s 1(3) express identification.
- Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999 ss 1-8: see above.

## Debate and critique
- **Why reform?** The Law Commission (Report No 242, paras 3.1-3.28) gave three main reasons. (1) The rule thwarts the parties' intentions and so undermines the theory that contract enforces the will or bargain of the parties. (2) It is unjust to a third party who reasonably expected to be able to enforce and has relied on the contract. Where such reliance or assent exists, the third party's position should override the parties' later wish to vary. (3) The perverse result that the person who suffered loss cannot sue while the person who can sue has no loss (the "black hole"). It also risks unjust enrichment of the promisor. the materials adds that the exceptions had become complex, uncertain and costly, as in *The Eurymedon*. Judicial dissatisfaction appears in *Beswick*, *Woodar* and Steyn LJ in *Darlington*.
- **Defences of privity:** Kincaid, "Third Parties: Rationalising a Right to Sue" (1989) 48 CLJ 243; Smith, "Contracts for the Benefit of Third Parties: In Defence of the Third Party Rule" (1997) 17 OJLS 643.
- **Does the Act go far enough?** It does not abolish privity. s 1(3) (express identification) can exclude deserving third parties (*Avraamides*). Parties routinely contract out of the Act, especially in commerce. s 7(2) weakens UCTA protection for third parties. s 5 does not deal with recovery from a promisee who holds damages for the third party. Burrows [2000] LMCLQ 540; Andrews, "Strangers to Justice No Longer" (2001) 60 CLJ 353; Beale's review in Burrows and Peel (eds), *Contract Formation and Parties* (2010), which called the Act "useful but still underused". McKendrick thinks lawyers are now more willing to use it and that the Act is the first, and often last, port of call. Concentrate's model essay question argues that the Act's limits have pushed courts to be ever more ingenious about promisee damages.
- **Consistency with consideration.** the materials the Act is inconsistent with the traditional view that only those who have paid for a promise may enforce it. It is consistent with the alternative view that consideration only decides *which promises* are enforceable, with the Act simply adding to the *people* who may enforce them.
- **Performance interest.** Coote, "The Performance Interest, Panatown and the Problem of Loss" (2001) 117 LQR 81, supports the broad ground. Lord Millett's logic in *Panatown* is that compensation goes only to the person who suffered loss. The debate is about whether contract damages protect the bargain itself or only financial consequences.
- **Judicial development.** The Law Commission (para 5.10) did not want the Act to stop judges creating further exceptions, e.g. a doctrine of "vicarious immunity" for employees, or Lord Goff's "fully-fledged exception" suggested in *The Mahkutai*, perhaps following *London Drugs*. Whether courts will now hold back because parties could have used the Act is an open question (McKendrick).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Map the parties:** who promised what to whom; who is promisor, promisee and third party; who has suffered the loss.
2. **Is the claimant a party?** If not, privity bars a common law claim (*Tweddle*, *Dunlop*).
3. **Has the Act been excluded?** Check for an express exclusion clause, and for s 6 excluded contracts (e.g. employment terms against the employee, carriage by sea except for exclusions).
4. **s 1(1)(a):** does the contract expressly give the third party a right to enforce?
5. **s 1(1)(b):** does the term purport to confer a benefit on him as a purpose of the contract (*Dolphin*)? If yes, has the promisor shown from the contract that the parties did not intend enforceability (s 1(2); *Nisshin*; *Laemthong Glory*)?
6. **s 1(3):** is he expressly identified by name, class or description? Watch for vague descriptions ("warehouse staff" covering a clerk; *London Drugs*-type facts; *Avraamides*).
7. **Exemption clauses:** under s 1(6) the third party can rely on an exclusion or limitation. Then check incorporation, construction and UCTA/CRA control, remembering s 7(2) (no s 2(2) UCTA challenge by the third party) and s 3(6).
8. **Variation:** have the parties tried to change or cancel the benefit? Apply s 2(1) (assent, known or foreseeable reliance) and any s 2(3) reservation.
9. **Defences:** what can the promisor raise (s 3)? Defences and set-off related to the term, not counterclaims against the promisee.
10. **If the Act fails:** try agency/*Eurymedon* (Lord Reid's four conditions, with consideration from performing a duty owed to a third party); collateral contract (*Shanklin Pier*); trust (express intention needed; *Re Schebsman*); tort; assignment.
11. **Promisee remedies:** specific performance (*Beswick*); damages for the third party's loss under the group/convenience category (*Jackson*/*Woodar*), the narrow ground (*St Martins*, *Darlington*) unless the third party has its own remedy (*Panatown*), or the broad ground (performance interest, contested). Check for double recovery (s 5).
12. **Burden:** if a party seeks to bind a third party, the answer is generally no, unless inducing breach, sub-bailment or unconscionable conduct over property applies.
