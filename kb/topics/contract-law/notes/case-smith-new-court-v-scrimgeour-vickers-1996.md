# Smith New Court Securities Ltd v Scrimgeour Vickers (Asset Management) Ltd [1996] UKHL 3, [1997] AC 254 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself was not accessible (BAILII returned 403) and is not in the knowledge base.
> Check wording and pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
House of Lords, decided 21 November 1996; Lord Browne-Wilkinson gave the leading speech. The case is the leading authority on the **measure of damages in the tort of deceit** (fraudulent misrepresentation): the defrauded party recovers all loss directly flowing from the transaction, whether or not foreseeable.

## Facts
Roberts, who acted for the defendant brokers, told Smith New Court (falsely) that there were other close bids for shares in Ferranti. Smith bought roughly £23 million of shares in reliance. Unknown to everyone involved, the market price was inflated by a separate fraud on Ferranti itself. When that fraud came to light the price fell, and Smith sold the shares in parcels over the following months for about £11.3 million less than it had paid. It sued in deceit.

## Held
- The Court of Appeal had limited damages to the difference between the price paid and the true market value on the day of purchase (about £1.2 million). The House of Lords held Smith was entitled to its full loss of about £11.3 million.
- Lord Browne-Wilkinson's summary of principles (paraphrased):
  1. The wrongdoer must compensate for all loss **directly flowing** from the transaction.
  2. The loss need not have been foreseeable; foreseeability is not the test in deceit.
  3. The claimant can recover the price paid, less benefits received from the transaction.
  4. Benefit is generally the market value at acquisition, but that rule is not applied rigidly where it would under-compensate (for example where the fraud continued to operate after the purchase, or locked the claimant into holding the asset).
  5. Consequential losses caused by the transaction are recoverable.
  6. The claimant must take reasonable steps to mitigate once the fraud is discovered.

## Why it matters
- Fixes the deceit measure: directness, not remoteness by foreseeability, and flexible valuation of the benefit received.
- Frequently contrasted with contract and negligence measures of damages, and relevant to the debate about the "fiction of fraud" in *Royscot Trust v Rogerson* (see that note).
- Companion to *Derry v Peek* (what counts as fraud) when a problem question involves a dishonest statement.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Establish fraud first (*Derry v Peek* test: knowledge, no belief, or recklessness).
2. Calculate loss as price paid minus value actually received, then add direct consequential losses.
3. Ask whether the market value on the date of purchase fairly measures benefit, or whether the claimant was induced to keep the asset.
4. Check mitigation after discovery.
