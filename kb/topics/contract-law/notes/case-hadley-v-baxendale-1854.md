# Hadley v Baxendale (1854) 9 Exch 341 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Also reported at (1854) 156 ER 145. Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Exchequer, decided 23 February 1854; Alderson B delivered the court's judgment. The foundation of the **remoteness rule in contract**: a claimant recovers only loss that arises naturally from the breach, or that both parties reasonably contemplated when contracting as a probable result of breach.

## Facts
A mill stopped working when its crankshaft broke. The millers sent the broken shaft, through the defendant carriers, to engineers who were to use it as a pattern for a new one. Delivery was delayed by the carriers' negligence, the replacement arrived late and the mill stayed idle longer, so the millers lost profits. The jury awarded damages above the sum paid into court. The defendants argued the lost profits were too remote.

## Held
- The rule was made absolute and a **new trial** was ordered, with the jury to be directed on the correct principle.
- Damages should be those that may fairly and reasonably be considered either (1) arising naturally, in the usual course of things, from the breach, or (2) as within the reasonable contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as the probable result of breach.
- Where special circumstances were communicated to the defendant, liability extends to the loss ordinarily following in those circumstances; if they were unknown, only to loss in the general run of cases.
- The only circumstances made known were that the item was a broken mill shaft and that the sender was a miller; that did not make lost mill profits a reasonable consequence of delay.

## Why it matters
- Source of the **two limbs** applied in later cases such as *Victoria Laundry v Newman* and *The Achilleas*.
- Encourages parties with unusual exposure to tell the other party, or to allocate the risk by contract.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the loss as ordinary (first limb) or special (second limb).
2. For special loss, ask what the defendant actually knew at the time of contracting.
3. Use *Victoria Laundry* to separate ordinary from extraordinary lost profits.
