# Mistake

## Overview
Mistake asks when an erroneous belief held by one or both parties at the time of contracting prevents a contract from forming, or makes it void, voidable or rectifiable. English law's doctrine is much narrower than in civil law systems. The courts put contractual certainty first and fear that a broad doctrine would let parties escape bad bargains. They also accept that enforcing a contract against what both parties actually intended can be unjust. McKendrick says bluntly that the law "is in a mess". The reasons he gives are inconsistent terminology, the split between law and equity, the many different forms a mistake can take, competing policies, the range of factors courts consider, and the different remedial results. The law stated is that of England and Wales. The Australian decision *McRae* and the European PECL provisions appear only as persuasive or comparative material.

## The law

### 0. Terminology and structure
The labels are used inconsistently:
- **Common mistake**: both parties make the *same* mistake about a fundamental assumption, so there is agreement but it rests on a false basis. In *Great Peace*, the CA preferred "common" to "mutual" for this category.
- **Mutual mistake** (cross-purposes): each party is mistaken in a different way and neither realises the other's error. The parties are at cross-purposes about the terms.
- **Unilateral mistake**: only one party is mistaken, and the other knows, or possibly is taken to know, of the mistake. Mistake as to identity is the main example.

In *Great Peace* [2002] EWCA Civ 1407 at [28]–[32], the CA described the functions of mistake as follows. Mistakes may:
1. prevent the mutual agreement needed for formation (cross-purposes, latent ambiguity);
2. lead to a document that fails to record a prior agreement (rectification);
3. be a shared false assumption underlying agreed terms (common mistake).

Two further categories sit nearby. Mistakes *induced* by false statements belong to misrepresentation. Mistakes about the nature of a signed document belong to non est factum.

**Consequences** (lecture; McKendrick):
- A *void* contract is a nullity from the start. Title cannot pass under it, so it affects third parties. the materials stresses that a buyer under a void contract must return the goods to the original owner.
- A *voidable* contract stands until rescinded, so third parties who acquire before rescission are protected.
- Sometimes mistake only bars a particular remedy, such as specific performance (*Denny v Hancock* (1870)).

Because of the *nemo dat quod non habet* rule, the void/voidable distinction is decisive in rogue cases.

### 1. Common mistake

**Step 1: does the contract allocate the risk?** Before applying any doctrine of mistake, the court must ask whether the contract, expressly or impliedly, allocates the risk of the mistaken assumption.
- Steyn J in *Associated Japanese Bank (International) Ltd v Crédit du Nord SA* [1989] 1 WLR 255/257: "Only if the contract is silent on the point is there scope for invoking mistake."
- Lord Phillips MR in *Great Peace*: if a party warrants that the subject matter exists, or that performance is possible, "there will be no scope to hold the contract void on the ground of common mistake". This parallels frustration, which applies only where the contract makes no provision.
- In *John Lobb SAS v John Lobb Ltd* [2022] EWHC 2306 (Ch), Edwin Johnson J said a warranty is only one way a party may assume responsibility. The wider question is whether responsibility was assumed at all. One reason the doctrine rarely succeeds is that the contract, or the general law, usually allocates the risk.

**Step 2: the *Great Peace* elements.** If the risk is not allocated, *Great Peace* (following *Associated Japanese Bank* and drawing on *Hobson v Pattenden* (1903)) requires:
1. a common assumption as to the existence of a state of affairs;
2. no warranty by either party that the state of affairs exists (the materials cites *SAW (SW) 2010 Ltd v Wilson* (2017));
3. the non-existence of the state of affairs not being attributable to the fault of either party;
4. the non-existence making performance of the contract **impossible**;
5. the state of affairs being the existence, or a vital attribute, of the consideration, or circumstances that must subsist if performance is to be possible.

McKendrick reads [84] of the judgment as a two-stage test: (a) has the contract become impossible to perform because of the unforeseen circumstances, and (b) on its true construction, has one party undertaken responsibility for the assumed state of affairs?

**Is impossibility essential?** The cases conflict (*Lehman Bros v Exotix* [2019] EWHC 2380 (Ch)).
- *Brennan v Bolt Burdon* [2004] EWCA Civ 1017 supports a strict impossibility requirement.
- *Triple Seven MSN 27251 Ltd v Azman Air Services* [2018] EWHC 1348 (Comm) and *John Lobb* suggest, by analogy with frustration, that performance being "radically different" should be enough. McKendrick prefers this view because it brings mistake and frustration together while keeping the doctrine narrow.
- *Chalcot Training Ltd v Ralph* [2020] EWHC 1054 (Ch) asked whether the commercial purpose had ceased to exist (Concentrate Q&A).
- *Triple Seven* also holds that it is not enough that a party would not have contracted had it known the truth.

**Categories within common mistake:**

*(a) Res extincta: the subject matter has ceased to exist.*
- *Couturier v Hastie* (1856) 5 HLC 673: corn believed to be in transit had already deteriorated and been sold by the ship's master. The buyer was not liable for the price, because the contract was for the corn, not an "interest in the adventure", and there was a total failure of consideration. The word "mistake" does not appear in the judgment. Lord Cranworth said the question turned on construction. The case was later rationalised as common mistake.
- **Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 6**: a contract for the sale of specific goods is void if, without the seller's knowledge, the goods have perished before the contract is made. McKendrick treats s 6 as a *default* rule that the parties can displace by promising that the goods exist.
- *Galloway v Galloway* (1914) (lecture): a separation agreement between a couple whose marriage was void because the husband's first wife was, unknown to him, still alive. The agreement was treated as never made.
- *Scott v Coulson* [1903] 2 Ch 349: a life policy sold when the insured had already died. *Great Peace* doubted it, but explained that a policy before death is fundamentally different from a policy after death.
- *British Red Cross v Werry* [2017] EWHC 875 (Ch): a settlement of an estate dispute, made on the footing of an intestacy, was set aside when a will was found. This is a rare modern success.

*(b) Res sua or impossibility of title.*
- *Cooper v Phibbs* (1867) LR 2 HL 149: A agreed to lease a fishery from B, but A was in fact already tenant for life and B appears to have had no title. Lord Atkin in *Bell* treated it as a case where a transfer of ownership was impossible ("naturali ratione inutilis"), and said the agreement "would appear to be void rather than voidable". the materials summarises it as a lessor without title, contract void.

*(c) Subject matter never existed.*
- *McRae v Commonwealth Disposals Commission* (1951) 84 CLR 377 (High Court of Australia): the Commission sold "an oil tanker lying on the Jourmaund Reef", but no tanker had ever existed. McRae had fitted out an expensive salvage expedition. The Commission's mistake defence failed because, on construction, it had *promised* the tanker existed. It was liable for breach and McRae recovered his reliance losses. *Great Peace* approved the result at [79]–[80].
- McKendrick notes the tension with s 6 (a tanker that never existed could not "perish") and prefers asking in every case what the contract promised.

*(d) Mistake as to quality.*
- **Bell v Lever Brothers Ltd** [1932] AC 161 (HL): Lever Bros paid Bell £30,000 and Snelling £20,000 to end their service contracts, not knowing that the pair's secret cocoa speculation would have justified dismissal without compensation. The majority (Viscount Haldane and Lord Warrington dissenting) held the agreements were *not* void. The mistake went to the quality of the service contracts, not the substance of what was contracted for.
- Lord Atkin's tests are often cited: a mistake as to quality will not affect assent "unless it is the mistake of both parties, and is as to the existence of some quality which makes the thing without the quality essentially different from the thing as it was believed to be". He gave the example of a picture both parties believed to be an old master that was really a worthless copy: the buyer has no remedy "in the absence of representation or warranty".
- the materials quotes Sir John Simon's formulation about a "contractual assumption". The Concentrate Q&A also gives Lord Atkin's first test, whether the new facts "destroy the identity of the subject matter".
- Lord Warrington, dissenting, thought the mistaken assumption "as fundamental to the bargain as any error one can imagine".
- *Leaf v International Galleries* [1950] 2 KB 86: both parties believed a painting was by Constable. This was not an operative mistake.
- *Associated Japanese Bank v Crédit du Nord* [1989]: the bank guaranteed a rogue's sale-and-leaseback of four machines that did not exist. The guarantee failed on construction because there was an express condition precedent that the machines existed. Steyn J said obiter that, otherwise, it would have been void for common mistake. His guiding principles were that the law should uphold rather than destroy apparent contracts, and that mistake (like frustration) deals with wholly exceptional circumstances.
- *Sheikh Brothers v Ochsner* [1957] AC 136 (PC): a licence was granted on the basis that the land could produce 50 tons of sisal a month, which it could not. The mistake went to the "very basis of the contract".
- **Great Peace Shipping Ltd v Tsavliris Salvage (International) Ltd** [2002] EWCA Civ 1407, [2003] QB 679: the "Great Peace" was chartered to stand by a stricken vessel, the "Cape Providence", on the shared belief that the two ships were close together. They were in fact about 39 hours apart. The hirers found a closer ship and cancelled without paying the cancellation fee. The contract was **not void**:
  1. the Great Peace could still provide part of the standby service;
  2. no term could be implied about her position, and the contract allocated cancellation risk;
  3. performance was not impossible.
- *Bank of Credit and Commerce International v Ali* [1999] (Concentrate): mistake is not a means of escaping bad bargains.

**Equity: the rise and fall of *Solle v Butcher*.**
- *Solle v Butcher* [1950] 1 KB 671: Denning LJ said a contract is liable "in equity to be set aside if the parties were under a common misapprehension either as to facts or as to their relative and respective rights, provided that the misapprehension was fundamental, and that the party seeking to set it aside was not himself at fault". (The case concerned a mistake as to whether a lease was subject to the Rent Restriction Acts. It was not enough at common law.)
- On this view, equitable mistake differed from mistake at law in three ways (McKendrick):
  1. "fundamental" was given a more liberal meaning;
  2. the contract was voidable, not void;
  3. the court could set the contract aside on terms, so the remedy was flexible and discretionary.
- Steyn J in *Associated Japanese Bank* saw "a narrow doctrine of common law mistake … supplemented by the more flexible doctrine of mistake in equity" as "entirely sensible and satisfactory".
- ***Great Peace*** held that *Solle* "cannot stand" with *Bell*, since the House of Lords could hardly have overlooked an equitable right to rescind. The *Bell* test therefore governs at law and in equity alike, and an operative common mistake makes the contract **void**. *Statoil ASA v Louis Dreyfus Energy Services* [2008] EWHC 2257 (Comm) applied the same denial to unilateral mistake.
- The Concentrate Q&A says it is "reasonably clear" that equitable rescission is unavailable for a contract valid at law (*Great Peace*; *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26). It adds that *FII Group Litigation v HMRC* [2020] UKSC 47 at [109] suggests the existence of an equitable jurisdiction has not been finally resolved.
- McKendrick says equity keeps some role in mistake, but it cannot be used to undermine binding House of Lords authority.
- Note a lecture slip: the handout describes *Solle* as an "implied terms" approach making contracts voidable. The textbooks describe it as a separate *equitable* doctrine with voidability as the remedy. Follow the textbook account.

### 2. Mutual mistake (cross-purposes) and the objective test
- Agreement is judged **objectively**. Blackburn J in *Smith v Hughes* (1871) LR 6 QB 597: if a party "so conducts himself that a reasonable man would believe that he was assenting to the terms proposed by the other party", and the other party contracts on that belief, he is bound "as if he had intended to agree". So subjective disagreement usually does not prevent a contract.
- *Wood v Scarth* (1858): a landlord intended a £500 premium as well as the £63 rent, but the tenant reasonably understood that only rent was payable. The contract was binding on the tenant's terms.
- *Scott v Littledale* (1858): tea sold by a sample that was poorer than the bulk. The contract was valid and the buyer could hold the seller to it.
- *Raffles v Wichelhaus* (1864) 2 H & C 906: cotton "to arrive ex Peerless from Bombay". There were two ships called Peerless, one sailing in October and one in December. This is a **latent ambiguity**: if each party meant a different ship and nothing objectively favoured either meaning, there was no agreement. McKendrick (drawing on AWB Simpson) warns that the case is obscure. It was decided on a demurrer with no reasons beyond "judgment for the defendants", and its fame owes more to nineteenth-century academic debate about consent than to what the court decided.
- *Scriven Bros v Hindley* [1913] 3 KB 564: the seller intended to sell tow and the buyer intended to buy hemp. The ambiguity was such that the subject matter could not be identified with certainty, so there was no contract. McKendrick uses it to show that a party at fault in causing, or failing to notice, the other's mistake may be unable to hold the other to the terms.
- *Henkel v Pape* (1870): no contract where a third party's error left the parties fundamentally at cross-purposes (Concentrate).

### 3. Unilateral mistake as to the terms
- **General rule: caveat emptor.** A mistake by one party about *quality*, or about the *motive* for contracting, does not affect the contract, **even if the other party knows of it**, provided the other party did not induce it and it is not a term.
  - *Smith v Hughes*: the buyer wanted old oats and was sold new oats. The contract was valid because there was no misrepresentation and no term that the oats were old. The CA distinguished two situations. If the seller merely knew the buyer *thought the oats were old*, the buyer was bound. If the seller knew the buyer thought *the seller was promising* old oats, the parties were at cross-purposes about the terms and the buyer was not bound.
  - the materials's examples follow this. The contract stands even if the seller silently knew the buyer's purpose, unless that purpose was specified in the contract or understood as a term.
- **Exception: a known mistake as to the terms.** A party cannot "snap up" an offer they know was not intended.
  - ***Hartog v Colin & Shields*** [1939] 3 All ER 566: the sellers mistakenly offered 30,000 Argentine hare skins at a price *per pound* rather than *per piece* (a piece was worth about a third of a pound). Negotiations and trade practice had been per piece, so the buyer must have known of the error. The contract was void.
  - the materials reverses the roles, saying the seller knew of the buyer's mistake. The textbook facts above are the correct ones.
- **Constructive knowledge?** *Centrovincial Estates v Merchant Investors Assurance* [1983] suggests it may be enough that a reasonable person ought to have known of the mistake (Concentrate). McKendrick notes a wider category where one party was at fault in not noticing, or caused, the other's mistake (*Scriven*).
- **the materials's policy rationale**: there is no duty of care between negotiating parties. It would be unfair to make the non-mistaken party carry the cost of the other's inattention. It would also be inefficient to require parties to share all their knowledge. Relief generally needs superior knowledge used unfairly, and if the mistake was induced by false information, misrepresentation is the better route.

### 4. Unilateral mistake as to identity
**The problem.** A deals with rogue B, believing B to be C (usually because B said so), and B sells the goods to innocent D. If the A–B contract is **void** for mistake, B never had title and D must return the goods or pay their value in conversion. If it is only **voidable** for fraud, B had title until A rescinded, so D keeps the goods if D bought first (compare *Car & Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525, where rescission came in time). As the materials puts it, only the mistaken party can rely on the void contract.

**Pre-*Shogun* requirements** (Concentrate Q&A). For an operative mistake of identity, A had to show:
1. A intended to contract with someone other than B;
2. identity was fundamental and material, as opposed to mere attributes such as creditworthiness;
3. B knew of A's mistake;
4. A took reasonable steps to verify B's identity.

**Dealings at a distance (in writing).**
- ***Cundy v Lindsay*** (1878) 3 App Cas 459 (HL): Blenkarn ordered handkerchiefs from 37 Wood Street, signing his letters so that they looked as if they came from Blenkiron & Co, a respectable firm the sellers knew of at 123 Wood Street. The contract was void because the sellers intended to deal only with Blenkiron & Co. Cundy, a good faith buyer from Blenkarn, was liable in conversion.
- *King's Norton Metal Co v Edridge Merrett & Co* (1897) 14 TLR 98: a rogue (Wallis) traded as the fictitious "Hallam & Co". There was no separate entity the sellers could have intended, so they intended to deal with the writer of the letters. The contract was voidable only, and the good faith buyer won.
- *Boulton v Jones* (1857) 2 H & N 564: Jones sent an order addressed to Brocklehurst, with whom he had dealt before and against whom he had a set-off, but the goods were supplied by Boulton. There was no contract with Boulton. (the materials spells the name "Bolton".)

**Face-to-face dealings.** There is a strong presumption that A intends to deal with the person in front of them.
- *Phillips v Brooks* [1919] 2 KB 243: North, claiming to be "Sir George Bullough" (whose address the jeweller checked in a directory), took a ring and pledged it. The contract was voidable, not void, and the pledgee kept the ring.
- *Ingram v Little* [1961] 1 QB 31 (CA, majority): two elderly sisters selling a car accepted a cheque only after checking "PGM Hutchinson's" address in a telephone directory. The contract was void, and the dealer who bought from the rogue was liable in conversion. Devlin LJ dissented and proposed that the loss be split.
- *Lewis v Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198 (CA): a rogue posing as the actor Richard Greene showed a Pinewood Studios pass. The contract was voidable, and Mr Averay, who bought the car from the rogue in good faith, kept it. This was a mistake as to attributes, not identity.
- the materials also lists *Lake v Simmons* (1927) as taking the "void" approach in a fraud case. No detail is given in the sources.

**Shogun Finance Ltd v Hudson** [2003] UKHL 62, [2004] 1 AC 919 (HL, 3–2):
- *Facts*: a rogue used Mr Durlabh Patel's stolen driving licence to take a car on hire purchase. The dealer faxed the licence and a draft agreement signed in Patel's name to Shogun, which ran a credit check on Patel and approved the deal. The rogue sold the car to Mr Hudson, who bought in good faith.
- *Held*: the hire-purchase agreement was a written contract naming Mr Patel. The rogue was not the "debtor", so the Hire Purchase Act 1964 s 27 did not protect Hudson. There was no contract between Shogun and the rogue, and Shogun recovered the car or its value.
- *Majority reasoning*:
  - Lord Hobhouse relied on the parol evidence rule: the written terms identify the parties and extrinsic evidence cannot contradict them.
  - Lords Phillips and Walker took an "intent" approach: to whom was the offer or acceptance objectively directed?
  - The majority affirmed *Cundy*.
- *Minority*: Lords Nicholls and Millett would have overruled *Cundy*, holding that a contract is made with the person physically dealt with, whether in person or by correspondence. They preferred to protect the innocent third-party purchaser.
- All their Lordships supported the face-to-face presumption (*Phillips*, *Lewis*), leaving *Ingram* hard to justify.
- *Telephone and video dealings*: unresolved. Lords Millett and Nicholls saw no logic in excluding them from "face-to-face". Lord Walker accepted the presumption might extend to them but would be easier to rebut. Lord Phillips kept the ordinary meaning. Lord Hobhouse expressed no view.
- *After Shogun*: failing to verify identity no longer seems fatal (Concentrate). McKendrick argues that, read with Stevens, the question is one of construction: was the identity of the counterparty a term of the bargain? Face-to-face dealing points one way and arm's-length dealing the other, but neither is conclusive.
- *Lecture's summary of current rules*:
  - the offer must objectively be directed at someone else (*Boulton v Jones*);
  - where identity is stated in a written contract, the contract is only with the named party (*Cundy*, *Shogun*);
  - the intended party must be an existing, identifiable person, otherwise the contract is voidable only (*King's Norton*);
  - face-to-face contracts are voidable, not void (*Phillips*, *Lewis*, *Shogun*).

**Statutory protection for third parties.** A rogue who is a buyer in possession may pass title under Factors Act 1889 s 9 and SGA 1979 s 25(1), which can override even a *Caldwell*-style rescission (*Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560, per the Concentrate Q&A).

### 5. Non est factum
- This is a narrow defence that lets a signer say a signed document "is not my deed". It is narrow because third parties rely on signatures.
- **Saunders v Anglia Building Society** (sub nom *Gallie v Lee*) [1971] AC 1004 (HL): Mrs Gallie, aged 78, had broken her glasses. She signed what Lee told her was a deed of gift of her house to her nephew. It was in fact an assignment to Lee, who mortgaged the house to the building society and defaulted. The plea **failed**.
- **Requirements** (McKendrick, from Lord Reid):
  1. the signer was permanently or temporarily unable, through no fault of their own, to have any real understanding of the document without explanation (for example blindness, illiteracy, illness, innate incapacity or defective education);
  2. there was a real or substantial difference between the document signed and the document they believed they were signing;
  3. the signer was not careless and took all reasonable precautions. The burden is heavy. Someone "too busy or too lazy" to read cannot rely on the plea (Lord Reid), and *United Dominions Trust v Western* [1976] QB 513 holds that carelessness or failing to read bars it.
- Lord Wilberforce (quoted in the materials) kept the plea for a "residue of difficult cases", such as illiterate or senile signers or people tricked into signing, as a rare "instrument of justice".
- the materials adds that carelessness matters especially in three-party cases, and that misrepresentation or unconscionability may give alternative relief.

### 6. Rectification
- **Nature**: an equitable, discretionary order correcting a written document that fails to record what the parties agreed (*Daventry DC v Daventry & District Housing Ltd* [2011] EWCA Civ 1153: discretionary but principled). It corrects the *recording*, not the making, of the contract, and it cannot create new terms the parties never agreed (lecture, citing *Daventry*). Lord Hoffmann, speaking extrajudicially, identified two forms based on different moral principles:
  - parties should keep their promises (common mistake rectification);
  - negotiating parties must observe standards of good faith (unilateral mistake rectification).
- **Common mistake rectification** (*Swainland Builders v Freehold Properties* [2002], Peter Gibson LJ). The claimant must show:
  1. a common continuing intention about the matter in question, whether or not amounting to an agreement;
  2. an outward expression of accord;
  3. that the intention continued when the document was executed;
  4. that, by mistake, the document does not reflect it.
- **Objective or subjective intention?**
  - In *Chartbrook Ltd v Persimmon Homes* [2009] UKHL 38, Lord Hoffmann said obiter that the test is objective: what a reasonable observer would take the common intention to be. the materials presents this as the standard.
  - ***FSHC Group Holdings Ltd v GLAS Trust Corp*** [2019] EWCA Civ 1361, [2020] Ch 365 (Leggatt LJ) departed from *Chartbrook*. Where the prior accord is a binding contract, the objective test applies. Where it is a non-binding common understanding, the court looks at the parties' **subjective** intentions, which must be accompanied by an outward expression of accord. Uncommunicated coinciding intentions are not enough.
  - The Privy Council endorsed the *FSHC* distinction in *Porter v Stokes* [2023] UKPC 11.
  - Denning LJ's objective dictum in *Frederick E Rose (London) Ltd v William H Pim Jnr & Co* [1953] 2 QB 450 is explained as belonging to a time before *Joscelyne v Nissen* [1970] 2 QB 86, which held that a prior concluded contract is not needed.
- **Unilateral mistake rectification**:
  - Unilateral mistake alone is not enough (*Riverlate Properties v Paul* [1975] Ch 133: equity "operates on conscience", and a clear conscience at the time of the transaction does not justify disturbing it).
  - It is available where the other party *knew* of the mistake and took advantage of it (*A Roberts & Co v Leicestershire CC* [1961] Ch 555).
  - It is also available where A intended B to be mistaken, diverted B's attention with misleading statements, and B made the intended mistake, even though A only suspected it (*Commission for the New Towns v Cooper (GB) Ltd* [1995] Ch 259).
  - Showing "unconscionable conduct" in arm's-length bargaining is hard (*George Wimpey UK v VI Components* [2005] EWCA Civ 77, Sedley LJ).
- **Bars** listed in the materials: affirmation, and a bona fide purchaser.
- **Relationship with interpretation**: "corrective interpretation" (Lord Hoffmann's fourth and fifth *ICS* principles; *Chartbrook*; *Cherry Tree Investments v Landmain* [2012] EWCA Civ 736) can fix obvious errors. Burrows argued this makes rectification "largely superfluous". Rectification still matters because prior negotiations are admissible in rectification but not in interpretation, because it can reach omitted clauses, and because it may use subjective evidence. See `interpretation-of-contracts.md`.

## Key authorities
- *Bell v Lever Bros* [1932] AC 161 (HL): mistake as to quality must make the thing "essentially different". On the facts the compensation agreements stood.
- *Great Peace Shipping v Tsavliris* [2002] EWCA Civ 1407, [2003] QB 679 (CA): the five elements and the impossibility test. Rejected the *Solle* equitable jurisdiction, so an operative common mistake makes the contract void.
- *Associated Japanese Bank v Crédit du Nord* [1989] 1 WLR 255 (Steyn J): risk allocation first, then a narrow doctrine. Non-existent machines.
- *Couturier v Hastie* (1856) 5 HLC 673 (HL); SGA 1979 s 6: perished goods.
- *McRae v Commonwealth Disposals Commission* (1951) 84 CLR 377 (HCA): the seller promised that a non-existent tanker existed, so it was liable for reliance loss.
- *Cooper v Phibbs* (1867) LR 2 HL 149: the buyer already owned what was sold (res sua).
- *Solle v Butcher* [1950] 1 KB 671 (CA): equitable mistake, now disapproved.
- *Brennan v Bolt Burdon* [2004] EWCA Civ 1017: common mistake of law; the impossibility test.
- *Smith v Hughes* (1871) LR 6 QB 597: the objective test; the quality / terms distinction (old oats).
- *Hartog v Colin & Shields* [1939] 3 All ER 566: an offer the buyer knew was mistaken (per pound / per piece). Contract void.
- *Raffles v Wichelhaus* (1864) 2 H & C 906: latent ambiguity (two ships called Peerless).
- *Scriven v Hindley* [1913] 3 KB 564: tow and hemp; no contract.
- *Cundy v Lindsay* (1878) 3 App Cas 459 (HL): written dealing, identity crucial, void.
- *King's Norton Metal v Edridge Merrett* (1897): a fictitious entity, so voidable only.
- *Phillips v Brooks* [1919] 2 KB 243; *Lewis v Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198: face to face, voidable.
- *Ingram v Little* [1961] 1 QB 31: face to face but held void. Out of line with the others.
- *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62, [2004] 1 AC 919 (HL): a written contract naming a third party; no contract with the rogue.
- *Saunders v Anglia Building Society* [1971] AC 1004 (HL): the narrow limits of non est factum.
- *Chartbrook v Persimmon* [2009] UKHL 38; *FSHC Group v GLAS Trust* [2019] EWCA Civ 1361; *Daventry DC v Daventry & District Housing* [2011] EWCA Civ 1153: rectification.
- *Roberts v Leicestershire CC* [1961] Ch 555; *Commission for the New Towns v Cooper* [1995] Ch 259: rectification for unilateral mistake.
- **Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 6**: specific goods that have perished without the seller's knowledge before the contract make it void.

## Debate and critique
- **Is there a doctrine of mistake at all?** One view, associated with Dixon and Fullagar JJ in *McRae* and with Slade's "The Myth of Mistake in the English Law of Contract" (1954) 70 LQR 385 (on the materials list; its argument is not summarised in the sources), is that the "mistake" cases are really about construction, implied conditions, risk allocation and offer and acceptance. *Bell*, *Associated Japanese Bank* and *Great Peace* nonetheless accept a separate, narrow doctrine that applies once construction has run out. The Concentrate Q&A also notes the suggestion that unconscionability doctrines could do the work instead.
- **Substance and quality.** The Concentrate essay question asks whether "the courts will not relieve mistakes of quality". *Bell*'s facts could hardly have been more extreme (paying £50,000 for nothing), which suggests that quality mistakes almost never qualify. *Great Peace*'s impossibility test drops the substance/quality vocabulary but, the authors argue, only moves the difficulty into the meaning of "impossibility". In *Swynson v Lowick Rose* [2017] UKSC 32, Lord Sumption declined to enter the "controversy".
- **Was *Great Peace* right to abolish equitable mistake?** McKendrick calls it "a bold decision", correct on its facts but arguably beyond the CA's proper role, because *Solle* had been followed for 50 years and the facts did not require it to be overruled. He says the CA removed flexibility and then invited Parliament to restore it (at [161]), and calls the analogy with the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 dubious. Toulson J at first instance suggested *Solle* was explicable because the surveyor-landlord had caused the mistake, which raises the question whether "unconscionable dealing" can get around *Great Peace*. The *FII* dictum keeps the question open.
- **Should operative common mistake always make the contract void?** Chandler, Devenney and Poole, "Common Mistake: Theoretical Justification and Remedial Inflexibility" [2004] JBL 34, argue for a more flexible, discretionary remedy. The all-or-nothing nature of voidness is criticised.
- **Reform models.**
  - PECL arts 4:102–4:105: initial impossibility does not invalidate a contract. Avoidance is allowed for a fundamental mistake caused by the other party, known to the other party contrary to good faith, or shared, unless the mistake was inexcusable or its risk was assumed. The court can adapt the contract.
  - Beale, *Mistake and Non-Disclosure of Facts* (2012), calls English law's refusal to relieve a mistake the other party knows about "difficult to square with morality". He proposes avoidance where the other party knew of the mistake and its importance and, contrary to good faith, left the mistaken party in error (not covering value-only mistakes or assumed risks), with damages.
- **Identity cases and the two innocent parties.** The question is who should bear a rogue's fraud.
  - English law starts from *nemo dat*, whereas some systems apply *possession vaut titre*.
  - The *Shogun* minority favoured the third-party buyer. The majority (Lord Hobhouse) said reform was for Parliament, since *nemo dat* is in the SGA.
  - McKendrick asks why a finance company that accepts credit risk should be able to shift the risk of lies about *name* onto an innocent buyer when it could not shift the risk of lies about *finances*.
  - Devlin LJ's dissent in *Ingram* proposed apportioning the loss between the two innocent parties, but the Law Reform Committee's 12th Report (Cmnd 2958, 1966) rejected this as too uncertain. It recommended instead that such contracts be treated as voidable so far as third parties' title is concerned (Concentrate Q&A).
  - The Hobhouse written/oral distinction is criticised as resting on a parol evidence rule of declining importance (McLauchlan (2005) 121 LQR 9). Stevens's construction-based approach is offered as an alternative. MacMillan [2005] CLJ 711 gives the historical background.
- **Objectivity and mistake.** Chen-Wishart, "Objectivity and Mistake: The Oxymoron of *Smith v Hughes*" (2009), and Stevens, "Objectivity, Mistake and the Parol Evidence Rule" (2007), are both on the materials list. the materials that the objective approach has been called absurd, because it can produce a contract neither party wanted.
- **Rectification after *FSHC*.** Commentary by Stevens (2020) 136 LQR 599, Beale and Beale [2020] LMCLQ 1 and Peel (2020) 136 LQR 205 debates the departure from Lord Hoffmann's *Chartbrook* view. Davies (2016) 75 CLJ 62 (lecture list) addresses rectification versus interpretation.
- **Further lecture reading, listed only**: Atiyah and Bennion (1961) 24 MLR 421; McMeel [2006] LMCLQ 49; MacMillan, "How Temptation Led to Mistake" (2003) 119 LQR 625 (on *Bell*); Smith (1994) 110 LQR 400.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Sort the mistakes.** Is it shared (common), crossed (mutual), or one-sided and known (unilateral)? Is it about identity, about what a signed document is, or about how the agreement was recorded (rectification)? Was it *induced* by a false statement? If so, run misrepresentation as well, because voidability for fraud is often the realistic fallback.
2. **Common mistake checklist.**
   - Risk allocation first: express or implied warranties or conditions precedent (*McRae*, *Associated Japanese Bank*, *John Lobb*). In sales, consider caveat emptor or caveat venditor. Antique buyers hoping for a "hidden gem" take the value risk.
   - Then the *Great Peace* elements: a shared assumption; no warranty; no fault; impossibility, or perhaps "radically different" performance; and the assumption going to the existence or a vital attribute of the consideration.
   - Perished specific goods: apply SGA s 6, unless the seller promised the goods existed.
   - Quality: almost never enough (*Bell*, *Leaf*). A painting believed to be by a famous artist is still a painting.
   - Remember there is no equitable fallback after *Great Peace* (but mention *FII* for extra marks).
3. **Mutual mistake.** Apply the objective test: would a reasonable observer see agreement on one meaning (*Wood v Scarth*, *Scott v Littledale*) or genuine ambiguity (*Raffles*, *Scriven*)? Third-party errors, such as a misprinted advertisement or a misinformed friend, can produce fundamental cross-purposes (*Henkel v Pape*).
4. **Unilateral mistake as to terms.** Did the other party know, or ought they to have known, that the mistaken party was wrong about *the promise itself* (*Hartog*; *Centrovincial*)? Or was the mistake only about quality or value (*Smith v Hughes*), in which case there is a contract?
5. **Identity.**
   - Was the deal in writing, naming a real third person? If so, *Shogun* and *Cundy* point to void.
   - Was the third person fictitious (*King's Norton*)? If so, voidable.
   - Was it face to face? If so, voidable (*Phillips*, *Lewis*). Note telephone and email uncertainty.
   - Then deal with the third party: whether rescission came in time (*Caldwell*); buyer-in-possession statutes (SGA s 25, Factors Act s 9); HPA 1964 s 27 for motor vehicles on hire purchase.
6. **Non est factum.** Look for a signer under a disability or deceived about the *type* of document, a radical difference between what was signed and what was intended, and *no carelessness*. Expect the plea to fail for a capable but careless signer (*Saunders*; *UDT v Western*).
7. **Rectification.** Ask:
   - Is there a prior common intention with an outward expression of accord, still continuing at signing?
   - Was the prior accord a contract (objective test) or a common understanding (subjective test, *FSHC*)?
   - For unilateral mistake, did the other party know and take advantage, or engage in deliberate diversion (*Roberts*; *Commission for the New Towns v Cooper*)?
   - Could corrective interpretation solve the problem more simply?
   - Are there bars, such as a bona fide purchaser or affirmation?

**Typical traps**:
- going straight to *Bell* without asking whether the contract allocated the risk;
- saying *Solle v Butcher* is good law;
- treating any knowledge of the other party's mistake as fatal (it must concern the terms);
- forgetting that "void" defeats innocent third parties while "voidable" protects them;
- confusing mistake (the assumption is false at the time of contracting) with frustration (a later event).
