# Interpretation of Contracts

## Overview
Once it is known what the terms are, the court must decide what they mean. A great many contract disputes are really disputes about meaning, and the answer often decides whether there has been a breach at all. English law (England and Wales) interprets contracts **objectively**: the question is what a reasonable person with the parties' background knowledge would have understood the words to mean, not what either party privately intended. The modern story runs from a literal "four corners" approach, through the contextual restatement in *Investors Compensation Scheme* (1998), to a partial swing back towards the text in *Arnold v Britton* (2015) and the reconciliation in *Wood v Capita* (2017).

## The law
1. **Traditional literal approach.** Words were given their plain grammatical meaning, and the meaning was found within the "four corners" of the document. the materials cites *In the Goods of Peel* (1870) LR 2 P & D 46 and *Shore v Wilson* (1842) 9 Cl & Fin 355. McKendrick quotes Cozens-Hardy MR in *Lovell and Christmas Ltd v Wall* (1911) 104 LT 85: it is "irrelevant and improper" to ask what the parties intended before signing. There were limited exceptions, e.g. evidence of trade usage that "1000 rabbits meant 1200". The advantage was speed and cheapness. The drawback was an unrealistic divorce from context.
2. **The turn to context.** In *Prenn v Simmonds* [1971] 1 WLR 1381, Lord Wilberforce said agreements are no longer isolated from the "matrix of facts" in which they were set. *Reardon Smith Line v Yngvar Hansen-Tangen* [1976] 1 WLR 989 continued this.
3. **Lord Hoffmann's five principles: *Investors Compensation Scheme Ltd v West Bromwich Building Society* [1998] 1 WLR 896.** He said "almost all the old intellectual baggage" of legal interpretation had been discarded.
   - (1) Interpretation is "the ascertainment of the meaning which the document would convey to a reasonable person having all the background knowledge which would reasonably have been available to the parties in the situation in which they were at the time of the contract."
   - (2) The background (the "matrix of fact") includes "absolutely anything" reasonably available to the parties that would have affected how the language was understood.
   - (3) Previous negotiations and declarations of subjective intent are excluded. They are admissible only in rectification.
   - (4) The meaning of a document is not the same as the dictionary meaning of its words.
   - (5) The background may show that something has gone wrong with the language. If so, the law "does not require judges to attribute to the parties an intention which they plainly could not have had". Compare Lord Diplock in *The Antaios* [1985] AC 191: semantic analysis that "flouts business common sense" must yield to it.
4. **Rainy Sky.** In *Rainy Sky SA v Kookmin Bank* [2011] UKSC 50, Lord Clarke said the aim is to find what a reasonable person would have understood the parties to have meant. Where there are rival meanings, the court may prefer the one more consistent with business common sense. But "where the parties have used unambiguous language, the court must apply it" (at [23]).
5. **Marley v Rawlings** [2014] UKSC 2 (Lord Neuberger, quoted in the materials). Meaning is found in light of five things: natural and ordinary meaning, the document's overall purpose, other provisions, the facts known or assumed when it was executed, and common sense. Subjective evidence of intention is ignored.
6. **Arnold v Britton** [2015] UKSC 36, [2015] AC 1619 (Lord Neuberger). the materials gives the citation as [2015] UKSC 46; the textbook gives UKSC 36. The court focuses on the meaning of the words in their documentary, factual and commercial context. That meaning is assessed by six factors: (i) natural and ordinary meaning, (ii) other relevant provisions, (iii) overall purpose of the clause and the contract, (iv) facts and circumstances known or assumed at the time, (v) commercial common sense, but (vi) disregarding subjective evidence of intention. Lord Neuberger then emphasised several points:
   - Commercial common sense and surrounding circumstances must not be used to undervalue the language. Save perhaps in a very unusual case, meaning is "most obviously to be gleaned from the language of the provision", because the parties control the language and were focusing on the issue.
   - The worse the drafting, the readier the court may be to depart from the natural meaning. But the court must not go looking for drafting errors.
   - Commercial common sense is not to be invoked retrospectively. That a contract has worked out badly, even disastrously, for one party is no reason to depart from its words.
   - The court should be very slow to reject the natural meaning just because the term looks imprudent. The purpose is to identify what the parties agreed, not what the court thinks they should have agreed.
7. **Wood v Capita Insurance Services Ltd** [2017] UKSC 24, [2017] AC 1173 (Lord Hodge; the materials attributes the passage to "Lord Bridge"). Textualism and contextualism "are not conflicting paradigms in a battle for exclusive occupation of the field". They are tools whose usefulness varies with the contract:
   - Sophisticated, professionally drafted agreements may be interpreted mainly by textual analysis.
   - Informal or brief agreements, or those made without professional help, call for more weight on the factual matrix.
   - Even professionally drawn contracts may lack clarity because of compromise or deadlines.
   Interpretation is a "unitary exercise" and an "iterative process" (citing *Re Sigma Finance* [2010]). *Rainy Sky* and *Arnold* "were saying the same thing", and the history is "one of continuity rather than change".
8. **The exclusionary rule for negotiations: *Chartbrook Ltd v Persimmon Homes Ltd* [2009] UKHL 38.** The House of Lords kept the rule that pre-contractual negotiations are inadmissible, in the interest of certainty, predictability and lower litigation costs. The "safety devices" are rectification and estoppel by convention.
   - Negotiations that are part of the factual matrix (e.g. to show the "genesis and aim" of the transaction) may be admissible: *Oceanbulk Shipping and Trading SA v TMT Asia Ltd* [2010] UKSC 44. A mere negotiating position of one party is not admissible (*Northrop Grumman v BAE Systems* [2015] EWCA Civ 844).
   - Evidence of the parties' conduct after the contract was made is inadmissible (*Schuler AG v Wickman* [1974] AC 235).
9. **Corrective interpretation.** Where it is clear that something has gone wrong with the language *and* clear what a reasonable person would have understood the parties to mean, the court may read the words differently. There is "not ... a limit to the amount of red ink" (*Chartbrook* at [25]; *Mannai* "12th" for "13th"). But it "requires a strong case" (*Chartbrook* at [15]). The fact that a provision favours one side is not enough; the result must make the provisions "arbitrary and irrational". Judges should not see *Chartbrook* as "an open sesame for reconstructing the parties' contract" (Rix LJ, *ING Bank v Ros Roca* [2011] EWCA Civ 353).
10. **Special rules for exclusion clauses** (see `exemption-clauses-and-unfair-terms.md`). Clear words are needed for a party to give up a valuable right (*Triple Point Technology v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29). The contra proferentem rule still applies to ambiguity. In consumer contracts, the meaning most favourable to the consumer prevails (CRA 2015 s 69(1)).
11. **Relationship with implication.** Express terms must be interpreted before any question of implication arises. The two are different processes (*Marks & Spencer v BNP Paribas* [2015] UKSC 72, rejecting Lord Hoffmann's merger of them in *Belize Telecom*). See `terms-of-the-contract.md`.

## Key authorities
- *Prenn v Simmonds* [1971] 1 WLR 1381 (HL): contracts are read in their matrix of fact.
- *Investors Compensation Scheme v West Bromwich BS* [1998] 1 WLR 896 (HL): Lord Hoffmann's five principles of contextual interpretation.
- *Rainy Sky SA v Kookmin Bank* [2011] UKSC 50: where meanings compete, the more commercially sensible construction wins; unambiguous words must be applied.
- *Marley v Rawlings* [2014] UKSC 2: Lord Neuberger's list of factors.
- *Arnold v Britton* [2015] UKSC 36: primacy of the language; no retrospective use of commercial common sense; courts do not rescue parties from bad bargains.
- *Wood v Capita Insurance Services* [2017] UKSC 24: text and context are complementary tools, weighted by the type of contract.
- *Chartbrook v Persimmon Homes* [2009] UKHL 38: prior negotiations stay excluded; corrective interpretation needs a clear mistake and a clear intended meaning.
- *Oceanbulk v TMT Asia* [2010] UKSC 44: negotiations admissible as part of the factual matrix.
- *Schuler v Wickman* [1974] AC 235 (HL): post-contractual conduct is inadmissible; an unreasonable result is a guide to the parties' intention.
- *Mannai Investment Co v Eagle Star* [1997] AC 749: an obvious slip in a notice was corrected by interpretation.
- Consumer Rights Act 2015 s 69(1): ambiguity in a consumer contract is resolved in the consumer's favour.

## Debate and critique
- **Certainty vs contextual justice.** Lord Hoffmann's restatement was criticised for generating uncertainty, widening the room to depart from natural meaning, and adding to the cost and complexity of litigation. McKendrick reads *Arnold* and *Wood* as the "judicial pendulum" swinging back towards certainty, especially for sophisticated contracts drafted with legal advice. Lord Hodge presents the law as continuous rather than as a change in direction.
- **Is ICS really new?** Lord Bingham, writing extrajudicially ('A new thing under the sun? The interpretation of contracts and the ICS decision', Edin LR Vol 12, 374), showed that putting the court in the parties' shoes has a long pedigree. Lord Hodge relied on this in *Wood*.
- **Corrective interpretation vs rectification.** Sir Richard Buxton ('"Construction" and Rectification after Chartbrook' [2010] CLJ 253) called Lord Hoffmann's fifth principle "revolutionary". In his view it overrides the old understanding that, rectification apart, a court cannot find an agreement different from the document, and it confuses what the parties said with what they meant to say. Lord Clarke in *Oceanbulk* accepted a "close relationship" between interpretation and rectification without endorsing that criticism.
- **The exclusionary rule.** *Chartbrook* accepts that the rule will sometimes bind parties to terms a full look at the negotiations would show they did not intend. Lord Hoffmann justified this by "economy and predictability", left reform to empirical study (e.g. by the Law Commission), and relied on rectification and estoppel by convention as safety nets. McKendrick doubts how much protection those safety nets give, because both are narrow. The *Oceanbulk* exception helps but is hard to police (*Merthyr (South Wales) Ltd v Merthyr Tydfil CBC* [2019] EWCA Civ 526).
- **Purposive interpretation.** Robertson, 'Purposive Contractual Interpretation' (2019) 39 Legal Studies 230, is optional reading on the theory behind the modern approach.
- **Interpretation and implication.** Whether implication is part of interpretation (Lord Hoffmann in *Belize*; Kramer; McLauchlan) or a separate process (Lord Neuberger in *M&S*) is a live essay theme. See `terms-of-the-contract.md`.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the disputed words and the rival meanings each party puts forward.
2. Start with the natural and ordinary meaning in the context of the whole document (*Arnold*), including other clauses and the document's structure.
3. Ask what background was reasonably available to *both* parties at the time of contracting (*ICS* principle 2). Exclude negotiations and subjective intent (*Chartbrook*), but consider whether some negotiation facts are part of the matrix (*Oceanbulk*). Exclude post-contract conduct (*Schuler*).
4. Consider the type of contract (*Wood*). A sophisticated, lawyer-drafted contract leans to text. An informal or brief one leans to context.
5. If the words are ambiguous, prefer the more commercially sensible construction (*Rainy Sky*). Do not rewrite a clear but bad bargain with hindsight (*Arnold*).
6. If one party says there was a drafting mistake, test it: is it *clear* that something went wrong *and* clear what was meant (*Chartbrook*)? If not, consider rectification instead.
7. For exclusion or limitation clauses, apply the clear-words principle and contra proferentem. For consumer contracts, apply CRA s 69(1).
8. **Traps:**
   - Citing *ICS* as if it licensed any commercially sensible rewrite.
   - Relying on what the parties said in negotiations.
   - Treating a bad bargain as evidence of a drafting error.
   - Overlooking the distinction between interpretation and implication.
