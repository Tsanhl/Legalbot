# Royscot Trust Ltd v Rogerson [1991] 2 QB 297 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Balcombe LJ and Ralph Gibson LJ), decided 21 March 1991 ([1991] EWCA Civ 12). The case holds that damages under **s 2(1) Misrepresentation Act 1967** are assessed on the **tort of deceit measure**, even where the misrepresentation was not fraudulent.

## Facts
Rogerson acquired a used Honda Prelude on hire purchase through a dealer, with Royscot Trust providing the finance. The dealer overstated the price (£8,000) and the deposit (£1,600) on the forms sent to Royscot, so the deposit appeared to meet Royscot's required minimum percentage. Royscot would not have lent on the true figures. Rogerson later sold the car without having title and stopped paying. Royscot sued the dealer for its loss (about £3,625). The dealer argued that the loss was caused by Rogerson's wrongful sale, not its misstatement, and that it should not be liable for unforeseeable loss.

## Held
- The words "shall be so liable" in s 2(1) mean the representor is liable as if the misrepresentation had been **fraudulent**. Damages therefore follow the deceit measure: all direct loss, including loss that was not reasonably foreseeable.
- Rogerson's wrongful sale was in any case foreseeable and did not break the chain of causation.
- The dealer's appeal failed and Royscot recovered its loss.

## Why it matters
- Leading authority on the s 2(1) measure of damages; known as the "fiction of fraud" approach.
- Widely criticised as giving innocent or careless representors liability as heavy as fraudsters, and for not mentioning *Derry v Peek*. Commentators have preferred a foreseeability-based measure.
- Read with *Smith New Court*, which sets out the deceit measure itself.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm a false statement of fact that induced the contract, and that the representor cannot prove reasonable belief (s 2(1)).
2. Assess damages on the deceit measure (direct consequences, no foreseeability limit), then address causation and mitigation.
3. Note the criticism and any argument that the measure should instead be foreseeability-based.
