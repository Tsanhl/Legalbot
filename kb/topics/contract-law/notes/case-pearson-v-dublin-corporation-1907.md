# S Pearson & Son Ltd v Dublin Corporation [1907] AC 351 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them. Also cited as [1907] UKHL 960.

## Overview
House of Lords (on appeal from Ireland), decided 30 May 1907; Lord Loreburn LC gave reasons on the contract terms. The case establishes that a contractual clause requiring a party to satisfy itself about the facts does **not protect against fraud**, and that a **principal is liable for fraud of its agent** in making a representation.

## Facts
Pearson contracted to build sewage works for Dublin Corporation. The Corporation's engineers prepared plans showing an existing wall extending nine feet below Ordnance datum. The wall did not extend so deep, and Pearson said the engineers had no reasonable basis for the statement. Pearson said it tendered a lower price in reliance. The contract required the contractor to satisfy itself about existing works and not to rely on the plans. Pearson sued in deceit. The trial judge withdrew the case from the jury because of the contract terms.

## Held
- The appeal was allowed and a **new trial** ordered; the issue of fraud had to go to the jury.
- The contract clauses did not bar a claim in deceit: in Lord Loreburn's words, they "contemplate honesty on both sides and protect only against honest mistakes".
- The Corporation could be liable for fraud by its engineers, although the engineers had not themselves made the statements to Pearson, because the principal is answerable for the agent's fraud in the course of the agent's role.
- The House did not itself find fraud proved or award damages.

## Why it matters
- Core authority that **exclusion clauses cannot exclude liability for the maker's own fraud**; later reflected in the reasoning that a clause excluding fraud cannot be reasonable (see *Thomas Witter*).
- Authority on fraud by agents and attribution to the principal.
- Related to *Derry v Peek* (definition of fraud) and *Smith New Court* (damages in deceit).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. If a clause purports to exclude reliance on statements, ask whether fraud is alleged; fraud is not excluded.
2. If the person making the representation is an agent or employee, ask whether the principal is liable for the agent's fraud.
3. Then apply the *Derry v Peek* test and the damages principles.
