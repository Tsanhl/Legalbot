# Transfield Shipping Inc v Mercator Shipping Inc (The Achilleas) [2008] UKHL 48 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report ([2009] 1 AC 61) before citing them. BAILII and the parliamentary judgment page could not be read when this note was written.

## Overview
House of Lords, unanimous result (Lord Hoffmann gave the leading reasoning). The case concerns **remoteness of damage in contract** where a charterer redelivered a ship late and the owners lost a more lucrative follow-on charter. It is cited for the idea that remoteness turns on the **scope of the liability the defendant can reasonably be taken to have assumed**, not only on what was foreseeable.

## Facts
Under a time charter the vessel was redelivered late. The owners had already fixed the ship to new charterers at a rate much higher than the original rate, because the market had risen. When the ship missed the new charterers' cancellation date, the owners kept the fixture only by agreeing a reduced hire rate for the follow-on charter. They claimed that loss from the original charterers. The arbitrators (by a majority), the Commercial Court and the Court of Appeal all held the loss recoverable.

## Held
- The House of Lords allowed the charterers' appeal. Liability was limited to the difference between the charter rate and the market rate for the overrun period; the lost follow-on fixture was **too remote**.
- Lord Hoffmann treated contractual liability as voluntarily assumed and judged objectively: a reasonable charterer would not have understood that it was taking on this type of risk, which was in practice unquantifiable (it could not know the timing, length or terms of the owners' next fixture).
- He also relied on the shipping market's general understanding that liability for late redelivery was limited in this way.
- The other Lords' reasoning was more cautious, and the exact status of the assumption-of-responsibility approach is still debated (see below).

## Why it matters
- Leading modern authority on remoteness after *Hadley v Baxendale* and *The Heron II*; it reframes the question as one about what the contract-breaker assumed responsibility for.
- Shows that **market understanding** can limit recoverable loss for late performance.
- Practical lesson: a party exposed to an unusual risk should draw it to the other side's attention before contracting.
- Later courts (e.g. *Sylvia Shipping v Progress Bulk Carriers* (2010), as reported in the Wikipedia account) have treated the orthodox *Hadley* test as the normal approach, with the broader assumption-of-responsibility reasoning reserved for exceptional cases. Check current authority.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the type of loss claimed and apply the two limbs of *Hadley v Baxendale* first.
2. Where the loss is unusual, unquantifiable or outside market expectations, ask whether the defendant can fairly be taken to have assumed responsibility for that type of loss.
3. Note the arguments both ways on how far *The Achilleas* departs from ordinary foreseeability.
