# Promissory Estoppel

## Overview
Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine (England & Wales) that stops a party from going back on a clear promise not to enforce its strict legal rights, where the other party has relied on that promise and it would be inequitable to let the promisor resile. It matters because it gives limited effect to variation promises that are not supported by consideration, especially promises to accept less (the *Foakes v Beer* problem). Its limits are central to the topic. It needs an existing legal relationship, it generally suspends rights rather than extinguishing them, and in English law it is "a shield, not a sword": it cannot create a new cause of action (*Combe v Combe*). This sets English law apart from Australia (*Waltons Stores v Maher*) and the US (Restatement §90).

## The law

### 1. The idea of estoppel
- Cooke, *The Modern Law of Estoppel* (2001): estoppel, crudely, is a mechanism for preventing inconsistency.
- Lord Denning MR in *Moorgate Mercantile Co Ltd v Twitchings* [1975] 3 All ER 314, 323: estoppel is not a rule of evidence or a cause of action but "a principle of justice and equity". Where a person's words or conduct have led another to believe in a state of affairs, he will not be allowed to go back on it when that would be unjust or inequitable.
- **Historical obstacle:** *Jorden v Money* (1854) 5 HLC 185 confined estoppel by representation to representations of *existing fact*, not statements of intention or promises. So a mere promise could not found an estoppel by representation. Promissory estoppel grew from a different line of cases (*Hughes*, revived by *High Trees*).

### 2. Origins
1. *Hughes v Metropolitan Railway Co* (1877) 2 App Cas 439 (HL). Lord Cairns (at 448): where parties to a contract enter into negotiations that lead one of them to suppose that strict rights (e.g. a forfeiture) will not be enforced, or will be held in suspense, the other will not be allowed to enforce them where it would be inequitable having regard to the dealings between them.
2. *Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd* [1947] KB 130 (Denning J). Wartime agreement to halve the ground rent of a block of flats; flats full by 1945. Full rent was recoverable from mid-1945. Obiter: the landlord could not have recovered the full rent for the war years, despite the absence of consideration. Denning J said a promise to accept a smaller sum in discharge of a larger one, if acted upon, is binding without consideration, "and if the fusion of law and equity leads to this result, so much the better". Denning J had to choose between *Jorden v Money* and *Hughes*.
3. *Ajayi v R T Briscoe (Nigeria) Ltd* [1964] 1 WLR 1326 (PC). Lord Hodson named the doctrine "promissory estoppel" and set out its qualifications: (i) the promisee must have altered its position; (ii) the promisor can resile on giving reasonable notice (which need not be formal) so the promisee can resume its position; (iii) the promise becomes final and irrevocable only if the promisee cannot resume its position.
4. **Waiver:** Lord Denning in *W J Alan & Co Ltd v El Nasr Export and Import Co* [1972] 2 QB 189 (CA) treated waiver as an instance of the *Hughes*/*High Trees* principle. If one party leads the other to believe strict rights will not be insisted on, intending the other to act on that belief, and he does, the first party cannot later insist on them where that would be inequitable. No consideration, detriment or writing is needed. Rights are at least suspended while the waiver lasts. Denning LJ in *Charles Rickards Ltd v Oppenheim* [1950] 1 KB 616 likewise called waiver "a kind of estoppel" (McKendrick).

### 3. Requirements
1. **An existing legal relationship.** The doctrine applies to *variation* promises (promises that forgo or alter existing rights), not to formation (Concentrate, citing *Umrish Ltd v Gill* (2020); *Combe v Combe*).
2. **A clear and unequivocal promise or representation** that strict rights will not be enforced (in whole or part). *Woodhouse AC Israel Cocoa SA v Nigerian Produce Marketing Co Ltd* [1972] AC 741 (HL) (cited in the materials as "Woodhouse Ltd v Nigerian Products Ltd"): a letter saying payment "can be made in sterling" was too ambiguous to vary the currency of account, so it could not found an estoppel either. Lord Hailsham doubted an ambiguous statement had ever founded a promissory estoppel. The requirement may be met contextually, by implication or even silence, though that will be rare (lecture). It protects promisors: they should lose rights only by clearly giving them up (McKendrick).
3. **Intended to be acted on, and in fact acted on: reliance / alteration of position.** The promisee must reasonably have relied on the promise and altered its position. Reliance is essential, but it **need not be detrimental**: *W J Alan v El Nasr*; *High Trees* (tenants only paid less, no obvious detriment). Robert Goff J in *The Post Chaser* [1982] 1 All ER 19 (lecture: [1981] 2 Lloyd's Rep 695): detriment is not necessary, but not every act of reliance makes it inequitable to resile.
4. **Inequitable for the promisor to go back on the promise.** This overlaps with reliance and is easier to show where there has been detrimental reliance. It is not inequitable where the promise was not freely given. *D & C Builders v Rees* [1966] 2 QB 617 (CA): debtor, knowing the builders were in financial straits, offered £300 for a debt of about £480, effectively "take it or nothing". Lord Denning: there was no "true accord", so the creditor was not barred from claiming the balance. *The Post Chaser*: buyers waived a defect by asking for the documents to be passed to sub-buyers, but because only a short time had passed and the sellers had suffered no prejudice, it was not inequitable for the buyers to reject the documents.

### 4. Effect: suspensory or extinctive?
1. **General rule: suspensory.** Rights are suspended, not extinguished, and can be revived on reasonable notice: *Tool Metal Manufacturing Co Ltd v Tungsten Electric Co Ltd* [1955] 1 WLR 761 (HL) (suspended patent "compensation" payments became payable again after reasonable time following notice). In *Hughes* the landlord could have restarted the six-month repair period by giving notice once negotiations broke down.
2. **Extinctive as to the past / instalments already due.** In *High Trees* the landlord could revive full rent for the future but not claim the shortfall for the war years. So estoppel is generally suspensory for future obligations but may be extinctive for performance already rendered in reliance (McKendrick). In the materials's words, "in some cases" it can extinguish as well as suspend: *High Trees*; *Ajayi*; *Collier v P & MJ Wright (Holdings) Ltd* [2007] EWCA Civ 1329.
3. **Final where position cannot be resumed** (*Ajayi*, Lord Hodson's third qualification).
4. **How it ends.** Either the conditions that founded it cease (as in *High Trees*, the end of wartime conditions) or reasonable notice is given and expires (*Tool Metal*). Concentrate advises that showing reasonable notice is the safer analysis.
5. **Part payment of a lump-sum debt.** *Collier v Wright*: Arden LJ (at [42]) said that if (1) a debtor offers to pay part, (2) the creditor voluntarily accepts, and (3) the debtor pays that part in reliance, the creditor is bound by promissory estoppel to accept it in full satisfaction. It is inequitable to resile, and the estoppel extinguishes the balance. The CA only held this was an *arguable* case, and Longmore LJ was noticeably more cautious, so firm conclusions are hard to draw (lecture). Compare Concentrate's warning that, absent consideration, a creditor might go back on a promise if it learnt two days later that the debtor had come into money.
6. In *MWB v Rock Advertising* [2016] EWCA Civ 553 the CA said (obiter) that MWB would *not* have been estopped: it told Rock of its change of mind two days after the oral variation, which was reasonable notice, and Rock had not been prejudiced (McKendrick).

### 5. A shield not a sword
- *Combe v Combe* [1951] 2 KB 215 (CA). On divorce the husband promised his ex-wife £100 a year. She did not seek maintenance (not at his request). She sued for arrears. The trial judge applied *High Trees*, but the CA reversed: the principle "never stands alone as giving a cause of action in itself". Denning LJ: it cannot abolish the need for consideration where consideration is an essential part of the cause of action. Consideration is "too firmly fixed to be overthrown by a side-wind". It remains essential to the *formation* of contracts, though not to their *modification or discharge*.
- *Baird Textile Holdings Ltd v Marks & Spencer plc* [2001] EWCA Civ 274: M&S's long supplier could not sue on promissory estoppel to enforce an uncertain, non-contractual relationship. The *Waltons Stores* approach was argued and rejected (Concentrate).
- **The metaphor needs care.** Halson, "The Offensive Limits of Promissory Estoppel" [1999] LMCLQ 257, describes a spectrum of uses. Estoppel can be (1) a defence only; (2) used by a claimant with an existing cause of action to defeat a defence; (3) used to prove one element of a recognised cause of action (*Robertson v Minister of Pensions* [1949] 1 KB 227); (4) used to prove all elements (*The Henrik Sif*); or (5) a new cause of action (rejected in *Combe*). Points (1)-(2) are uncontroversial. Points (3)-(4) are contested; some courts read the sword rule to exclude them too (*Riverside Housing Association v White* [2005] EWCA Civ 1385).

### 6. Other estoppels (compare and contrast)
| Type | Essence | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Estoppel by representation | Representation of existing fact relied on to detriment; permanent effect; rule of evidence | *Jorden v Money* |
| Estoppel by convention | Parties act on a shared assumption as the basis of their transaction; each is estopped from denying it where unjust | *Amalgamated Investment & Property Co v Texas Commerce International Bank* [1982] QB 84 (guarantee covering loan by the bank's subsidiary); *Dixon v Blindley Heath Investments* [2015] EWCA Civ 1023 (not limited to mistakes; the difficulty is evidential); described as "notoriously difficult to pin down" in *Tinkler v HMRC* [2021] UKSC 39 |
| Proprietary estoppel | Assurance about an interest in property (usually land), reasonable reliance, detriment; **can found a cause of action** | *Crabb v Arun DC* [1976] Ch 170; *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18 (Lord Walker's three elements); *Yeoman's Row v Cobbe* [2008] UKHL 55 (unconscionability alone is not enough); Land Registration Act 2002 s 116 |
| Contractual estoppel | A contract term (e.g. non-reliance clause) stops A asserting reliance on B's pre-contract statements, apparently without needing unconscionability | *Peekay Intermark v ANZ* [2006] EWCA Civ 386; *Springwell Navigation v JP Morgan Chase* [2010] EWCA Civ 1221; *Axa Sun Life v Campbell Martin* [2011] EWCA Civ 133 |

McKendrick notes key differences. Promissory estoppel needs a clear promise, while proprietary estoppel tolerates imprecise assurances. Estoppel by representation concerns fact and is permanent; promissory estoppel concerns the future and is suspensory. Detriment is required for some estoppels but not promissory estoppel. Only proprietary estoppel can found a cause of action (*Tinkler* at [75]). Courts have not been keen to unify the estoppels (*Tinkler*).

## Key authorities
- *Hughes v Metropolitan Railway* (1877) 2 App Cas 439 (HL): landlord's repair notice; tenant led to think time would not run during negotiations; forfeiture not allowed. Source of the principle.
- *Central London Property Trust v High Trees House* [1947] KB 130: wartime rent reduction binding while conditions lasted; obiter, past shortfall irrecoverable. Revival of the principle.
- *Combe v Combe* [1951] 2 KB 215 (CA): shield not sword; no cause of action.
- *Ajayi v R T Briscoe* [1964] 1 WLR 1326 (PC): name of the doctrine; alteration of position; resiling on reasonable notice; finality where position cannot be resumed.
- *Tool Metal v Tungsten Electric* [1955] 1 WLR 761 (HL): suspensory effect; rights revived after reasonable notice.
- *Woodhouse AC Israel Cocoa v Nigerian Produce Marketing* [1972] AC 741 (HL): promise must be clear and unequivocal.
- *W J Alan v El Nasr* [1972] 2 QB 189 (CA): waiver as part of the principle; reliance need not be detrimental.
- *D & C Builders v Rees* [1966] 2 QB 617 (CA): no estoppel where the creditor's agreement was extracted by pressure; no "true accord".
- *The Post Chaser* [1982] 1 All ER 19: detriment unnecessary, but it must be inequitable to resile; short time and no prejudice meant no estoppel.
- *Collier v P & MJ Wright (Holdings)* [2007] EWCA Civ 1329: arguable that estoppel can extinguish the balance of a debt where part payment is voluntarily accepted.
- *Baird Textile v Marks & Spencer* [2001] EWCA Civ 274: no promissory estoppel cause of action; *Waltons* not adopted.
- *Moorgate Mercantile v Twitchings* [1975]: Denning's general statement of estoppel as a principle of justice.
- *Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd v Maher* (1988) 164 CLR 387 (High Court of Australia): prospective landlord demolished and began building after the prospective tenant's solicitors indicated agreement was imminent. The tenant went quiet and then withdrew; estoppel was available as a cause of action. Rationale: estoppel protects against detrimental reliance, whereas contract protects expectations.
- US Restatement (2d) of Contracts §90(1): a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, and which does, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement; the remedy may be limited as justice requires.

## Debate and critique
- **Does *High Trees* outflank *Foakes v Beer*?** If a creditor's promise to accept less is binding once acted on, the part-payment rule loses much of its force. But the effect is usually only suspensory and depends on equity. *D & C Builders* shows the doctrine filtering out pressured agreements, which some argue is a better tool than consideration (duress-based control). The Concentrate guide stresses that estoppel is a "lower form of enforceability" than consideration, so analyse consideration first.
- **Why no sword?** *Combe*'s justification is that a sword would destroy consideration. Critics (McKendrick; Barnes, "Estoppels as Swords" [2011] LMCLQ) ask why proprietary estoppel can found a claim but promissory estoppel cannot, and why estoppel may supply part of a cause of action but not all of it. *Waltons Stores* answers that estoppel and contract protect different interests (reliance vs expectation) and that the remedy can be limited to the minimum needed to avoid detriment. Australia therefore allows estoppel at formation stage and as a cause of action, as does §90 of the US Restatement. English law has refused (*Baird*).
- **Limits listed by Concentrate:** it works only for variation promises (least controversial); only as a defence; and the meaning of "suspensory" is unclear.
- **Uncertain scope of extinctive effect.** Arden LJ in *Collier* went further than earlier authority; Longmore LJ was more guarded. McKendrick identifies uncertainty over remedies, the relationship between the estoppels, and the sword question as reasons the law is not in a satisfactory state.
- **Relationship with *Roffey*.** Arguments to extend estoppel draw strength from criticism of *Williams v Roffey* as "invented consideration". If reliance were the test for enforcing variations, it would be more honest than finding a practical benefit (Concentrate; Atiyah's reliance theory vs Treitel). *Antons Trawling Co v Smith* (2003) (NZ CA) enforced a variation on the basis of reliance.
- **Contractual estoppel** is controversial: McMeel, "Documentary Fundamentalism in the Senior Courts: The Myth of Contractual Estoppel" [2011] LMCLQ 185; Trukhtanov (2014) 130 LQR 3; Loi [2015] LMCLQ 346.
- **Taxonomy:** Birks, "Equity in Modern Law: An Exercise in Taxonomy" (1996) 26 UWALR 1, on the different kinds of equity.
- **Singapore view:** *Gay Choon Ing v Loh Sze Ti Terence Peter* [2009] 2 SLR 332 noted "pockets of controversy" in promissory estoppel (sword vs shield, the role of detriment, suspensory effect) when considering it as a replacement for consideration (McKendrick).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **First exhaust consideration.** Is the variation supported by consideration: something extra, *Roffey* practical benefit for promises to pay more, or for part payment *Foakes*/*Selectmove* (and the *MWB* CA argument)? If yes, the variation is fully binding and estoppel is unnecessary.
2. **Is there a pre-existing legal relationship?** If the claim is to *create* a new obligation (as in *Combe*), estoppel fails. Note Australian law (*Waltons*) for an essay or evaluative point.
3. **How is estoppel being used?** As a defence to a claim for the full sum or for forfeiture (fine), or as the basis of a claim (fails: *Combe*, *Baird*)? Remember Halson's spectrum for intermediate uses.
4. **Clear and unequivocal promise** not to enforce rights? Look at the words and conduct; ambiguity defeats it (*Woodhouse*).
5. **Reliance:** did the promisee act on it (pay the lower rent, carry on business, delay repairs)? Detriment helps but is not essential (*W J Alan*, *Post Chaser*).
6. **Inequitable to resile?** Was the promise freely given, or extracted by pressure or by exploiting the creditor's difficulties (*D & C Builders*)? How long has passed? Has the promisee been prejudiced (*Post Chaser*)?
7. **Effect.** For periodic payments: arrears during the estoppel period are probably extinguished (*High Trees*), but full payments revive for the future. For a lump-sum debt: discuss *Collier* (possible extinction) vs the orthodox suspensory view. Can the promisee resume its position (*Ajayi*)?
8. **Ending.** Has the promisor given reasonable notice (*Tool Metal*), or have the circumstances behind the promise ended (*High Trees*)? Assess whether the notice period is reasonable on the facts.
9. **Typical traps:** a landlord agreeing to accept reduced rent (examiners expect *High Trees*); a creditor's acceptance under pressure (duress overlap); a promise to pay *more*, where estoppel is not the natural tool and *Roffey* consideration plus duress is the analysis; an oral variation in the face of a no-oral-modification clause (*Rock Advertising* SC; estoppel might be argued, but McKendrick records the CA's obiter view that it failed on the facts).
10. **Land?** If the assurance concerns an interest in land and the claimant relied to its detriment, consider proprietary estoppel, which can found a claim (*Thorner v Major*; *Crabb v Arun DC*).
