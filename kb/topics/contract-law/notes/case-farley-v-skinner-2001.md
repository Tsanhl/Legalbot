# Farley v Skinner [2001] UKHL 49 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report ([2001] 4 All ER 801) before citing them. BAILII and the parliamentary judgment page could not be read when this note was written.

## Overview
House of Lords (decided 11 October 2001). The case concerns **damages for non-pecuniary loss** (distress, discomfort, loss of enjoyment) for breach of contract, and when they are recoverable outside contracts whose whole purpose is pleasure or peace of mind.

## Facts
Farley bought a large country house (Riverside House, Sussex) after instructing a surveyor, Skinner, to investigate specifically whether aircraft noise would affect it. Skinner reported that the property was unlikely to be affected. After the purchase the noise proved intolerable, particularly early in the morning when aircraft held over the house. The trial judge found no financial loss (Farley had paid no more than someone who knew of the noise) but awarded **£10,000** for distress and discomfort. The Court of Appeal reversed, holding damages were available only for physical discomfort, not mere inconvenience.

## Held
- The House of Lords **restored the £10,000 award**.
- Damages for discomfort and loss of amenity were recoverable even without physical injury, where freedom from that discomfort was an **important part of what the contract was for**; it was enough that both parties knew the term mattered, whether or not peace of mind was the contract's main object.
- The claim can be framed as loss of the contractual benefit or as consequential loss, but there is no double recovery if the breach also reduces the property's value.
- Mere disappointment is not recoverable; surveyors will not ordinarily be liable for distress from a defective house unless the contract contains a known, important term on the point (as the noise instruction here).

## Why it matters
- Confirms and widens the exception to the general rule against damages for distress in contract (the line of authority including *Addis v Gramophone*, which restricts recovery).
- Gives a more flexible test than "the contract's main object is enjoyment".

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the loss non-pecuniary (distress, inconvenience, loss of amenity)? State the general rule that it is not recoverable.
2. Then ask whether the term broken was an important part of the bargain and known to be so; if so, damages are available, subject to remoteness.
3. Check for double recovery and keep the award modest.
