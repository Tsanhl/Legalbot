# Testamentary capacity, knowledge and approval, undue influence and fraudulent calumny

## Overview
A will is valid only if the testator had capacity, knew and approved its contents, and acted free from coercion or fraud. Capacity is measured by the common-law test in *Banks v Goodfellow*, not the Mental Capacity Act 2005, though the Law Commission (2025) has recommended that the statutory test replace it. Challenges are heard in the Business and Property Courts (Chancery) and typically turn on medical evidence and the circumstances of preparation.

## The law

### Capacity: the Banks v Goodfellow test
*Banks v Goodfellow* (1870) LR 5 QB 549 (Cockburn CJ): the testator must
1. understand the nature of the act (making a will) and its effects;
2. understand the extent of the property of which he is disposing;
3. be able to comprehend and appreciate the claims to which he ought to give effect; and
4. have no disorder of the mind that perverts his sense of right or prevents the exercise of his natural faculties, and no insane delusion that influences the disposal of the property.
Capacity is judged at the time of execution, subject to the rule in *Parker v Felgate* (1883) 8 PD 171: if instructions were given while the testator had capacity and the will was prepared in accordance with them, it is valid if at execution the testator remembered that he had given instructions and believed the will carried them out.

### Burden and presumptions
- The propounder (the person asserting the will) bears the legal burden of proving due execution, capacity and knowledge and approval. If the will was duly executed and is rational on its face, capacity is presumed, so the challenger must raise evidence of incapacity. Once the challenger raises a real doubt (for example evidence of dementia or a mental illness), the burden passes back to the propounder to prove capacity (*Ledger v Wootton* [2007] EWHC 2599 (Ch); *Key v Key*).
- The "golden rule" (suggested by Templeman J in *Kenward v Adams* (1975) *The Times*, 29 November): where an elderly or seriously ill testator makes a will, a medical practitioner should witness or approve it and record an assessment. *Key v Key* [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch) (Briggs J) emphasised that the golden rule is good practice, not a rule of law: failing to follow it does not make a will invalid.
- *Key v Key*: an 89-year-old farmer made a new will a week after the death of his wife of 65 years, switching the main gifts from his sons to his daughters. The solicitor took instructions without a medical assessment or even an attendance note. Briggs J held the will invalid: the testator was suffering a severe affective disorder caused by bereavement, combined with mild cognitive impairment, and the propounders had not proved that he could exercise the decision-making powers a testator needs. Briggs J treated this as a modest development of *Banks v Goodfellow* to take account of modern psychiatric understanding (decision-making, not just comprehension). He also held, in the alternative, that knowledge and approval was not proved because a daughter's suggestion of the change raised a suspicion that was not dispelled. It was common ground that the 2005 Act had not displaced *Banks v Goodfellow* (the will predated the Act).

### The Mental Capacity Act 2005 debate
- The MCA 2005 ss 2 and 3 set a decision-specific functional test (inability to understand, retain, use or weigh information because of an impairment or disturbance in the functioning of the mind or brain). Its presumption of capacity is in s 1.
- For wills made by people who lack capacity, the Court of Protection can authorise a statutory will (MCA 2005 ss 16 to 18, Sch 2), which uses the best-interests test.
- For a testator who makes their own will, the common-law test applies: *Walker v Badmin* [2014] EWHC 71 (Ch) and later first-instance decisions hold that *Banks v Goodfellow*, not the 2005 Act, governs a will made after 2007. The Law Commission's 2025 report recommends adopting the MCA test (with a code of practice) for wills; no legislation had been introduced by November 2026.

### Knowledge and approval
- A testator must know and approve of the contents of the will (*Barry v Butlin* (1838) 2 Moo PC 480). Capacity and knowledge and approval are different questions: someone may have capacity but not know what the will contains.
- Presumption: where a testator who can read signs a will, he is presumed to know and approve its contents. The presumption does not arise if the testator was blind, illiterate or if the will was read to them by a person who benefits (the court looks at the circumstances).
- Suspicious circumstances (a beneficiary who prepared or procured the will, a very late change, a testator who was frail, a will inconsistent with earlier declarations): the propounder must dispel the suspicion by proving knowledge and approval (*Fulton v Andrew* (1875) LR 7 HL 448; *Wintle v Nye* [1959] 1 WLR 284 (HL), where a solicitor drafted a will under which he was a major beneficiary; the House held that he did not discharge the duty to dispel the suspicion). In *Fuller v Strum* [2001] EWCA Civ 1879 the Court of Appeal held that the more serious the suspicion, the stronger the evidence needed to dispel it. In *Gill v Woodall* [2010] EWCA Civ 1430 the Court of Appeal stressed that knowledge and approval is a single question decided on all the evidence, and that a will read over to or by a testator of sound mind is strong evidence of knowledge and approval.
- If the testator did not know and approve of part of the will, that part may be omitted from probate; if the whole will is affected, the will fails.
- Rectification under s 20 AJA 1982 is a separate route to correct clerical errors or failure to understand instructions.

### Undue influence
- Undue influence in wills means coercion: *Wingrove v Wingrove* (1885) 11 PD 81 (Hannen P): influence must amount to coercion, so that the will is not the testator's own; persuasion, appeals to affection or ties of kindred, or pressure that is not an overpowering of the will do not suffice. *Hall v Hall* (1868) LR 1 P&D 481: pressure of any kind, working on fears or hopes, that overpowers the testator's will without convincing his judgment is a form of restraint that prevents a valid will.
- There is no presumption of undue influence arising from a relationship of trust and confidence in wills law (unlike inter vivos gifts); the person alleging it must prove it (*Craig v Lamoureux* [1920] AC 349 (PC): the burden is on those alleging undue influence). Evidence is often circumstantial, but the court is slow to infer coercion where the alternative of free choice is possible.
- If undue influence is proved the will (or the affected part) is invalid.

### Fraudulent calumny
- A will is invalid if the testator was induced to exclude a person by false statements which the maker of those statements knew were false, intended to poison the testator's mind against the person. In *Edwards v Edwards* [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch), where the will was set aside for undue influence, Lewison J explained that the person alleging fraudulent calumny must prove that false statements were made to the testator, that they were made knowing they were false (or recklessly), and that the testator believed them and that they influenced the testator.
- The distinction from knowledge and approval: if the testator makes a will under a mistake created by honest falsehoods, the will is not invalid, though rectification or other remedies may apply.

### Consequences
- A will found invalid on one of these grounds is not admitted to probate; the last valid earlier will takes effect (an earlier will is not "revived": the invalid will never revoked it); otherwise intestacy. Costs normally follow the event, but a losing party may get costs from the estate where the testator or beneficiaries caused the litigation, or where the circumstances reasonably called for investigation (*Spiers v English* [1907] P 122).
- Claimants also consider the 1975 Act and proprietary estoppel in the alternative.

## Key authorities
- *Banks v Goodfellow* (1870) LR 5 QB 549: capacity test.
- *Key v Key* [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch): bereavement-induced affective disorder can remove capacity; will held invalid; golden rule is guidance, not a rule of validity.
- *Wingrove v Wingrove* (1885) 11 PD 81: undue influence in wills requires coercion.
- *Wintle v Nye* [1959] 1 WLR 284 (HL): suspicion where the draftsman benefits; the burden on propounder.
- *Fuller v Strum* [2001] EWCA Civ 1879 (CA): the extent of the suspicion needed.
- *Edwards v Edwards* [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch): summary of the principles of undue influence and fraudulent calumny.
- *Barry v Butlin* (1838) 2 Moo PC 480: propounder to remove suspicion and prove knowledge and approval.

## Applying it
1. Identify the ground: lack of capacity; lack of knowledge and approval; undue influence; fraud (including fraudulent calumny).
2. Capacity: apply the four limbs; consider medical evidence, the solicitor's file, the will's rationality and any previous wills.
3. Knowledge and approval: who drafted, who attended, the opportunity to read, whether suspicion arises.
4. Undue influence: look for coercion; isolation; dependence; pressure; changes in the will.
5. Burden: propounder for capacity and knowledge/approval (the starting point), claimant for undue influence and fraud.
6. Consider the golden rule, the cost consequences, and alternative claims.
7. Common traps: applying the MCA test to a testator who makes their own will; treating a will's unfairness as evidence of undue influence; treating the golden rule as mandatory; using persuasion as undue influence.
8. Everyday question: "My relative changed their will shortly before dying. Can I challenge it?" Possible grounds are lack of capacity, lack of knowledge and approval, undue influence, or fraud; get medical and solicitor records and consider the time limits (a caveat can be entered while the grant is pending).

## Debates and reform
- Should the MCA 2005 functional test replace *Banks v Goodfellow*, given its modern approach to decision-specific capacity, and what of the "insane delusion" limb?
- The adequacy of protections for the elderly testator: the golden rule is not mandatory; some argue for mandatory capacity assessments or a statutory presumption.
- The Law Commission's proposals on capacity, a statutory presumption, and a dispensing power (see formalities note).

## Recent changes and points to check
- No legislation implementing the Law Commission's 2025 proposals (MCA capacity test; court able to infer undue influence where there are reasonable grounds to suspect it) had been introduced by November 2026.
