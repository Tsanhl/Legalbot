# Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 and Ilott v Mitson

## Overview
The 1975 Act lets certain family members and dependants apply to the court for reasonable financial provision from a deceased person's estate where the will or intestacy fails to make it. A surviving spouse or civil partner can claim provision that is reasonable in all the circumstances; everyone else is limited to "maintenance". Claims must be brought within six months of the grant. *Ilott v Mitson* confirmed that testamentary freedom is weighed against need, and that "maintenance" is more than mere subsistence but less than a general entitlement.

## The law

### Who can apply (s 1(1))
- (a) the spouse or civil partner of the deceased;
- (b) a former spouse or civil partner who has not remarried (or formed a new civil partnership);
- (ba) a person who, during the whole of the period of two years ending immediately before death, lived in the same household as the deceased as if they were spouses or civil partners (s 1(1A) and (1B)): cohabitants;
- (c) a child of the deceased (of any age);
- (d) a person (not a child of the deceased) treated by the deceased as a child of the family, in relation to a marriage or civil partnership of the deceased or (since 2014) any family in which the deceased stood in the role of a parent;
- (e) a person who was being maintained, wholly or partly, by the deceased immediately before death (s 1(3): the deceased was making a substantial contribution in money or money's worth towards the person's reasonable needs, other than a contribution made for full valuable consideration under an arrangement of a commercial nature).
Jurisdiction: the deceased must have died domiciled in England and Wales (s 1(1)); domicile remains the connecting factor for the Act even after the IHT reform.

### Standard of provision (s 1(2))
- For a surviving spouse or civil partner: "such financial provision as it would be reasonable in all the circumstances of the case for a husband or wife to receive, whether or not that provision is required for his or her maintenance" (the surviving-spouse standard), unless a decree of judicial separation was in force and the separation continuing at death, in which case the maintenance standard applies.
- For all other applicants: "such financial provision as it would be reasonable in all the circumstances of the case for the applicant to receive for his maintenance" (the maintenance standard).

### Process: two stages
1. Has the will or intestacy (or combination) failed to make reasonable financial provision for the applicant (s 1(1))? The test is objective, as at the date of hearing.
2. If so, should the court exercise its power under s 2, and in what manner? Orders: periodical payments, a lump sum, transfer of property, settlement of property, acquisition of property for transfer, variation of settlements (s 2(1)); interim payments (s 5); variation (s 6).

### Guidelines (s 3)
- All applicants (s 3(1)): the applicant's financial resources and needs now and in the foreseeable future; those of other applicants; those of beneficiaries; obligations and responsibilities of the deceased towards applicants and beneficiaries; size and nature of the net estate; physical or mental disability of any applicant or beneficiary; any other matter, including the conduct of the applicant or any other person.
- Spouse/civil partner (s 3(2)): age, the duration of the marriage, the applicant's contribution to the welfare of the family, and the provision the applicant might reasonably have expected on divorce (not a cap, but a yardstick: s 3(2)(a)-(b), which provides that in the case of a surviving spouse regard is to be had to what they might have received on divorce).
- Cohabitant (s 3(2A)): the applicant's age, the length of cohabitation, the applicant's contribution; the court must take into account the length of the cohabitation but need not use divorce as a yardstick.
- Child (s 3(3)): manner in which the applicant was being or might be educated or trained; relationship with the deceased and any assumption of responsibility.
- Child of the family (s 3(3)): also whether the deceased assumed responsibility, whether they knew the applicant was not their child, and the liability of any other person to maintain the applicant.
- Dependant (s 3(4)): the extent to which the deceased had assumed responsibility for maintenance.

### Time limits, procedure and tax
- s 4: an application must be made within six months from the date on which representation was first taken out (it may also be made before a grant); the court has an unfettered discretion to permit a late claim. The guidelines in *Re Salmon* [1981] Ch 167 (Megarry V-C): the applicant must show substantial grounds; whether they acted promptly and why they were late; whether negotiations began within time; whether the estate has been distributed before notice of the claim; and whether refusal would leave the applicant without a remedy (for example against their solicitors). *Re Dennis* [1981] 2 All ER 140 adds that the claim must be arguable. In *Cowan v Foreman* [2019] EWCA Civ 1336 the Court of Appeal allowed a late claim delayed by a standstill agreement and approved such agreements. Applications are made in the High Court (Chancery or Family Division) or the County Court/family court.
- s 8 and s 9: nominated property and donationes mortis causa form part of the net estate (s 8); the court can treat the deceased's severable share of a joint tenancy as part of the net estate (s 9), but only if the application is made within the six-month period.
- s 10 and s 11: the court can order a donee to provide money or property where the deceased, less than six years before death, made a disposition with the intention of defeating an application, without full valuable consideration (s 10); similarly for contracts to leave property by will (s 11).
- s 19(1): an order takes effect as if made by the will or under the intestacy, including for IHT (IHTA 1984 s 146).
- Costs generally follow the event; an unsuccessful claimant may have to pay the beneficiaries' costs, which is a substantial risk in small estates.

## Key authorities
- *Ilott v Mitson* [2017] UKSC 17 (SC, unanimous; Lord Hughes gave the main judgment, Lady Hale a concurring one): the deceased left her estate of about £486,000 to three animal charities, having been estranged from her only child for 26 years since the daughter left home at 17. The daughter lived in a rented housing association home, largely on state benefits. In 2007 the District Judge held that reasonable provision had not been made and awarded £50,000. After the High Court reversed that finding, the Court of Appeal ([2011] EWCA Civ 346) restored it and remitted quantum; a second appeal on quantum failed in the High Court but the Court of Appeal ([2015] EWCA Civ 797) substituted £143,000 to buy her home plus an option of up to £20,000 in capital, structured to protect her benefits. The Supreme Court allowed the charities' appeal and restored the £50,000. Held: (1) "maintenance" is not mere subsistence but means provision for the applicant's everyday living expenses, and can be given by a capital sum, but it does not extend to anything that would merely be desirable; (2) an adult child capable of earning has no special claim and need not show a "moral claim", but must show need; (3) the testator's wishes and reasons, the long estrangement and the absence of any expectation are relevant (s 3(1)(g)); (4) charities chosen by the testator are legitimate beneficiaries even without "needs"; (5) the District Judge had made no error of principle (neither in failing to quantify what the daughter would have received but for the estrangement, nor on the benefits position), so the Court of Appeal should not have interfered.
- *Re Coventry* [1980] Ch 461 (Oliver J, affirmed by the CA): a 46-year-old son capable of supporting himself claimed against his father's estate, which passed on intestacy to his estranged mother; the claim failed because the Act provides maintenance, not a legacy for an adult child merely because he is in need.
- *Re Hancock* [1998] 2 FLR 346 (CA): an adult child's claim; special circumstances or a moral obligation are not a precondition, and an increase in the estate's value after death can be taken into account.
- *Cunliffe v Fielden* [2005] EWCA Civ 1508 (CA): surviving spouse after a short marriage; the divorce comparison is a cross-check, not a ceiling or floor.
- *Re Salmon* [1981] Ch 167: time extension factors.
- *Cowan v Foreman* [2019] EWCA Civ 1336 (CA): late claims and standstill agreements.

## Applying it
1. Standing: category within s 1(1); domicile; time within six months (or the discretion).
2. Which standard: spouse (general) or maintenance (others, including cohabitants).
3. Stage 1: has reasonable provision been made? Look at the applicant's needs, resources, obligations, size of estate and the testator's reasons.
4. Stage 2: what order? Consider capital versus income; the impact on benefits; the beneficiaries' needs (especially charities and dependants).
5. Anti-avoidance: nominated property, joint tenancy and lifetime gifts (ss 8 to 11).
6. Common traps: stating that the applicant must show a moral claim; assuming that estrangement defeats the claim (it is a factor); forgetting that an adult child's need must be shown; applying the spouse standard to a cohabitant; missing the six-month limit.
7. Everyday question: "I was left out of my parent's will. Can I claim?" As an adult child you can apply for maintenance if you were not provided for and have a need; time is short (six months from the grant).

## Debates and reform
- The Law Commission's report, *Intestacy and Family Provision Claims on Death* (Law Com No 331, 2011), led to the Inheritance and Trustees' Powers Act 2014, which amended the 1975 Act (dependants, child-of-the-family claims, claims before a grant). The cohabitant category itself was added by the Law Reform (Succession) Act 1995 (deaths from 1 January 1996).
- *Ilott* triggered debate on testamentary freedom: whether England should adopt forced heirship or retain discretionary relief; the Supreme Court left the law little changed while stressing the judge's discretion and the effects of benefits.
- Interaction with benefits and the "maintenance" concept for adult children remains disputed.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Domicile remains the jurisdictional test for the 1975 Act; the 2025 IHT move to long-term residence did not amend it.
- The Law Commission's *Modernising Wills Law* (May 2025) would make spouses who marry after the will rely on the 1975 Act if revocation by marriage is abolished; not enacted by November 2026.
