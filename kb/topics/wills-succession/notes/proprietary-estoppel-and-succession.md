# Proprietary estoppel and succession: Thorner v Major, Gillett v Holt, Guest v Guest

## Overview
Where a person is promised, expressly or by conduct, that they will inherit property (typically a farm or a home), relies on that assurance to their detriment, and the promisor then goes back on it, equity can give a remedy through proprietary estoppel. The claim is made against the estate or the beneficiaries. It sits alongside the 1975 Act and challenges to wills. After *Guest v Guest* the remedy is discretionary and aimed at preventing unconscionability, with the expectation as an important but not automatic measure.

## The law

### The elements
1. **Assurance:** a representation or assurance, express or implied, that the claimant has or will have a right in or over property of the defendant (here, that the claimant will inherit).
2. **Reliance:** the claimant acted in reliance on the assurance.
3. **Detriment:** the claimant suffered detriment as a result of that reliance.
4. **Unconscionability:** it would be unconscionable for the promisor (or successor) to go back on the assurance.
These elements are interrelated: *Gillett v Holt* [2001] Ch 210 (CA) emphasised a broad merits-based approach to unconscionability rather than rigid separate boxes.

### Assurance
- *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18, [2009] 1 WLR 776 (HL): David Thorner, a relative of an unmarried farmer (Peter), worked on Peter's farm for nearly 30 years without pay, on the understanding (never put into explicit words) that he would inherit. The House held that the assurance need only be clear enough, in context, for the claimant reasonably to understand it as a promise of inheritance, with the context including the nature of the relationship and family farming culture; the assurance can be inferred from conduct and hints, and it need not be specific as to which property. It must be intended to be relied on and reasonably understood as such.
- Distinguish a mere statement of testamentary intention (which can be changed, as with a will) from an assurance that the claimant will have the property irrevocably: the question is whether the promisor knew or should have known that the claimant would rely on it (see also *Yeoman's Row Management Ltd v Cobbe* [2008] UKHL 55, [2008] 1 WLR 1752 (HL), which restricts estoppel in a commercial negotiation: the claimant knew that no binding agreement had been reached; the House insisted on a clear promise and the need for certainty about the interest).
- *Wayling v Jones* (1993) 69 P & CR 170 (CA): promise to leave a hotel to a partner; services performed with a view to the promise; the claimant did not need to prove that he would not have done the same without the promise if the promise was at least a cause.

### Reliance and detriment
- Detriment is assessed broadly; it need not be financial and may include working for little pay, giving up opportunities, or spending: *Gillett v Holt* (CA, Robert Walker LJ): the claimant worked for a farmer (not a relative) for about 40 years on repeated assurances that he would inherit the farming business, and was cut out after a falling-out. Assurances repeated over years, in the context of the relationship, were enough even though the promisor could in theory change his will; detriment was judged over the whole course of dealing. He was awarded the freehold of one farm and £100,000.
- There is a presumption (or at least a strong inference) that a claimant who acted consistently with the assurance relied on it; the burden shifts to the defendant to show that they did not rely: *Greasley v Cooke* [1980] 1 WLR 1306 (CA).

### Remedy
- The court has a wide discretion. The aim is to do justice and to avoid unconscionability. It may order the promise to be fulfilled (a transfer of the property or a share), a lump sum, a licence, a lesser interest, or compensation reflecting detriment (reliance), and can combine remedies.
- *Crabb v Arun District Council* [1976] Ch 179 (CA, Scarman LJ) spoke of the "minimum equity to do justice"; after *Guest* this is read as limiting the remedy to what removes the unconscionability, not as making the claimant's detriment the measure.
- *Jennings v Rice* [2002] EWCA Civ 159 (CA): relief must be proportionate to the detriment; a claimant who had provided unpaid help and care for an elderly woman who had promised him her property was given a sum assessed by reference to the value of what he had given, not the full value of the promised house, because the expectation was out of proportion to the detriment. 
- *Habberfield v Habberfield* [2019] EWCA Civ 890 (CA): a daughter worked for about 30 years on the family dairy farm for low pay in reliance on a promise that she would take it over; the Court of Appeal upheld a lump sum of £1.17 million (rather than a transfer of the farm), assessed with the expectation in view but adjusted for the circumstances.
- *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27 (SC, 3-2): Andrew Guest worked for over 30 years on his parents' farm for low pay on assurances that he would inherit a substantial share; after a family dispute his parents disinherited him while still alive. The trial judge ordered an immediate lump sum equal to 50% of the farming business and 40% of the freehold farm, and the Court of Appeal upheld it. Lord Briggs (with Lady Arden and Lady Rose) held: (1) the aim of the remedy is to remedy the unconscionability of the promisor's repudiation of the promise, not primarily to compensate the detriment; (2) the starting point (an assumption, not a presumption) is to hold the promisor to the promise, or give its monetary equivalent; (3) the promisor bears the burden of showing that this would be out of all proportion to the detriment, in which case the court cuts the remedy back only to the extent needed; (4) where the promise was of an inheritance and the promisor is alive, a remedy that accelerates the benefit must be discounted for early receipt. The appeal was allowed on that last point only. The parents were given a choice: either an immediate lump sum on the judge's basis but with a substantial discount for accelerated receipt, or a trust giving Andrew his 50% and 40% shares in remainder after their life interests; figures not agreed were remitted to the Chancery Division. Lord Leggatt (with Lord Stephens) dissented: the doctrine protects against detriment from reliance, not enforcement of the promise ("not a form of contract lite"), so the remedy should be measured by compensating that detriment.

### Estoppel against a deceased's estate
- Estoppel binds the estate: the claimant may bring a claim after death against the personal representatives. The claim can be combined with a claim under the 1975 Act, and the court can take the estoppel claim into account when deciding what provision was reasonable.
- Where the promise was to leave property by will, the estoppel bites on the unconscionability of the estate's refusal to honour it; an interest can arise during the promisor's lifetime ("inchoate equity") and can be assigned to the claimant on death.
- Formalities: in *Thorner* Lord Neuberger considered that s 2 of the Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 does not bar an estoppel based on a promise to leave property by will, since that is not a contract for the sale of land. Lord Scott's obiter remark in *Yeoman's Row* that s 2 may bar estoppel where the parties intended a contract leaves the position for failed contracts less certain.
- Linked: constructive trusts of the common intention type (*Stack v Dowden*, *Jones v Kernott*) are for family homes; and mutual wills involve a contract rather than an assurance.

## Key authorities
- *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18, [2009] 1 WLR 776 (HL): clarity of assurance; implied from conduct and context.
- *Gillett v Holt* [2001] Ch 210 (CA): broad approach; detriment; unconscionability.
- *Jennings v Rice* [2002] EWCA Civ 159 (CA): proportionality.
- *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27 (SC, 3-2): remedy aimed at unconscionability; expectation the starting point, cut back only if out of all proportion to detriment; discount for accelerated receipt.
- *Habberfield v Habberfield* [2019] EWCA Civ 890 (CA): expectation award upheld in a farm case.
- *Yeoman's Row Management Ltd v Cobbe* [2008] UKHL 55 (HL): limits in commercial negotiations.

## Applying it
1. Assurance: was there a clear enough promise (express or implied) of inheritance? Look at statements, the context, and the intentions of the promisor.
2. Reliance: did the claimant act because of the promise (a presumption helps)?
3. Detriment: what was given up (wages, career, savings, care)?
4. Unconscionability: considering all factors, would it be unconscionable to allow the estate to resile?
5. Remedy: expectation, reliance, or an intermediate; proportionality; the effect on others; discount for early receipt if the promisor is alive.
6. Interaction: 1975 Act claims; any challenge to the will (capacity or undue influence); IHT and CGT implications of an order.
7. Common traps: confusing a mere statement of testamentary intent with an assurance; ignoring the need for the claimant to rely; treating the remedy as automatically the whole promised property; forgetting that the claim is against the estate and that time limits run.
8. Everyday question: "I looked after my relative for years because they promised me the house, but left it to someone else." A proprietary estoppel claim may give a remedy, but you need to show a clear promise, reliance and detriment.

## Debates and reform
- The remedy has been debated between expectation and reliance. *Guest* chose expectation as the starting point by a bare majority, over a strong reliance-based dissent; commentary asks whether the majority gives too little weight to detriment and too much to informal promises.
- Relationship to testamentary freedom and formalities: estoppel enforces informal promises that would be unenforceable under s 2 of the 1989 Act and the Wills Act; critics argue for stricter limits, others for broader relief.
- The Law Commission's work on wills (see formalities note) does not directly address estoppel.

## Recent changes and points to check
- *Guest v Guest* (19 October 2022) is the leading modern remedy decision; later cases apply its expectation-based starting point and acceleration discount.
