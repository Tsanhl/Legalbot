# Personal representatives: probate, administration, duties, time limits and the solicitor's duty (White v Jones)

## Overview
Personal representatives (PRs) are the executors named in a will or the administrators appointed by the court on intestacy. Their title to the estate depends on a grant (probate or letters of administration), they must collect assets, pay debts and taxes and distribute to the beneficiaries, and they are personally liable for breach of duty. Solicitors who botch a will can be liable to disappointed intended beneficiaries (*White v Jones*).

## The law

### Appointment and grants
- **Executors:** named in a will; their authority comes from the will and exists from death, but they need a grant of probate to prove title to third parties (especially land and bank accounts). They can renounce before acting. An executor who intermeddles without authority can be an "executor de son tort" (liable for the estate dealt with but with no title).
- **Administrators:** where there is no will or no executor willing to act. Priority (Non-Contentious Probate Rules 1987 r 20 for letters with the will annexed, and r 22 for intestacy): the spouse or civil partner, then children, then parents, siblings, etc.
- **Number:** a maximum of four can take a grant in respect of the same estate (Senior Courts Act 1981 s 114). A minor cannot act; a person lacking capacity cannot act.
- **Grants:** probate (executor), letters of administration with the will annexed, letters of administration (intestacy). The application is made to the Probate Registry (in practice through the online service for probate applications, with an IHT account first where IHT is due). If an inheritance tax account is required, the PRs must file it and pay the IHT due (other than tax on instalment-option property they elect to pay by instalments) before the grant is issued; the probate application fee is £526 for estates over £5,000 (from 13 July 2026) and nil below that.
- **Caveats:** a person may enter a caveat (valid for six months, renewable) to prevent a grant issuing while they consider a challenge.
- **Vesting:** on death the estate vests in the Public Trustee until a grant is made if there is no executor (Administration of Estates Act 1925 s 9 and the Public Trustee), and in the PRs from the date of the grant (administrators' title relates back to death).
- **Removal and replacement:** the court may remove or replace PRs (Administration of Justice Act 1985 s 50), guided by the welfare of the beneficiaries as in *Letterstedt v Broers* (1884) 9 App Cas 371 (PC).

### Duties
- To collect and get in the estate; to pay the funeral expenses (priority) and debts; to pay IHT and report to HMRC; to give effect to the will (or the intestacy rules); to distribute to those entitled; to keep accounts.
- General standard: PRs are trustees for the purposes of the Trustee Act 1925 and the Trustee Act 2000 (the statutory duty of care in s 1 applies to the exercise of investment and other powers); they must act in the best interests of the beneficiaries, avoid conflicts and not profit from their position (strict fiduciary duties). Unlike trustees, PRs can generally act alone in dealing with personal property, but a conveyance of land requires all the proving PRs (AEA 1925 s 2(2)).
- Solvent estate: the order in which assets are used to pay debts is in AEA 1925 s 34(3) and Sch 1 Part II (undisposed-of property first, then residue, then property given for payment of debts, then pecuniary legacies, then specific gifts), subject to the will. Insolvent estate: funeral, testamentary and administration expenses come first, then debts in bankruptcy order (Insolvency Act 1986 s 421 and the Administration of Insolvent Estates of Deceased Persons Order 1986, SI 1986/1999).
- Advertisement: under the Trustee Act 1925 s 27, PRs can protect themselves against unknown creditors by placing notices in the London Gazette and a local newspaper and giving at least two months for claims; they are then protected from personal liability for distributions made afterwards, though the claimants can follow the assets. Beneficiary claims against PRs are protected where PRs act honestly and reasonably (Trustee Act 1925 s 61).
- Protection against 1975 Act claims: PRs are not liable for distributing property after the six-month period from the grant if they had no notice of a claim (1975 Act s 20(1)).

### Time limits
- **Executor's year:** PRs are not bound to distribute before the end of one year from death (AEA 1925 s 44). This is protective, not a prohibition: PRs may distribute earlier but at their own risk of claims.
- Interest on general legacies is generally payable from the end of the executor's year, at the rate payable on the court's basic account (CPR PD 40A para 15) unless the will provides otherwise; specific legacies carry the income of the property from death.
- Claims for the estate: claims to personal estate of a deceased person are subject to a 12-year limitation period (Limitation Act 1980 s 22), and claims for breach of trust against PRs generally six years (s 21(3)), with no limit for fraud or for recovering trust property in the trustee's hands (s 21(1)).
- IHT: tax on death is due six months after the end of the month of death (interest runs from then), though submission of the account is due within 12 months. Land and certain assets can be paid by instalments.
- Income tax and CGT: PRs must report and pay tax on estate income and gains under the usual rules; CGT is charged on disposals by PRs at 24% (for all assets since 30 October 2024), and the PRs' annual exempt amount (£3,000) is available for the tax year of death and the following two tax years. Assets acquired by PRs take their probate value (no CGT on death).
- The 1975 Act time limit of six months from the grant is dealt with in its own note.

### Distribution and assents
- Pecuniary legacies, specific gifts and residue are paid in the order set out in the will, subject to the abatement rules. An assent transfers the property to the beneficiary and for land must be in writing and signed by the PRs (AEA 1925 s 36).
- Interest and expenses: expenses are paid from the estate as part of administration; PRs are entitled to reimbursement and cannot claim remuneration unless the will, the beneficiaries, the court or statute (such as Trustee Act 2000 s 29 for professionals) allows.

### Solicitors' liability: White v Jones
- *White v Jones* [1995] 2 AC 207 (HL): a father cut his daughters out of his will after a quarrel, was reconciled with them, and instructed solicitors to make a new will giving them £9,000 each; the solicitors delayed and he died before the will was prepared. The House of Lords (3:2, with Lords Keith and Mustill dissenting) held the solicitors liable in negligence to the disappointed daughters for the lost legacies. Lord Goff's reasoning extended the Hedley Byrne principle of assumption of responsibility to impose liability on a solicitor towards intended beneficiaries where otherwise there would be a "lacuna" (the testator's estate has no loss and the intended beneficiaries would be without remedy).
- Earlier: *Ross v Caunters* [1980] Ch 297 (Megarry V-C): a solicitor who sent the will to the testator for signature without warning that a witness's spouse could not be a beneficiary was liable to the beneficiary whose gift failed under s 15.
- *Carr-Glynn v Frearsons* [1999] Ch 326 (CA): the duty extends to failing to take reasonable steps to carry out the testator's intentions, including to sever a joint tenancy so that the intended beneficiary received the property.
- Limits: the duty does not extend to those who might have benefited if the testator had made a different will; it arises where the testator's intention is clear and the solicitor's negligence prevents it. Where rectification under s 20 AJA 1982 is available, the disappointed beneficiary is expected to pursue it first, as part of mitigating loss (*Walker v Geo H Medlicott & Son* [1999] 1 WLR 727, CA).
- Wills Act, rectification (s 20 AJA 1982) and claims against PRs often interact: a solicitor's negligence may be cured by rectification, which avoids a negligence claim.

## Key authorities
- *White v Jones* [1995] 2 AC 207 (HL): duty owed to intended beneficiaries.
- *Ross v Caunters* [1980] Ch 297 (Ch): earlier first-instance recognition of the duty.
- *Carr-Glynn v Frearsons* [1999] Ch 326 (CA): duty extends to inter vivos steps needed to give effect to the plan.
- *Letterstedt v Broers* (1884) 9 App Cas 371 (PC): removal of trustees on the welfare of the beneficiaries principle.
- *Walker v Geo H Medlicott & Son* [1999] 1 WLR 727 (CA): rectification to be pursued before a negligence claim where available.

## Applying it
1. Who is entitled to take the grant? Identify the executors (will), or the administrators (priority).
2. What is the estate; assets and debts; IHT account; probate application.
3. Duties: the PRs' standard of care; conflicts; payment order; distribution; protection steps (Trustee Act 1925 s 27; s 61; 1975 Act six months).
4. Dispute: removal; breach of trust; account; surcharge; limitation.
5. Solicitor negligence: duty to intended beneficiary; the testator's intention; loss; causation; rectification as an alternative.
6. Common traps: assuming that PRs must wait for the grant to value or protect the estate (they can protect assets from death); treating the executor's year as a deadline to distribute; ignoring IHT payment before the grant; failing to protect themselves against unknown creditors or claims.
7. Everyday question: "How long does probate take and can I distribute before the year?" Often several months; no strict duty to distribute earlier; wait for the 6-month 1975 Act window to protect yourself.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the *White v Jones* exception is justified; critics said it breaks the principle of compensatory loss and involves a "fiction" of assumption of responsibility; supporters say it fills a gap.
- The probate process: criticisms of delays, the sharp rise in the flat probate fee (£300 to £526 in July 2026) and the move to the online service.
- Reform of PRs' duties and executors' liabilities (Law Commission work on trustees' duties and on wills) is limited.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Probate fee £526 for estates over £5,000 from 13 July 2026 (previously £300).
- From 6 April 2027 PRs must report and pay IHT on unused pension funds (Finance Act 2026 ss 66-71), which will lengthen many administrations; APR/BPR claims are subject to the £2.5 million allowance from 6 April 2026.
