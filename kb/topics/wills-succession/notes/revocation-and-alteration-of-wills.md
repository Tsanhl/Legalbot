# Revocation and alteration of wills: ss 18-22, dependent relative revocation and revival

## Overview
A will can be revoked by marriage or civil partnership (s 18, s 18B), by a later will or codicil or a writing executed like a will (s 20), or by destruction with intent to revoke (s 20). Divorce does not revoke the will but treats the former spouse as dead for gifts and appointments (s 18A, s 18C). Unattested alterations are ineffective (s 21). Dependent relative revocation (DRR) can save a revoked will where the revocation was conditional on a mistaken belief. Revival needs re-execution or a codicil (s 22).

## The law

### Revocation by marriage or civil partnership
- s 18(1): a will is revoked by the testator's marriage. s 18B(1): the same for formation of a civil partnership.
- Exceptions: a disposition in exercise of a power of appointment takes effect unless the property would pass to the personal representatives in default (s 18(2)); the will is not revoked if it appears from the will that at the time it was made the testator was expecting to marry a particular person and intended that the will should not be revoked by the marriage (s 18(3)); if that expectation appears and the intention is that only a particular disposition survive, that disposition and any other disposition take effect unless the will shows otherwise (s 18(4)). The same applies to civil partnerships (s 18B(3) to (6)).
- Conversion of a civil partnership into a marriage (and vice versa) does not revoke the will (ss 18(5), 18D, 18E).
- The expectation must appear from the will itself, not from outside evidence, though the will can be a codicil or a document referred to. Extrinsic evidence may be used to identify the person.

### Dissolution and annulment (ss 18A and 18C)
- After a final divorce or dissolution order (a "decree absolute" before 6 April 2022) or annulment, the will remains valid, but provisions appointing the former spouse as executor or trustee or conferring a power of appointment take effect as if the former spouse had died on the date of dissolution, and gifts to the former spouse pass as if the spouse had died then (s 18A(1)), unless a contrary intention appears in the will. The former spouse can still apply under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 (s 18A(2)). s 18C is the civil partnership equivalent.
- Separation or a pending divorce does not change the will.

### Revocation by a later will, codicil or writing (s 20)
- s 20: a will is revoked by another will or codicil, or by a writing declaring an intention to revoke it executed in the manner required for a will, or by burning, tearing or otherwise destroying it by the testator, or by someone in his presence and by his direction, with the intention of revoking it.
- Express revocation: usually clause 1 of the later will: "I revoke all former wills". Implied revocation: a later will is inconsistent with an earlier one, so the earlier will is revoked only to the extent of the inconsistency; the later will must be valid.
- Revocation clauses can be conditional: a revoking clause in a later will does not take effect if the later will is invalid.
- Destruction needs both an act and an intention. The act must be one of burning, tearing or otherwise destroying, directed at the will (or an essential part such as the signature), done by the testator or by another in his presence and at his direction. The intention to revoke must be present at the time of the act. Accidental destruction, destruction by a third party without direction in the testator's presence, or destruction while the testator lacks capacity does not revoke. Symbolic acts are not enough: striking through the will and writing "this is revoked" on it did not revoke it (*Cheese v Lovejoy* (1877) 2 PD 251, CA). But scribbling over the signature so heavily that it is no longer apparent destroys an essential part and revokes the whole will (*Re Adams* [1990] Ch 601). s 19 provides that no will is revoked by presumption of an intention on the ground of an alteration of circumstances; the circumstances in which marriage and divorce affect wills are those in ss 18 to 18E.
- Presumption of destruction: where a will was last in the testator's possession and cannot be found at death, it is presumed to have been destroyed with intention to revoke, but this can be rebutted (*Sugden v Lord St Leonards* (1876) 1 PD 154 (CA), *Eckersley v Platt* (1866) LR 1 P&D 281). A copy or draft of a lost will can be admitted to probate if its contents are proved and the presumption is rebutted.

### Alterations (s 21)
- Under s 21, an alteration made after the will was executed has no effect unless it is executed in the manner required for a will; the testator's signature and the witnesses' signatures (or initials) in the margin or near the alteration satisfy this, as does a re-execution of the will or a codicil.
- Alterations that appear on the face of the will are presumed to have been made after execution, unless there is evidence that they were made before (the presumption can be rebutted, for example by the testator's contemporaneous declarations or by internal evidence).
- An unattested alteration that obliterates words can still leave the original words in the will if they remain "apparent" (readable by natural means such as a magnifying glass, not by physical interference or forensic techniques); if so, the original words are admitted to probate and the unattested alteration is ignored. If the original words are not apparent, they are treated as revoked and the will is admitted with a blank (the unattested substituted words are never admitted). But if the obliteration was conditional on the substitution taking effect, DRR applies and the original words may be proved by any means, including infra-red photography (*Re Itter* [1950] P 130).

### Dependent relative revocation (DRR)
- DRR is a doctrine of construction and of conditional revocation: a revocation made on the assumption that a later will, or an alteration, is valid, can be ignored if the assumption fails and the testator would not have intended revocation otherwise. The court admits the earlier will to probate. It applies particularly where the revocation by destruction is conditional on a mistaken belief that another will is valid or that a codicil is effective.
- Cases: *Onions v Tyrer* (1716) 1 P Wms 343 is the early authority for the idea that the revocation of a will may be conditional on a belief about a replacement will. *In the Estate of Southerden* [1925] P 177 (CA): a husband burned his will in the mistaken belief that his wife would take all his property on intestacy; the revocation was conditional on that belief, so DRR applied and the will was admitted to probate. *Re Jones* [1976] Ch 200 (CA): a testatrix cut the signatures off her will, saying she intended to make a new one, but died first; the court inferred an unconditional intention to revoke, so DRR did not apply and she died intestate. *Re Finnemore* [1991] 1 WLR 793 (Ch): each of three successive wills contained a revocation clause; a gift to a beneficiary in the last will failed under s 15 because her husband was a witness; the revocation clause was held conditional on the new gift taking effect, so the matching gift to her in the earlier will survived.
- Practical points: the court requires clear evidence that the revocation was conditional; the mistake may be one of fact or law (as in *Southerden*); and DRR applies only where the testator would have preferred the earlier will to the result that actually follows (usually intestacy).

### Revival (s 22)
- A will, or part of one, that has been revoked can be revived only by re-execution of it (in the manner of s 9) or by a codicil executed in accordance with s 9 and showing an intention to revive. Where a will is revoked and then partially revived, the revival has effect only to the extent shown.
- If a later will which revoked an earlier will is itself revoked by destruction, the earlier will is not revived by that act (revival requires s 22 formalities); this is a trap.

## Key authorities
- *Sugden v Lord St Leonards* (1876) 1 PD 154 (CA): lost will can be proved by secondary evidence; presumption of destruction.
- *Onions v Tyrer* (1716) 1 P Wms 343 (Ch): early DRR authority.
- *Cheese v Lovejoy* (1877) 2 PD 251 (CA): symbolic cancellation is not destruction.
- *In the Estate of Southerden* [1925] P 177 (CA): DRR applied; revocation conditional on a mistaken belief about intestacy.
- *Re Jones* [1976] Ch 200 (CA): DRR rejected; the revocation was unconditional.
- *Re Finnemore* [1991] 1 WLR 793 (Ch): DRR applied to a revocation clause.
- *Re Itter* [1950] P 130: obliteration conditional on substitution; original words admitted.
- *Marley v Rawlings* [2014] UKSC 2 (SC): see formalities note.

## Applying it
1. Was there a will? Has it been revoked by a later will, codicil, destruction, marriage or civil partnership? Check s 20 (act and intention), and s 18 exceptions (expectation of marriage).
2. If the divorce has occurred: s 18A treats the former spouse as dead for gifts and appointments.
3. If there are alterations: were they attested; are they apparent?
4. If the revocation was based on a mistake: DRR: was the revocation conditional, and would the testator have preferred the earlier will to intestacy?
5. Revival: a re-execution or codicil showing intention.
6. Common traps: assuming divorce revokes the whole will; marrying after making a will (a new will is needed); forgetting the expectation-of-marriage exception must appear from the will; thinking destruction by a third party revokes unless in the testator's presence and by direction.
7. Everyday question: "I'm getting married, do I need a new will?" Yes unless the will shows a contemplated marriage.

## Debates and reform
- Revocation by marriage surprises many testators and can be exploited by "predatory marriage" to a person losing capacity. The Law Commission's *Modernising Wills Law* (May 2025) recommends abolishing the rule (the new spouse would rely on the 1975 Act).
- The treatment of divorce (s 18A) is narrower than some systems; arguments continue that separation or a petition should also affect gifts.
- DRR is criticised as inconsistent: the cases are fact-specific and the courts may be reluctant to extend the doctrine.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No legislation implementing the Law Commission's 2025 recommendations (including abolition of revocation by marriage) had been introduced by November 2026.
- ss 18B and 18C (civil partnership) were inserted by the Civil Partnership Act 2004; ss 18(5), 18D and 18E (conversion between civil partnership and marriage does not revoke) by the Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 and later legislation.
