# Formalities of wills: Wills Act 1837 s 9, witnesses, privileged wills and electronic wills reform

## Overview
A valid will in England and Wales must satisfy section 9 of the Wills Act 1837: in writing, signed by the testator (or by another in their presence and at their direction), with the signature made or acknowledged in the presence of two witnesses present at the same time, each of whom then signs or acknowledges their signature in the testator's presence. A beneficiary who witnesses loses their gift (s 15). Soldiers on active service and mariners at sea may make informal "privileged" wills. The Law Commission (May 2025) has recommended electronic wills and a court power to dispense with defective formalities, but none of this was law by November 2026.

## The law

### Section 9 (as substituted by the Administration of Justice Act 1982 s 17)
No will is valid unless:
- (a) it is in writing, and signed by the testator, or by some other person in his presence and by his direction;
- (b) it appears that the testator intended by his signature to give effect to the will;
- (c) the signature is made or acknowledged by the testator in the presence of two or more witnesses present at the same time; and
- (d) each witness either attests and signs the will or acknowledges his signature, in the presence of the testator (but not necessarily in the presence of any other witness).
No particular form of attestation is needed. Publication is not required (s 13).

### Elements
- **Writing:** any permanent form (typed, handwritten, printed); no need for a specific medium.
- **Signature:** a mark suffices if made with the intention of authenticating the will. The signature need not be at the end of the will since the 1982 amendments, but it must appear that the testator intended it to give effect to the will (s 9(1)(b)). Signature by another at the testator's direction and in his presence is permitted.
- **Acknowledgment:** the testator may sign beforehand and acknowledge the signature to the witnesses; acknowledgment can be by words or conduct, and the witnesses must see or have the opportunity to see the signature at the time (they must be able to see it, not necessarily actually see it).
- **Presence:** the witnesses must be physically present and capable of seeing the testator sign. Witnessing by video link was permitted only temporarily, by the Wills Act 1837 (Electronic Communications) (Amendment) (Coronavirus) Order 2020 (SI 2020/952), as extended: it covered wills made between 31 January 2020 and 31 January 2024 and required a clear line of sight in real time. For wills made after 31 January 2024 the witnesses must again be physically present.
- **Witness's presence in the testator's presence:** each witness signs in the testator's presence; the witnesses need not see each other sign.

### Witnesses
- s 14: a will is not void because a witness is incompetent at the time of attestation or later becomes so.
- s 15: if a person who attests the will, or the spouse or civil partner of such a person, is given a gift under the will, that gift is void so far as concerns the witness (or the spouse or those claiming under them); the witness remains a competent witness. At common law a charging clause in favour of a solicitor-executor who witnessed the will was a gift and failed (*Re Pooley* (1888) 40 Ch D 1, CA). For deaths after 1 February 2001, Trustee Act 2000 s 28(4) treats a professional trustee's or personal representative's charges as remuneration for services, not a gift, for s 15 purposes, so a witnessing professional executor can still charge.
- The Wills Act 1968 s 1 provides that if the will would have been valid without the signature of the interested witness (that is, there are at least two other witnesses), the gift is not void.
- A witness who is an executor may witness (s 17), and a creditor may witness (s 16).
- Good practice: use independent adult witnesses with capacity, who are not beneficiaries or their spouses, and make sure that the testator and witnesses are present together.

### Privileged wills
- s 11 of the 1837 Act and the Wills (Soldiers and Sailors) Act 1918 allow a soldier (including airmen and naval personnel) in actual military service, or a mariner or seaman at sea, to make a will without any formality, even orally, and a minor in those categories can make a will. "Actual military service" is construed broadly (for example a soldier serving in Northern Ireland during the Troubles: *Re Jones* [1981] Fam 7).
- A privileged will remains valid after the privilege ends. While the privilege lasts it can be revoked informally (a privileged minor may also revoke it: Family Law Reform Act 1969 s 3(3)); once it ends, revocation must follow the ordinary rules (s 20).

### Age and capacity
- s 7: a will made by a person under 18 is invalid (apart from privileged wills). Testamentary capacity is dealt with in a separate note.

### Rectification and curing defects
- Administration of Justice Act 1982 s 20: the court may rectify a will to carry out the testator's intentions where it fails to do so because of a clerical error or a failure to understand instructions. The application must be made within six months of the grant unless the court gives permission (s 20(2)). The 1982 Act s 21 permits extrinsic evidence to interpret a will where words are meaningless or ambiguous.
- *Marley v Rawlings* [2014] UKSC 2 (SC, Lord Neuberger): a husband and wife each signed, by mistake, the will prepared for the other. The Supreme Court held that the will Mr Rawlings signed was a "will" capable of rectification, that the solicitor's error was a "clerical error" within s 20(1)(a) AJA 1982 (read broadly as an office mistake in routine work), and that once rectified (substituting the text of his intended will) it satisfied s 9, since he signed it intending to give effect to it. Wills are interpreted on principles closely aligned to contracts.

## Key authorities
- *Re Groffman* [1969] 1 WLR 733 (Ch): a will's witnesses must be present at the same time when the testator signs or acknowledges; no formal "form" of acknowledgment is required but there must be a communication.
- *Couser v Couser* [1996] 1 WLR 1301 (Ch): acknowledgment of a signature can be by conduct; a witness who signs after the testator signed will meet s 9 if the testator's conduct amounts to acknowledgment.
- *Marley v Rawlings* [2014] UKSC 2, [2015] AC 129 (SC): wrongly signed wills; the Supreme Court applied the contract approach to interpretation and rectification.

## Applying it
1. Is there a document in writing signed or acknowledged by the testator?
2. Was the signature made, or acknowledged, in front of two witnesses who were present at the same time?
3. Did each witness sign (or acknowledge) in the testator's presence?
4. Did the testator intend the signature to give effect to the will?
5. Is anyone who witnessed, or their spouse, a beneficiary? If so, s 15: that gift fails unless Wills Act 1968 s 1 applies.
6. Is a privilege (military or sea) available?
7. If there is a defect, can it be cured: rectification (s 20 AJA 1982), presumption of due execution (*omnia praesumuntur rite esse acta*, which applies where there is a properly attested-looking will), or an application to propound the will in solemn form.
8. Common traps: witnesses who sign after the testator leaves; a witness who is a beneficiary's spouse; wills signed in two places; "signing" a page of a will on an electronic device without a recognised mark; not knowing the order of events in a home-made will.
9. Everyday question: "Can I make a will on my own?" Yes, but follow s 9 exactly; do not use a beneficiary or their spouse as a witness.

## Debates and reform
- The Law Commission's report *Modernising Wills Law* (16 May 2025), following its 2017 consultation, recommended a new Wills Act (draft Bill attached): electronic wills made using a "reliable system"; a court power to dispense with formalities where the record shows clear and genuine testamentary intentions unchanged at death; the Mental Capacity Act 2005 test for testamentary capacity; lowering the age for making a will to 16; abolishing revocation by marriage or civil partnership; letting the court infer undue influence where there are reasonable grounds to suspect it; and wider rectification. The government gave only a short initial response; its full response, promised within 12 months, had not appeared by autumn 2026, and no Bill had been introduced.
- Critics of the dispensing power argue that strict formalities protect against fraud and uncertainty; supporters point to other jurisdictions (for example several Australian states and Canadian provinces) where a dispensing power operates without a surge in litigation.
- Remote witnessing: the pandemic-era measures showed demand for remote execution but also raised concerns about undue influence and fraud.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The temporary video-witnessing order ended for wills made after 31 January 2024.
- No Wills Bill implementing the Law Commission's 2025 recommendations had been introduced by November 2026.
- Wills are revoked by marriage and civil partnership (see the revocation note), which is a formality point for newly married testators.
