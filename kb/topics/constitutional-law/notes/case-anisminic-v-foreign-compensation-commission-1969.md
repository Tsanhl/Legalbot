# Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission [1969] 2 AC 147 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. Do not quote wording or paragraph numbers from this
> note. Commentary on the facts is inconsistent about dates, so dates of the Egyptian seizure are not given.

## Overview
House of Lords, 17 December 1968 (Lords Reid, Morris, Pearce, Wilberforce and Pearson). Decided by a **3-2 majority**;
the sources describe Lords Reid, Pearce and Wilberforce as giving reasoning for the majority. The case is the leading
authority that an "ouster clause" does not stop the courts reviewing a decision made in error of law that takes the
decision-maker outside its powers.

## Facts
Anisminic, an English company, owned mining property in Egypt. After the property was seized by the Egyptian
authorities, it was sold to an Egyptian organisation (TEDO). Under the Foreign Compensation Act 1950, the Foreign
Compensation Commission handled claims to compensation. The Commission rejected Anisminic's claim, ruling that TEDO,
a "successor in title", was not British, and Anisminic could not qualify. Anisminic claimed the Commission had
misread the relevant Order in Council. The Act provided that the Commission's determination "shall not be called in
question in any court of law" (section 4(4)). Anisminic won in the High Court (Browne J), lost in the Court of Appeal and
won in the House of Lords.

## Decision
- The House of Lords granted the declaration for Anisminic. The Commission had misconstrued the Order, so it had no
  power to make the decision it made.
- A decision made outside the decision-maker's powers is a **nullity**, not a "determination" at all, so the ouster clause
  had nothing to protect. Lord Reid is reported to have said the Commission had acted on a ground it had no right to
  consider.
- Lord Pearce and Lord Wilberforce are reported to have reasoned that limits fixed by statute would mean little if an ouster
  clause allowed tribunals to ignore them.

## Significance
Later judges read the case as largely removing the old distinction between errors of law that go to jurisdiction and
those that do not (a common reading of the case in commentary).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. If a statute says a decision "shall not be questioned in any court", identify whether the claimant alleges an error
   that takes the body outside its powers (a misreading of the governing law, a wrong question asked, or a
   consideration it had no right to take into account).
2. Apply *Anisminic*: such a decision is a nullity, so a clause protecting "determinations" does not shield it.
3. Consider whether Parliament's language is clearer or reaches further (for example, a time-limit clause or a
   clause restricted to a final court).
4. Conclude on whether judicial review remains available and on what ground.

## Common errors to avoid

- Avoid saying Anisminic held any error of law makes a determination a nullity. The majority held the Commission exceeded its powers by imposing an irrelevant successor-in-title condition. Treating all errors of law as jurisdictional came later (Re Racal; O'Reilly v Mackman; ex p Page). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1746/q13#0]
