# Judicial review - grounds, legitimate expectation, consultation, standing, time limits and remedies

## Overview
Judicial review is the High Court's (Administrative Court's) supervisory jurisdiction over the lawfulness (not the merits) of decisions of public bodies. The traditional grounds, from Lord Diplock in *GCHQ*, are illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety; proportionality applies where Convention rights or EU-derived law are engaged and has influence beyond. Claims are brought by Part 54 CPR claim form, need permission, and must be started promptly and no later than three months from the grounds arising. Remedies are discretionary and have been shaped by the Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015 and the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022.

## The law
**Amenability.** The claimant must show a public law decision by a body exercising public functions (*R v Panel on Take-overs and Mergers, ex p Datafin plc* [1987] QB 815: source of power is not decisive; public function and public consequences). Private law remedies are not available by JR; contract and private disputes are not amenable. Procedural exclusivity (*O'Reilly v Mackman* [1983] 2 AC 237) has been relaxed.

**Illegality.** The decision-maker must understand correctly the law regulating its powers and act within them: errors of law (*Anisminic*; see the case note), acting beyond the power (ultra vires), improper purpose or irrelevant considerations (*Padfield v Minister of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food* [1968] AC 997), fettering of discretion (rigid adherence to a policy; unlawful delegation), and breach of the principle of legality.

**Irrationality.** *Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v Wednesbury Corporation* [1948] 1 KB 223 (CA): a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have come to it. *GCHQ* formulation: a decision "so outrageous in its defiance of logic or of accepted moral standards that no sensible person who had applied his mind to the question to be decided could have arrived at it." Under anxious scrutiny (*Bugdaycay v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [1987] AC 514), the court scrutinises more closely where fundamental rights are at stake. Proportionality is the standard where Convention rights are engaged (*Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2)* [2013] UKSC 39; see the Human Rights Act note) and the courts have so far declined to replace *Wednesbury* by a general proportionality test for all domestic review (*Pham v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2015] UKSC 19 considered it without deciding; in *R (Keyu) v Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs* [2015] UKSC 69 the five-justice panel declined to make the change, saying a step of that importance would need a larger panel).

**Procedural impropriety.** Failure to observe statutory procedures, or the common law duty of fairness (natural justice): the rule against bias (*R v Bow Street Metropolitan Stipendiary Magistrate, ex p Pinochet Ugarte (No 2)* [2000] 1 AC 119; *Porter v Magill* [2001] UKHL 67: fair-minded observer test), and the right to a fair hearing (*Ridge v Baldwin* [1964] AC 40; *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Doody* [1994] 1 AC 531: the content of fairness depends on context, including a duty to give reasons in some cases; *Osborn v Parole Board* [2013] UKSC 61: oral hearing where facts in dispute or the individual's liberty at stake; Lord Reed at [54]-[63] also held that the HRA does not supersede the common law's protection of rights, which should be considered first). In *Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2)* [2013] UKSC 39 the majority (Lord Sumption) held the Treasury's direction unlawful both because the bank should have been given notice and a chance to make representations before it was made (the later right to challenge in court did not cure this) and because it was disproportionate; the dissents (including Lord Reed) did not carry the day. Do not cite a dissent in *Bank Mellat* as its holding.

**Legitimate expectation.** A public body may be held to a promise or a consistent practice. *R v North and East Devon Health Authority, ex p Coughlan* [2001] QB 213 (CA) identifies three categories: (a) the authority is required only to bear its previous policy or representation in mind and give it the weight it thinks right before changing course, and the court reviews on *Wednesbury* grounds; (b) a procedural expectation (consultation, a hearing) enforced as a matter of fairness; (c) a substantive expectation, where the promise is specific and made to a few people and the authority's departure would amount to an abuse of power: the court asks whether there is a sufficient overriding interest to justify the departure from what was promised. In *Coughlan*, a health authority had promised a disabled patient that Mardon House would be her "home for life" and could not close it without an overriding public interest justification. Conditions for substantive expectation: a clear, unambiguous and unqualified representation by the authority, reliance (*R (Bancoult) v Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs (No 2)* [2008] UKHL 61 on the clarity requirement), and that the promise was not made ultra vires. Later authority: *R (Niazi) v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2008] EWCA Civ 755.

**Consultation.** A duty to consult arises under statute, under an express promise, or because fairness requires it. The "Gunning" criteria (*R v Brent LBC, ex p Gunning* (1985) 84 LGR 168, Hodgson J), adopted by the Supreme Court in *R (Moseley) v Haringey LBC* [2014] UKSC 56, [2014] 1 WLR 3947: (1) consultation must be at a time when proposals are still at a formative stage; (2) the proposer must give sufficient reasons for the proposal to permit intelligent consideration and response; (3) adequate time must be given; (4) the product of consultation must be conscientiously taken into account. *Moseley* concerned the statutory duty to consult on a draft council tax reduction scheme (Local Government Finance Act 1992 Sch 1A para 3). The Supreme Court held that, to be fair, the consultation had to tell consultees, at least briefly, about the alternative ways of meeting the funding shortfall and why they had been rejected; because it presented the council's preferred scheme as the only option, it was unlawful. Lord Wilson gave the leading judgment: the content of a statutory duty to consult is shaped by the common law requirement of fairness.

**Standing.** SCA 1981 s 31(3): permission requires "a sufficient interest in the matter to which the application relates". *IRC v National Federation of Self-Employed and Small Businesses Ltd* [1982] AC 617 (HL): sufficient interest is assessed with the merits, a mixed question. Pressure groups and public interest claimants have standing where the issue is of public importance, there is no better challenger and the claimant has expertise (*R v Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, ex p World Development Movement Ltd* [1995] 1 WLR 386 (DC), the Pergau Dam case; *R v Inspectorate of Pollution, ex p Greenpeace Ltd (No 2)* [1994] 4 All ER 329). *Walton v Scottish Ministers* [2012] UKSC 44 recognised public interest standing in Scottish proceedings.

**Time and procedure.** CPR 54.5(1): the claim form must be filed promptly and in any event not later than three months after the grounds to make the claim first arose; the court may not extend the time by agreement of parties (54.5(2)) but may extend it itself for good reason (CPR 3.1(2)(a)). Different periods apply to planning challenges (six weeks, CPR 54.5(5)) and to procurement challenges (30 days under 54.5(6)). "Promptly" can be shorter than three months (*R (Hardy) v Pembrokeshire CC* [2006] EWCA Civ 240: delay can bar relief if substantial). Permission is required (CPR 54.4); the Pre-Action Protocol is expected. The claim is for the Administrative Court (CPR 54.1 and PD 54A, as amended); cases may be transferred to the Upper Tribunal for immigration and some other classes. An alternative remedy (e.g. statutory appeal) usually has to be exhausted.

**Remedies** (SCA 1981 ss 29-31; CPR 54.2): quashing order (certiorari), mandatory order (mandamus), prohibiting order, declaration, injunction, and damages (only if combined with a private law cause of action, or under the Human Rights Act 1998 s 8). Remedies are discretionary. **Section 31(2A)** (inserted by Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015): the High Court must refuse to grant relief on a JR application if it appears highly likely that the outcome for the applicant would not have been substantially different if the conduct complained of had not occurred (the "no difference" principle); s 31(2B) allows the court to disregard this for reasons of exceptional public interest; s 31(2C) requires the court to certify that exceptional public interest reason. The same "highly likely" test applies at the permission stage: the court must refuse permission if it appears highly likely that the outcome would not have been substantially different (s 31(3C)-(3F)). **JRCA 2022 s 1** inserted s 29A into the Senior Courts Act 1981: a quashing order may provide that the quashing does not take effect until a specified date (suspended) and/or remove or limit any retrospective effect (prospective-only), subject to conditions; until then, or to that extent, the impugned act is upheld and treated as valid (s 29A(1)-(5)). In deciding whether to use these powers the court must have regard to the nature of the defect, detriment to good administration, the interests and expectations of those who would benefit from quashing and of those who relied on the act, any action or undertaking by the public authority, and any other relevant matter (s 29A(8)). There is no statutory presumption in favour of the new powers in the enacted Act. The Act also restricts the *Cart* line (JRCA s 2, inserting s 11A of the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007: see the ouster clauses note).

## Key authorities
- *Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service* [1985] AC 374 (HL): the three heads; proportionality might be a head in future (Lord Diplock).
- *Associated Provincial Picture Houses v Wednesbury Corporation* [1948] 1 KB 223 (CA): unreasonableness.
- *Padfield v Minister of Agriculture* [1968] AC 997 (HL): improper purpose frustrating statute.
- *Ridge v Baldwin* [1964] AC 40 (HL): natural justice for dismissal of chief constable.
- *R v North and East Devon HA, ex p Coughlan* [2001] QB 213 (CA): legitimate expectation categories.
- *R (Moseley) v Haringey LBC* [2014] UKSC 56 (Supreme Court): fair consultation duties.
- *R v Brent LBC, ex p Gunning* (1985) 84 LGR 168: the four principles.
- *IRC v National Federation of Self-Employed* [1982] AC 617 (HL): standing.
- *R v Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, ex p World Development Movement* [1995] 1 WLR 386: public interest standing.
- *R (Miller) v Prime Minister; Cherry* [2019] UKSC 41: reviewability of prerogative (see the prerogative note).

## Applying it
Order: (1) Is the body amenable and is the claimant a person with sufficient interest? (2) Have time limits been met (and any alternative remedy used)? (3) Identify the ground: (a) legality/illegality (wrong power, error of law, fettering, improper purposes); (b) rationality or proportionality (*Wednesbury*; if Convention right, use *Bank Mellat*); (c) procedure (bias, hearing, reasons, statutory procedure, consultation). (4) Legitimate expectation: identify the promise, its clarity, who made it, to whom, and whether it is procedural or substantive (*Coughlan*). (5) Remedy: discuss quashing, declaration; consider s 31(2A) and the prospective quashing powers; damages are not usual. Common traps: treating JR as an appeal on the merits; forgetting the time limit; overlooking the s 31(2A) argument; citing *Wednesbury* where a Convention right engages proportionality. Everyday: a person who objects to a council decision can write using the pre-action protocol, then must apply for permission within three months at the latest.

## Debates and reform
- Whether JR is a necessary check or encroaches on democratic decision-making: the Independent Review of Administrative Law (chaired by Lord Faulks, set up in 2020, reported March 2021) recommended modest changes, including suspended quashing orders and reversing *Cart*; the Government's 2021 consultation "Judicial Review Reform" led to JRCA 2022 (quashing order reform and the *Cart* ouster).
- Whether proportionality should replace *Wednesbury* (the Supreme Court in *Kennedy v Charity Commission* [2014] UKSC 20 and *Pham* left it open).
- The "highly likely" test in s 31(2A): critics say it asks courts to decide merits, and the public interest exception allows courts to preserve their supervision.
- Legitimate expectation: whether substantive protection is consistent with the separation of powers (Craig and Forsyth debate); *Coughlan* approach versus the narrower "abuse of power".
- Consultation: whether the *Moseley* standard is workable for large-scale policy; review of Government's consultation principles.

## Recent changes and points to check
- JRCA 2022 received Royal Assent on 28 April 2022; s 1 (quashing order provisions) and s 2 (*Cart* exclusion) came into force on 14 July 2022; s 1 applies only to proceedings commenced on or after that date (s 1(4)) and s 2 only to Upper Tribunal decisions made on or after it (s 2(2)).
- Time limits: CPR 54.5 (three months; six weeks for planning; 30 days for procurement). The Administrative Court Judicial Review Guide is updated annually and sets out current practice.
- The Immigration and Asylum Bill 2026 would exclude judicial review of a new immigration appeals body: see the ouster clauses note.

## Common errors to avoid

- Do not say Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2) [2013] UKSC 39 treated a later court challenge as curing the lack of a prior hearing: the majority (Lord Sumption) held failing to invite representations first was unlawful; the curing view was Lord Reed's dissent.
