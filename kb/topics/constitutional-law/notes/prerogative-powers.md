# Prerogative powers and their judicial control

## Overview
Prerogative powers are the residual common law powers of the Crown, now exercised by ministers (making treaties, deploying the armed forces, granting honours and passports, and, since 2022 once more, dissolving Parliament). Since the *GCHQ* case the courts review them by their subject matter, not by their source, though some areas (high policy, war, treaty-making) are less justiciable. The prerogative cannot be used to override an Act of Parliament or to frustrate its purpose, and where a statute covers the same ground the statute prevails (*De Keyser*).

## The law
**Definition.** Dicey described the prerogative as "the residue of discretionary or arbitrary authority which at any given time is legally left in the hands of the Crown." Blackstone confines it to powers peculiar to the Crown. Modern courts emphasise that the prerogative can be curtailed by statute, and that no new prerogative powers can be created (*BBC v Johns* [1965] Ch 32; *Case of Proclamations* (1610) 12 Co Rep 74).

**Limits.**
1. *Statute prevails.* The Bill of Rights 1689 forbids the Crown to suspend or dispense with laws. Where a statute covers the field, the prerogative is displaced or in abeyance (*Attorney General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel Ltd* [1920] AC 508, HL: the Defence of the Realm Act regime on acquiring land, with compensation, meant the Crown could not rely on the prerogative to take possession without paying).
2. *Prerogative cannot defeat a statutory scheme.* *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Fire Brigades Union* [1995] 2 AC 513 (HL): the Home Secretary could not use prerogative to introduce a tariff scheme for criminal injuries compensation different from the one Parliament had legislated for but not yet brought into force (the statutory scheme was enacted; the minister's decision not to commence it and substituting a different scheme frustrated Parliament's intent).
3. *Domestic law and rights.* The Crown cannot use the prerogative to change domestic law or remove rights conferred by statute or the common law without statutory authority: *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union* [2017] UKSC 5.
4. *Judicial review of the exercise.* *Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service* [1985] AC 374 (HL) ("GCHQ"): the Prime Minister, as Minister for the Civil Service, used the prerogative (Order in Council) to ban union membership at GCHQ without consultation. The House of Lords held that the exercise of prerogative power is in principle reviewable on the three heads of illegality, irrationality and procedural impropriety, depending on the subject matter; the legitimate expectation of consultation was defeated by evidence of national security, which the executive is better placed to judge. The key point is that the decision-maker's evidence on national security prevailed, not that review was excluded.
5. *Justiciability.* Some prerogative subjects have been regarded as unsuitable for review on merits: making treaties, defence of the realm, dissolution, the grant of honours, the prerogative of mercy (Lord Roskill's list in *GCHQ* at 418; now reviewed more flexibly). *R v Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs, ex p Everett* [1989] QB 811 (CA): passport decisions are reviewable. *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Bentley* [1994] QB 349: mercy prerogative reviewable. *R (Abbasi) v Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs* [2002] EWCA Civ 1598: foreign policy decisions in principle reviewable in narrow circumstances but no enforceable right to diplomatic protection.

**Treaty-making and *Miller I*.** The Crown makes and unmakes treaties. *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union* [2017] UKSC 5, [2018] AC 61 (Supreme Court, 8-3): the government could not use the prerogative to give notice under TEU art 50 to leave the EU, since doing so would change domestic law (the European Communities Act 1972 made EU rights part of domestic law, and withdrawal would remove sources of rights) and the Crown cannot change domestic law by prerogative; an Act of Parliament was required. The Supreme Court unanimously held that the Sewel convention, and the requirement for devolved legislatures' consent, was not legally enforceable. Parliament then passed the European Union (Notification of Withdrawal) Act 2017.

**Prorogation.** *R (Miller) v The Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland* [2019] UKSC 41, [2020] AC 373 (Supreme Court, 11 justices, unanimous): the advice to the Queen to prorogue Parliament for five weeks in September 2019 was justiciable: the existence and limits of a prerogative power are for the courts, even if its exercise is political. Held: a limit on the power to prorogue arises from the constitutional principles of parliamentary sovereignty and parliamentary accountability; a decision to prorogue (or advice to do so) will be unlawful if it has the effect of frustrating or preventing, without reasonable justification, the ability of Parliament to carry out its constitutional functions of legislating and holding the executive to account. The prorogation was thus unlawful, null and of no effect; Parliament was not prorogued. The Court did not need to decide on the Prime Minister's motive.

**Dissolution.** The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 removed the prerogative to dissolve Parliament. The Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 repealed that Act (s 1) and provided that the prerogative powers to dissolve Parliament and call a new one "are exercisable again, as if the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 had never been enacted" (s 2). Section 3 ("Non-justiciability of revived prerogative powers") provides: "A court or tribunal may not question (a) the exercise or purported exercise of the powers referred to in section 2, (b) any decision or purported decision relating to those powers, or (c) the limits or extent of those powers." Section 4 dissolves a Parliament automatically at the beginning of the fifth anniversary of the day it first met. The ouster is designed to pre-empt any *Miller II*-style challenge to a dissolution (*Miller II* itself concerned prorogation, not dissolution). The Act says nothing about prorogation, so *Miller II* still governs prorogation.

## Key authorities
- *Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service* [1985] AC 374 (HL): reviewability by subject matter; Lord Diplock's three heads of review; national security exception on the facts.
- *Attorney General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel* [1920] AC 508 (HL): statute displaces prerogative.
- *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Fire Brigades Union* [1995] 2 AC 513 (HL): prerogative cannot frustrate statute.
- *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU* [2017] UKSC 5: no use of the prerogative to trigger art 50; Sewel not enforceable.
- *R (Miller) v Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland* [2019] UKSC 41: prorogation justiciable and unlawful.
- *R (Bancoult) v Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs (No 2)* [2008] UKHL 61, [2009] 1 AC 453: Orders in Council made under prerogative for colonies are reviewable (the order barring the Chagos islanders from returning was upheld by a 3-2 majority).
- *R v Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs, ex p Rees-Mogg* [1994] QB 552 (DC): challenge to ratification of the Maastricht Treaty failed; ratification was a lawful exercise of the prerogative not barred by the European Parliamentary Elections Act 1978, and the argument that the treaty's foreign and security policy provisions transferred prerogative power unlawfully was not justiciable.
- *R (Abbasi)* [2002] EWCA Civ 1598 (CA): no enforceable duty of diplomatic protection.

## Applying it
Steps: (1) Is the power prerogative, statutory or both? If statute covers the field, apply *De Keyser* and see whether the statute excludes or merely abrogates the prerogative. (2) Does the exercise alter rights in domestic law? If so, *Miller I* requires legislation. (3) Is the exercise reviewable? Ask whether the subject matter is justiciable (national security, defence, treaty making, mercy). (4) Apply the three heads; show how courts treat deference in the national security case (*GCHQ*). (5) If the question concerns prorogation, apply the *Miller II* test: (a) does the action have the effect of frustrating Parliament's functions? (b) was there reasonable justification? (c) remedy: declaration of unlawfulness; the prorogation null. (6) If a question concerns dissolution after 2022, say that the Act removes court supervision. For an everyday question ("can the Prime Minister suspend Parliament?"): Parliament can be prorogued by the Crown on advice of ministers, but not for a period that stops it doing its job without good reason.

## Debates and reform
- *Miller I* and *II* have been called the high-water mark of judicial constitutionalism, or an overreach into politics. Supporters stress the rule of law and parliamentary accountability; critics (for example John Finnis and Martin Loughlin) see judges creating new constitutional principles.
- Whether the prerogative should be put into statute: the Public Administration Select Committee's *Taming the Prerogative* (2004) and the Governance of Britain Green Paper (2007) proposed parliamentary approval of war powers and treaties. The Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010 Part 2 (CRaG) requires treaties to be laid before Parliament for 21 sitting days before ratification; a Commons resolution against ratification can delay it, and repeated resolutions can delay it indefinitely, but there is no outright veto. War powers: a practice of a Commons vote before military action grew after the 2003 Iraq and 2013 Syria votes, but governments have since used force without a prior vote (Syria 2018, Yemen 2024), so whether a convention exists is disputed; there is no statute.
- The Joint Committee on the Fixed-term Parliaments Act (report March 2021) recommended repeal of the 2011 Act; some argue the revival of the prerogative without safeguards (for example, a requirement to dissolve only with reasons) places too much power with the Prime Minister. The ouster of review is controversial because it removes a check the Supreme Court recognised.
- Prorogation: no statutory reform has been enacted, so the *Miller II* limit remains the only legal control.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022: Royal Assent 24 March 2022; in force on Royal Assent (s 6(3)). The text of ss 2-3 quoted above is from the Act as enacted.
- No statute governs war powers or (beyond CRaG 2010) treaty-making as at November 2026.
