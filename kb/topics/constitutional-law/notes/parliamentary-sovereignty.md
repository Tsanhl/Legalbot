# Parliamentary sovereignty

## Overview
In the orthodox (Diceyan) account, Parliament, meaning the Crown in Parliament, can make or unmake any law, no body can override or set aside its legislation, and no Parliament can bind its successors. Modern authority (EU membership, the Human Rights Act, devolution, "constitutional statutes") has produced qualifications in practice, and judges have floated, obiter, that sovereignty is a common law construct that has limits. After EU withdrawal the courts again apply Acts of Parliament without any competing supremacy principle, but the debate about whether sovereignty is "political fact" or "legal rule" remains central to essays.

## The law
**Dicey's account** (A V Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution*, 1885) has three limbs: Parliament may legislate on any subject matter; no person or body may override or set aside its Acts; and no Parliament can bind a later Parliament. Courts apply the latest Act: where two Acts conflict, the later impliedly repeals the earlier (*Ellen Street Estates v Minister of Health* [1934] 1 KB 590, Maugham LJ).

**Express and implied repeal.** The ordinary doctrine of implied repeal applies to most statutes. *Thoburn v Sunderland City Council* [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin), [2003] QB 151 (Laws LJ) held that "constitutional statutes" (and fundamental rights) cannot be impliedly repealed: only express words, or words so specific that the inference of an intention to repeal is irresistible, will do. This is a common law development, not a rule laid down by Parliament.

**Manner and form.** The *Jackson* case (below) accepted that Parliament can legislate how later legislation is to be enacted, as with the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949, which allow the Commons to enact certain Bills after delay without Lords consent.

**EU membership.** The European Communities Act 1972 s 2(1) and (4) gave directly effective EU law priority over inconsistent domestic law. In *R v Secretary of State for Transport, ex p Factortame Ltd (No 2)* [1991] 1 AC 603 the House of Lords disapplied the Merchant Shipping Act 1988 to the extent it conflicted with directly effective EU rights, and granted interim relief against the Crown. In *Factortame (No 1)* [1990] 2 AC 85 the House had held that English law gave no power to grant an interim injunction against the Crown or to suspend an Act, and referred the question to the Court of Justice; after the Court of Justice ruled (Case C-213/89) that a national rule barring interim relief must be set aside where it prevents the protection of EU rights, the House granted the relief in *No 2*. The accepted domestic explanation is that Parliament itself, in the 1972 Act, directed the courts to give priority to EU law, so the sovereignty of Parliament was exercised, not abandoned. The contrary view is that a Parliament cannot bind successors, so the courts accepted a new "rule of recognition". *Thoburn* treated the 1972 Act as a constitutional statute: it could be repealed, but only expressly.

**Withdrawal.** The European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 repealed the 1972 Act (s 1) and created "retained EU law" from "IP completion day" (31 December 2020 at 11pm). The Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 ("REUL Act"):
- ended the principle of supremacy of EU law in domestic law and abolished the general principles of EU law (ss 3 and 4), both from 1 January 2024;
- repealed EUWA 2018 s 4 (the saving for directly effective rights), with effect from the end of 2023 (s 2);
- renamed what remains "assimilated law" (s 5), and replaced "retained EU case law" with "assimilated case law" so that higher courts (the Supreme Court and, in prescribed circumstances, the Court of Appeal and equivalents) may depart from it more readily;
- revoked many named instruments listed in a Schedule at the end of 2023, and gave ministers powers to restate, revoke or replace assimilated law. The original "sunset" for all retained EU law was replaced by the Schedule list before enactment.

The effect for sovereignty is that domestic legislation now prevails over assimilated direct legislation (EUWA 2018 s 5(A2), inserted by REUL Act s 3: assimilated direct legislation is read compatibly with domestic enactments where possible and is otherwise subject to them; the ministerial power in REUL Act s 7 to reverse that priority for specified provisions could not be used after 23 June 2026), and there is no longer any power to disapply an Act for inconsistency with EU law. (Even under EUWA 2018 as originally enacted, supremacy was preserved only against pre-exit domestic law.) The main exception is the Withdrawal Agreement: EUWA 2018 s 7A gives rights and obligations arising under the Withdrawal Agreement (including the Windsor Framework for Northern Ireland) direct effect, and domestic law must be read and given effect subject to them, so a form of priority survives in that field.

**The Human Rights Act 1998** is the main other qualification: courts must interpret legislation compatibly "so far as it is possible to do so" (s 3) but cannot strike down an Act; the remedy is a declaration of incompatibility (s 4), which does not affect validity (s 4(6)). Parliament's sovereignty is thus formally preserved. See the note on the Human Rights Act.

**Devolution.** The Scotland Act 1998 s 28(7) says the Scottish Parliament's power to make laws does not affect Westminster's power to make laws for Scotland. The Sewel convention (a convention that Westminster does not normally legislate on devolved matters without consent) is not legally enforceable (*Miller I*; see the devolution note).

## Key authorities
- *R (Jackson) v Attorney General* [2005] UKHL 56, [2006] 1 AC 262 (HL): challenge to the Hunting Act 2004, enacted under the Parliament Act 1949. Held the 1949 Act was valid, so the Hunting Act was valid. Obiter, Lord Steyn, Lord Hope and Baroness Hale suggested the courts might in an extreme case decline to recognise legislation abolishing judicial review or the rule of law; Lord Bingham restated the orthodox view that the supremacy of Parliament is the bedrock of the constitution. Not binding on the point.
- *R v Secretary of State for Transport, ex p Factortame (No 2)* [1991] 1 AC 603 (HL): priority of directly effective EU rights over later UK Act; discussed above.
- *Thoburn v Sunderland CC* [2002] EWHC 195 (Admin) (Divisional Court): "Metric martyrs". The traders argued that the Weights and Measures Act 1985 had impliedly repealed the Henry VIII power in the European Communities Act 1972 s 2(2), so the metrication regulations made under it were invalid. Held there was no inconsistency, and in any event the 1972 Act, as a constitutional statute, could not be impliedly repealed; Laws LJ (obiter) set out the hierarchy of ordinary and constitutional statutes (Magna Carta, Bill of Rights 1689, Acts of Union, Representation of the People Acts, HRA 1998, devolution Acts and ECA 1972 given as examples).
- *R (HS2 Action Alliance Ltd) v Secretary of State for Transport* [2014] UKSC 3, [2014] 1 WLR 324: a challenge based on the Bill of Rights 1689 art 9 and EU law to the hybrid Bill procedure failed. Lord Neuberger and Lord Mance (obiter) suggested that, if there were a clash between EU law and fundamental constitutional principles, the UK courts would have to consider which prevails, and that the 1972 Act could not authorise the abrogation of fundamental rights.
- *Pickin v British Railways Board* [1974] AC 765 (HL): the courts will not look behind an Act to examine how Parliament passed it.
- *Ellen Street Estates v Minister of Health* [1934] 1 KB 590 (CA): implied repeal; one Parliament cannot bind its successors as to the form of legislation.
- *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU* [2017] UKSC 5: Parliament's authority needed to trigger withdrawal; see the prerogative note.
- *Ahmed v HM Treasury* [2010] UKSC 2: ministers' Orders in Council under the United Nations Act 1946 could not override fundamental rights absent clear authority; relevant to the principle of legality.
- *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Simms* [2000] 2 AC 115 (HL): principle of legality: fundamental rights cannot be overridden by general or ambiguous words (Lord Hoffmann).

## Applying it
For a problem question on whether a court may disapply or refuse to apply an Act: (1) identify the Act and whether it is clear on its face; (2) state the orthodox rule that courts apply it (*Pickin*); (3) test for conflict with an earlier Act and consider implied repeal (*Ellen Street*), subject to *Thoburn* if the earlier statute is "constitutional"; (4) if the argument is about the Human Rights Act, move to s 3 and s 4; (5) remember that the principle of legality will require clear words before a court finds that Parliament intended to override a fundamental right. For an everyday question ("can a court strike down an Act of Parliament?"), the answer is no: it can interpret it, in some cases declare it incompatible with Convention rights, but must apply it.

## Debates and reform
- Legal or political sovereignty: Dicey distinguished legal sovereignty (Parliament) from political sovereignty (the electorate). H W R Wade ("The Basis of Legal Sovereignty" [1955] CLJ 172) argued the rule of recognition is a political fact that the courts may alter, as with the EU membership "revolution". Others (such as Jeffrey Goldsworthy, *The Sovereignty of Parliament*, 1999) defend the traditional view as a rule grounded in judicial acceptance.
- Common law constitutionalism: Sir John Laws, Lord Steyn and others have argued that the supremacy of Parliament is a creation of the common law, so the courts could define its limits. Against this, *Jackson* obiter statements are untested.
- Brexit and sovereignty: whether EU law's primacy was ever truly lawful limits, or a revocable licence, was resolved in practice by the withdrawal legislation; whether the REUL Act's ministerial powers to restate and revoke assimilated law concentrate too much power in ministers (Henry VIII clauses) is a separate rule-of-law debate.
- Entrenchment: Parliament cannot entrench ordinary legislation, but manner and form arguments and the *Jackson* dicta keep the question open. A written constitution would replace sovereignty with a higher law; no UK government proposes it.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 January 2024: supremacy of EU law and general principles of EU law ended in domestic law; "retained EU law" renamed "assimilated law" (REUL Act 2023 ss 3-5).
- Withdrawal Agreement and Windsor Framework law continues to have direct effect and priority under EUWA 2018 s 7A; it is the one remaining area where domestic law gives way to an EU-derived norm.
