# The Human Rights Act 1998

## Overview
The Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA) gives domestic effect to most of the Convention rights in the European Convention on Human Rights without making them entrenched: courts must interpret legislation compatibly if possible (s 3), may declare incompatibility (s 4) but cannot strike down an Act, and public authorities must not act incompatibly (s 6). Parliamentary sovereignty is formally preserved. Debate focuses on the relationship with Strasbourg, the width of "public authority", the remedial scheme, and proposals to replace or reform the Act.

## The law
**Section 2: interpreting Convention rights.** A court determining a question about a Convention right "must take into account" judgments, decisions and opinions of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) and Commission, so far as relevant. Domestic courts are not bound: *R (Ullah) v Special Adjudicator* [2004] UKHL 26 ("no more, but certainly no less" than Strasbourg), but a clear and constant line of Strasbourg authority will normally be followed, while a domestic court may decline if it has concerns about a fundamental aspect of English law, so as to start a dialogue (*R v Horncastle* [2009] UKSC 14; *Manchester City Council v Pinnock* [2010] UKSC 45). *R (AB) v Secretary of State for Justice* [2021] UKSC 28 confirmed the "no more, no less" principle.

**Section 3: interpretation.** "So far as it is possible to do so, primary legislation and subordinate legislation must be read and given effect in a way which is compatible with the Convention rights." The duty applies to legislation whenever enacted (s 3(2)(a)), and does not affect validity (s 3(2)(b)). *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* [2004] UKHL 30, [2004] 2 AC 557: Rent Act 1977 Sch 1 para 2(2) ("as his or her wife or husband") was read to include a same-sex surviving partner, avoiding discrimination contrary to art 14 with art 8. Lord Nicholls: s 3 may require the court to depart from the unambiguous meaning of the legislation and from Parliament's intention at the time, and to read in or read down words, but it cannot go "against the grain" of the legislation or the essential principles of the Act; it is for Parliament, not courts, to make decisions requiring broad policy choice or important practical repercussions. *Re S (Minors) (Care Order: Implementation of Care Plan)* [2002] UKHL 10 found that reading in a supervisory regime went too far. *Bellinger v Bellinger* [2003] UKHL 21: no possible reading of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 s 11(c) to recognise a transgender woman as female, so a declaration of incompatibility was made.

**Section 4: declaration of incompatibility.** Higher courts (High Court and above) may declare that a provision of primary legislation (or, in limited cases, subordinate legislation) is incompatible with a Convention right where s 3 cannot be used (s 4(2), (4)). A declaration does not affect the validity or enforcement of the provision and is not binding on the parties (s 4(6)). The Crown is entitled to notice and to be joined (s 5). Example: *A v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2004] UKHL 56 (the Belmarsh case), where indefinite detention of foreign nationals suspected of terrorism under the Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001 s 23 was disproportionate and discriminatory; the Act was repealed and replaced.

**Section 6: public authorities.** It is unlawful for a public authority to act in a way incompatible with a Convention right (s 6(1)), unless as a result of primary legislation it could not have acted differently (s 6(2)); courts and tribunals are public authorities (s 6(3)(a)), but Parliament is not (s 6(3)); and "public authority" includes "any person certain of whose functions are functions of a public nature" (s 6(3)(b)); in relation to a particular act, a person is not a public authority if the nature of the act is private (s 6(5)). Pure (core) public authorities (e.g. government departments, local authorities, police) are bound in all they do; hybrid or functional public authorities are bound only when exercising public functions. *Aston Cantlow PCC v Wallbank* [2003] UKHL 37: a parochial church council enforcing chancel repair liability was not a core public authority and, as to the act, not exercising a public function; factors include public funding, statutory powers, and the relationship with the state. *YL v Birmingham City Council* [2007] UKHL 27 (3-2): a private care home providing accommodation under contract with a local authority to a resident whose care the council funded was not exercising a function of a public nature; Parliament reversed the effect for publicly arranged care home placements in the Health and Social Care Act 2008 s 145, now replaced in England by the Care Act 2014 s 73 (registered care providers arranged or funded by a local authority are treated as exercising a function of a public nature). *Poplar Housing and Regeneration Community Association v Donoghue* [2001] EWCA Civ 595: a housing association providing social housing under local authority arrangements was a hybrid public authority.

**Sections 7 and 8: victims and remedies.** Section 7(1): a person who claims a public authority has acted unlawfully under s 6(1) may bring proceedings against it in the appropriate court or tribunal, or rely on the right in any legal proceedings; they must be a "victim" (s 7(1), (3), (7): same test as Convention art 34). Time limit: one year from the date of the act complained of, or such longer period as the court considers equitable (s 7(5)), subject to any rule imposing a stricter limit for the procedure. Remedy: s 8(1): such relief or remedy within the court's powers as it considers just and appropriate. Damages (s 8(3)-(4)): only from a court with power to award damages; only if necessary to afford just satisfaction, taking account of all the circumstances including any other relief and the consequences of the act; the court must take into account Strasbourg principles on compensation, which are modest. Section 9: proceedings in respect of judicial acts may be brought only by appeal, judicial review or in a forum prescribed by rules (s 9(1)). Damages for a judicial act done in good faith may be awarded only (a) to the extent required by art 5(5); (b) for a judicial act incompatible with art 6 where, but for the incompatibility, the person would not have been detained or not detained for so long (added by the Human Rights Act 1998 (Remedial) Order 2020, SI 2020/1160, following *Hammerton v UK*); and (c) for a judicial act incompatible with art 8 because it was done in such a procedurally defective way as to breach art 8's requirements of procedural fairness (added by the Human Rights Act 1998 (Remedial) Order 2026, SI 2026/1205, in force 17 November 2026 and applying to judicial acts before as well as after that date, following *SW v United Kingdom*). Awards are made against the Crown (s 9(3)-(4)).

**Section 10 and Schedule 2: remedial orders.** Where a provision has been declared incompatible and the appeal period has ended, or a Strasbourg ruling shows incompatibility, a minister may, if there are compelling reasons, amend the legislation by order to remove the incompatibility, using the standard or urgent procedure with parliamentary approval.

**Section 19: statements of compatibility.** A minister in charge of a Bill must, before Second Reading, make a statement that the Bill's provisions are compatible with Convention rights, or state that the government wishes to proceed although it is unable to make that statement. Section 19(1)(b) statements are rare: examples are the Communications Bill 2002, the Illegal Migration Bill 2023 and the Safety of Rwanda (Asylum and Immigration) Bill 2023.

**Proportionality.** Where a Convention right can be qualified (arts 8-11), restrictions must be in accordance with law, pursue a legitimate aim and be necessary and proportionate. *Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2)* [2013] UKSC 39, [2014] AC 700: Lord Sumption's four questions: (i) is the objective sufficiently important to justify limiting a fundamental right? (ii) is the measure rationally connected to it? (iii) could a less intrusive measure have been used? (iv) on a fair balance, do the benefits outweigh the harm to the affected person? Lord Reed, with whom others agreed, set out a similar approach, emphasising the court's role of assessing the justification rather than applying a deferential *Wednesbury* test, though discretionary areas of judgment remain. The intensity of review depends on the context (*R (Daly) v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2001] UKHL 26).

## Key authorities
- *Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza* [2004] UKHL 30 (HL): s 3 is a strong interpretive obligation.
- *A v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2004] UKHL 56 (HL): s 4 declaration, Belmarsh.
- *Ullah* [2004] UKHL 26; *Horncastle* [2009] UKSC 14; *Pinnock* [2010] UKSC 45: s 2.
- *Aston Cantlow v Wallbank* [2003] UKHL 37; *YL v Birmingham CC* [2007] UKHL 27: "public authority".
- *Bank Mellat v HM Treasury (No 2)* [2013] UKSC 39: proportionality test.
- *R (Daly) v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2001] UKHL 26: proportionality in domestic law; prisoners' legal correspondence.
- *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* [2014] UKSC 38: the court's role in declaring incompatibility on contested policy issues (assisted suicide); the majority declined to make a declaration then, leaving the matter to Parliament.
- *Re S* [2002] UKHL 10; *Bellinger v Bellinger* [2003] UKHL 21: limits of s 3.

## Applying it
Problem questions: (1) Which Convention right is engaged (arts 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, 14 etc.), absolute or qualified? (2) Who is the respondent? Is it a core public authority, or a hybrid body exercising a public function (s 6(3)(b), s 6(5))? Apply *YL*. (3) Is there a statute that required the act (s 6(2))? If so, s 3: can it be read compatibly without going against the grain? If not, s 4 declaration but the act stands. (4) Qualified rights: lawfulness, legitimate aim, proportionality (*Bank Mellat*). (5) Victim status and one-year time limit (s 7(5)); remedy under s 8, with damages rarely awarded. (6) For Bills: s 19 statement. Common traps: treating a declaration as striking the law down; using s 6 against a private company; forgetting the one-year time limit and that JR time limits (three months) may bar claims brought by JR. Everyday question: "Can I sue a hospital trust for breach of my human rights?" Yes, as it is a core public authority; sue within a year; damages are modest.

## Debates and reform
- Strasbourg: whether s 2 produces a "mirror principle" that stunts domestic development of rights, or properly keeps domestic courts in step with the Convention (see *Ullah*, and the statement of the position in *AB* (2021)).
- Judicial power: s 3 as a form of "judicial legislation" versus s 4 as preserving dialogue; *Nicklinson* and the Court's institutional competence.
- Reform: the Independent Human Rights Act Review (chaired by Sir Peter Gross) reported in December 2021, published alongside the Conservative Government's consultation "Human Rights Act Reform: A Modern Bill of Rights". The Bill of Rights Bill introduced in June 2022 would have repealed the HRA, but was withdrawn in June 2023. The Labour Government (elected July 2024) has kept the HRA; the Conservative Party and Reform UK have proposed leaving the ECHR.
- Article 8 and immigration: the Government says it remains committed to the ECHR but wants to reset the balance between Article 8 and the public interest in immigration cases. The Immigration and Asylum Bill (introduced 30 June 2026) would add to the public interest considerations in Part 5A of the Nationality, Immigration and Asylum Act 2002, to which courts must have regard; under *Rhuppiah v Secretary of State for the Home Department* [2018] UKSC 58 such provisions cannot require a decision inconsistent with Article 8 itself. The Bill passed the Commons on 17 November 2026 and is not yet law.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 17 November 2026: the Human Rights Act 1998 (Remedial) Order 2026 (SI 2026/1205, made 16 November 2026) came into force, adding s 9(3)(c) (damages for procedurally unfair judicial acts breaching Article 8), implementing *SW v United Kingdom*; it applies to judicial acts done before as well as after it came into force.
- The Immigration and Asylum Bill (Article 8 public interest provisions) passed the Commons on 17 November 2026 and awaits Lords second reading.
