# Separation of powers, the Crown, ministerial responsibility and conventions

## Overview
The UK has no strict separation of powers: ministers sit in Parliament, the executive controls most legislation through its majority, and Parliament is sovereign. The system rests instead on functional checks, institutional independence of the judiciary, and conventions such as ministerial responsibility. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 made the separation between the judicial and the other branches more formal (a Supreme Court; a statutory guarantee of judicial independence; removal of the Lord Chancellor as head of the judiciary). Conventions bind politically, not legally, though courts may recognise them.

## The law
**Montesquieu and the UK.** Legislature, executive and judiciary have distinct functions. In the UK the overlaps are: (a) ministers are members of Parliament, by convention drawn from the Commons or the Lords; (b) the Crown is part of Parliament and heads the executive; (c) until 2005 the Lord Chancellor sat as a judge, head of the judiciary and a Cabinet minister; (d) the Lords Law Lords were the final court. Many accounts say the judicial separation is the strongest (Lord Diplock in *Duport Steels Ltd v Sirs* [1980] 1 WLR 142 said the courts' role is to apply the law, not make policy, and that Parliament makes law; courts must not usurp that role). Later decisions have been less deferential to executive action (see the judicial review note).

**The Crown.** The Crown is the legal personification of the state: the monarch as head of state, plus ministers who exercise Crown powers (statutory powers and the royal prerogative). Ministers act in the Crown's name; civil servants act for ministers (the *Carltona* principle: *Carltona Ltd v Commissioners of Works* [1943] 2 All ER 560 (CA)). The Crown Proceedings Act 1947 permits civil claims against the Crown in contract and tort; the Crown itself is not criminally liable, but individual ministers and officials can be prosecuted. *M v Home Office* [1994] 1 AC 377 (HL) established that the courts may grant final and interim injunctions against ministers and that ministers can be held in contempt in their official capacity.

**Judicial independence** (Constitutional Reform Act 2005):
- s 3(1): the Lord Chancellor, other ministers and persons with responsibility for the administration of justice must uphold the continued independence of the judiciary; s 3(5): they must not seek to influence particular judicial decisions through any special access to the judiciary; s 3(6): the Lord Chancellor must have regard to the need to defend that independence, the need for the judiciary to have the support necessary to enable them to exercise their functions, and the need for the public interest in regard to matters relating to the judiciary or otherwise to the administration of justice to be properly represented in decisions affecting those matters.
- The Lord Chief Justice became head of the judiciary of England and Wales (s 7); the Lord Chancellor no longer sits as a judge.
- The Judicial Appointments Commission selects judges by merit for appointment (Part 4); the Lord Chancellor retains limited power to reject or ask for reconsideration of selections.
- The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom replaced the appellate committee of the House of Lords (Part 3), opening in October 2009.
- Security of tenure: High Court and Court of Appeal judges hold office during good behaviour, subject to removal by the monarch on an address presented by both Houses (Senior Courts Act 1981 s 11(3)); Justices of the Supreme Court have the same protection (CRA 2005 s 33).

**Ministerial responsibility.** Collective responsibility: ministers must support government policy publicly, or resign; Cabinet discussions are confidential (*Attorney General v Jonathan Cape Ltd* [1976] QB 752, Lord Widgery CJ, which held that the convention of Cabinet confidentiality was recognised by the court, which could restrain publication of diaries on it, but that confidentiality diminishes with time, and that it was not absolute). Individual responsibility: ministers answer to Parliament for their department's policies and administration, and for their own conduct. Resignation is a matter of political judgment, and the convention has been affected by the growth of agencies and the *Ministerial Code* (a government document; the Prime Minister decides on breaches and sanctions, advised by the Independent Adviser on Ministerial Standards, as the post was renamed in November 2024; since then the Adviser can begin an investigation after formally notifying the Prime Minister).

**Conventions.** Rules of political practice not enforced by courts. The Jennings test asks: are there precedents, did the actors believe they were bound, and is there a reason for the rule? (Sir Ivor Jennings, *The Law and the Constitution*, 1959). Examples: the Monarch assents to Bills; the PM must command the confidence of the Commons; the Lords does not block manifesto Bills (the Salisbury convention); ministers answer to Parliament; the Sewel convention on devolved legislation. Courts will recognise but not enforce them: the *Patriation Reference* [1981] 1 SCR 753 (Supreme Court of Canada), regularly cited in UK writing, held that conventions are not enforceable legally but can be recognised. In *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union* [2017] UKSC 5 (see the prerogative and devolution notes) the Supreme Court said that the judges are neither the parents nor the guardians of political conventions, they can recognise the operation of a convention in the context of a legal question but are not concerned with its scope or operation, and that the Sewel convention was therefore not legally enforceable, even though it had been put into statute with the word "recognised" in the Scotland Act 1998 s 28(8).

**Parliamentary privilege.** The Bill of Rights 1689 art 9 protects freedom of speech and proceedings in Parliament from being impeached or questioned in any court, preserving the separation between courts and Parliament. *R v Chaytor* [2010] UKSC 52 held that the expenses fraud prosecutions of MPs were not barred by exclusive cognisance of Parliament, because claiming expenses was not part of "proceedings in Parliament".

## Key authorities
- *Duport Steels Ltd v Sirs* [1980] 1 WLR 142 (HL): Lord Diplock: the constitutional function of the judiciary is to interpret the law, not to remake it.
- *M v Home Office* [1994] 1 AC 377 (HL): ministers, as officers of the Crown, are subject to injunctions and contempt jurisdiction.
- *Attorney General v Jonathan Cape Ltd* [1976] QB 752: Cabinet confidentiality as a convention recognised by law in breach-of-confidence terms.
- *Carltona Ltd v Commissioners of Works* [1943] 2 All ER 560 (CA): civil servants exercise a minister's powers in the minister's name, the minister remaining answerable.
- *R v Chaytor* [2010] UKSC 52 (Supreme Court): limits of parliamentary privilege.
- *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU* [2017] UKSC 5 (Supreme Court): conventions are not law, though courts can recognise them.
- *R (Miller) v Prime Minister; Cherry* [2019] UKSC 41 (Supreme Court): applied constitutional principles of parliamentary sovereignty and accountability to review an exercise of prerogative power.
- *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Fire Brigades Union* [1995] 2 AC 513 (HL): the court polices the boundary between statutory scheme and executive prerogative.

## Applying it
Questions of this type are usually essays ("The UK has no meaningful separation of powers"). Plan: (1) define the three functions; (2) show the overlaps (executive in Parliament; the Crown in Parliament; judicial review as executive check; the CRA 2005 reforms); (3) evaluate checks (committees, courts, the Lords) against executive dominance; (4) apply a test case: *Cherry/Miller II*, *UNISON*, or the Lord Chancellor's role. For a problem question on a convention, say whether the matter is one of law or politics; courts will not enforce; if a statute refers to a convention, the question of whether it applies is justiciable only to the extent the statute makes it so. For ministerial responsibility, identify who was at fault (minister, official, agency) and whether the Ministerial Code was breached. Everyday question ("must a minister resign if the department errs?"): no legal duty; political convention depends on personal fault, public confidence and the Prime Minister.

## Debates and reform
- Is the executive too dominant (Lord Hailsham's "elective dictatorship", 1976)? Counter: select committees, the House of Lords as a revising chamber, the courts.
- Whether Lord Chancellor reforms went far enough: the Lord Chancellor remains a Cabinet minister, and the 2005 Act removed the requirement that the office-holder be a senior lawyer (it now requires only that the person appears qualified by experience); some argue this weakens the office's capacity to defend judicial independence.
- Codification of conventions: the Sewel convention in the Scotland Act s 28(8) and the Wales Act has been legislated, but *Miller I* shows that putting a convention in statute may not make it enforceable. Compare the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which restored prerogative powers.
- Ministerial Code: should it be placed on a statutory basis? The November 2024 revisions let the Independent Adviser initiate investigations on notice to the Prime Minister, but the Prime Minister remains the final arbiter of breach and sanction, which critics say leaves enforcement political.
- The Supreme Court's role: critics (including some ministers) argue that after *Miller II* the Court overreached; supporters say it protected Parliament. A relevant measure is the 2022 Act that ousted review of the prerogative power of dissolution.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 (in force on Royal Assent, 24 March 2022): repealed the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 (s 1), revived the prerogative powers of dissolution and calling a new Parliament (s 2), ousted court review of them (s 3), and provides for automatic dissolution on the fifth anniversary of a Parliament's first meeting (s 4).
- The Ministerial Code and the Independent Adviser's terms of reference are non-statutory and were last substantially revised in November 2024.
- The Courts and Tribunals Bill was in the House of Lords in November 2026 (report stage set for 1 December 2026); it is not yet law.
