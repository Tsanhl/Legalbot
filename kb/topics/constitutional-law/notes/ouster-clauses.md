# Ouster clauses and the limits on excluding judicial review

## Overview
An ouster (or exclusion) clause is a statutory provision saying a decision "shall not be questioned in any court". The courts read such clauses restrictively: since *Anisminic* [1969] a total ouster does not protect a decision that is a nullity because of an error of law (see the existing Anisminic case note). Parliament has since moved to partial and carefully drafted exclusions (the Cart ouster in the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022). The *Privacy International* decision (2019) is often misdescribed: the Supreme Court held that the particular ouster in the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 did not exclude judicial review of the Investigatory Powers Tribunal for error of law, but the judges differed about what an ouster could achieve if the wording were clearer.

## The law
**Types of clause.** (1) Finality clauses ("the decision shall be final"): never enough to exclude review for jurisdictional error. (2) "Shall not be questioned" clauses: construed to protect valid decisions only (*Anisminic*). (3) Time-limit clauses, which allow challenge only in a short period (for example, six weeks for planning orders) and are accepted: *Smith v East Elloe RDC* [1956] AC 736 (HL), where an allegation of bad faith was barred once the statutory time had passed; the House of Lords treated the time limit as effective even against such an allegation, and *R v Secretary of State for the Environment, ex p Ostler* [1977] QB 122 (CA) applied the same approach to a challenge for breach of natural justice made after the statutory period. (4) "Conclusive evidence" clauses. (5) Total ousters in express and specific words ("not even for an error of law"), which authorities treat as cautiously effective.

**Anisminic.** The House of Lords held the Foreign Compensation Commission's decision, which ought to have been protected from being "called in question in any court of law" by the Foreign Compensation Act 1950 s 4(4), was a nullity as made on an error of law that took the Commission outside its jurisdiction. The "jurisdictional error" doctrine was expanded: nearly all errors of law go to jurisdiction (*Re Racal Communications Ltd* [1981] AC 374 (HL), and *R v Lord President of the Privy Council, ex p Page* [1993] AC 682 (HL), which limited the reach of the doctrine for bodies other than inferior tribunals). See the case note for the *Anisminic* facts and reasoning.

**Privacy International.** *R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal* [2019] UKSC 22, [2020] AC 491 (Supreme Court, 4-3). A challenge was brought to the Investigatory Powers Tribunal's ruling that GCHQ's "computer network exploitation" activities could be authorised by a "thematic" warrant under the Intelligence Services Act 1994 s 5. RIPA 2000 s 67(8) provided that determinations, awards and other decisions of the Tribunal (including decisions on whether they have jurisdiction) "shall not be subject to appeal or be liable to be questioned in any court". The majority (Lord Carnwath giving the leading judgment) held that s 67(8) did not oust judicial review for error of law: the rule of law and the principle in *Anisminic* required very clear words, and the courts should construe such clauses on the assumption that Parliament intended the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Court to remain, even though the Tribunal was a specialist body whose decisions Parliament intended to be final. Lord Carnwath (with whom Lady Hale and Lord Kerr agreed) held that judicial review can be excluded only by "the most clear and explicit words" (*Cart*, para 31), and the parenthesis about jurisdiction did not supply them. Lord Lloyd-Jones agreed on that first issue but expressed no view on the second. On the second issue (whether Parliament can exclude review at all), Lord Carnwath said obiter (para 144) that there is "a strong case" that, consistently with the rule of law, binding effect cannot be given to a clause purporting wholly to exclude the High Court's supervisory jurisdiction over an inferior court or tribunal, and that it is ultimately for the courts to determine the extent to which such a clause should be upheld. Lord Sumption (with Lord Reed) dissented: s 67(8), read with its parenthesis and in the context of a judicial body of high standing like the IPT, did exclude review for errors of law within the tribunal's jurisdiction, and Parliament may lawfully do this. Lord Wilson dissented separately. The majority's construction is the law; the para 144 dicta are obiter and represent the view of three justices.

**Cart.** *R (Cart) v Upper Tribunal* [2011] UKSC 28, [2012] 1 AC 663 (Supreme Court): the Upper Tribunal is not a court of equivalent standing to the High Court for these purposes, so its refusals of permission to appeal are in principle amenable to judicial review; but to limit use of scarce resources, review should lie only on the "second-tier appeals" test (an important point of principle or practice, or some other compelling reason; Baroness Hale). Hence *Cart* judicial reviews, which had a low success rate.

**JRCA 2022 s 2.** This inserted s 11A into the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007. It applies to a decision of the Upper Tribunal to refuse permission (or leave) to appeal on an application under s 11(4)(b), that is, an application to the Upper Tribunal for permission to appeal to it from a decision of the First-tier Tribunal after the First-tier Tribunal has refused permission. Such a decision is final and not liable to be questioned or set aside in any other court (s 11A(2)); the Upper Tribunal is not to be regarded as having exceeded its powers by reason of any error made in reaching it, and no application for judicial review may be brought in relation to it (s 11A(3)). The exceptions in s 11A(4) apply where the decision gives rise to a question whether (a) the Upper Tribunal had a valid application before it, (b) it was properly constituted, or (c) it acted in bad faith or in such a procedurally defective way as to amount to a fundamental breach of the principles of natural justice. "Decision" includes any purported decision (s 11A(7)). The ouster does not apply where provision for the First-tier Tribunal's jurisdiction could be made by the Scottish Parliament or (in most cases) the Northern Ireland Assembly (s 11A(5)), and the High Court is not to entertain a judicial review of the First-tier Tribunal's decision that it would not have entertained anyway (s 11A(6)). The section applies to Upper Tribunal decisions made on or after 14 July 2022, when s 2 came into force (JRCA s 2(2)). The effect is to reverse *Cart* for these permission decisions; it is a partial ouster that names the grounds on which review remains.

**Oceana.** *R (Oceana) v Upper Tribunal (Immigration and Asylum Chamber)* [2023] EWHC 791 (Admin) (Saini J, sitting alone, 4 April 2023): the first full consideration of s 11A, tried as a preliminary issue on jurisdiction. Saini J held that the claim (alleging an unfair refusal of permission) did not fall within the natural justice exception in s 11A(4)(c)(ii), and that s 11A is "a clear, binding and effective partial exclusion of the common law supervisory jurisdiction of the High Court"; it was for Parliament to decide the limits of that jurisdiction. The claim was dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**LA (Albania).** *R (LA (Albania)) v Upper Tribunal (Immigration and Asylum Chamber)* [2023] EWCA Civ 1337 (Underhill LJ, Dingemans LJ and Lewis LJ, 16 November 2023): the Court of Appeal held that *Oceana* was right and that s 11A effectively limits the High Court's supervisory jurisdiction to the s 11A(4) exceptions. A mere assertion that an exception applies is not enough: the claimant must show a "genuinely disputable" question that it applies (Dingemans LJ). Where the court finds it has no jurisdiction, the correct order is to dismiss the claim rather than refuse permission. Underhill LJ doubted that complaints about the First-tier Tribunal's procedure could by themselves bring a claim within s 11A(4)(c)(ii), which is directed at the Upper Tribunal's own conduct.

**Parliamentary sovereignty and the courts.** *Jackson* [2005] UKHL 56 obiter dicta (Lord Steyn, Lord Hope, Baroness Hale) suggested courts might not give effect to an Act purporting to abolish judicial review altogether; the position is untested. The Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 (s 3) ousts review of dissolution prerogative powers (see the prerogative note).

## Key authorities
- *Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission* [1969] 2 AC 147 (HL): jurisdictional error; ouster clause did not protect a nullity. See the existing case note.
- *R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal* [2019] UKSC 22 (Supreme Court, 4-3): RIPA s 67(8) did not exclude review for error of law.
- *R (Cart) v Upper Tribunal* [2011] UKSC 28 (Supreme Court): JR of Upper Tribunal permission refusals, on second-tier appeals criteria.
- *R (Oceana) v Upper Tribunal* [2023] EWHC 791 (Admin) (Saini J): s 11A a clear and effective partial ouster.
- *R (LA (Albania)) v Upper Tribunal* [2023] EWCA Civ 1337 (Court of Appeal): s 11A effective; claimant must show a genuinely disputable question that an exception applies.
- *Smith v East Elloe Rural District Council* [1956] AC 736 (HL): time-limit clause effective against allegations of bad faith.
- *Secretary of State for the Home Department v AF (No 3)* [2009] UKHL 28: not an ouster case but relevant to Parliament's attempts to limit procedural protection; see the existing note.

## Applying it
Problem question steps: (1) Read the clause: is it a "shall not be questioned" provision, a finality clause, a time limit, or a clear and specific bar? (2) Is the alleged defect an error of law of the sort that would, post-*Anisminic*, be treated as a jurisdictional error? If so, the traditional clause will not protect the decision. (3) Is the body a specialist tribunal whose decisions the statute designates as final (*Privacy International*)? The majority required specific words to oust review for error of law. (4) For Upper Tribunal permission refusals, apply s 11A and its exceptions; the claimant must show a genuinely disputable question that the claim fits one of the s 11A(4) exceptions (no valid application, improperly constituted tribunal, bad faith, or a procedurally defective decision amounting to a fundamental breach of natural justice) (*LA (Albania)*). (5) Consider whether the claimant can bring the challenge in another way (appeal route, the statutory appeal; human rights remedies). Note that s 11A bars JR by the High Court but a statutory route may remain. Everyday: a person refused permission to appeal by the Upper Tribunal can no longer apply for judicial review as a matter of course, save in exceptional situations.

## Debates and reform
- Whether Parliament can legally exclude judicial review at all: parliamentary sovereignty says yes (Lord Sumption's dissent, Oceana); common law constitutionalism says there are limits grounded in the rule of law (Lord Carnwath in *Privacy International*, *Jackson* dicta). The academic literature (for example Mark Elliott, Paul Craig and Timothy Endicott) is extensive.
- The Independent Review of Administrative Law (March 2021) recommended abolishing *Cart* judicial reviews but not wider ouster clauses; the Government's 2021 consultation adopted the *Cart* proposal, which became JRCA s 2.
- Whether *Cart* JRs were wasteful: the Independent Review relied on a very low success rate (well under 1 per cent), a figure critics argued undercounted successful claims; rule of law groups said the claims protected vulnerable claimants in immigration and asylum cases.
- Wider use: the Immigration and Asylum Bill (introduced 30 June 2026) would replace the First-tier Tribunal in immigration and asylum appeals with an Independent Immigration Appeals Authority, and as introduced (clause 1(8)) would allow its decisions to be questioned in legal proceedings only through the Bill's internal review and an appeal on a point of law to the Upper Tribunal, applying the s 11A-style ouster to that appeal route; the Law Society, Bar Council and ILPA criticised the exclusion of judicial review. The Bill passed the Commons on 17 November 2026 and had its Lords first reading on 18 November 2026; it is not law and its clause numbering may change.
