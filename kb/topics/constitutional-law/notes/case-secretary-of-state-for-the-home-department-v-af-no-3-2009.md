# Secretary of State for the Home Department v AF (No 3) [2009] UKHL 28 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. "Gisting" is a commentators' term; the sources
> describe the requirement as disclosure of enough information to give effective instructions. Do not quote paragraph
> numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 10 June 2009, an enlarged panel of nine Lords who decided unanimously. The case concerns control
orders under the Prevention of Terrorism Act 2005 and the right to a fair hearing under Article 6 ECHR when the
Secretary of State relies on **closed material** that the controlled person cannot see.

## Facts
The appellants (AF, AN and AE) were subject to non-derogating control orders. At the hearings supervising the orders
(under section 3(10)), the judge could consider closed material withheld from them on national security grounds, with
a special advocate representing their interests in the closed sessions. They argued that this breached Article 6.
Earlier, in *SSHD v MB and AF* [2007] UKHL 46, the House had read the Act so that a fair hearing was possible.

## Decision
- The Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights decided *A v United Kingdom* before the House ruled.
  The House of Lords treated that judgment as binding on the Article 6 point (even where individual Lords had doubts, as
  one source reports).
- A controlled person must be given **sufficient information about the allegations** to enable effective instructions
  to the special advocate. Where the open material is only general assertions and the case rests solely or decisively
  on closed material, the requirements of a fair trial are not met, however strong the closed case appears.
- The House did not follow the *MB* approach of asking what difference disclosure might have made.
- Section 3(10) of the Act was read down so that it is applied consistently with this requirement.
- The appeals were allowed and the cases remitted to the judges. Afterwards the Secretary of State, rather than make
  further disclosure to some of those concerned, revoked the orders (reported in search results of High Court
  decisions; treat as indicative).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify whether the person faces a control order or comparable measure that uses closed material.
2. Ask whether the open case gives enough detail to challenge the central allegations; if not, and the case rests
   decisively on closed material, Article 6 is breached, whatever the strength of the closed material.
3. Note the limits: the requirement is about the core allegations, not every item of evidence, and it depends on how
   far the case rests on closed material.
4. Consider the remedy: the Act is read down under section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998, so the order cannot be
   upheld unless sufficient disclosure is made.
5. Link to the judicial-review theme: courts insist on fair procedures even where statute allows secrecy.

## Common errors to avoid

- Do not cite JSA 2013 s 17 for closed material in a judicial review: s 17 only bars Norwich Pharmacal-type (residual disclosure jurisdiction) orders for sensitive information.
