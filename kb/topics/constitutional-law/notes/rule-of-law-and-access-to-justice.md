# The rule of law and access to justice

## Overview
The rule of law is a constitutional principle (recognised in the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 s 1) that everyone, including the government, is subject to law that is accessible, applied equally and enforceable in independent courts. Modern English authority treats access to the courts as a constitutional right that general words in a statute or a delegated instrument will not remove, and treats executive attempts to nullify a court's decision as inconsistent with the rule of law. Essays commonly contrast Dicey's formal account with Bingham's fuller substantive list.

## The law
**Dicey's three meanings** (*Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution*, 1885): (1) the supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power (no punishment except for a breach of law established in the ordinary courts); (2) equality before the law: officials and citizens answer in the same ordinary courts; (3) the constitution is the result of ordinary law applied by courts (rights come from judicial decisions rather than a declaration). Famous illustrations are *Entick v Carrington* (1765) 19 St Tr 1029, where a Secretary of State's warrant was held to need a legal basis, and *Liversidge v Anderson* [1942] AC 206, where the majority took a deferential view of wartime detention power (Lord Atkin dissenting).

**Bingham's eight sub-rules** (Lord Bingham, *The Rule of Law*, 2010): the law must be accessible, intelligible, clear and predictable; questions of legal right and liability should ordinarily be resolved by law, not discretion; laws should apply equally to all, save where objective differences justify differentiation; ministers and public officers must exercise their powers in good faith, fairly, for the purpose for which they were conferred, without exceeding their limits and not unreasonably; the law must afford adequate protection of fundamental human rights; means must be provided for resolving civil disputes the parties cannot resolve themselves, without prohibitive cost or inordinate delay; adjudicative procedures must be fair; the state must comply with its obligations in international law.

**Formal versus substantive.** Raz, Fuller and (in the UK) Dicey are linked with formal or thin conceptions (clarity, prospectivity, generality, enforceability). Dworkin, Bingham and Lord Steyn associate the principle with substantive protections such as human rights. This affects whether a statute that offends rights can still be said to comply with the rule of law.

**Statutory recognition.** Constitutional Reform Act 2005 s 1 states that the Act does not adversely affect "the existing constitutional principle of the rule of law" or the Lord Chancellor's existing role in relation to it. Section 3 imposes a duty on the Lord Chancellor and other ministers and persons with responsibility for the administration of justice to uphold the continued independence of the judiciary, and prohibits them from seeking to influence particular judicial decisions through any special access to judges. The Lord Chancellor swears an oath to respect the rule of law and defend the independence of the judiciary (s 17).

**Access to justice as a constitutional right.** The common law treats the right of access to the courts as a right that cannot be taken away except by clear statutory words or necessary implication. *R v Lord Chancellor, ex p Witham* [1998] QB 575 (Laws J) quashed an order increasing court fees that effectively prevented the poor suing, since the Lord Chancellor's general power to set fees did not clearly authorise barring access. *R v Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p Leech (No 2)* [1994] QB 198 (CA) applied the same principle to prisoners' correspondence with solicitors.

## Key authorities
- *R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor* [2017] UKSC 51, [2020] AC 869 (Supreme Court, Lord Reed): the Employment Tribunals and Employment Appeal Tribunal Fees Order 2013 (fees to bring employment claims) was unlawful and quashed. Held that the right of access to the courts is inherent in the rule of law; delegated legislation that prevents or effectively prevents access to the courts is unlawful if it hinders access to justice, not just if it makes it impossible; the fees were unaffordable for many and were disproportionate even where they could be afforded, since they deterred meritorious claims; the order was also indirectly discriminatory contrary to the Equality Act 2010. The Fees Order was quashed ab initio and fees repaid.
- *R (Evans) v Attorney General* [2015] UKSC 21, [2015] AC 1787 (Supreme Court): letters from the Prince of Wales to government departments. The Upper Tribunal ordered disclosure under the Freedom of Information Act 2000; the Attorney General issued a certificate under FOIA s 53 saying the departments' decision to withhold was reasonable. Held (5-2) that the certificate was invalid. Lord Neuberger (with Lord Kerr and Lord Reed) held that a statute allowing a member of the executive to override a judicial decision merely because he disagrees with it needs crystal clear words, so s 53 permits a certificate after a tribunal or court decision only where there is a material change of circumstances or the decision was demonstrably flawed in fact or law; he also held that a s 53 certificate was incompatible with EU law so far as the request concerned environmental information. Lord Mance (with Lady Hale) reached the same result on a narrower basis: the Attorney General could not reasonably reject the Upper Tribunal's detailed findings without clear justification. Lord Wilson and Lord Hughes dissented. Lord Neuberger's reasoning is the leading statement that executive override of a judicial decision offends the rule of law, but it was the view of three of the seven justices.
- *R (Privacy International) v Investigatory Powers Tribunal* [2019] UKSC 22: a statutory ouster clause did not exclude judicial review; see the ouster clauses note.
- *Anisminic Ltd v Foreign Compensation Commission* [1969] 2 AC 147 (HL): existing case note in this knowledge base.
- *Secretary of State for the Home Department v AF (No 3)* [2009] UKHL 28: existing case note; relevant to fair procedure and closed material.
- *R v Lord Chancellor, ex p Witham* [1998] QB 575 (QBD) and *Leech (No 2)* above.
- *Entick v Carrington* (1765) 19 St Tr 1029 (King's Bench): the executive needs legal authority for interference with property.
- *M v Home Office* [1994] 1 AC 377 (HL): ministers in their official capacity are subject to the court's coercive jurisdiction, including contempt, in judicial review.
- *R (Miller) v Prime Minister; Cherry v Advocate General for Scotland* [2019] UKSC 41: the Supreme Court said the executive may not frustrate Parliament's constitutional functions; relied on rule of law and sovereignty (see the prerogative note).
- *Fire Brigades Union* [1995] 2 AC 513 (HL): ministers may not use prerogative to defeat a statutory scheme.

## Applying it
Typical question forms: (a) a Minister makes regulations or fixes a fee that makes bringing a claim much harder (*Witham*; *UNISON*): ask whether the enabling power clearly authorises restricting access, whether the restriction deters real claimants (look at the evidence of falling claim numbers, affordability and the likelihood of recovery), and whether it is proportionate; (b) a statute gives a Minister power to override a court decision (*Evans*): strict construction; (c) an exclusion clause ("shall not be called in question"): see the ouster clauses note. Structure: identify the right or principle, source of the power, test whether Parliament has spoken in clear terms, then remedy (quashing, declaration). In an essay, state the Dicey/Bingham contrast, then pair a formal point (clarity, generality) with a substantive point (access, rights). For an everyday question ("can the government ignore a court order?"), no: ministers are bound (*M v Home Office*), and the courts will resist statutory efforts to override a decision without clear words.

## Debates and reform
- Is the rule of law a legal principle that can invalidate, or only a principle of interpretation? *Jackson* dicta flirt with the first; the orthodox view is the second, along with the principle of legality (*ex p Simms*).
- Thin or thick: whether the rule of law includes human rights and equality or only formal legality (Raz versus Dworkin and Bingham).
- Legal aid and the cost of litigation: the Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 cut civil legal aid scope; *UNISON* shows how fees can themselves deny access; the reform debate concerns court fees generally, legal aid for early advice, and "advice deserts".
- Ouster clauses and executive override: see the related notes on ouster clauses and judicial review. Criticism of the Judicial Review and Courts Act 2022 and of later proposals is framed by the rule of law, as is the debate about the Lord Chancellor's role after 2005.
- The Government's duty to comply with international law (Bingham's eighth principle) was contested, e.g. around the Internal Market Act 2020 and the Illegal Migration Act 2023 debates; there is no settled judicial answer on whether it is part of the domestic rule of law.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Employment tribunal fees stopped being charged immediately after *UNISON* (26 July 2017) and the Government set up a refund scheme.
- The Courts and Tribunals Bill (introduced 25 February 2026, carried over to the 2026-27 session) passed the Commons on 13 October 2026 and had its Lords committee stage in November 2026, with Lords report stage set for 1 December 2026. It is not yet law, so do not state any of its changes to court structure or procedure as current law.
