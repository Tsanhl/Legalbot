# Devolution - Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

## Overview
The UK Parliament has devolved legislative and executive powers to the Scottish Parliament, the Welsh Senedd and the Northern Ireland Assembly by Acts of Parliament, but remains sovereign and can legislate for the whole UK and amend or repeal the devolution statutes. Devolution is asymmetric: Scotland and Wales now use a "reserved powers" model (everything not reserved to Westminster is devolved), while Northern Ireland has a three-tier model of excepted, reserved and transferred matters. Courts police the boundaries through "devolution issues" and pre-enactment references. The Sewel convention is political, not legally enforceable.

## The law
**Scotland.** Scotland Act 1998 (SA 1998). The Scottish Parliament may make laws ("Acts of the Scottish Parliament") (s 28(1)). Section 28(7): the power to make laws does not affect the power of the UK Parliament to make laws for Scotland. Section 29(1): an Act (or provision) is not law so far as outside the Parliament's legislative competence; s 29(2): a provision is outside competence if (a) it would form part of the law of a country other than Scotland (or confer powers outside Scotland); (b) it relates to reserved matters; (c) it is in breach of the restrictions in Sch 4 (protected enactments, including the SA 1998 itself with limits, and the Human Rights Act 1998); (d) it is incompatible with Convention rights (the former restriction for incompatibility with EU law was removed at the end of the Brexit transition period, 31 December 2020); (e) it would remove the Lord Advocate from the position as head of the systems of criminal prosecution and investigation of deaths. Reserved matters are listed in Sch 5 (constitution, foreign affairs, defence, immigration, macroeconomic policy, much of social security (with later transfers), broadcasting, etc.). Section 28(8) (inserted by the Scotland Act 2016): "it is recognised that the Parliament of the United Kingdom will not normally legislate with regard to devolved matters without the consent of the Scottish Parliament" (the Sewel convention). Section 63A (inserted 2016) declares that the Scottish Parliament and Scottish Government are a "permanent part of the United Kingdom's constitutional arrangements" which can only be abolished following a referendum in Scotland.

Controls on Bills: before a Bill is introduced, the member in charge must state its competence (s 31); the Presiding Officer also states a view. After passage there is a four-week window in which the Law Officers (Advocate General for Scotland, Lord Advocate, Attorney General) may refer the question of competence to the Supreme Court (s 33), and a UK Secretary of State may make an order prohibiting submission for Royal Assent on grounds of an adverse effect on reserved matters or international obligations (s 35). Devolution issues also arise in ordinary litigation (Sch 6): any court can determine them, with appeal to the Supreme Court.

**Wales.** Government of Wales Act 2006 (GOWA 2006), as amended by the Wales Act 2014 and the Wales Act 2017, which introduced a reserved powers model from 1 April 2018 (Sch 7A lists reserved matters; Sch 7B contains general restrictions). The Senedd can make Acts (s 107): a provision is outside competence if it relates to reserved matters, breaches Sch 7B restrictions, or is incompatible with Convention rights (s 108A). The Wales Act 2017 added a recognition that the UK Parliament will not normally legislate for devolved matters without the Senedd's consent (s 107(6) GOWA 2006) and declared the Senedd and the Welsh Government to be a permanent part of the UK's constitutional arrangements (s A1). The Senedd and Elections (Wales) Act 2020 renamed the Welsh Assembly the Senedd Cymru (Welsh Parliament), lowered the voting age to 16, and extended the franchise to qualifying foreign nationals. The Senedd Cymru (Members and Elections) Act 2024 increased the Senedd to 96 members elected by closed-list proportional representation in 16 six-member constituencies, first used at the May 2026 election. The Counsel General or the Attorney General can refer a Senedd Bill to the Supreme Court on competence (GOWA 2006 s 112).

**Northern Ireland.** Northern Ireland Act 1998 (NIA 1998), made to implement the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement 1998, and since amended (St Andrews Agreement, 2006; Stormont House Agreement; New Decade, New Approach, 2020). Section 1 declares that NI remains part of the UK unless a majority of the people of NI vote for Irish unity in a referendum (the "principle of consent"). Matters are "excepted" (Sch 2: e.g. the Crown, defence, international relations, nationality), "reserved" (Sch 3) or "transferred" (everything else: s 4(1)); the Assembly legislates on transferred matters, and on reserved matters only with the Secretary of State's consent (s 8). An Assembly Act is not law so far as outside competence (s 6), including incompatibility with Convention rights or with the Windsor Framework arrangements given effect by EUWA 2018 s 7A. Power-sharing: the First Minister is nominated by the largest party in the Assembly and the deputy First Minister by the largest party of the largest other designation (unionist or nationalist), and the two offices are joint and of equal status; the institutions have been suspended several times, most recently from February 2022 until 3 February 2024, with Westminster legislation extending the period for forming an Executive in the meantime. Section 75 places a statutory equality duty on public authorities; s 76 prohibits discrimination by public authorities on grounds of religious belief or political opinion. The Windsor Framework (2023) changes the arrangements under the Northern Ireland Protocol for goods and gives the Assembly a "Stormont brake" mechanism over certain new EU rules, alongside the Assembly's democratic consent vote on continuing the arrangements (see the EU law area).

**Sewel and Miller I.** *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU* [2017] UKSC 5: the devolved institutions argued that their consent was legally required before Parliament legislated on triggering withdrawal. The Supreme Court (unanimously on this point) held that the Sewel convention, though recognised in statute (SA 1998 s 28(8), as inserted), is a political convention: the courts cannot enforce it, and the legislative consent of the devolved legislatures was not a legal requirement; the relevant statutes do not stop the UK Parliament legislating for devolved matters, and the use of the word "recognised" does not convert the convention into a rule of law. The court also held that the Northern Ireland Act 1998 did not require the consent of NI's people or the Assembly for Brexit. The Sewel convention has been overridden politically (e.g. the Scottish Parliament withheld consent to the EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018 and the EU (Withdrawal Agreement) Act 2020, but those Acts proceeded; the UK Internal Market Act 2020 was also enacted despite refusal of consent by the Scottish Parliament and the Senedd).

**Pre-enactment references.** *Reference by the Attorney General and the Advocate General for Scotland — United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (Incorporation) (Scotland) Bill* [2021] UKSC 42 (Supreme Court, under SA 1998 s 33): the Court (Lord Reed, unanimous, 6 October 2021) held that all four referred provisions of the UNCRC Bill were outside legislative competence. Sections 19(2)(a)(ii), 20(10)(a)(ii) and 21(5)(b)(ii), which would have allowed Scottish courts to read down or declare incompatible, or strike down, provisions of Acts of the UK Parliament, would modify s 28(7) of the Scotland Act by limiting Westminster's power to make laws for Scotland. Section 6 (making it unlawful for public authorities to act incompatibly with the UNCRC requirements) was outside competence as drafted: it did not clearly exclude reserved matters and acts done under Westminster legislation, and the Scottish Government could not rely on the reading-down rule in s 101(2) to save a provision deliberately drafted to reach the outer limits of competence, because the law must be clear to those who apply it; Lord Reed said it would need to be redrafted. The two referred provisions of the European Charter of Local Self-Government (Incorporation) (Scotland) Bill failed for the same s 28(7) reason. The UNCRC Bill was amended and enacted as the UNCRC (Incorporation) (Scotland) Act 2024 (Royal Assent 16 January 2024; main duties, limited to powers under Acts of the Scottish Parliament and the common law, in force 16 July 2024). *Reference by the Lord Advocate of devolution issues under para 34 of Sch 6 to the Scotland Act 1998* [2022] UKSC 31 (unanimous): the Lord Advocate could not be confident that a draft Scottish Independence Referendum Bill (to ask "Should Scotland be an independent country?") was within competence, and referred the question. The Court accepted the reference as a devolution issue; held that the proposed Bill would relate to reserved matters (the Union of the Kingdoms of Scotland and England, and the Parliament of the UK, under Sch 5 Pt 1 paras 1(b) and (c), via SA 1998 s 29(2)(b)), because it had more than a loose or consequential connection to those matters; its purpose was to hold a lawful and politically significant expression of the will of the people of Scotland even if advisory; so the Scottish Parliament has no power to legislate for it unless Westminster alters the reserved matters, by Order in Council under s 30 or by legislation. The Court rejected the argument that self-determination under international law provides a basis for competence.

## Key authorities
- *R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the EU* [2017] UKSC 5: Sewel convention not legally enforceable; NI consent not required.
- *Reference re UNCRC (Incorporation) (Scotland) Bill* [2021] UKSC 42: Bill provisions outside competence for modifying s 28(7); limits on pre-enactment check.
- *Reference by the Lord Advocate* [2022] UKSC 31: no power for a Scottish independence referendum Bill.
- *Reference by the Attorney General and the Advocate General for Scotland — UK Withdrawal from the EU (Legal Continuity) (Scotland) Bill* [2018] UKSC 64: the Supreme Court held that the Bill as a whole was not outside competence (it did not relate to the reserved matter of foreign affairs), but that s 17 (requiring Scottish Ministers' consent to certain UK subordinate legislation) was outside competence when passed because it would modify s 28(7). The European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018, which received Royal Assent after the Bill was passed but before the reference was decided, became a protected enactment under Sch 4; since competence is judged when the question is decided, many further provisions of the Bill became outside competence because they would modify the 2018 Act.
- *Imperial Tobacco Ltd v Lord Advocate* [2012] UKSC 61: Scottish tobacco display ban held within devolved health competence, not a reserved consumer protection matter.
- *AXA General Insurance Ltd v Lord Advocate* [2011] UKSC 46: Acts of the Scottish Parliament are not open to common law review for irrationality or unreasonableness as if they were delegated legislation; the justices mentioned, obiter, that review on fundamental rule of law grounds might arise in exceptional cases.

## Applying it
Typical problem: "The Scottish/Welsh/NI legislature passes a law on X. Is it valid?" Steps: (1) identify the statute and the issue; (2) is X reserved/excepted (Sch 5 SA; Sch 7A GOWA; NIA excepted and reserved matters)? Apply the "purpose" and "relates to" test (looking at the purpose, having regard to the effect in all circumstances: SA s 29(3)); incidental or consequential effect on reserved matters is not enough, but the proposed provision must not have more than a loose or consequential connection (*Reference [2022]*); (3) the restrictions on modifying protected enactments; (4) Convention rights; (5) procedure: a s 33 reference within four weeks; s 35 orders; (6) if the question is whether Westminster may legislate in a devolved area, answer yes (s 28(7)), noting the Sewel convention and that courts do not enforce it. In essays, link to parliamentary sovereignty and the constitutional status of devolution ("permanent part" provisions), and use the independence reference to show courts' limits. Everyday: Scottish Parliament cannot hold a legally binding independence referendum without Westminster's agreement.

## Debates and reform
- Permanence and sovereignty: SA s 63A and GOWA s A1 are expressions of political commitment; as the 2021 and 2022 references show, Parliament remains sovereign. Does the Sewel convention need legal force? The Supreme Court says no; critics (including devolved governments) say the UK Government's practice has weakened it, especially in relation to the UK Internal Market Act 2020.
- Asymmetry and the West Lothian question: English votes for English laws (Standing Orders 2015) were abolished in 2021.
- Intergovernmental relations: the Joint Ministerial Committee was replaced by a new structure in 2022; the Independent Commission on the Constitutional Future of Wales (final report January 2024) recommended further devolution.
- Whether a lawful route to a referendum on independence (a s 30 order, the 2014 route) is a political, not legal, decision; UK Governments have refused requests for a further s 30 order since 2017.
