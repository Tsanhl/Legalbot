# Secret trusts, supporting cases (case notes)

> No official copies exist for these cases. Re Stead, Re Gardner (No 2), Re Young and McCormick v Grogan were checked
> against scans of the Law Reports (OCR text, so check wording against a clean copy before quoting). *Tee v Ferris*
> was not read: it is described only as Farwell J summarised it in *Re Stead*. *Re Bateman's WT* was not read: it is
> taken from textbooks. See secret-trusts.md for the doctrine as a whole.

## McCormick v Grogan (1869) LR 4 HL 82 (HL)
- **Facts.** A testator left everything by a short will to his friend Grogan. Dying of cholera, he told Grogan of the
  will. When Grogan asked whether that was right, the testator said he would have it no other way, and told him a
  letter would be found with the will. The letter listed many intended gifts and annuities but left Grogan to use
  his own judgment and not to act strictly on it. Grogan paid some of the listed persons but not the appellant.
- **Held.** No trust bound Grogan. Lord Hatherley LC (at 89) said the doctrine "requires to be carefully restricted"
  and has been applied only in clear cases of fraud. Lord Westbury (at 97) said the jurisdiction "is founded
  altogether on personal fraud". Fraud must be proved by the clearest evidence, and the claimant must show that the
  legatee accepted the obligation. The letter's terms were in any case not obligatory.
- **Use.** The classic fraud basis of secret trusts, and the need for an intention to impose a binding (not merely
  moral) obligation. The high standard of proof was later confined by *Re Snowden* [1979] Ch 528 to cases of actual
  fraud.

## Tee v Ferris (1856) 2 K & J 357
- As stated by Farwell J in *Re Stead* ([1900] 1 Ch 237, 241): where A induces the testator to make, or to leave
  unrevoked, a gift to A and C **as tenants in common** by promising that both will carry out the testator's wishes,
  and C knows nothing of it, A is bound but C is not. The reason given (2 K & J 357, 368) is that holding otherwise
  would let one beneficiary deprive the others of their benefits by setting up a secret trust.

## Re Stead [1900] 1 Ch 237 (Farwell J)
- **Facts.** The testatrix left her residue to two friends, Mrs Witham and Mrs Andrew, as joint tenants. Mrs Witham
  said she had been told, before the will was made, of a trust for the testatrix's nephew and charities. Mrs Andrew
  had been told nothing in the testatrix's lifetime.
- **Held.** Mrs Witham had not proved communication before the will. Treating the trust as communicated to and
  accepted by her after execution, Mrs Andrew was not bound and took her share beneficially.
- **Rules stated (at 241):** tenants in common: only those who promised are bound (*Tee v Ferris*). Joint tenants,
  will made on the faith of an earlier promise by one: both are bound (*Russell v Jackson*; *Jones v Badley*), since
  no one may claim under another's fraud. Joint tenants, will left unrevoked on the faith of a later promise: only
  the promisor is bound (*Burney v Macdonald*; *Moss v Cooper*). Farwell J saw no real difference between the two
  joint-tenancy cases but followed the authorities.

## Re Gardner (No 2) [1923] 2 Ch 230 (Romer J)
- **Facts.** By a 1909 will the testatrix left her estate to her husband for life, "knowing that he will carry out my
  wishes". Four days later she signed an unattested memorandum dividing the money equally among a nephew and two
  nieces on his death. The Court of Appeal (*Re Gardner* [1920] 2 Ch 523) held that the husband held the corpus on
  that trust. One niece died in 1917, before the testatrix (1919).
- **Held.** The deceased niece's share passed to her personal representative and did not lapse. The rights were as if
  the husband had declared an inter vivos trust when the memorandum was communicated to him.
- **Use.** The usual illustration of the "dehors the will" theory. It is widely criticised, because nothing passes
  until the testator dies.

## Re Young [1951] Ch 344 (Danckwerts J)
- **Facts.** The will left the estate to the testator's wife and required her to give effect to the purposes and
  small legacies "she knows" he wished. Before the will was made he had told her these included a £2,000 legacy to
  his chauffeur, who was an attesting witness.
- **Held.** Valid secret trusts were established by parol evidence. The chauffeur kept his legacy despite Wills Act
  1837 s 15, because his interest arose under the trust imposed on the widow, not under the will.

## Re Bateman's Will Trusts [1970] 1 WLR 1463; [1970] 3 All ER 817 (Pennycuick V-C)
- **Facts (secondary sources).** A sum (£24,000, according to secondary sources) was left to trustees for such persons "as
  shall be stated by me in a sealed letter", contemplating a communication after the will.
- **Held.** The half-secret trust failed. A will that refers to a future communication cannot be supplemented by one,
  applying the *Blackwell v Blackwell* [1929] AC 318 rule that communication must come before or at execution (see
  also *Re Keen* [1937] Ch 236). Textbooks note that the rule was applied as if beyond doubt.

## Applying them to a problem question
1. Type of trust: fully secret (absolute gift) or half-secret (trust on the face of the will)?
2. Communication and acceptance: when, and to whom? For half-secret trusts, before or at execution and consistent
   with the will (*Bateman*, *Keen*).
3. Several legatees: tenants in common, or joint tenants, and promise before or after the will (*Re Stead*, *Tee v
   Ferris*).
4. Intention: a binding obligation, not a moral one (*McCormick v Grogan*).
5. Side issues: a witness-beneficiary (*Re Young*), or a beneficiary who predeceased the testator (*Re Gardner (No
   2)*).
