# Variation and termination of trusts

## Overview
Trusts can last for decades, and tax law, family circumstances and beneficiaries' needs change. Settlors cannot control a completed trust, so the law provides ways for beneficiaries or the court to end or change one: the rule in *Saunders v Vautier* (termination by adult, absolutely entitled beneficiaries), the Variation of Trusts Act 1958 (court approval on behalf of those who cannot consent), and "Benjamin" orders (distribution when beneficiaries cannot be found). Key concepts: a *contingent* interest depends on an uncertain event (including a discretionary object's interest); an interest is *vested* when it is certain even if enjoyment is postponed; it is *in possession* when the holder can currently enjoy it.

## The law
**1. The rule in *Saunders v Vautier* (1841) 4 Beav 115; Cr & Ph 240.** Beneficiaries who between them are absolutely entitled, all aged 18 or over and of sound mind and agreed, can require the trustees to transfer legal title and so end the trust. Facts: stock was held to accumulate until Vautier was 25; at 21 he wanted the money to marry. Held: he had a vested gift with only enjoyment postponed (the stock was separated off and the gift was to him, "his executors, administrators, or assigns"), so he could demand it. Elements:
- all beneficiaries adult and of sound mind;
- between them absolutely entitled: no unborn or unascertained further beneficiaries; together they hold the whole beneficial interest, concurrently or successively (e.g. B for life, C in remainder);
- discretionary trusts too, if there is a complete list and between them they can have the whole fund applied for them; whether a power to add beneficiaries blocks this is unclear but unlikely (*Orb ARL v Ruhan* [2015]);
- agreement on termination and on how to divide the property;
- a single adult entitled to a fixed share of divisible personal property can sever it (*Stephenson v Barclays Bank* [1975] 1 All ER 625), but not where this would disadvantage the others (*Lloyds Bank v Duker* [1987] 3 All ER 193).

**2. Variation by the beneficiaries.** Since beneficiaries could end the trust and resettle, they may do the lesser act of varying it. A variation can further the settlor's purpose. Problems arise if some beneficiaries are minors or unborn. The court's inherent jurisdiction to approve compromises was narrowed in *Chapman v Chapman* [1954] 1 All ER 798, which prompted the Variation of Trusts Act 1958.

**3. Variation of Trusts Act 1958 s 1(1).** The court may approve an arrangement varying or revoking trusts or enlarging trustees' powers on behalf of: (a) a person with a vested or contingent interest who is incapable of consenting through infancy or incapacity; (b) a person (ascertained or not) who may in future become entitled as a member of a class or description (but not if they would be in that class if the date or event occurred at the date of the application); (c) any unborn person; (d) a person with a discretionary interest under protective trusts where the principal beneficiary's interest has not failed or determined. Anyone, including the settlor, may propose the arrangement. Further points:
- the court consents on behalf of the person; it does not itself vary the trust (*Re Hambleden's Will Trusts* [1960] 1 All ER 353);
- the variation is informal and exempt from the s 53(1)(c) LPA 1925 writing requirement (*Re Holt's Settlement* [1969] 1 Ch 100);
- those subject to a fiduciary power have an "interest" for s 1(1)(a) (*Harvey v Van Hoorn* [2023] EWHC 1298 (Ch));
- (b) covers mere expectants: *Knocker v Youle* [1968], *Re Moncrieff's Settlement Trust* [1962] (next of kin who may exist in future); excluded where the person would qualify at the date of hearing (*Re Suffert's Settlement* [1961]);
- all adult beneficiaries must themselves consent.

**4. The benefit requirement.** Except for category (d) (*Re Steed's Will Trusts* [1960]), the court may approve only if the arrangement benefits the person for whom it consents. Benefit is a threshold, not the whole discretion. Megarry J in *Re Holt's Settlement*: benefit is "plainly not confined to financial benefit, but may extend to moral or social benefit". Most variations are tax-driven and courts accept tax avoidance as an object, but *Re Weston's Settlements* [1968] 1 Ch 223 (Denning LJ) refused approval for moving a trust to Jersey to avoid tax: the court must protect those who cannot protect themselves, must consider educational and social as well as financial benefit, and "the avoidance of tax may be lawful, but it is not yet a virtue". Family harmony may count as a benefit: *Re Tinker's Settlement* [1960] (not enough to outweigh one sibling's children's loss) versus *Re Remnant's Settlement Trusts* [1970] (harmony recognised, with a compensating additional change).

**5. Settlor's intention.** *Goulding v James* [1997] 2 All ER 239: where capable beneficiaries agree an arrangement that they could lawfully make, the testator's wishes are irrelevant (the court said evidence of what the testator would have approved or disapproved had no relevance).

**6. Benjamin orders.** *Re Benjamin* [1902] 1 Ch 723: trustees of a fixed trust may distribute among known beneficiaries where some cannot be found, leaving the missing beneficiaries' interests alive (they can later trace into the recipients' hands: *Re Evans* [1999] 2 All ER 777). *Re MF Global UK (No 3)* [2013] EWHC 1655 (Ch) extended this to beneficiaries whose claims were rejected and who did not bring proceedings by a bar date. *Re Pritchard Stockbrokers* [2019] EWHC 137 (Ch) permitted an "Alpari order" modifying the statutory client-money trust so that unclaimed or very small interests are extinguished. Norris J listed considerations: scale of the problem, steps taken to identify clients and quantify claims, every reasonable step to distribute (having regard to claim size, cost and difficulty, who bears the cost, and prompt return of assets), details of the mechanism, and why extinguishment is preferred over an MF Global-type order that preserves interests.

## Key authorities
- *Saunders v Vautier*; *Stephenson v Barclays Bank*; *Lloyds Bank v Duker*.
- *Chapman v Chapman*; Variation of Trusts Act 1958 s 1(1); *Re Hambleden*; *Re Holt*; *Harvey v Van Hoorn*.
- *Re Weston's Settlements*; *Re Tinker*; *Re Remnant*; *Goulding v James*.
- *Re Benjamin*; *Re Evans*; *Re MF Global (No 3)*; *Re Pritchard Stockbrokers*.

## Debate and critique
- The court prioritises current beneficiaries' benefit over the settlor's original intention (*Goulding*): is variation really in the settlor's interest, or does it undermine the settlor's control over their own property?
- Tax avoidance is lawful and routinely approved, but courts (Denning LJ in *Weston*) resist where it endangers wider welfare; this tension is the usual essay material.
- Insolvency "cut-off" orders (MF Global, Alpari) balance the interests of beneficiaries with agreed claims in timely and cost-effective payment against trustees' duty to all beneficiaries, and shade into extinguishing proprietary rights.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Can all beneficiaries, as adults of sound mind, agree? If so, *Saunders v Vautier* (check absolute entitlement, no unborn or unascertained beneficiaries, agreement on division).
2. If any beneficiary is a minor, incapable, unborn or a mere expectant, use the 1958 Act: identify the s 1(1) category.
3. For each person consented for (except category (d)), show real benefit: financial and, if relevant, educational, social or moral. Consider tax-driven aims and *Weston*.
4. Remember adult beneficiaries must consent personally; the settlor's objection is not decisive (*Goulding*).
5. If beneficiaries are missing: consider a Benjamin order (and MF Global or Alpari orders in insolvency contexts).
