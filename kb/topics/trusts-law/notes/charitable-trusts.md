# Charitable trusts

## Overview
Charitable (public) trusts are purpose trusts that the law favours. There are roughly 169,000 registered charities in England and Wales with income of nearly £80 billion (handout). To be charitable a trust must be (1) for a **charitable purpose** within the Charities Act 2011 s 3(1), (2) for the **public benefit** (s 2(1)(b), s 4), and (3) **exclusively charitable** (s 1(1)(a)). Charities enjoy tax privileges and validity privileges: no beneficiary principle, relaxed certainty of objects, no rule against perpetual duration. The Charities Act 2006 first defined charity in statute and abolished the presumption of public benefit; the Charities Act 2011 consolidated it without changing the law. the materials asked students to discuss "To be charitable, a purpose must always be for the public benefit". The law is England & Wales.

## The law

### 1. Privileges
**Tax** (handout): exemption from income tax on investments and corporation tax; trading profits may be exempt; gifts exempt from inheritance tax; Gift Aid; exemption from capital gains tax, stamp duty and the National Insurance surcharge.
**Validity**:
1. Exempt from the beneficiary principle: valid purpose trusts, enforced by the **Attorney-General** for the Crown (see *Leahy*; private-purpose-trusts.md). The Charity Commission regulates; the Charity Tribunal and High Court supervise. Charities Act 2011 s 115 also lets "any person interested" bring charity proceedings (Hui Jing, below).
2. Relaxed certainty: a gift "for charity" is valid; where the precise purposes are uncertain, the Commission and court can make a **scheme**.
3. Exempt from the rule against perpetual duration (inalienability); see public-policy-limits-on-trusts.md for vesting and accumulation.
4. A benevolent approach to construction ("benignant construction": Lord Hailsham in *IRC v McMullen* [1981] AC 1, 14).

### 2. History and sources
- Preamble to the Charitable Uses Act 1601 (Statute of Elizabeth I): relief of aged, impotent and poor people; sick and maimed soldiers and mariners; schools and scholars; repair of bridges, ports, churches, highways; orphans; houses of correction; marriages of poor maids; young tradesmen; relief of prisoners; aid of poor inhabitants with taxes. Repealed by the Charities Act 1960 but still used as a guide.
- *Income Tax Special Purposes Commissioners v Pemsel* [1891] AC 531 (Lord Macnaghten): four heads: relief of poverty; advancement of education; advancement of religion; other purposes beneficial to the community. Before 2006, public benefit was presumed for the first three heads, not the fourth.
- *Scottish Burial Reform and Cremation Society v Glasgow Corporation* [1968] AC 138, 154 (Lord Wilberforce): purposes must benefit the public "within the intendment of the preamble", meaning the decisions on its scope, which keep the law moving "as new social needs arise or old ones become obsolete or satisfied".
- *Gilmour v Coates* [1949] AC 426, 448–449 (per Lord Simon in the handout): the law of charity has been built up "not logically but empirically".

### 3. Charitable purposes: Charities Act 2011 s 3(1)
(a) prevention or relief of poverty; (b) advancement of education; (c) advancement of religion; (d) advancement of health or saving of lives; (e) citizenship or community development; (f) arts, culture, heritage or science; (g) amateur sport; (h) human rights, conflict resolution or reconciliation, religious or racial harmony, equality and diversity; (i) environmental protection or improvement; (j) relief of those in need because of youth, age, ill-health, disability, financial hardship or other disadvantage; (k) animal welfare; (l) efficiency of the armed forces, police, fire and rescue, or ambulance services; (m) any other purposes recognised under s 5 or the old law, or analogous to or within the spirit of (a)–(l) or purposes so recognised. Terms with an established charity-law meaning keep it (s 3(3)).

**(a) Poverty**
- Not defined; relative. *Re Coulthurst* [1951] Ch 661 (CA, Evershed MR): poverty is not destitution; it covers people who have to "go short", having regard to their status in life. See also *Re Clarke* [1923], *Re Young* [1951], *Re Segelman* [1995], *Re De Carteret* [1933].
- *Re Sanders' WT* [1954] Ch 265: dwellings for "the working classes" not charitable; working class does not connote poverty (Harman J). Contrast *Re Niyazi's WT* [1978] 1 WLR 910: a hostel for "working men" was charitable.
- *Re Gwyon* [1930] 1 Ch 255: clothing for boys in an area, excluding paupers, was not for poverty since affluent boys could apply.
- Charity Commission guidance (2008): people lacking necessities or quasi-necessities for a modest but adequate standard of living; financial hardship can be relieved even if not poor; eligibility for state benefits is irrelevant.

**(b) Education**
- Wide meaning. *IRC v McMullen* [1981] AC 1 (HL): promoting sport in schools and universities was educational; Lord Hailsham (18) on education of the young as "a balanced and systematic process of instruction, training and practice" with spiritual, moral, mental and physical elements.
- Teaching institutions and ancillaries (chairs, schools if non-profit (*Customs and Excise v Bell Concord Educational Trust* [1990] 1 QB 1040), salaries, prizes, a students' union: *AG v Ross* [1986] 1 WLR 252); vocational training (*Re Koettgen's WT* [1954] Ch 252; *Construction Industry Training Board v AG* [1973] Ch 173).
- Research: *Re Shaw* [1957] 1 WLR 729 (increase of knowledge alone not charitable without teaching; a narrow view); *Re Hopkins' WT* [1965] (Wilberforce J: research must be of educational value or pass into the store of educational material); *McGovern v AG* [1982] Ch 321, 352 (Slade J: useful subject, knowledge to be disseminated, public benefit). Research, arts and sport now have their own heads ((d), (f), (g)).

**(c) Religion**
- Old cases required belief in a god (Lord Parker in *Bowman* even suggested one god). *Re South Place Ethical Society* [1980] 1 WLR 1565 (Dillon J: religion concerns man's relation with God, ethics man's relation with man; worship and faith in a god required); Freemasonry not religious (*Free Mason v Holborn Borough Council* [1957] 1 WLR 1080, as cited in the handout: it merely encouraged moral lives).
- **s 3(2)(a)**: religion includes one involving belief in more than one god and one involving no belief in a god, so *South Place* is no longer good law on that point. The Commission's Scientology decision (17 November 1999) held its core practices were not worship. Commission guidance (2008) lists: belief in a supreme being or entity; a relationship shown by worship, reverence or veneration; cogency, cohesion, seriousness and importance; and a beneficial moral or ethical framework.

**(d)–(l) other heads** (with handout examples)
- Health includes prevention or relief of sickness, disease or human suffering (s 3(2)(b)); non-profit hospitals (*Re Resch's WT* [1969] 1 AC 514); alternative therapies may be charitable (*Funnell v Stewart* [1996] 1 WLR 288); saving lives: voluntary fire brigade (*Re Wokingham Fire Brigade Trusts* [1951] Ch 373), RNLI (*Re David* (1889)).
- Citizenship/community development includes regeneration and promoting volunteering and charity efficiency (s 3(2)(c)).
- Arts, culture, heritage, science: *Royal Choral Society v IRC* [1943] 2 All ER 101; *Re Delius* [1957] Ch 299.
- Amateur sport: once not charitable (*Re Nottage* [1895] 2 Ch 649, yacht racing prize); now "sport" means sports or games promoting health by physical or mental skill or exertion (s 3(2)(d)). (Registered community amateur sports clubs cannot be charities: s 6.)
- Human rights etc.: a change from *McGovern* (Amnesty objects non-charitable because political); the Commission recognised human rights promotion in 2005 by analogy with moral improvement.
- Environment: *Re Verrall* [1916] (National Trust).
- Relief of need: includes provision of accommodation or care (s 3(2)(e)); *Joseph Rowntree Memorial Trust Housing Association v AG* [1983] (homes for the elderly on long leases).
- Animal welfare: *Re Wedgwood* [1915] 1 Ch 113 (humane slaughter; elevating humanity); *Tatham v Drummond* (1864) (RSPCA); *Hanchett-Stamford v AG* [2009] Ch 173 (the 2006 Act did not change the law; an organisation needing a change in the law was not charitable).
- Recreational trusts: s 5 (facilities for recreation or leisure in the interests of social welfare, meeting the "basic conditions" of improving conditions of life and either need or availability to the public at large).

### 4. Public benefit
- **s 2(1)(b)**, **s 4**: purposes must be for the public benefit; **s 4(2)**: it is not to be presumed that a purpose of any description is for the public benefit; **s 4(3)**: "public benefit" has its existing charity-law meaning. **s 17**: the Commission must publish guidance.
- **Two aspects** (Commission's *Analysis of the law relating to public benefit*): (1) the **benefit aspect**: identifiable benefit, provable by evidence where not obvious, and outweighing any detriment; (2) the **public aspect**: benefit to the public or a sufficient section of it. Private benefits must be no more than incidental.
- **Benefit and detriment**: *National Anti-Vivisection Society v IRC* [1948] AC 31 (HL): not charitable because political and because harm to medical research outweighed moral benefit. Views of benefit change (cigarettes for hospital patients, once charitable).
- **Section of the public**: potential beneficiaries must not be negligible; restrictions must be legitimate, proportionate, rational and justifiable given the aims.
  - Unjustified restrictions: *IRC v Baddeley* [1955] AC 572 (Viscount Simonds' example of a bridge for Methodists only).
  - Geographic limits acceptable unless tiny (a street).
  - Restrictions based on charitable need fine; personal characteristics depend on the aim (men's health v a village hall).
- **Personal nexus**: *Oppenheim v Tobacco Securities Trust* [1951] AC 297 (HL): education for children of employees and ex-employees of British American Tobacco (110,000) not charitable. Lord Simonds: the class must be not numerically negligible and the distinguishing quality must not depend on a relationship to a particular individual ("propositus"). Lord MacDermott dissented: the personal/impersonal split is arbitrary (railwaymen generally v one railway's staff; nationalised industries); prefer a general survey of the circumstances.
- **Poverty exception**: *Dingle v Turner* [1972] AC 601 (HL): pensions for "poor employees" of a company were charitable; the personal nexus test does not apply to poverty trusts. Lord Cross (obiter) endorsed Lord MacDermott's flexible approach. The "poor relations" cases remain (*Gillam v Taylor* (1873); *AG v Duke of Northumberland* (1877); *Re Scarisbrick's WT* [1951] Ch 622). The Commission accepts that for poverty charities the courts treat public benefit as satisfied by the benefit aspect alone.
- **Fees**: charging does not prevent charitable status unless run for private profit. *Re Resch's WT* (PC): a private hospital charging roughly cost price was charitable; Lord Wilberforce: indirect benefits count, and the poor were not excluded. *R (Independent Schools Council) v Charity Commission* [2011] UKUT 421 (TCC): fee-paying pupils are beneficiaries too; admissions can favour payers where fees are needed; but trustees must provide more than minimal or tokenistic benefit for the poor, the rest being at their discretion; focus on direct benefits related to the charitable purposes. Ways to show benefit to those unable to pay include bursaries, sharing facilities with state schools, free NHS beds, concessionary tickets (Commission list).
- **Incidental private benefit**: *London Hospital Medical College v IRC* [1976] 1 WLR 613 (students' union benefits incidental).
- **Debate on removing the presumption**: Hackney (2008) 124 LQR 347; Harding (2008) 71 MLR 159; Saunders (2007); Rahmatian (2009) 73 Conv 12 (listed in handout).

### 5. Exclusively charitable
- s 1(1)(a): "established for charitable purposes only". Ancillary non-charitable purposes are acceptable (*Funnell v Stewart*; *Re Coxen* [1948] Ch 747: trustees' annual dinner).
- *Chichester Diocesan Fund v Simpson* [1944] AC 341: "charitable or benevolent" objects failed because "benevolent" lacks charity's precision and the words were alternatives (Viscount Simon LC).
- Benignant construction: *Re Best* [1904] 2 Ch 354 ("charitable and benevolent" read conjunctively, valid).
- Severance only exceptionally, where shares are clearly apportioned (*Salusbury v Denton* (1857) 3 K & J 529).

### 6. Political purposes
- Not charitable: promoting political parties (*Bonar Law Memorial Trust v IRC* (1933); *Re Ogden* [1933] Ch 678; *Re Hopkinson* [1949] 1 All ER 346); purposes requiring a change in the law (*National Anti-Vivisection Society*).
- *McGovern v AG* [1982] Ch 321 (Slade J): (1) the court cannot judge whether a change in the law is for the public benefit; (2) it must assume the law is right, or it would usurp the legislature. Amnesty's objects (releasing prisoners of conscience; abolishing torture) were political. The bar extends to changing foreign law and to reversing government policy or administrative decisions at home or abroad.
- Ancillary political activity is permitted (*National Anti-Vivisection Society*); many charities (e.g. Shelter) campaign. See Commission guidance CC9 (*Speaking out*).

## Debate and critique
- **Is the charitable trust special?** Fowles ("The charitable trust: not so special after all?", Trusts & Trustees) welcomes the Court of Appeal's decision in *Lehtimaki v Children's Investment Fund Foundation (UK)* that the court should not control a charity fiduciary's discretion absent actual or threatened breach of duty; this sets historically sound limits on treating charities as exceptional. He notes Warburton's earlier argument against insisting on sui generis treatment, against Lord Macnaghten's "peculiar favour" for charitable trusts in *Pemsel*.
- **Public benefit always required?** (the materials.) Since 2006 there is no presumption (s 4(2)), but the poverty exception (*Dingle*; poor relations) means public benefit can be satisfied by the benefit aspect alone; is that consistent? Is the personal nexus test arbitrary (Lord MacDermott; Lord Cross)? Should public schools keep charitable status (*ISC*)? Scholarship cited in the handout (Hackney, Harding, Saunders, Rahmatian) debates what removing the presumption actually changed.
- **Religion**: Chevalier-Watts ("In defence of religious charitable trusts") defends religious charities' place, including where they engage in commerce, using Australian, New Zealand and Hong Kong cases: commercial activity does not oust religious or charitable status if profits are not for private benefit. Others question how the public benefit of religion can be proved now the presumption has gone (Harding (2008) 71 MLR 159 is listed in the handout), and the Scientology decision shows the limits of the definition.
- **Political purposes**: Slade J's reasons in *McGovern* (no way to judge; respect for Parliament) sit uneasily with the 2006 inclusion of human rights; the line between charitable campaigning and political purposes is managed by guidance.
- **Enforcement**: Hui Jing ("Enforcing charitable trusts: a study on the English necessary interest rule") examines s 115's "any person interested" standing (dating from 1853), which Parliament never defined; its core concern is securing due administration, and it informs the beneficiary-enforcer debate about express trusts.
- **"Rag bag" criticism**: Mulgan and Landry (Demos, 1996): the sector is defined by "a rag bag of outdated rules and definitions", inappropriate tax privileges and paternalistic governance.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the purpose and match it to s 3(1)**: use old case law for meaning (s 3(3); s 3(1)(m)).
2. **Public benefit**: benefit aspect (evidence; net of harm: *Anti-Vivisection*) and public aspect (section of public; personal nexus: *Oppenheim*; unjustified restrictions: *Baddeley*; poverty exception: *Dingle*; fees: *Resch*, *ISC*). No presumption (s 4(2)).
3. **Exclusively charitable?** Watch "or benevolent" (*Chichester*) v "and benevolent" (*Re Best*); ancillary non-charitable elements (*Re Coxen*).
4. **Political?** Changing the law (UK or foreign) or government policy is fatal as a main purpose (*McGovern*); ancillary campaigning is fine.
5. **If not charitable**: test as a private purpose trust (beneficiary principle; *Denley*; anomalous exceptions) or gift to an unincorporated association (private-purpose-trusts.md).
6. **If charitable**: no beneficiary principle; uncertain purposes cured by scheme; perpetuity relaxations.
7. **Traps**: forgetting the 2006 removal of the presumption; applying the personal nexus test to poverty trusts; treating "working class" as poverty; overlooking s 3(2) definitions (religion, sport).
