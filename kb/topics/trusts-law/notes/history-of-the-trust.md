# History of equity and the trust

## Overview
Equity is a distinct body of English law, with its own rules, rights and remedies, which grew up in the Court of Chancery as a corrective to a rigid medieval common law. The trust is equity's most important creation: it began as the medieval "use", survived Henry VIII's attempt to abolish it, and re-emerged in the seventeenth century as the modern trust. Knowing this history explains why legal and equitable title are separate, why equity speaks of "conscience" and acts *in personam*, and why trusts have always been tied to tax planning, land and family wealth. It also supplies essay material on fusion and on the continuing role of "unconscionability".

## The law (historical narrative as taught)

### 1. The rigidity of the medieval common law
1. After 1066, most cases were heard locally (shire and hundred courts, local lords). The Crown kept certain business in royal courts: the Exchequer (taxes), King's Bench (cases concerning the sovereign or great persons) and Common Pleas (ordinary cases better decided by the King, e.g. land). These courts built judge-made law common to all England: the "common law".
2. Claims depended on **writs** issued from Chancery under the royal seal. The King's prerogative included remedying "unjust judgment" and "default of justice".
3. Barons sought to limit royal justice: Magna Carta 1215, the Provisions of Oxford 1258, and the Statute of Westminster II 1285. The last two stopped Chancery issuing novel writs without council approval, so claimants had to fit an existing writ.
4. The result was inflexibility in (a) causes of action, (b) evidence (e.g. only title deeds proved title to land; the defendant often could not be examined) and (c) remedies (damages were usually the only remedy, even where inadequate).

### 2. Petitions to the Chancellor and "conscience"
1. Disappointed litigants petitioned the King by "bill"; from the late twelfth century these were delegated to the Lord Chancellor, a learned cleric and member of the privy council. Petitions became routine by the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries.
2. The Chancellor, "keeper of the king's conscience", allowed "merciful exceptions" to the general law so that the King's conscience stood right before God. Relief therefore turned on the *defendant's* conscience: a defendant could not act unconscionably, e.g. by relying on a common law right or a lack of formality against a vulnerable party.
3. Decrees bound the defendant personally, enforced by imprisonment for contempt. A common form was an **injunction** against enforcing a common law judgment.
4. The Black Death (mid-fourteenth century) swamped the common law courts; the Chancellor took on extra work and by the century's end sat on his own authority at Westminster Hall. Through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries Chancery grew and its decisions became a separate body of authority.

### 3. Conflict with the common law: *The Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615)
- A defendant who had lost at common law, alleging the judgment was obtained by fraud, sought an injunction from Lord Ellesmere LC. Coke CJ indicted him; Ellesmere granted the injunction, arguing it did not overrule the common law but operated *in personam* on the conscience of the successful party.
- In 1616 King James I, advised by Sir Francis Bacon, sided with Chancery: where equity and law conflict, equity prevails. (The slides cite the report as (1615) 21 ER 485; The case law as (1615) 1 Ch Rep 1.)

### 4. From discretion to a body of law
- Early equity did not follow precedent and was attacked as arbitrary. Selden's seventeenth-century jibe: equity varies with the length of "the Chancellor's foot".
- From 1675 (Lord Nottingham, "father of modern equity") until the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 removed the Lord Chancellor's judicial role, Lord Chancellors were lawyers. Precedent and law reports took hold; equity still acted on conscience, but rules determined when conduct was unconscionable, while remedies stayed partly discretionary.
- Lord Eldon in *Gee v Pritchard* (1818) 2 Swan 402, 414 (slides): equity's doctrines should be settled and almost as uniform as the common law; he would regret anything that justified the "Chancellor's foot" reproach.

### 5. A dual system and its breakdown
Equity's jurisdiction covered three situations: (a) a common law claim with no adequate remedy; (b) a common law claim that procedural rules prevented being proved or defended; (c) a claim recognised only in equity.
By the early nineteenth century Chancery was overwhelmed, partly by Industrial Revolution litigation that equity's flexibility attracted. Reforms (Master of the Rolls sitting from 1729, with true concurrent jurisdiction only from 1833; a Vice-Chancellor in 1813; two more in 1841; two Lords Justices in Chancery in 1851) left only seven equity judges by the 1870s. Cases bounced between courts and claimants who chose the wrong court had to start again. Dickens satirised this in *Bleak House* (*Jarndyce v Jarndyce*, an estate consumed by costs). The Court of Chancery Procedure Act 1852 tried to address procedure.

### 6. Fusion: the Judicature Acts 1873–75
- Abolished the separate courts and created the High Court (with a Chancery Division) and a Court of Appeal; all judges administer both law and equity.
- **Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873 s 25(11)** preserved the *Earl of Oxford* rule: where rules of equity and common law conflict on the same matter, equity prevails (see also Supreme Court Act 1981 s 49(1), per the materials).
- Fusion was *administrative*: legal and equitable interests remain jurisprudentially distinct and produce different outcomes, although some academics argue for full assimilation. Equity today pervades land law, estates, company law, remedies (specific performance, injunctions) and doctrines such as promissory estoppel.

### 7. The history of the trust itself
1. **The use**: land conveyed to *feoffees* to the use of a *cestui que use*. Its origins are obscure but it was used by landowners going on crusade, to get round strict succession rules and to evade the Statutes of Mortmain (which barred vesting land in religious orders).
2. The common law recognised only the feoffee's legal title, so a faithless feoffee could not be sued at law. The Chancellor could not alter legal title, but acting *in personam* could order the feoffee to hold for, or convey to, the cestui que use. As this became settled, the cestui que use could be said to have equitable title: the origin of split title. Chancellors also came (for reasons not well understood) to bind the feoffee's successors in title.
3. **Tax avoidance and feudal incidents**: by conveying to feoffees to one's own use, and replacing feoffees as they died, legal title never descended, so feudal death duties and the Crown's rights where an owner died without an heir were avoided. By Henry VIII's reign most land was held to uses.
4. **Statute of Uses** (the handout gives both 1535 and 1536): "executed" the use by vesting legal title in the beneficiary, so A to B to the use of C became a conveyance to C. It mainly affected land; uses of personalty were untouched.
5. **The use upon a use**: once feudal dues declined and tax law moved on, the policy reason disappeared. Draftsmen wrote A to B to the use of C to the use of D. Chancery held the Statute executed the first use but the second took effect (A to C for D). In the seventeenth century Chancellors enforced the second use as a "trust"; draftsmen dropped "unto and for the use of", giving "to C in trust for D". The modern trust was born.

### 8. Summary timeline (handout)
12th c. Chancellor advises the King; 13th c. writs and the use; 14th c. Chancellor relieves common law strictness; 15th c. Court of Chancery grows; 16th c. Chancellors become lawyers, Henry VIII attacks uses; 17th c. equity supreme in conflict, modern trust established; 18th c. trust and mortgage doctrine developed; 19th c. Judicature Acts; 20th c. statutory consolidation and reform; 21st c. international popularity of the trust as a commercial device, including in non-equity jurisdictions.

## Key authorities
- *The Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615): equity's injunction operates in personam; resolved in 1616 in favour of Chancery.
- *Gee v Pritchard* (1818) 2 Swan 402 (Lord Eldon): equity's doctrines should be settled, not change with each judge.
- Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873 s 25(11): equity prevails in conflict.
- Statute of Uses (1535/1536): executed uses of land; circumvented by the use upon a use.
- Constitutional Reform Act 2005: ended the Lord Chancellor's judicial functions.
- Modern invocations of unconscionability noted on the slides: *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26, [2013] 2 AC 108; *Pennington v Waine* [2002] EWCA Civ 227.

## Debate and critique
- **Maitland's verdict** (slides): the trust is the largest and most important exploit of equity and the most distinctive English achievement in jurisprudence. Gierke's reply, "I cannot understand your trust", points to the difficulty civilian lawyers have with split title (see trusts-in-international-context.md).
- **Code and gloss**: Maitland also said the relation of common law and equity is not between two conflicting systems but between "code and supplement", "text and gloss"; equity presupposes the common law and without it would be "a castle in the air".
- **Fusion debate** (slides): "equity purists" versus "equity pragmatists" (Penner is named as a pragmatist). The handout notes some academics favour total assimilation, but equity remains distinct in substance.
- **Maxims as guides, not rules** (Watt, ch 16 in the case_law folder): maxims guide discretion and some function almost as rules (e.g. first in time prevails), but they have no measurable weight and there is no rule for which maxim wins when two conflict. Watt quotes Gardner on their "Delphic quality" and suggests they are as often *employed* to reach desired results as *followed*. Watt's list: equity acts in personam; will not suffer a wrong without a remedy; follows the law; looks to substance not form; will not permit a statute to be used as an instrument of fraud; clean hands; he who seeks equity must do equity; sees as done that which ought to be done; imputes an intention to fulfil an obligation; where the equities are equal the first in time prevails; where equity and law conflict, equity prevails.
  - Watt argues "no wrong without a remedy" does not let equity invent new wrongs: with the old rigidity of forms of action gone, a modern gap (e.g. secret photography of a celebrity) should be filled by the common law, as in *Donoghue v Stevenson*. He criticises *Douglas v Hello! Ltd* for "plugging the gap" with breach of confidence, noting Lord Nicholls' preferred label "misuse of private information".
  - "Equity follows the law": equity smooths the law's edges but does not erode it; its contractual remedies are available only where damages are inadequate.
  - "Statute as an instrument of fraud": upholds a statute's integrity rather than defying Parliament. *Shah v Shah*: a signatory could not deny a deed for lack of proper attestation, though equity would not go so far as to dispense with a signature.
- **Is history useful today?** An opinion piece in the reading (author not stated in the file) argues the use was never a simple precursor of the trust: Tudor lawyers used it to avoid feudal dues and secretly convey land to defraud creditors, so trusts have always been linked to tax planning and creditor avoidance, much like some modern offshore abuse. But the trust survived because of more respectable aims, the preservation of family estates in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and its principles (later the Trustee Act 1925) grew from practical problems rather than abstract design. The piece suggests the trust's legitimacy rests on that long, practical history, which is hard to explain to civil law settlors, given that trusts are private, unregistered and largely unsupervised.

## Applying it to a problem question
History is rarely a problem-question issue on its own, but it is useful:
1. **Explaining why equity intervenes**: frame equitable intervention as preventing unconscionable reliance on legal title or on lack of formality (e.g. secret trusts, Rochefoucauld v Boustead, constitution cases).
2. **Priority conflicts**: where a legal and an equitable rule clash on the same matter, equity prevails (s 25(11) 1873 Act).
3. **Remedial discretion**: equitable remedies are discretionary; consider clean hands and "he who seeks equity must do equity" (relevant to illegality, see public-policy-limits-on-trusts.md).
4. **Essay use**: questions on the nature of the trust, unconscionability, fusion, or whether the trust should exist in civil law systems benefit from this background.
