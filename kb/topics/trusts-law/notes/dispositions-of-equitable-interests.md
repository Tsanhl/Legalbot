# Dispositions of equitable interests: LPA 1925 s 53(1)(c)

## Overview
Once a trust exists, a beneficiary may want to deal with their equitable interest: give it away, sell it, redirect it, or create a sub-trust of it. LPA 1925 s 53(1)(c) requires a "disposition" of a subsisting equitable interest to be *in writing*, whatever the property (land or personalty). The difficult question is what counts as a "disposition". The leading cases (*Grey*, *Oughtred*, *Vandervell*, *Neville v Wilson*) were mostly attempts to avoid ad valorem stamp duty by keeping transactions oral, so the case law is shaped by tax context and is often hard to reconcile. In problem questions, the issue usually appears as: did the beneficiary's oral act transfer their interest, or is it void for lack of writing?

## The law

### 1. The statute
- **s 53(1)(c)**: "a disposition of an equitable interest or trust subsisting at the time of the disposition, must be in writing signed by the person disposing of the same, or by his agent thereunto lawfully authorised in writing or by will."
- **s 53(2)**: the section "does not affect the creation or operation of resulting, implied or constructive trusts."
- Key contrasts with s 53(1)(b) (see formalities-declaring-trusts.md):
  1. The disposition *itself* must be in writing; later written evidence of an oral disposition is not enough.
  2. The writing may be signed by an agent authorised in writing.
  3. It applies to equitable interests in land *and* personalty.
  4. Non-compliance makes the disposition void (handout, discussing *Grey*).
- Origin: Statute of Frauds 1677 s 9.
- **Purpose**: Lord Upjohn in *Vandervell v IRC* [1967] 2 AC 291 at 311: to prevent hidden oral transactions in equitable interests in fraud of those truly entitled, and to make it possible for trustees to know who their beneficiaries are.
- **Electronic signatures**: Watt notes an electronic signature has the same status as a wet-ink one, so typing a name into an email can satisfy a signature requirement; the Law Commission (2019, *Electronic Execution of Documents*) concluded electronic signatures are valid at common law.
- **Tax background**: ad valorem stamp duty was charged on the *instrument* transferring property, by reference to value. No writing meant no duty, so taxpayers tried to pass value orally. Duty is no longer charged on inter vivos gifts, but the cases still define s 53(1)(c).

### 2. What counts as a disposition (handout's seven categories)
1. **Direct assignment to a third party** (gift, contract or will): a disposition; writing required.
2. **Direction to trustees to hold on trust for another**: *Grey v IRC* [1959] 3 All ER 603 (HL). Mr Hunter, sole beneficiary of shares held by trustees, orally and irrevocably directed them to hold the shares in six parcels for his grandchildren; weeks later the trustees executed deeds confirming the trusts. Held: the oral direction was a disposition and void without writing, so the deeds made the disposition and attracted duty. (Watt gives the detail: 18,000 shares, direction on 18 February 1955, written declarations on 25 March.)
3. **Self-declaration as sub-trustee**: depends on what the beneficiary is left with. Upjohn J in *Grey* at first instance ([1958] Ch 375, 382) said a *bare* sub-trustee "disappears from the picture", so the declaration operates as an assignment needing writing; academic opinion is divided. Watt: if the declaration merely passes on the subsisting interest and the declarant has no continuing duties, it is in substance an outright disposition; if it creates a genuinely new equitable interest with active duties, it is a declaration of trust needing no writing. In practice this turns on the facts.
4. **Specifically enforceable contracts to assign an equitable interest**:
   - A specifically enforceable contract makes the vendor a constructive trustee for the purchaser at once ("equity sees as done that which ought to be done"). Land contracts must be in writing (Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 s 2), but contracts for shares need not be. Shares in a private company are not available on the market, so a contract for them (or for a beneficial interest in them) is specifically enforceable.
   - **Oughtred v IRC** [1960] AC 206 (HL, 3:2): trustees held 200,000 private company shares for Mrs Oughtred for life, remainder to her son Peter. They agreed orally that Peter would give her his remainder in exchange for her own shares (the handout says 72,000 and 72,700 in different places; Watt gives 72,700). Formal documents followed, including the trustees' transfer of the 200,000 shares to her. She argued Peter's interest had already passed to her under a constructive trust (exempt by s 53(2)), so the later transfer passed nothing of value. The majority held duty was payable on a proper reading of the Stamp Act 1891 whether or not the interest had passed orally, and did not decide the s 53(1)(c) point. Watt explains the majority's analogy with land sales, where the completing deed has always been stampable though a constructive trust arises on contract.
   - **Lord Radcliffe (dissent, 227)**: by the oral agreement Peter became a trustee "sub modo" of his reversion for his mother; s 53(2) meant s 53(1) did not stop that trusteeship arising by operation of law.
   - **Neville v Wilson** [1997] Ch 144 (CA): shareholders of J Neville Ltd orally agreed, on an informal liquidation, to divide its equitable interest in shares of Universal Engineering Ltd among themselves. Held: each shareholder's agreement created a constructive trust for the others; s 53(2) applies, so s 53(1)(c) did not bar it. Nourse LJ (158) could find no convincing reason to deny s 53(2) its natural effect, and noted that restricting general words would invite fine distinctions. *Oughtred* can be distinguished because there the oral agreement was followed by a formal written transfer.
   - **LA Micro Group (UK) Ltd v LA Micro Group Inc** [2023] EWCA Civ 214: an oral agreement (for consideration) failed s 53(1)(c), but the CA held a vendor–purchaser constructive trust arose under s 53(2) even though the intended recipient of the beneficial interest was the head trustee itself. Nugee LJ: A holds on trust for B; B contracts to pass his interest to A; B holds it on constructive trust for A; once A can call for completion, B drops out and A holds the whole legal and beneficial interest.
5. **Transfer of legal and equitable title together**: **Vandervell v IRC** [1967] 2 AC 291 (HL). Mr Vandervell, beneficiary of shares held by his bank as bare trustee, orally directed the bank to transfer the shares to the Royal College of Surgeons (to endow a chair via dividends), subject to an option to repurchase. Held: where the beneficiary directs the trustee to transfer the legal title and intends the beneficial interest to go with it, s 53(1)(c) does not apply; it is concerned with dispositions of the equitable interest separately from the legal title. (What happened to the option is a resulting trusts issue: see resulting-trusts.md.)
6. **Nomination under a pension or life policy**: not a disposition; no writing (*Gold v Hill* [1999] 1 FLR 54).
7. **Disclaimer or extinguishment**: disclaimer operates by avoidance, not disposition (*Re Paradise Motor Co Ltd* [1968] 2 All ER 625, CA); extinguishment by the bona fide purchaser rule needs no writing either.
- Watt adds: dispositions as part of an arrangement under the Variation of Trusts Act 1958 fall outside s 53(1)(c) (see variation-of-trusts.md); and, arguably but not established, transfers of units in pooled cryptocurrency held by a trustee.

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 s 53(1)(c), s 53(2); LP(MP)A 1989 s 2.
- *Grey v IRC* [1959] 3 All ER 603 (HL): oral direction to trustees is a disposition; void without writing.
- *Oughtred v IRC* [1960] AC 206 (HL): stamp duty payable on the later transfer; Lord Radcliffe's dissent on constructive trusts and s 53(2).
- *Vandervell v IRC* [1967] 2 AC 291 (HL): s 53(1)(c) does not catch a transfer of legal title carrying the beneficial interest with it; Lord Upjohn on the purpose of the section.
- *Neville v Wilson* [1997] Ch 144 (CA): specifically enforceable oral contract passes the equitable interest by constructive trust.
- *LA Micro Group (UK) Ltd v LA Micro Group Inc* [2023] EWCA Civ 214: vendor–purchaser constructive trust even where the purchaser is the head trustee.
- *Gold v Hill* [1999] 1 FLR 54; *Re Paradise Motor Co* [1968] 2 All ER 625.

## Debate and critique
- **Coherence of the case law**: Green ("Grey, Oughtred and Vandervell: A Contextual Reappraisal" (1984) 47 MLR 385) argues the cases arose from tax disputes and a family quarrel, so the judges decided the specific fiscal issues without answering "what is the ambit of s 53(1)(c)?". He sets out to dismantle their internal reasoning, expose inconsistencies and build a coherent account of which transactions are caught.
- **Grey and Vandervell side by side**: in both, a sole beneficiary orally instructs a bare trustee. In *Grey* the instruction (to hold for others) is a void disposition; in *Vandervell* the instruction (to transfer legal title outright) is effective. The difference rests on whether the equitable interest is dealt with separately from legal title, which serves the purpose Lord Upjohn identified (trustees know whom they hold for) but looks formalistic.
- **Constructive trusts swallowing s 53(1)(c)**: if every specifically enforceable oral contract passes the equitable interest under s 53(2) (*Neville v Wilson*), the writing requirement may be emptied of content for private company shares. Watt observes that when the Revenue resists avoidance of s 53(1)(c) it usually wins (the Revenue was not a party in *Neville*), and that Mrs Oughtred's mistake was to follow her oral deal with formal documents.
- **Sub-trusts**: whether a beneficiary who declares a bare sub-trust "drops out" (Upjohn J in *Grey*) is unsettled. In *LA Micro*, Nam and Yean (case note, Trusts & Trustees 2023) argue the circular structure (A on trust for B, B on constructive trust for A) is conceptually untenable and that the outcome is better seen as a sui generis equitable order than a true sub-trust; the case stretches the "elasticity" Maitland praised to breaking point.
- **Is the rule still needed?** With stamp duty on gifts gone, the policy behind s 53(1)(c) is Lord Upjohn's anti-fraud and trustee-protection rationale; whether the case law serves it is a good essay point.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there a subsisting equitable interest?** If the transaction *creates* a trust (a declaration), go to s 53(1)(b) (land) or no formality (personalty). If it deals with an *existing* beneficial interest, consider s 53(1)(c).
2. **Classify the transaction**:
   - outright assignment to X: writing needed;
   - direction to trustees to hold for X (*Grey*): writing needed;
   - direction to trustees to transfer legal title to X with the beneficial interest (*Vandervell*): no writing;
   - declaration of sub-trust: bare (writing probably needed) or active duties (no writing)?
   - contract to assign: is it specifically enforceable (unique property, e.g. private company shares; land needs s 2 LP(MP)A writing)? If so, a constructive trust may pass the interest without writing (*Neville v Wilson*; Lord Radcliffe in *Oughtred*; *LA Micro*);
   - nomination under pension or policy (*Gold v Hill*) or disclaimer (*Re Paradise Motor*): no writing.
3. **Was there signed writing by the disponor or their written-authorised agent?** Check timing: the writing must be the disposition, not later evidence of it. Emails can count.
4. **Consequence of failure**: the beneficial interest stays with the original beneficiary (e.g. *Grey*: the later deeds were the effective disposition).
5. **Traps**: applying s 53(1)(b)'s "manifested and proved" rule to s 53(1)(c); forgetting s 53(1)(c) applies to personalty; assuming *Oughtred* decided the constructive trust point (only the dissent did); ignoring whether a contract is specifically enforceable; overlooking the knock-on resulting trust issue in Vandervell-type facts.
