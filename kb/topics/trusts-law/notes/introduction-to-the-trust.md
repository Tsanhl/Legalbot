# Introduction to the trust

## Overview
A trust exists where the person who holds legal title to property (the trustee) must hold and manage it for the benefit of someone else (the beneficiary, or *cestui que trust*). Legal and equitable (beneficial) title are split between different people. Understanding this split, and how the trust differs from gifts, debts, bailment, contract, companies and agency, is the foundation for every later topic in the module (creation, certainties, implied trusts, duties, breach and remedies). The syllabus stresses that the trust is unusually flexible: it is used in family, commercial, tax, inheritance, land, financial and charity contexts, from pensions to the care of pets after death (England & Wales law throughout).

## The law

### 1. Ownership and property
- "Ownership" carries a normative claim (who *ought* to have the thing). Common law and equity sometimes answer that question differently, each using its own rules to allocate property rights. The trust is equity's central tool for doing so.
- Property law is mainly about private rights to things arising from dealings between parties (corrective justice), not social justice as such. "Things" include land, physical objects and rights capable of being controlled to the exclusion of others.
- Equity's function is to temper the injustice that follows from unconscionable reliance on legal rights.

### 2. Definitions used on the course
- **Penner**: a trust exists when a fund of property is held by a legal owner "on trust" for a beneficiary; the legal owner has all the legal powers to deal with the property but must use them entirely for the beneficiary, who has the benefit or enjoyment.
- **Gardner**: property is vested in a trustee who is under legally recognised obligations, at least some proprietary, to handle it in a certain way and to the exclusion of any personal interest; the obligations may be consciously created by the previous owner (settlor) or arise because of other legally significant circumstances.
- Both definitions stress that the trustee is *obliged* to perform the trust. The obligations are mandatory: while they remain trustee they must perform, and a failure lets the beneficiary sue for breach of trust.

### 3. Legal title and equitable title
- **Legal title** is what we normally call ownership: the holder can treat the thing as theirs, use it up, transfer it or take income. Legal title usually passes on transfer plus intention to part with ownership and acceptance.
- **Equitable (beneficial) title** is the right to the benefits of property (use, income). It exists only where titles are split. An absolute owner (e.g. someone who buys a chocolate bar) has no separate equitable title.
- Equity regards the beneficiary as the true owner. Neither trustee nor beneficiary is an absolute owner: each lacks one aspect of full ownership.
- **Limit on equitable ownership**: an equitable interest does not bind a bona fide purchaser of the legal title for value without notice ("equity's darling"). The legal owner can exclude the whole world; the equitable owner can exclude the world minus that purchaser.

### 4. Equity, the Chancery and fusion (short version; see history note)
- Maitland: equity is the body of rules which, but for the Judicature Acts, would be administered only by courts of equity.
- Equity grew from the King's prerogative exercised through the Chancellor ("keeper of the king's conscience") to correct unconscionable judgments; it acted *in personam*, used maxims rather than detailed rules, and became the Court of Chancery.
- *Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615) 1 Ch Rep 1: the rivalry between Chancery and the common law courts, settled in 1616 by King James in favour of Chancery ("where equity and law conflict, equity prevails").
- Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873 s 25(11) (see also Supreme Court Act 1981 s 49(1)): where rules of equity and common law conflict on the same matter, equity prevails. The Acts fused *administration*, but legal and equitable interests remain distinct and produce different outcomes. Trust cases are now heard in the Chancery Division of the High Court.

### 5. Proprietary versus obligational features
- A **personal right** (in personam) binds only the parties who created it and does not attach to a thing (e.g. a contract buyer cannot claim the car from the seller's brother).
- A **proprietary right** (in rem) attaches to a thing and can bind third parties into whose hands the thing passes (e.g. you can recover your laptop from whoever receives it).
- The trust has both: contractual-looking features (settlor and trustee; the trustee's obligations) and proprietary features (beneficiary can recover property transferred to a non-purchaser, e.g. trust money wrongfully gifted to the trustee's niece). But the beneficiary does not control the property as a legal owner would, and the bona fide purchaser rule limits the reach of their right. The settlor keeps no rights against the trustee once the trust is established.
- Most commentators treat the trust as a property arrangement, but the point is contested.

### 6. The elements of a trust
1. **Settlor** – absolute owner who transfers property to trustees, or declares themselves trustee. Called "testator" where the trust is created by will. Not part of the arrangement unless also trustee or beneficiary.
2. **Trust property** – any asset capable of being owned: land, chattels, choses in action (e.g. a bank balance, an interest in insurance proceeds). May be specific property or a fund of changing assets.
3. **Trust instrument** – sets the terms; a unilateral act of the settlor (trustee and beneficiary do not draft it).
4. **Trustee** – holds legal title; can do anything the common law allows an owner to do, but is restricted by the trust instrument and equity. May be the settlor (self-declaration).
5. **Beneficiary** – holds beneficial title as the instrument specifies; may be the settlor; some (not all) beneficiaries may also be trustees.

### 7. The trust distinguished from other arrangements
| Arrangement | Key difference from a trust |
|---|---|
| Absolute gift | Needs only intention, delivery, acceptance; no split of legal and beneficial title. (Express trusts are, however, a species of gratuitous disposition.) |
| Debt / chose in action (e.g. bank account) | A bank may use the money for itself; a trustee may not. If trust property is destroyed without the trustee's fault, the beneficiary's rights end, but a debt survives. If a debtor goes bankrupt the claim may be worthless, whereas beneficiaries can claim their property from the trustee's hands. "Owning versus owing." |
| Bailment | Transfers possession only, not legal or equitable title; a purchaser from a bailee without notice does not get legal title, but a purchaser from a trustee does. |
| Contract | Negotiated, generally enforceable only between the parties (subject to the Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999); trusts can arise on death or by operation of law and give a third party (the beneficiary) enforcement rights. A contract to transfer does not usually change beneficial ownership (but note specifically enforceable contracts). |
| Corporation | Has legal personality; owns its own assets while shareholders own shares, so there is no split of title in the same thing. Directors are not personally responsible to shareholders as trustees are to beneficiaries. |
| Agency | Depends on intention. A trustee must keep property separate, use it only for the beneficiary and account; an agent may treat property as his own and is contractually bound to return it. A requirement to keep money separate suggests, but does not alone prove, a trust. |

### 8. Types of trust
- **Public (charitable) trusts**: for purposes the law treats as benefiting society; no individual beneficiaries; enforced by the Charity Commission (see charitable-trusts.md).
- **Private trusts**: for individuals or groups; with small exceptions they need identifiable beneficiaries who can enforce them (see private-purpose-trusts.md).
  - **Express trusts** (intentionally created, inter vivos or by will, or required by statute):
    - *Fixed trusts*: beneficiaries have fixed shares; trustees must distribute accordingly; each beneficiary can claim their share once of age (subject to the terms). Example: house on trust for four children in equal shares.
    - *Discretionary trusts*: trustees must distribute among a class but choose how. A beneficiary can compel performance but cannot claim any particular share. Example: £100,000 to be distributed among nephews and nieces as trustees think fit.
  - **Implied trusts** (arise by operation of law; also called "imputed"):
    - *Resulting trusts*: the beneficial interest "results back", typically where someone contributes to the purchase price without getting title, or an express trust fails (see resulting-trusts.md).
    - *Constructive trusts*: imposed where the legal owner's conscience is affected so as to prevent denial of another's rights, e.g. a family home in one partner's name, or unauthorised fiduciary profits. English law is said not to recognise a "remedial" constructive trust (debated): courts cannot impose a trust merely because it would be fair (see constructive-trusts.md).

## Key authorities
- *Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615) 1 Ch Rep 1: conflict between Chancery and common law; resolved in 1616 in favour of equity.
- Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1873 s 25(11); Supreme Court Act 1981 s 49(1): equity prevails in conflict.
- Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999: mentioned as the exception to privity when contrasting contract and trust.
- Lecture illustration (slides): Jack (£2) and Linda (£8) pool cash; Jack buys a £10 bottle of wine. At law Jack owns it; in equity Jack and Linda own it in proportion to their contributions. This anticipates the contribution-to-purchase-price situation in which, per the handout, resulting trusts commonly arise.

## Debate and critique
- **Property or obligation?** The classic controversy: is the beneficiary's equitable interest a property right in the trust assets, or merely a personal right against the trustee? Jaffey notes the personal-right view is associated with Maitland and the property view with Scott.
- **Jaffey, "Explaining the trust" (2015) LQR**: argues the express trust has two *dimensions* operating in tandem, not a hybrid right. (1) A *property dimension*, arising from the settlor's declaration, allocates control to the trustee and benefit to beneficiaries; proprietary claims to trust property and traceable proceeds belong here. (2) A *contract dimension*, arising from the trustee's undertaking (agreement in a broad sense, not common law contract), grounds the duty to administer; claims for breach of trust and accessory liability belong here. A recipient of misapplied property is subject to the proprietary claim but has undertaken nothing; if they knew or ought to have known of the improper transfer, liability is really negligence-based, which Jaffey says underlies knowing receipt. On this view the law/equity division and "unconscionability" explain the trust's history but not its modern principled structure. A trust with only the contract dimension may resemble civil law trust-like devices; it lacks protection on trustee insolvency.
- **Hansmann and Mattei, "The Functions of Trust Law" (1998) 73 NYU L Rev 434**: asks whether contract and agency could replicate the trust. They argue the trust's key contribution is not ordering relations among settlor, trustee and beneficiary (default rules contract could supply) but ordering relations with *third parties*, especially *asset partitioning*: a discrete pool of assets shielded for separate treatment by creditors. Its role is therefore property-like. Corporate law does much the same partitioning, so they ask whether trust and corporation might converge into a single form.
- **Handbook framing**: the course invites critique of trusts as a tool both for private wealth management (e.g. offshore structures; see the Pandora papers activity on the week 1 slides) and for doing good, and of the tensions and contradictions within trusts law.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the parties**: who is settlor/testator, who holds legal title (trustee), who is meant to benefit?
2. **Is there a split of title at all?** If the recipient was meant to take absolutely, it is a gift, not a trust. If someone merely owes money, it is a debt (owning v owing). If only possession passed, consider bailment; if a party acts for another under contract, consider agency.
3. **Which kind of trust?** Express (fixed or discretionary) created intentionally, or implied (resulting/constructive) arising by law? Public (charitable) or private?
4. **What rights does the beneficiary have?** Personal right to have the trust performed; proprietary right in the assets that can follow them into third parties' hands, except against a bona fide purchaser for value without notice.
5. **Insolvency matters**: if the trustee/debtor is insolvent, a trust (property in the trustee's hands belongs in equity to the beneficiary) gives priority over unsecured creditors; a mere debt does not (see quistclose-trusts.md).
6. **Traps**: assuming the settlor keeps rights to enforce (they generally do not once the trust is constituted); treating a discretionary beneficiary as entitled to a share; forgetting that a separation-of-funds term does not by itself create a trust.
