# Knowing receipt

## Overview
Knowing receipt is a personal claim against a third party who received trust property that was transferred in breach of trust or fiduciary duty and who had the requisite knowledge. It sits alongside dishonest assistance as a form of third-party (stranger) liability. It is fault-based, not strict: the recipient must have known something. Liability can be explained either as reversing an unjust enrichment or as responding to a recipient whose conscience is affected. The recipient must account for the value of what was received even if they no longer hold it, and may have to give up profits made from it. The hard part is the meaning of "knowledge", which remains unsettled.

## The law
**1. Requirements (*El Ajou v Dollar Land Holdings* [1994] BCC 143):**
1. a disposal of assets in breach of trust or fiduciary duty;
2. beneficial receipt of assets traceable as representing the claimant's assets;
3. knowledge by the defendant that the assets are traceable to a breach.

**2. Beneficial receipt.** The recipient must have received the property "for his own use and benefit" (Millett J in *Agip (Africa) Ltd v Jackson* [1990] Ch 265, 292). A mere agent who receives ministerially for another is not liable. For banks: paying or collecting money for a customer is acting as agent, but if the collecting bank uses the money to reduce or discharge the customer's overdraft it receives for its own benefit (*Agip*). "Traceable" does not require the original property: proceeds and substitutes suffice. But *Byers v Saudi National Bank* [2023] UKSC 51 requires that the beneficiary's rights in the assets must have survived the transfer, so that if the defendant had kept them the beneficiary would have had a proprietary interest in them.

**3. Timing of knowledge.** A stranger is liable from receipt if they then knew the assets were traceable to a breach. If they did not know at receipt but later learn of the breach and then retain or deal with the assets inconsistently with the trust, they are liable from the moment of knowledge. Never learning of the breach means no liability.

**4. Knowledge versus notice.** Knowledge here is conceptually distinct from notice for the "bona fide purchaser for value without notice" defence. Notice decides whether a proprietary claim lies against the asset; knowledge decides whether the stranger is at fault enough to be personally liable to account after the asset has gone. A purchaser with notice might therefore face a proprietary claim but not be a constructive trustee personally liable.

**5. Meaning of knowledge: three phases.**
- *Pre-Akindele.* Peter Gibson J in *Baden Delvaux and Lecuit v Société Générale* [1983] BCLC 325 listed five degrees: (1) actual knowledge; (2) wilfully shutting one's eyes to the obvious; (3) wilfully and recklessly failing to make inquiries an honest and reasonable person would make; (4) knowledge of circumstances which would indicate the facts to an honest and reasonable person; (5) knowledge of circumstances which would put an honest and reasonable person on inquiry. Categories 2 and 3 are treated as actual knowledge; 4 and 5 as constructive. *Re Montagu's Settlement Trusts* [1987] Ch 264 (Megarry V-C): knowledge includes at least types (ii) and (iii); whether (iv) and (v) suffice is "at best doubtful" since carelessness does not normally amount to want of probity (facts: the 10th Duke of Manchester received chattels that the trustees had forgotten should have been selected into a settlement). In commercial transactions only categories (1)-(3) were enough (*Eagle Trust plc v SBC Securities* [1993] 1 WLR 484; also *Cowan de Groot Properties v Eagle Trust* [1991]), leaving scope for constructive knowledge in non-commercial cases. Scott LJ in *Polly Peck v Nadir (No 2)* [1992] warned the Baden categories are not rigid, and the real question is whether the transaction should have raised a suspicion of a breach; Millett J in *El Ajou* added the test is whether it would do so for an honest and reasonable person.
- *Akindele.* *BCCI v Akindele* [2001] Ch 437: Akindele invested $10m under an arrangement guaranteeing a 15% return and received about $16.7m on termination, not knowing it was part of a fraudulent scheme by BCCI management (the first instance judge found no dishonesty). The Court of Appeal (Nourse LJ) held dishonesty is not required and there is a single test: "the recipient's state of knowledge should be such as to make it unconscionable for him to retain the benefit of the receipt". No categorisation is needed. Akindele's knowledge fell short of that.
- *Post-Akindele.* Some courts blend the two. In *Armstrong DLW GmbH v Winnington Networks* [2012] EWHC 10 (Ch) (commercial context), Baden types (1)-(3) make receipt unconscionable (the possibility of impropriety suffices); types (4) and (5) do so only if, on the facts actually known to the defendant, a reasonable person would have appreciated the transfer was probably in breach or would have made inquiries that revealed the probability. *Arthur v Attorney-General of the Turks and Caicos Islands* [2012] UKPC 30: knowledge means not merely notice but such knowledge as makes the recipient's conduct unconscionable and gives rise to equitable fraud.

## Key authorities
- *El Ajou* (three requirements); *Agip v Jackson* (beneficial receipt, banks); *Byers v Saudi National Bank* [2023] UKSC 51 (surviving proprietary rights).
- *Baden Delvaux* (five categories); *Re Montagu*; *Eagle Trust*; *Polly Peck (No 2)*.
- *BCCI v Akindele* (unconscionability); *Armstrong v Winnington*; *Arthur v AG of Turks and Caicos*.

## Debate and critique
- Constructive versus actual knowledge: whether negligence-type states (Baden 4 and 5) should attract personal liability, especially for commercial parties who "should not continually have to look over their shoulders" (*Eagle Trust*).
- Strict liability: some judges and writers argue liability for receipt in breach of fiduciary duty should be strict, subject to change of position, aligning equity with the common law; this clashes with unconscionability as the basis of liability.
- *Akindele*'s "unconscionability" test is flexible but vague; later cases blend it with Baden, and the discussion concludes the law "remains unsettled".
- The unjust-enrichment explanation (reverse the enrichment) versus the conscience explanation (fault-based).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was there a breach of trust or fiduciary duty and a transfer of assets?
2. Can the claimant trace the assets into the defendant's hands (and do the claimant's rights survive, per *Byers*)?
3. Was the receipt beneficial (own use, e.g. a bank reducing an overdraft) or only as agent?
4. Knowledge: what did the defendant know, and when? Apply *Akindele* unconscionability; use Baden categories as guidance; consider commercial versus non-commercial setting (*Armstrong*).
5. Remedy: personal liability to account, including profits; contrast a proprietary claim where notice may be enough.
6. Trap: do not require dishonesty (contrast dishonest assistance); do not conflate notice and knowledge.
