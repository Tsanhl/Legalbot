# Public policy limits on trusts

## Overview
A trust may be properly declared, certain and constituted, yet still be invalid, wholly or in part, because it offends public policy. The case law groups the limits as: uncertain or repugnant conditions; trusts that aim to defeat creditors or others; sham and illusory trusts; restraints on marriage and religion; and trusts linked to an illegal purpose (*Tinsley v Milligan*, *Patel v Mirza*). The rules against perpetuity, inalienability and accumulation are also public policy limits (summarised from Watt below; since they matter for purpose trusts and charities). These limits apply to express, resulting and constructive trusts. Usually only the offending *condition* fails and the trust survives; if the whole trust fails, the property returns to the settlor (or the testator's residuary estate).

## The law

### 1. Conditions precedent, conditions subsequent and determinable interests
1. **Condition precedent**: governs *when an interest begins* (e.g. a beneficiary receives an interest once they complete a law degree).
2. **Condition subsequent**: governs *when an existing interest ends* (e.g. the interest reverts to the settlor if the beneficiary fails to get a law degree).
3. **Uncertainty**: both are void if too uncertain, and the test is **stricter for conditions subsequent** (*Re Tuck's Settlement Trusts* [1978] 1 All ER 1047).
4. **Effect of a void condition**:
   - void condition precedent: the gift fails if the property is realty, but takes effect free of the condition if personalty;
   - void condition subsequent: the beneficiary keeps the interest free of the condition.
5. **Restraint on alienation**: a condition subsequent that tries to limit the owner's future dispositions is void, and the owner takes free of it. But a **determinable interest** with the same practical effect is valid. The difference is drafting: "shall terminate if the beneficiary seeks to..." (condition subsequent) versus "until the beneficiary seeks to..." (determinable). E.g. an interest determining on the beneficiary's bankruptcy and resettling the property on new trusts is valid.

### 2. Trusts that aim to defeat others' claims
- **Protective trusts** use determinable interests to protect the *beneficiary's* property from the beneficiary's creditors. Watt: the principal beneficiary has a life interest until a "determining event" (typically bankruptcy or any act that would send income to someone else); thereafter the trustees hold on discretionary trusts for a class that often includes the principal beneficiary. Limits (Watt): (a) a life interest "until bankruptcy" is valid but a condition *forfeiting* it on bankruptcy is void; (b) a life interest for the *settlor* until the settlor's bankruptcy is void against his trustee in bankruptcy; (c) the terms are presumed to be those in Trustee Act 1925 s 33 unless the instrument says otherwise. Watt calls the until/forfeit distinction "a fine one, to say the least".
- **Settlor's own creditors**: determinable interests cannot protect the settlor's own property. Under insolvency legislation the court can set aside gratuitous transactions or transactions at an undervalue entered into to defeat creditors and restore the previous position (handout). Watt: under the Insolvency Act 1986 a voluntary declaration of trust or other transaction at an undervalue is voidable, whether or not the settlor was insolvent, if a substantial purpose was to prejudice creditors; purpose is decisive (e.g. a transfer of the home to a wife to persuade her not to divorce was not caught). Watt also summarises the bankruptcy time limits for preferences (six months; two years for connected persons) and undervalue transfers (two years; up to five years in some circumstances). *Re Butterworth*: a baker settled his property before starting a risky grocery business; the settlement was set aside (Jessel MR: a man cannot settle everything so that if he fails "they [the creditors] will bear the loss").
- Similar powers exist on divorce (not covered in detail on the course).

### 3. Sham and illusory trusts
- **Sham**: the parties did not intend a trust in substance (a question of subjective intention). **Illusory**: the arrangement itself, on its terms, does not amount to a trust because the settlor keeps so much control (a question of construction). Russell and Graham ("Illusory Trusts", editorial, citing *Lewin*) stress the two are distinct and that the illusory test avoids the evidential problems of proving a sham.
- *JSC Mezhdunarodniy Promyshlenniy Bank v Pugachev* [2017] EWHC 2426 (Ch): five discretionary trusts settled by Mr Pugachev were in substance bare trusts for him; as protector he had personal (non-fiduciary) powers, including removing trustees, and was also a discretionary beneficiary. Watt notes the judge preferred to identify "the true effect of the trusts" rather than use sham/illusory labels, which helps claimants because no intention to deceive need be proved.
- *Erlam v Rahman* (Watt): a wife's unregistered declaration of trust claiming 74% of land, prepared "to be available, if needed" to shield the husband's assets from costs claimants, was a sham.
- Family courts have struck down (especially offshore) trusts as shams to make funds available on divorce; Watt notes a judicial caution that the trust must be judged by its governing law.

### 4. Restraint of marriage
- A trust that forfeits an interest if the beneficiary marries is contrary to public policy, though restraints on marrying particular persons or classes have been upheld in the past (Watt gives examples, noting they may now offend discrimination law).
- A gift *until* marriage is valid (*Re Lovell* [1920] 1 Ch 122). Watt: restraint of *remarriage* is valid.
- Conditions that could encourage separation or divorce are void (*Re Johnson's Will Trust* [1967] Ch 387: more income if the beneficiary left her husband). Watt adds that clauses encouraging separation of parent and child are also void.

### 5. Religion and discrimination
- *Blathwayt v Cawley* [1976] AC 397 (HL): a condition ending a benefit if the beneficiary became a Roman Catholic was upheld; the predecessor of the Equality Act did not apply to private dispositions under an express trust. Lord Wilberforce: "discrimination is not the same thing as choice". Lord Cross: it is not against public policy for an adherent of one religion to prefer co-religionists in disposing of property.
- Charitable (public) trusts face stricter rules; the courts and Charity Commission try to keep the charitable intention while removing offensive discriminatory terms (Watt).
- The handout doubts that clauses based on race or sexual orientation would be enforced today, because the court is itself a public authority, so such conditions may be practically unenforceable.
- Watt contrasts the Canadian *Re Murley* (inheritance conditional on staying in an orthodox Christian denomination struck down) and notes the ECHR cuts both ways: non-discrimination versus the right to peaceful enjoyment of possessions (A1P1).

### 6. Trusts linked to an illegal purpose
Where parties hide ownership for an illegal purpose (e.g. putting property in one name to commit benefit fraud or defeat creditors), can one later claim a beneficial interest under a resulting or constructive trust?
- **Clean hands**: "he who comes to equity must come with clean hands" (the minority, Lord Goff, in *Tinsley v Milligan* [1993] 3 All ER 65).
- **Tinsley v Milligan** (majority, Lord Browne-Wilkinson): the **reliance principle** (the "Bowmakers rule"): a claimant succeeds if she need not rely on the illegality to prove her claim. Milligan could rely on the presumption of a resulting trust arising from her contribution without pleading the fraud. Both sides rejected the Court of Appeal's "public conscience" test as too discretionary for property rights. Lord Goff would have let the estate "lie where it falls".
- **Arbitrariness**: the reliance test turned on presumptions. Watt: in *Tinker v Tinker* a husband who put the house in his wife's name to defeat trade creditors faced the **presumption of advancement** (handout: father to child, husband to wife transfers presumed gifts) and could rebut it only by relying on his illegal purpose, which *Tinsley* forbade; he lost the house.
- **Patel v Mirza [2016] UKSC 42** (nine Justices): Patel paid £620,000 to Mirza to bet on RBS shares using insider information; the scheme never happened and Mirza refused to repay. All nine allowed recovery. The majority (Lord Toulson) held the reliance principle **no longer represents the law**. Whether allowing a tainted claim would harm the integrity of the legal system requires considering:
  1. the underlying purpose of the prohibition transgressed;
  2. any other relevant public policies that denying the claim may affect;
  3. the possibility of overkill, applying the law with a due sense of proportionality (Virgo notes factors such as seriousness of the conduct, its centrality to the contract, and disparity of culpability; the list is not closed).
- **Withdrawal (locus poenitentiae)**: the handout lists the "withdrawal exception" (*Tribe v Tribe* [1996] Ch 107) and says *Patel* has made it redundant. Watt explains the background: parties could traditionally repent of an unperformed illegal plan (*locus poenitentiae*) and be restored, but sincere repentance was thought necessary. Watt: genuine repentance is no longer needed.
- After *Patel*, recovery according to contractual and property entitlement is the default unless specific policy reasons outweigh it; *Stoffel & Co v Grondona* (Watt) applied *Patel* to let a client sue negligent solicitors despite mortgage misrepresentations to a lender. *O'Kelly v Davies* (CA) applied a similar approach to common intention constructive trusts (decided under the *Tinsley* reliance test, per Watt).

### 7. The rules against perpetuity (Watt, ch 6.3)
1. **Remoteness of vesting**: a contingent interest is void if it might vest outside the perpetuity period (traditionally lives in being plus 21 years). The Perpetuities and Accumulations Act 2009, for trusts taking effect on or after 6 April 2010, sets a single 125-year period with "wait and see", confines the rule to successive interests and powers of appointment, and removes it from options, pre-emption rights and pension schemes. Trustees of older trusts may fix a 100-year period by deed if lives cannot practicably be traced. Three regimes (common law, 1964 Act, 2009 Act) now run in parallel.
2. **Inalienability of capital**: a non-charitable trust is void if capital may be tied up beyond lives in being plus 21 years (the statutory 80-year period may be adopted for trusts for beneficiaries). Crucial for private purpose trusts (see private-purpose-trusts.md). Not reformed by the 2009 Act.
3. **Excessive accumulation**: from *Thellusson v Woodford* and the Accumulations Act 1800 to LPA 1925 s 164; the 2009 Act s 13 removed limits for non-charitable trusts created on or after 6 April 2010; s 14 keeps a 21-year limit for charities.
- Charities: vesting must occur in charity within the period, but a gift over from one charity to another may vest later; inalienability does not apply to exclusively charitable capital.

## Key authorities
- *Re Tuck's Settlement Trusts* [1978] 1 All ER 1047: stricter certainty test for conditions subsequent.
- *Re Lovell* [1920] 1 Ch 122; *Re Johnson's Will Trust* [1967] Ch 387: marriage and divorce conditions.
- *Blathwayt v Cawley* [1976] AC 397: religious condition upheld in a private trust.
- *JSC Mezhdunarodniy Promyshlenniy Bank v Pugachev* [2017] EWHC 2426 (Ch): trusts held in substance bare trusts for the settlor.
- *Tinsley v Milligan* [1993] 3 All ER 65: reliance principle (now overruled); Lord Goff's clean hands dissent.
- *Tribe v Tribe* [1996] Ch 107: the withdrawal exception (now redundant after *Patel*).
- *Patel v Mirza* [2016] UKSC 42: trio of considerations; reliance principle no longer the law.
- Trustee Act 1925 s 33 (protective trusts); Insolvency Act 1986 (transactions defrauding creditors, per Watt); Perpetuities and Accumulations Act 2009 ss 13–14.

## Debate and critique
- **Patel v Mirza: certainty versus flexibility**. Virgo ("Patel v Mirza: one step forward and two steps back", Trusts & Trustees 2016) argues the trio of considerations will create *more* uncertainty. Proportionality needs an objective yardstick that the majority never supplied; the majority did not explain why insider dealing was criminal or which policies would be affected, so the test risks collapsing back into something like the rejected "public conscience" test. He prefers Lord Sumption's minority approach (with Lord Clarke agreeing and Lord Mance concurring): illegality is governed by rules, and the majority's approach turns a legal principle into a discretion with the vices of "complexity, uncertainty, arbitrariness and lack of transparency". Lord Toulson's reply: the old law was already uncertain, and certainty matters little to those contemplating unlawful acts.
- **Forfeiture concerns**: Watt argues that refusing to recognise property rights because of an unrelated wrong borders on forfeiture, which the law has historically confined to extreme cases; leaving the windfall with the equally guilty recipient is just as objectionable, and innocent creditors of the transferor may suffer.
- **Freedom of disposition versus discrimination**: *Blathwayt* treats religious preference as legitimate private choice. Watt questions whether religion is truly a "choice" (e.g. Judaism) and notes the oddity of protecting the settlor's religious choice while penalising the beneficiary's. The ECHR does not resolve the tension.
- **Formalism of drafting**: the determinable/condition subsequent distinction (and "until bankruptcy" versus "forfeit on bankruptcy") produces different results for functionally identical clauses; Watt calls it a fine distinction.
- **Shams and offshore trusts**: courts' willingness to look to the "true effect" (*Pugachev*) rather than subjective sham intention strengthens creditors' and spouses' hands, linking to the "irreducible core" debate (trusts-in-international-context.md).
- **Perpetuity reform**: some argue the 2009 compromise is too complex and the rule should simply be abolished (Watt).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify any conditions**: is it precedent or subsequent? Is it certain enough (stricter for subsequent: *Re Tuck*)? What happens if void (realty/personalty; trust survives free of condition)?
2. **Spot restraints**: on alienation (void as condition subsequent, valid as determinable limitation); on marriage (general restraint void; "until marriage" valid; incentives to divorce void: *Re Johnson*); on religion (*Blathwayt*, but consider modern discrimination concerns and public authority status of courts).
3. **Creditor issues**: is the settlor protecting his *own* property (void/voidable) or protecting a beneficiary (protective trust, s 33)? Consider Insolvency Act avoidance where a substantial purpose was to prejudice creditors.
4. **Sham or illusory?** Who really controls the assets? Look at reserved powers, protector roles, whether powers are personal or fiduciary (*Pugachev*).
5. **Illegality**: was property put in another's name for an illegal purpose? Apply *Patel v Mirza*'s trio (purpose of the rule broken; competing policies, e.g. not letting the other party keep a windfall; proportionality). Do not apply the reliance test, but explain why it was abandoned (*Tinker v Tinker* arbitrariness).
6. **Perpetuity**: for purpose trusts or trusts with remote contingencies, check the 125-year period (post-2010) and inalienability (lives plus 21 years).
7. **Outcome**: condition struck out (trust survives) or trust fails (resulting trust for settlor/residue)?
