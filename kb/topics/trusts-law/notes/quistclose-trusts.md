# Quistclose trusts

## Overview
A *Quistclose* trust arises where money is paid (usually lent) for a specific purpose on terms that it is not to be at the recipient's free disposal. If the purpose is carried out, the money is spent and the lender is left with an ordinary debt; if the purpose fails (typically because the borrower becomes insolvent first), the money is held on trust for the lender, who therefore escapes the borrower's general creditors. The device sits at the meeting point of trusts, contract and insolvency law. It was covered in the week 8 resulting trusts lecture and was the subject of the materials. The two big debates are (1) how to classify it (express, resulting, constructive, sui generis, or no trust at all) and (2) whether the law should recognise it, since it works like unregistered security.

## The law

### 1. Barclays Bank Ltd v Quistclose Investments Ltd [1970] AC 567 (HL)
- Facts (handout): Quistclose (Q) lent money to Rolls Razor Ltd (RR), a company it knew was in financial difficulty, expressly so RR could pay a declared dividend. The money was paid into a separate account at Barclays. RR went into voluntary liquidation before paying the dividend. Barclays, which knew the purpose and was owed money on RR's overdrawn accounts, sought to set off the loan money. Held: Q had a proprietary interest; the money was held on trust and Barclays could not take it.
- **Lord Wilberforce (580)**, for a unanimous House: arrangements for paying a person's creditors by a third person give rise to "a relationship of a fiduciary character or trust, in favour, as a primary trust, of the creditors, and secondarily, if the primary trust fails, of the third person", recognised "in a series of cases over some 150 years".
- This is the **two-trust** model: primary (express) trust for the shareholders/creditors; secondary (resulting) trust for the lender when the primary purpose becomes impossible.
- Revision 11 adds that Lord Wilberforce inferred the trust from the parties' *mutual intention* that the money should not become part of RR's general assets, shown by the specific purpose and separate account.

### 2. Questions the two-trust model leaves (handout)
1. Who are the beneficiaries of the primary trust?
2. Who can enforce the primary trust?
3. What equitable right did Q keep after advancing the money?
4. What role does the purpose play?
The handout notes the result may be defensible on policy grounds, e.g. encouraging rescue lending to companies at risk of insolvency.

### 3. Carreras Rothmans Ltd v Freeman Mathews Treasure Ltd [1985] Ch 207
- Rejected the analysis in which the lender retains the beneficial interest; instead the beneficial interest is **in suspense** until payment to the intended recipients, who, while not beneficiaries, have an enforceable right to have the purpose carried out (the handout attributes this to "Gibson LJ"; revision 11 to Peter Gibson J).
- Handout critique: does this give the intended payees an improper preference if the trustee becomes insolvent?

### 4. Twinsectra Ltd v Yardley [2002] 2 AC 164 (HL)
- A loan was made on a solicitor's undertaking that the money would be used only to acquire property (the case is also the leading dishonest assistance authority: see dishonest-assistance.md).
- **Lord Hoffmann**: the money was held on trust for Twinsectra until applied in accordance with the undertaking (handout).
- **Lord Millett** (whose analysis of the trust is the most cited, though he dissented on dishonesty), as quoted in revision 11:
  - at [74]: the question in every case is whether the parties intended the money to be at the recipient's free disposal;
  - at [76]: a person who makes money available for a particular purpose only places trust and confidence in the recipient to ensure it is properly applied (hence fiduciary character);
  - at [100]: the lender does not part with the entire beneficial interest, and in so far as he does not, it is held on a **resulting trust for the lender from the outset**, with the borrower having a power (or duty) to apply it to the purpose.
  - Grower notes Lord Millett called such arrangements "akin... to a retention of title clause (though with a different object)".
- The handout concludes that which analysis prevails may depend largely on the terms of the particular arrangement.

### 5. Other classifications (revision 11)
- **Express trust with variable limbs**: Gummow J in *Re Australian Elizabethan Theatre Trust* (1991) 102 ALR 681 (Australia): no special "Quistclose trust"; the nature of the trust depends on the intention shown by the transaction.
- **Constructive trust**: Smolyansky (2010), to prevent unconscionable retention.
- **No trust at all**: Swadling argues the arrangement is contractual and *Quistclose* was wrongly decided.
- **Beneficiary trust for crowdfunding**: Wilde (Trusts & Trustees 2024) argues crowdfunded litigation funds are beneficiary trusts in which the litigant benefits only through the purpose of meeting costs, not non-charitable purpose trusts (relying on *Re Sanderson's Trust* (1857) 3 K & J 497). See private-purpose-trusts.md.
- **Insolvency typology**: Hofri-Winogradow and David (CLJ 2022, discussed in revision 11) identify Type A (paying creditors), Type B (investment) and Type C (customer prepayments) arrangements.

### 6. Requirements in practice (synthesis from the sources)
1. Money transferred for a stated purpose.
2. Mutual intention (objectively assessed) that it is not at the recipient's free disposal (*Twinsectra*, Lord Millett [74]); a separate account is good evidence (as in *Quistclose*), though Hudson notes courts have sometimes found trusts on thin evidence.
3. Consequence: if misapplied, the lender can claim proprietarily (and trace); if the purpose fails, the money returns to the lender ahead of general creditors; third parties with notice may be liable (Barclays in *Quistclose*; dishonest assistance in *Twinsectra*).

## Key authorities
- *Barclays Bank Ltd v Quistclose Investments Ltd* [1970] AC 567: two-trust model.
- *Carreras Rothmans Ltd v Freeman Mathews Treasure Ltd* [1985] Ch 207: beneficial interest in suspense.
- *Twinsectra Ltd v Yardley* [2002] 2 AC 164: resulting trust from the outset (Lord Millett); trust until applied (Lord Hoffmann).
- *Re Australian Elizabethan Theatre Trust* (1991) 102 ALR 681: express trust model (Australia).
- *Re Bond Worth Ltd*: Slade J treated a "retention of equitable ownership" clause as an unregistered charge, void (relied on by Grower).
- *Cooper v PRG Powerhouse Ltd* [2008] EWHC 498 (Ch): trust found from an arrangement to discharge a car loan (cited by Hudson via revision 11).
- Companies Act 2006 ss 859A, 859H: registration of company charges (Grower).

## Debate and critique
- **Why recognise a trust at all? Hudson ("A Normative Approach to the Quistclose Trust" (2017) 80 MLR)**: most writing asks how to classify the trust; she asks why the law should add a trust to the underlying loan. She examines four justifications: respecting party intention, unconscionability, fairness, and incentivising desirable transactions (rescue finance). Each has difficulties; intention is probably the most convincing. The law and commentary lack coherence, with a mismatch between normative and doctrinal analysis and a failure to address the consequences in insolvency.
- **Fiduciary character: Hughes-Davies ("Redefining the Quistclose trust" [2015] Conv 26)**: purpose and segregation alone are not enough; there should be a genuine fiduciary relationship in which the recipient must put the lender's interest first. On that view *Quistclose* (a rescue context) is justified but an arm's-length commercial loan like *Twinsectra* is not; he draws on warnings in *Hospital Products v United States Surgical Corp* against imposing fiduciary duties in commercial dealings.
- **Risk management: Brown ("Trusts and Credit Risk", Trusts & Trustees 2023)**: the *Quistclose* trust is most important for mitigating credit risk on a borrower's default; it changes the normal arm's-length character of lending by adding obligations of a fiduciary character.
- **Abolish it: Grower ("Time to call time on the Quistclose trust" [2023] Conv 109)**: parties use it because it is a cheap *quasi-security* giving priority in the borrower's insolvency, with the same effect as a non-possessory security interest but without registration under Companies Act 2006 s 859A (an unregistered charge is void against a liquidator and other secured creditors under s 859H). Registration lets other lenders assess risk, serving the same fairness as the pari passu rule; unregistered *Quistclose* trusts produce "considerable and unprincipled unfairness". Courts should take the *Re Bond Worth* approach and look at substance.
- **Secured transactions context: Bridge ("The Quistclose Trust in a World of Secured Transactions" (1992) 12 OJLS 333)** examines the device against the law of security, the same fault line Grower relies on.
- **Defend it**: a UCL Juris article in the journal folder argues the trust is "too useful in commercial practice to be abandoned" (e.g. securitisations) and supports Lord Millett's restitution-inspired rationalisation.
- **Classification matters** (analysis): on Lord Millett's resulting trust the lender has the beneficial interest throughout, which arguably avoids beneficiary-principle and standing problems; the two-trust model raises the questions in §2; beneficial interest in suspense (*Carreras*) is hard to square with the rule that beneficial ownership cannot be "in the air" (cf. Lord Wilberforce in *Vandervell*, resulting-trusts.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Was money paid for a specific purpose?** Identify the purpose and the evidence (separate account, undertakings, correspondence).
2. **Was it intended not to be at the recipient's free disposal?** Apply Lord Millett's test (*Twinsectra* [74]); contrast an ordinary loan.
3. **Has the purpose been carried out, failed, or been misapplied?**
   - Carried out: lender is an unsecured creditor for repayment.
   - Failed (e.g. insolvency before payment): trust for the lender; money not available to general creditors or the liquidator (*Quistclose*).
   - Misapplied: lender has a proprietary claim and can trace (see tracing-and-proprietary-claims.md); third parties who knowingly helped may be liable (dishonest-assistance.md; knowing-receipt.md).
4. **Classify and explain consequences**: compare Lord Wilberforce (primary/secondary), *Carreras* (suspense), Lord Millett (resulting trust from outset), Gummow J (express).
5. **Set-off and bank's notice**: a bank that knows the purpose cannot set the money off against the borrower's overdraft (*Quistclose*).
6. **Essay angle**: legitimacy versus insolvency policy (Hudson, Grower, Brown, Hughes-Davies).
7. **Traps**: assuming a separate account alone creates a trust; ignoring whether the purpose has already been performed; forgetting the beneficiary-principle issue under the two-trust model.
