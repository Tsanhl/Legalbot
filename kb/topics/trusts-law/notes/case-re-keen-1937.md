# Re Keen; Evershed v Griffiths [1937] Ch 236 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the judgment was not read. Lord Wright MR's judgment is the one cited; the rest
> of the Court of Appeal panel and the date of judgment are not confirmed and are omitted. The sources describe the
> reasons for the decision in slightly different ways, so the account of reasons is cautious. Do not quote wording or
> paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
Court of Appeal, reported 1937; Lord Wright MR gave the judgment usually cited. The case concerns whether a half-secret trust was valid when the testator gave
his trustee the beneficiary's name in a sealed envelope, to be opened on death, before making a will that referred to
future notification. It is the standard authority, alongside *Blackwell v Blackwell*, that the terms of a half-secret
trust must be communicated before or at the time of the will, and it also shows that a will cannot reserve
a power to notify further terms later.

## Facts
Harry Augustus Keen's will left £10,000 to two trustees, to be distributed among persons or charities as he would notify them
during his lifetime. He had earlier handed one trustee, Mr Evershed, a sealed envelope containing the name and address
of the person he wished to benefit, with instructions that it should not be opened until after his death. Mr Evershed
had it unopened until death. The other trustee knew nothing of the arrangement until then. A summons asked whether the
testator had created a valid trust for the person named.

## Decision
- The trust **failed**; the Court of Appeal held the half-secret trust invalid, so the £10,000 fell into residue.
  The Court of Appeal affirmed the first-instance judge, Farwell J, who had also held the trust invalid but on a
  different ground (that the trustees had not been notified during the testator's lifetime, as the will required).
- The sources give two linked reasons. First, the communication in the envelope was made before the will was
  executed, while the will referred to notification at some later time, so the will and the communication were
  inconsistent. Second, a will cannot give the testator power to make future unattested dispositions by notifying
  trustees later, as that would conflict with the formality requirements of the Wills Act 1837.
- Lord Wright MR accepted that handing over a sealed envelope can in principle be a sufficient communication, if the
  trustee knows it contains the terms of the trust and accepts on that basis (his analogy was a ship sailing under
  sealed orders). The trust failed for the reasons above, not because of the envelope as such.

## Effect
The case is cited for the rule that, in a half-secret trust, communication of the terms must be made before or at
the time of the will, and for the proposition that the will's wording must be consistent with the actual
communication. It is distinguished from a fully secret trust, where later communication up to death is sufficient.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Check the wording of the will: does it say property is held on trust for purposes "to be communicated" or "as I shall
   notify"? That suggests a half-secret trust and the *Re Keen* risk.
2. Check when the trustee was told the terms and whether they accepted. Communication after the will, or
   notification reserved for the future, is a problem.
3. Where the will speaks of future notification but the terms were given earlier (for example in a sealed envelope),
   apply *Re Keen* and argue the trust fails and the property goes to residue or on intestacy.
4. Contrast a fully secret trust, and note that the true ratio of *Blackwell* and the timing rule are debated.
