# Constructive trusts (including the family home)

## Overview
A constructive trust arises by operation of law, not by the parties' agreement. For course purposes it is imposed where it would be **unconscionable** for the legal owner to deny another a beneficial interest in property. It is exempt from writing (LPA 1925 s 53(2)). The handout frames implied trusts against four background principles: property should move only for a recognised reason; equity facilitates legitimate transfers; equity dislikes suspicious, unexplained or unlawful transfers; and property should not be ownerless ("equity abhors a beneficial vacuum"). Resulting trusts send back property that moved without a clear reason; constructive trusts often complete a transaction that got "stuck" (*Re Rose*, *Pennington*) or move beneficial title to restore justice (bribes, misappropriated property, arguably secret trusts, third-party liability). This note concentrates on the **common intention constructive trust of the family home**, which has largely displaced the purchase-money resulting trust for cohabitants. English law is said not to recognise a *remedial* constructive trust.

## The law

### 1. Definition and character
1. "English law provides no clear and all-embracing definition of a constructive trust" (*Carl Zeiss Stiftung v Herbert Smith & Co (No 2)* [1969] 2 Ch 276, 300, Edmund-Davies LJ).
2. The unconscionability is about **denying an underlying interest**, not bad conduct generally; the constructive trust "is not a discretionary remedy for unconscionable conduct, but arises from concrete property relations" (handout).
3. **Examples from the course**: *Re Rose* [1952] Ch 499 (beneficial title passed when Mr Rose completed the transfer forms in March 1943, although registration was in June; he could not then withdraw); *Pennington v Waine* [2002] (unconscionable for Ada to withdraw); *Rochefoucauld v Boustead*; secret trusts (arguably); fiduciary bribes and secret commissions (*FHR European Ventures v Cedar Capital Partners* [2014] UKSC 45, listed in revision 4); traced misappropriated property; specifically enforceable contracts (*Neville v Wilson*; *Oughtred*, Lord Radcliffe); *donationes mortis causa*.
4. **Trustee duties**: involuntary, so unless the constructive trustee knows (actually or constructively) of the beneficiary's interest he has no management duties or personal liability; he simply must give the beneficiary the property.
5. **What it gives**: an interest reflecting the unconscionability of the denial, which may exceed the claimant's financial contribution (unlike a resulting trust). Both the proprietary priority and possible windfall are controversial where the trustee has creditors.
6. **Institutional, not remedial**: the courts cannot impose a trust just because it is fair or achieves a policy aim. Raphael notes the Supreme Court in *FHR European Ventures* at [47] stated the remedial constructive trust is not part of English law, whereas Australia (Deane J in *Muschinski v Dodds* (1985)), New Zealand and Canada have adopted it.

### 2. The family home: frameworks
- Starting point: **equity follows the law**. Joint legal owners: presumed 50/50 joint beneficial owners. Sole legal owner: presumed 100% beneficial owner.
- Married couples can rely on the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 discretion on divorce; trusts matter mainly for **unmarried cohabitants** (and for disputes with third-party creditors, as in *Rosset*).
- The constructive trust is a "melting pot" of three approaches: **property law** (title and financial contribution), **family law** (recognising the home as a shared family enterprise), and **contract** (finding agreements or intentions).
- **Contributions**: direct financial contributions (price, mortgage) always count; indirect financial contributions (one pays bills so the other can pay the mortgage) partly count; domestic work and childcare rarely count, though recent decisions are shifting.
- **Two stages**: *acquisition* (does the claimant have any beneficial interest?) and *quantification* (how much?). Sole-name cases need both; joint-name cases only quantification (*Stack v Dowden* [2007] UKHL 17).

### 3. Express declarations
- A declaration (e.g. on the conveyancing form the handout calls "T1", choosing joint tenancy or tenancy in common in equal or unequal shares) fixes the beneficial interests. It can be altered only by later agreement or proprietary estoppel (*Clarke v Meadus* [2010] EWHC 3117), not by a constructive or resulting trust (*Pankhania v Chandegra* [2012] EWCA Civ 1438).

### 4. Presumed (purchase-money) resulting trusts in the home
- Rigid: you get back what you put in. After *Jones v Kernott* [2011] UKSC 53 its role in family home disputes is limited, but *Marr v Collie* [2017] UKPC 17 (PC) suggests it may still apply where a domestic couple contributed unequally and had no common intention that joint legal ownership should mean joint beneficial ownership.
- *Pettitt v Pettitt* [1970] AC 777 and *Gissing v Gissing* [1971] AC 886: originally only direct contributions to the price at acquisition counted.
- Mortgages: assuming joint liability on a joint mortgage is a direct contribution (*Laskar v Laskar* [2008] Fam Law 638); repayments without assuming liability are not (*Curley v Parkes* [2004] EWCA Civ 1515). The handout describes the current position as ambiguous: repeated, substantial payments without liability *may* suffice, especially with an intention to acquire an interest.
- Improvements: ordinary renovations do not count; *Drake v Whipp* (1996) (first instance) suggests very substantial ones might. Household bills, domestic work and childcare do not (*Burns v Burns* [1984] Ch 317); nor a bills/mortgage split (*Gissing*); nor payments into a joint account from which the mortgage is paid (*Buggs v Buggs* [2004] WTLR 799).

### 5. Common intention constructive trust: foundation
- Lord Diplock in *Gissing v Gissing* [1971] AC 886, 905: a resulting, implied or constructive trust arises whenever the trustee has so conducted himself that it would be inequitable to deny the claimant a beneficial interest, which is so "if by his words or conduct he has induced the cestui que trust to act to his own detriment in the reasonable belief that by so acting he was acquiring a beneficial interest in the land". Sometimes called "Diplock estoppel".

### 6. Sole legal title: acquisition
**(a) Common intention** (*Lloyds Bank v Rosset* [1991] 1 AC 107, HL, Lord Bridge; described as "outdated and much criticised"). Mr Rosset bought in his sole name; Mrs Rosset made no financial contribution but renovated, decorated and supervised builders. No express common intention, and renovation could not support an inferred one.
- **Express common intention (Rosset 1)** (132): an agreement, arrangement or understanding, before acquisition or exceptionally later, that the property is to be shared beneficially, based on evidence of express discussions "however imperfectly remembered and however imprecise". "Excuse" cases find such agreements where the owner gave an excuse for not putting the claimant on the title (*Eves v Eves* [1975] 1 WLR 1338; *Grant v Edwards* [1986] Ch 638). The bar is now high: assurances not understood or intended as a promise of an interest do not qualify (*James v Thomas* [2007] EWCA Civ 1212).
- **Inferred common intention (Rosset 2)**: from conduct, namely direct financial contributions to the price or mortgage. Criticised as mirroring the resulting trust. A broader view in *Le Foe v Le Foe* [2001] 2 FLR 970 (HC), but the high hurdle was reaffirmed in *James v Thomas* ("near Herculean" labour driving a digger and business contributions insufficient) and *Morris v Morris* [2008] EWCA Civ 257 (inferred common intention only in "exceptional circumstances").
**(b) Detrimental reliance**: the claimant must have significantly changed position in reliance (unconscionability; equity will not assist a volunteer). For express intentions, detriment is broad (household expenditure may suffice) but must be referable to the intention, not explicable by other motives (*Grant v Edwards*). For inferred intentions, the contribution supplying the inference doubles as the detriment.

### 7. Sole legal title: quantification
- *Midland Bank v Cooke* [1995] 4 All ER 562 (Waite LJ): survey the whole course of dealing; only if inconclusive fall back on "equality is equity".
- *Oxley v Hiscock* [2004] EWCA Civ 546: give effect to any agreement on shares; otherwise ask what is fair having regard to the whole course of dealing. Chadwick LJ at [69]: this includes arrangements to meet outgoings (mortgage, council tax, utilities, repairs, insurance, housekeeping).
- *Stack v Dowden* (Baroness Hale at [61]) endorsed *Oxley* but preferred the Law Commission's "holistic approach" (*Sharing Homes*, 2002, para 4.27): the search is for what the parties must be taken to have intended, not what the court thinks fair.
- *Jones v Kernott*: in sole-name cases, where there is no evidence of an actual (express or inferred) agreement on shares, fairness is now permitted as a residual option.

### 8. Joint legal title: Stack v Dowden and Jones v Kernott
- In the domestic context the law has moved on from the resulting trust; the tool is the common intention constructive trust, with intention actual, inferred or (controversially) imputed. Lord Neuberger in *Stack* dissented, preferring to keep the resulting trust analysis and questioning equality regardless of contributions (slides).
- **Principles (Jones v Kernott, Lord Walker and Baroness Hale)**:
  1. Starting point: joint tenants in law and equity. Buying in joint names shows an "emotional and economic commitment to a joint enterprise" (at [19]); analysing long-term contributions is impractical.
  2. The presumption can be displaced by showing a different common intention at purchase or later.
  3. Common intention is deduced objectively from conduct and the course of dealing.
  4. If the parties' intention to depart from equality is clear but their intended shares cannot be inferred, the court may **impute** the share it considers fair having regard to the whole course of dealing.
  5. Each case turns on its facts; financial contributions are relevant but not decisive.
- The burden of displacing equal sharing is heavy; the facts must be unusual. The slides stress that fairness/imputation cannot be used at the first stage to displace the joint beneficial tenancy, only to quantify once an intention to depart is shown.
- *Hudson v Hathway* [2022] EWCA Civ 1648 (Watt): where joint owners later agree to vary their shares, the party claiming an increase must also show detrimental reliance on the new common intention.

### 9. Proprietary estoppel as an alternative
- Elements (handout): (1) a sufficiently specific representation or assurance of a present or future interest (actual, not inferred or imputed; vague statements fail: *Lissimore v Downing* [2003] 2 FLR 308, *Layton v Martin* [1986] 2 FLR 227, *James v Thomas*, *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18; contrast *Southwell v Blackburn* [2014] EWCA Civ 1347); (2) reliance referable to the assurance (holistic approach, *Southwell*); (3) detriment making denial unconscionable; detriment may be offset by countervailing benefits (*Sledmore v Dalby* (1996) 72 P & CR 196).
- The resulting equity is satisfied by the minimum necessary, with a wide discretionary range of remedies (reliance loss to expectation), unlike the fixed beneficial interest under a constructive trust.

## Debate and critique
- **Institutional v remedial**: Raphael ("Constructive trusts: comparing the institutional constructive trust with the remedial constructive trust") traces the institutional trust in Australia (*Black v S Friedman & Co* (1907); *Nolan v Nolan*) and the remedial trust from Deane J in *Muschinski v Dodds*, adopted in New Zealand and Canada but criticised in the UK and rejected in *FHR*. He stresses that courts *construe* rather than "construct" the trust: equity attaches consequences to circumstances. Yet the family home cases, with imputation and fairness, look remedial in practice.
- **Remedial consistency (Jaffey, "Remedial consistency and constructive trust claims")**: a remedy must be consistent with the primary relation it protects; proprietary claims should arise from a prior property right, not a breach of duty. Applied to three controversies: (1) mistaken or unauthorised payments: a proprietary claim should generally be available whether or not there was a trust or fiduciary breach; (2) fiduciary profits: distinguish the principal's power to confiscate profits made in breach of loyalty from a genuine proprietary claim based on the principal's ownership of an opportunity or information; (3) cohabitants: the common intention constructive trust conflates a property-based claim with a personal claim for financial support arising from the relationship; the two can operate together only where the owner must be taken to have intended a trust protecting the other's relationship-based claim. He contrasts this with a community property regime.
- **Rosset criticised**: too narrow and financially focused, ignoring domestic contributions; the bar for express intention (excuse cases) involves fictions. *Stack*/*Kernott* liberalised quantification but *Rosset* still governs acquisition in sole-name cases, creating a tension the handout calls "outdated".
- **Imputation and fairness**: Baroness Hale in *Stack* rejected a free-standing fairness test, yet *Kernott* allows imputing a fair share. Is this judicial discretion over property rights, undermining certainty (cf. Lord Neuberger's dissent in *Stack*)? The family law approach is reserved by Parliament for spouses (Matrimonial Causes Act 1973); extending it to cohabitants through trusts arguably usurps Parliament (Watt notes Parliament has not extended such allocation to unmarried cohabitants).
- **Resulting trust or constructive trust?** *Marr v Collie* shows the resulting trust is not dead; Mee argues the purchase-money resulting trust has been made irrelevant by the common intention constructive trust (see resulting-trusts.md).
- **Proprietary estoppel overlap**: Lord Diplock's formulation looks like estoppel; but estoppel needs an actual assurance and gives a discretionary remedy, whereas the trust can rest on inferred intention and gives a beneficial share.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there an express declaration of trust (e.g. on the transfer form)?** If valid, it is conclusive (*Pankhania*) unless varied by agreement or estoppel.
2. **Joint or sole legal title?**
3. **Joint names**: start at 50/50 (*Stack*; *Kernott*). Are there unusual facts showing a different intention at purchase or later (look at the whole course of dealing, not only financial contributions)? If so, can actual shares be inferred? If not, impute a fair share on the whole course of dealing. Later variation needs detrimental reliance (*Hudson v Hathway*). Consider *Marr v Collie* where contributions were unequal and there was no common intention that joint legal title meant joint beneficial ownership.
4. **Sole name**: acquisition first.
   - Express discussions (*Rosset* 1)? Excuses (*Eves*; *Grant v Edwards*)? Were assurances meant as promises (*James v Thomas*)?
   - Otherwise direct financial contributions to price or mortgage (*Rosset* 2; *Laskar*; *Curley*)? Domestic work alone fails (*Burns*); consider *Le Foe* and critiques.
   - Detrimental reliance referable to the intention.
   - Then quantify: agreed shares; otherwise whole course of dealing (*Oxley*; *Stack* at [61]; *Kernott*: fairness as residual).
5. **Alternatively**: proprietary estoppel (specific assurance, reliance, detriment, minimum equity) or resulting trust (strict contributions).
6. **Third parties**: a creditor or mortgagee may be involved (*Rosset*); a constructive trust interest gives priority issues.
7. **Other constructive trust contexts**: unauthorised fiduciary profits and bribes (fiduciary-duties.md), *Re Rose*/*Pennington* (constitution-of-trusts.md), *Rochefoucauld* (formalities-declaring-trusts.md), specifically enforceable contracts (dispositions-of-equitable-interests.md).
8. **Traps**: applying *Stack* quantification to acquisition in sole-name cases; using fairness to displace the joint-name presumption; treating childcare as a direct contribution; forgetting detrimental reliance.
