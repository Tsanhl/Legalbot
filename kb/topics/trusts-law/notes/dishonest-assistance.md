# Dishonest assistance in a breach of trust

## Overview
A beneficiary whose trustee has breached trust may be unable to recover from the trustee (insolvency, an exemption clause, or relief under Trustee Act 1925 s 61) and cannot assert a proprietary claim against a third party who no longer holds the property. Dishonest assistance is a personal claim against a "stranger" (solicitor, accountant, bank employee, company director and so on) who dishonestly helps the breach. The leading controversy for the last thirty years has been the meaning of dishonesty, which has now settled on an objective standard.

## The law (England and Wales)
**1. Nature of liability.** Liability is fault-based: assisting is not enough, it must be dishonest. It is often described as liability to account "as a constructive trustee", but this terminology is misleading because the liability is purely personal; the assister compensates for the loss caused to the trust fund and may also have to disgorge profits such as fees. It is the assister's own wrong (primary, not parasitic liability). Rationale (*Royal Brunei Airlines Sdn Bhd v Tan* [1995] 2 AC 378, 387, Lord Nicholls): beneficiaries are entitled to expect trustees will be left to perform their obligations without deliberate intrusion by third parties.

**2. Four requirements.**
- **(a) A trust or fiduciary relationship.** *Tan*: an agent company held ticket sale money on express trust; its managing director, who authorised using the money for ordinary business, was liable to account. The principle is not limited to trusts; it extends to other fiduciary relationships such as company directors.
- **(b) A breach of trust or fiduciary duty by someone other than the defendant.** The trustee's breach need not be dishonest or fraudulent: "what matters is the state of mind of the third party" (*Tan*, 385); a stranger may be liable for assisting an innocent or negligent breach if he does so dishonestly.
- **(c) Assistance.** Active conduct is straightforward (authorising use of trust money in *Tan*). Passive acquiescence is not enough in itself: in *Brinks Ltd v Abu-Saleh (No 3)* [1996] CLC 133 Rimer J held that a wife who accompanied her husband on trips carrying £3m of laundered proceeds to Switzerland did so as his wife, so did not assist. Acts that conceal or launder (assisting the continuing diversion) can amount to assistance: Millett LJ in *Twinsectra Ltd v Yardley* [2002] UKHL 12 said liability "extends to everyone who consciously assists in the continuing diversion of the money".
- **(d) Dishonesty.** See below.

**3. Does the assister need to know of the trust?** Rimer J in *Brinks* said (obiter) that the assister must know of the trust or the facts giving rise to it. *Barlow Clowes International Ltd v Eurotrust International Ltd* [2006] 1 WLR 1476 held knowledge of the trust is not required; a person can know or suspect that he assists in a misappropriation without knowing the money is held on trust.

**4. The dishonesty test: three stages.**
- *Stage 1: Tan (objective).* Lord Nicholls: acting dishonestly "means simply not acting as an honest person would in the circumstances", an objective standard; honesty is not an optional scale with each person's own values. Personal attributes (experience, intelligence, actual knowledge, the reasons for acting) are relevant. He rejected the Baden "scale of knowledge" as a defining framework (as reported in Dixon's article).
- *Stage 2: Twinsectra (combined test).* The House of Lords (Lord Hutton) set out three possible standards: purely subjective ("Robin Hood"), purely objective, and a combined test, which required that the conduct was dishonest by ordinary standards and that the defendant realised this. Lord Hoffmann said dishonesty required consciousness of transgressing ordinary standards. On the facts the solicitor Leach had been "misguided" rather than dishonest, and the first-instance finding was restored. Millett LJ dissented: the combined test was the criminal test (*R v Ghosh*, per Dixon) that Lord Nicholls had not adopted; he suggested returning to the label "knowing assistance".
- *Stage 3: Barlow Clowes and after.* The Privy Council (Lord Hoffmann) accepted Lord Hutton's speech might be ambiguous but said that the combined test requires only that the defendant be aware of the elements of the transaction which make participation contrary to ordinary standards of honesty, not that he reflect on those standards. Mr Henwood strongly suspected the funds were from investors in a fraudulent scheme and avoided inquiry; he was liable despite his own warped moral view. *Abou-Rahmah v Abacha* [2006] EWCA Civ 1492 (Arden LJ) said *Tan* and *Twinsectra* can be read together after *Barlow Clowes*; there a bank with general suspicions was not dishonest, given the commercial setting. *Starglade Properties Ltd v Nash* [2010] EWCA Civ 1314: the test is the ordinary standard of honest behaviour; the defendant's own view and any opinion that the standard is too high are irrelevant. *Ivey v Genting Casinos* [2017] UKSC 67 (confirmed in *Group Seven Ltd v Notable Services LLP* [2019] EWCA Civ 614) states the test: first find the defendant's actual state of knowledge or belief (subjectively; reasonableness is evidence only), then decide objectively by the standards of ordinary decent people whether the conduct was dishonest, with no need for the defendant to appreciate it was dishonest.

## Key authorities
- *Royal Brunei Airlines v Tan* [1995] 2 AC 378 (PC): foundation; objective dishonesty; no need for dishonest trustee.
- *Brinks v Abu-Saleh (No 3)* [1996] CLC 133: assistance, laundering, passive presence.
- *Twinsectra v Yardley* [2002] UKHL 12: combined test, Millett LJ's dissent.
- *Barlow Clowes v Eurotrust* [2006] 1 WLR 1476 (PC): reinterpretation; no need to know of trust.
- *Abou-Rahmah v Abacha* [2006] EWCA Civ 1492; *Starglade v Nash* [2010] EWCA Civ 1314.
- *Ivey v Genting Casinos* [2017] UKSC 67; *Group Seven v Notable Services* [2019] EWCA Civ 614: current objective test.

## Debate and critique
- **Plausibility of Barlow Clowes.** the materials calls the Privy Council's claim that *Twinsectra* and *Tan* are consistent "unconvincing" because they are plainly different tests; academics call it a reinterpretation, and the Privy Council could not overrule a House of Lords decision. Dixon's 2019 article (as summarised in the readings) says *Twinsectra* is no longer authoritative after *Ivey* and *Group Seven*, and supports the high threshold of *Tan* because liability is personal and the assister has not necessarily benefited.
- **Label.** Millett LJ preferred "knowing assistance" because calling professionals dishonest is a harsh label and may distract from intentional wrongdoing.
- **Business risk.** A fault threshold protects ordinary commerce; strict liability would affect innocent advisers and banks.
- **Constructive trustee terminology.** Misleading, because it implies proprietary remedies; the liability is personal.
- **Comparison with knowing receipt.** Dishonest assistance does not need receipt of property; knowing receipt does (see knowing-receipt.md), and their fault standards have been compared and debated.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there a trust or fiduciary relationship, and a breach by the trustee or fiduciary (innocent breach suffices)?
2. Did the defendant (a stranger) act to assist: authorising, facilitating, laundering or concealing? Mere presence or a role in some other capacity may not be assistance.
3. Dishonesty: establish what the defendant actually knew, suspected or believed (including deliberate avoidance of inquiry); then ask whether by ordinary standards of honest people, a defendant with that knowledge and experience acted honestly (*Ivey*, *Starglade*). Do not require that he appreciated the dishonesty. Account for professional status and commercial context.
4. Knowledge of the trust is not required (*Barlow Clowes*).
5. Remedy: personal liability for loss to the fund; possibly disgorgement of profits. Check the trustee's own liability (exemption clauses, s 61) as a reason why the beneficiary pursues the assister.
6. Trap: do not apply *Twinsectra*'s subjective limb as current law, and do not call it a proprietary claim.
