# Resulting trusts

## Overview
A resulting trust "jumps back" (Latin *resalire*) the beneficial interest to the person who provided the property when it cannot attach to anyone else, or when the law presumes the transferor did not mean to give it away. Resulting trusts arise by operation of law, so they are exempt from writing (LPA 1925 s 53(2)). The handout follows Megarry J's twofold classification in *Re Vandervell's Trusts (No 2)*: **automatic** resulting trusts (where an express trust fails to dispose of the whole beneficial interest) and **presumed** resulting trusts (voluntary transfers and purchase-money contributions). The theory is heavily contested (unjust enrichment v retention v presumed declaration), and the *Vandervell* saga is the standard vehicle for testing it. Resulting trusts also explain what happens when express trusts fail for uncertainty, when half-secret trusts fail, and (on one view) *Quistclose* trusts.

## The law

### 1. Two categories: Megarry J in Re Vandervell's Trusts (No 2) [1974] Ch 269, 294–295
1. If a transaction makes no effective disposition, it does nothing; that is not a resulting trust. "To yearn is not to transfer": an unexpressed intention does nothing.
2. A resulting trust needs an effective transaction that transfers or creates an interest.
3. Where A transfers to B without consideration and with no presumption of advancement:
   - **(a) Presumed resulting trust**: the transfer is *not* on any trust. Unless it appears B is to hold on trust or take beneficially, there is a rebuttable presumption that B holds on resulting trust for A. The presumption establishes both that there is a trust and what it is.
   - **(b) Automatic resulting trust**: the transfer *is* on trusts that leave some beneficial interest undisposed of. B automatically holds that part on resulting trust for A. It "does not depend on any intentions or presumptions" but is the consequence of A's failure to dispose of what was vested in him.
4. The same principles apply where trustees for A transfer to B at A's direction: A is the true transferor.

### 2. Automatic resulting trusts
1. **When**: an express trust fails or does not exhaust the beneficial interest (failed half-secret trusts; failure for uncertainty of subject matter shares or objects; surplus funds after a purpose is achieved). The legal owner is clearly not meant to benefit, so the law fills the gap.
2. **Presumed lack of intention to part with the surplus**: *Re Gillingham Bus Disaster Fund* [1958] Ch 300 (surplus of a fund resulted to donors).
3. **Rebuttal and bona vacantia**: if the settlor's actual intention shows he parted with the money out and out, the surplus goes to the Crown as bona vacantia (*Re West Sussex Constabulary's Widows, Children and Benevolent (1930) Fund Trusts* [1971] Ch 1). Lord Browne-Wilkinson in *Westdeutsche Landesbank Girozentrale v Islington LBC* [1996] (the handout cites [1966] 2 All ER 961, 991, which appears to be a typo for 1996): if the settlor has expressly or by necessary implication abandoned any beneficial interest, there is no resulting trust; the undisposed-of interest vests in the Crown as bona vacantia.
4. **Acceleration instead of resulting trust**: where (i) there are successive interests (A for life, remainder to B), (ii) B's interest is merely postponed, not conditional, and (iii) the settlor can be presumed to have intended B to take if A's interest fails, A's failure accelerates B's interest. That intention is harder to presume in a lifetime trust (*Re Flower's Settlement Trusts* [1957] 1 All ER 462).
5. **Wills**: a testamentary transfer carries a presumption of gift unless a trust is expressed. If a testamentary trust fails for lack of intention, the recipient takes as donee; there is no resulting trust for the estate (contrast failure of a trust that *was* clearly intended, e.g. a half-secret trust, which results to residue).

### 3. Presumed resulting trusts
1. **Voluntary transfer**: where A transfers property to B without consideration and without expressing an intention to give or to create a trust, B holds on resulting trust for A, on the presumption that A did not intend to part with the benefit. The law does not normally presume a gift.
2. **Mistaken payments** are problematic: a mistaken payer cannot easily be presumed to intend to retain the benefit. Watt (*Trusts & Equity*, p 159, as quoted in the handout) suggests a resulting trust should arise "not only in the absence of the transferor's contrary intention, but also in the absence of the transferee's contrary entitlement."
3. **Purchase-money resulting trust**: where A contributes to the price of property held in B's name (or A and B jointly), A takes a beneficial share proportionate to the contribution. Example from the week 1 slides: Linda puts £8 and Jack £2 towards a £10 bottle that Jack buys; in equity they share it in proportion. **Exception**: the family home, now governed by the common intention constructive trust (see constructive-trusts.md).
4. **Rebutting the presumption**:
   - evidence of an intention to give, express or inferred from conduct (*Fowkes v Pascoe*, cited in the handout as [1935] WN 68);
   - an intention to transfer full ownership under a contract;
   - the **presumption of advancement**: transfers from men to wives, fiancées or children and from women to their children (*Musson v Bonner* [2010] WTLR 1369) are presumed gifts. Advancement itself is rebuttable by contrary evidence (*McGrath v Wallis* [1995] 2 FLR 114; *Lavelle v Lavelle* [2004] EWCA Civ 223). It is due to be abolished when **Equality Act 2010 s 199** comes into force; until then it applies even to adult, financially independent children (*Wood v Watkin* [2019] EWHC 1311 (Ch)). The handout calls it doubtful in principle.
   - Illegality and the presumptions: see public-policy-limits-on-trusts.md (*Tinsley v Milligan*, *Tinker v Tinker*, *Patel v Mirza*).

### 4. The Vandervell saga
1. **1958**: Mr Vandervell (V) had his bank (bare trustee) transfer 100,000 shares to the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS), which granted an option to repurchase for £5,000 to Vandervell Trustees Ltd (VT), a trustee company also trustee of V's children's settlement and an employee fund. The option was held for unspecified beneficiaries.
2. **Vandervell v IRC** [1967] 2 AC 291: (a) the transfer to the RCS was outside s 53(1)(c) (see dispositions-of-equitable-interests.md); but (b) by 2:1 (Lord Donovan dissenting on this point, per the handout) the **option was held on resulting trust for V**, because no beneficiary was specified and VT was not shown to take absolutely; V was taxable on the dividends. Lord Wilberforce (329): no need for a presumption; the facts were found; the option was vested in VT on trusts not yet defined, and the beneficial interest "cannot remain in the air", so it remained in V. This was an **automatic** resulting trust.
3. **1961**: VT exercised the option using £5,000 from the children's settlement and told the Revenue the shares would henceforth be held on the children's settlement trusts; dividends were paid to that settlement.
4. **1965**: V executed a deed transferring any interest he had in the option or shares to VT for the children's settlement. **1967**: V died.
5. **Re Vandervell's Trusts (No 2)** [1974] Ch 269: V's executors claimed the 1961–65 dividends.
   - **Megarry J** (for the executors): an automatic resulting trust ignores V's intention not to get the shares back (298–299); and using the children's money to exercise V's option could not shift the beneficial interest to the children (299: allowing trustees to divert X's valuable option to Y because they used Y's money would be "monstrous").
   - **Court of Appeal (Lord Denning MR)** reversed. When the option was exercised the trust property changed character (option to shares), and a new, clearly manifested trust of the shares for the children's settlement arose: shown by use of the children's money, the letter to the Revenue, and payment of dividends to the settlement, all with V's assent. On s 53(1)(c), Lord Denning (319–320): a resulting trust "is born and dies without any writing at all"; it exists while there is a gap in beneficial ownership and ends when the gap is filled by a valid trust. A trust of personalty needs no writing, and V and VT had done everything necessary (*Milroy v Lord*).

### 5. Quistclose trusts (outline)
*Barclays Bank v Quistclose Investments* [1970] AC 567: loan money paid into a separate account for a specific purpose (paying a dividend) was held on trust; when the purpose failed, Q had a proprietary claim. Lord Wilberforce described a primary trust for the creditors and a secondary trust for the lender if the primary trust fails. *Carreras Rothmans v Freeman Mathews Treasure* [1985] Ch 207 (Gibson LJ, per the handout) preferred a beneficial interest in suspense; *Twinsectra v Yardley* [2002] 2 AC 164 (Lord Hoffmann) held the money on trust for the lender until applied for the purpose. Full treatment in quistclose-trusts.md.

## Key authorities
- *Re Vandervell's Trusts (No 2)* [1974] Ch 269: Megarry J's classification; CA on how a resulting trust ends.
- *Vandervell v IRC* [1967] 2 AC 291: automatic resulting trust of the option.
- *Westdeutsche Landesbank Girozentrale v Islington LBC* (Lord Browne-Wilkinson): abandonment leads to bona vacantia; resulting trusts and intention.
- *Air Jamaica Ltd v Charlton* [1999] 1 WLR 1399, 1412 (Lord Millett): a resulting trust responds to the absence of an intention to pass the beneficial interest to the recipient.
- *Re Gillingham Bus Disaster Fund* [1958] Ch 300; *Re West Sussex Constabulary* [1971] Ch 1: surpluses.
- *Re Flower's ST* [1957] 1 All ER 462: acceleration.

- Equality Act 2010 s 199 (abolition of advancement, not yet in force per the handout).

## Debate and critique
- **What is the role of intention in automatic resulting trusts?** Megarry J said intention is irrelevant ("automatic"); Lord Browne-Wilkinson in *Westdeutsche* said an intention to abandon displaces the resulting trust (bona vacantia). The handout asks whether Megarry J was right to ignore V's evident intention not to get the shares back, and whether Lord Millett's formulation in *Air Jamaica* ("absence of intention... to pass a beneficial interest to the recipient") explains things better.
- **Is Lord Denning's analysis convincing?** If the resulting trust simply "died" in 1961 when the new trust arose, why did V execute a deed in 1965 releasing his interest? And how can a new trust displace V's resulting interest without a disposition by V satisfying s 53(1)(c)? (Handout questions.)
- **Unjust enrichment theory (Birks and Chambers)**: resulting trusts reverse unjust enrichment, responding to a "non-beneficial transfer" (absence of intention to benefit the recipient), which could in principle extend to many unjust enrichment cases (e.g. mistaken payments).
- **Swadling ("Explaining resulting trusts" (2008) 124 LQR 72)**: the presumed resulting trust rests on a true *presumption of law* that the transferor *declared a trust in his own favour*, as in the seventeenth century. He attacks Birks/Chambers: gratuitous transfers outside advancement relationships are ambiguous, not "apparent gifts"; equity is not suspicious of gifts; "not-gift" is a legal conclusion, not a fact that can be presumed; and the law knows no "non-beneficial transfer" separate from declared trusts or security. Their extension to unjust enrichment generally is "unsustainable". For automatic resulting trusts there is no room for a presumption (all facts are proved, as Lords Upjohn and Wilberforce said in *Vandervell*), but the "retention" explanation also fails because the settlor retains nothing; so the automatic resulting trust "still defies legal analysis".
- **Mee, "Presumed resulting trusts, intention and declaration" (CLJ)**: rejects both Swadling and Chambers; the law reflects an old rule that on a voluntary transfer the fate of the beneficial interest depends on the transferor's intention; the presumption is of an intention to create a trust for the transferor, and it makes no difference if phrased negatively (no intention to pass the benefit), reflecting the historic idea of "retention".
- **Mee, "The past, present, and future of resulting trusts"**: the law rests on an old fiction that an owner holds a beneficial interest that may be *retained* when legal title passes; this is not doctrinally satisfying. He would abandon presumed resulting trusts, notes that the purchase-money resulting trust has already been made irrelevant by the common intention constructive trust (*Stack v Dowden*), but defends gap-filling (automatic) resulting trusts on a different justification.
- **Presumption of advancement**: gendered and outdated (Equality Act 2010 s 199), produces arbitrary results with illegality (*Tinker v Tinker*), and still applies to adult children (*Wood v Watkin*).
- **Mistaken payments**: Watt's "contrary entitlement" formulation versus the unjust enrichment view, which would readily see mistaken payments as non-beneficial transfers.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there an express trust that fails wholly or partly?** (Uncertainty, failed purpose, surplus, failed half-secret trust.) If yes: automatic resulting trust to the settlor/estate, unless (a) the settlor abandoned the money out and out (bona vacantia: *West Sussex*), (b) acceleration applies (*Re Flower*), or (c) the gift was by will without trust intention (the donee takes).
2. **Was there a gratuitous transfer with no stated intention?** Presumed resulting trust for the transferor. Can the transferee rebut it with evidence of gift (*Fowkes v Pascoe*) or a contract?
3. **Did someone contribute to the purchase price?** Purchase-money resulting trust in proportion to contributions, except for the family home (common intention constructive trust: *Stack*, *Jones v Kernott*; see constructive-trusts.md).
4. **Relationship giving advancement?** Husband to wife or fiancée, parent to child: presumed gift, rebuttable (*McGrath v Wallis*, *Lavelle*). Note s 199 and criticism.
5. **Illegality** in the background? Apply *Patel v Mirza*.
6. **Vandervell-type facts** (options, undefined trusts, trustee companies): identify any gap in beneficial ownership (automatic resulting trust), whether it was later filled by a new trust, and whether s 53(1)(c) bites.
7. **Traps**: calling every failed trust "presumed"; forgetting bona vacantia; using purchase-money resulting trusts for cohabitants' homes; treating a resulting trust as needing writing.
