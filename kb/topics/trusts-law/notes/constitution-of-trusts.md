# Constitution of trusts and imperfect gifts

## Overview
A trust is enforceable only once it is **constituted**: legal title to the trust property must be vested in the trustee as trustee. Otherwise trustees would owe duties over property they do not control. Where the settlor declares himself trustee, the declaration itself constitutes the trust; where property is to go to third-party trustees, the transfer must be completed according to the rules for that kind of property (deed and registration for land; stock transfer form and registration for shares). Testamentary trusts are constituted on death. Most problems arise where a trust (or gift) is clearly intended but the transfer goes wrong. The general rule is that equity will not assist a volunteer or perfect an imperfect gift (*Milroy v Lord*), but the rule has been relaxed (*Re Rose*, *Choithram*, *Pennington v Waine*) and has long-standing exceptions (*Strong v Bird*, *donationes mortis causa*). This is a high-yield topic for problem questions and a classic essay on certainty versus unconscionability.

## The law

### 1. Basic principles
1. **Constitution versus creation formalities**: do not confuse them. A trust of private company shares may be *declared* orally, but the *transfer* to trustees must satisfy the Stock Transfer Act 1963 and Companies Act 2006.
2. **Self-declaration**: no transfer needed; the declaration constitutes the trust (if intention is certain; see certainty-of-intention-and-subject-matter.md).
3. **Wills**: trusts under a will are constituted on the testator's death.
4. **Once constituted, a trust is irrevocable** (*Re Bowden* [1936] Ch 71) and enforceable by beneficiaries. An incompletely constituted trust is void; no trust exists until constitution.
5. **Gifts**: an outright gift needs intention, delivery (with any required formalities) and acceptance. A gratuitous promise is unenforceable without consideration; a completed gift needs no consideration.
6. **Maxims**: "equity will not assist a volunteer" (a volunteer is someone who gave no consideration) and "equity will not perfect an imperfect gift". Since beneficiaries are almost always volunteers, the maxims apply to trusts. If legal title *has* passed, the donee needs no help from equity.

### 2. Milroy v Lord: three ways to benefit a volunteer, and the bright line
- *Milroy v Lord* (1862) 4 De GF & J 264, Turner LJ at 274: a person may benefit another (1) by transferring the property to them outright; (2) by transferring it to a trustee for them; or (3) by declaring himself trustee of it. (1) and (2) need an effective transfer of legal title; (3) needs only a sufficiently certain declaration changing the capacity in which he holds.
- **The rule**: to make a voluntary settlement effective "the settlor must have done everything which, according to the nature of the property comprised in the settlement, was necessary to be done in order to transfer the property and render the settlement binding upon him".
- **No conversion**: equity will not treat a failed transfer as a declaration of trust (revision notes on Turner LJ). Facts: the settlor purported to transfer Bank of Louisiana shares to Lord on trust for Milroy's wife; the bank's rules required registration, which never happened (although dividends were received). The trust was not constituted.
- **Formalities by property**: land: deed (LPA 1925 s 52(1)) and, for registered land, entry on the register. Shares: at least a stock transfer form (Companies Act 2006 s 770(1); Stock Transfer Act 1963); private companies' articles typically require executed form, delivery of form and certificate to the transferee, delivery by the transferee to the company, and registration; directors may have power to refuse. Listed shares use CREST.
- *Re Fry* [1946] Ch 312 (revision 1.5): a transferor who signed forms but had not obtained a required Treasury consent had not done all in his power; no perfection.

### 3. Re Rose: "everything in his power"
- *Re Rose* [1952] Ch 499 (CA): Mr Rose executed share transfer forms and delivered them with certificates to the company in March; the company registered the transfer in June. Tax turned on whether he had divested himself before an April cut-off. Held: he had done all in his power in March, so beneficial ownership passed then and he held the legal title on trust until registration.
- Evershed MR (510): if the documents used are not apt to transfer the property, the court cannot reinterpret them as a declaration of trust; but if they are the proper way of transferring the property, a trust may arise to give effect to the transfer pending registration.
- The trust is not a self-declared trust (no intention to create one). Its nature is unclear: it has been argued to be a bare constructive trust, imposing no active duties but preventing the transferor dealing with the property inconsistently with the transfer.
- *Mascall v Mascall* (1985) 49 P & CR 119: applied to registered land. A father executed and handed his son a transfer on the Land Registry form; before the son registered, they fell out and the father tried to revoke. The son took in equity.

### 4. T Choithram International SA v Pagarani: equity will not "strive officiously to defeat a gift"
- [2001] 1 WLR 1 (PC): Mr Pagarani executed a deed setting up a charitable foundation of which he was one of several trustees and declared that all his wealth was to belong to it, but neither transferred assets to the other trustees nor declared himself trustee. Held: the gift to the foundation was effective. His vesting of the property in himself as one of the trustees bound him to hold it on trust for the foundation (the handout queries whether as a constructive trust).
- Rickett ("Completely constituting an inter vivos trust: property rules?" [2001] Conv 515) asks whether this falls between Milroy's categories: it resembles a self-declaration but in a multi-trustee setting, stretches the words used, and blurs the rule that a failed transfer is not converted into a declaration.

### 5. Pennington v Waine: unconscionability
- *Pennington v Waine (No 1)* [2002] EWCA Civ 227 (also cited as [2002] 1 WLR 2075). Ada Crampton signed a share transfer form for 400 shares in favour of her nephew Harold and told her accountant (Pennington) to take the steps needed to complete it. At her death the form had not been delivered to Harold or the company. Her will left Harold further shares; together they would have given him control.
- *Re Rose* could not apply on the majority view, because an agent's omissions are attributed to the principal, so Ada had not done all in her power (Clarke LJ, at [71]–[117], took a different view).
- Held: Harold took the shares. Arden LJ at [66]: it would have been unconscionable for Ada to recall the gift, so also unconscionable for her personal representatives to refuse to hand over the form; delivery was therefore unnecessary to perfect the gift.
- **Later cases**: *Curtis v Pulbrook* suggested unconscionability requires the donee's **detrimental reliance**, aligning the rule with proprietary estoppel. *Khan v Mahmood* [2021] EWHC 597 (Ch) held detrimental reliance is *not* necessary. The rule remains controversial.

### 6. Strong v Bird
- Applies where a donor makes an imperfect gift to someone who then becomes the donor's executor or administrator: vesting of legal title in them as personal representative perfects the gift in equity.
- *Strong v Bird* (1874) LR 18 Eq 315: Bird borrowed from his stepmother; repayment was by reduced rent; she later chose to pay full rent, intending to forgive the debt, but a release needed a deed. On her death £900 was still owed; Bird was her executor. Held: his appointment released the debt at law, and her continuing intention released it in equity.
- **Requirements**: (1) an intention to make an **immediate inter vivos** gift of **specific** property; (2) a **continuing** intention until death; (3) legal title vests in the donee as executor or administrator.
- *Re James* [1935] Ch 449: extends to administrators. John James, who inherited his father's house on intestacy, gave the deeds and furniture to Sarah (the long-serving unpaid housekeeper his father had promised the house to) and let her live there, but executed no conveyance. When John died intestate Sarah became joint administratrix and took the house.
- *Re Gonin* [1979] Ch 16: a daughter nursed her parents on the promise of the house. The mother, wrongly thinking she could not leave property by will to a daughter born before her marriage, wrote a cheque instead (uncleared at death). The daughter became administratrix but failed under *Strong v Bird*: there was no intention to make an immediate inter vivos gift of the house, and the cheque showed no continuing intention that she take the land.

### 7. Donationes mortis causa (DMC)
- A gift in contemplation of death, conditional on death, effective on death, independent of any will (the will cannot revoke it). A valid DMC raises a **constructive trust**: the personal representatives hold for the donee and equity compels completion.
- **No formalities**: not a conveyance (LPA s 52(1) does not apply); for land, the constructive trust falls within s 53(2) (*Sen v Headley* [1991] Ch 425); not testamentary, so Wills Act s 9 does not apply (*Moore v Darton* (1851) 4 De G & Sm 517). An anomalous survival from Roman law.
- **Requirements** (*Cain v Moon* [1896] 2 QB 283, 286, Lord Russell CJ; confirmed in *King v Dubrey* [2015] EWCA Civ 581):
  1. **Contemplation (not necessarily expectation) of death**: usually grave illness. Possibly a dangerous journey or military service (*Agnew v Belfast Banking Co* [1896] 2 IR 204); ordinary air travel risks are not enough (*Thompson v Mechan* [1958] OR 357, Canada); an 86-year-old confined to bed by feebleness sufficed (*Re Kirkley* (1909) 25 TLR 522). Dying of a different cause from the one contemplated does not matter (*Wilkes v Allington* [1931] 2 Ch 104: contemplated cancer, died of pneumonia). The gift lapses if death does not follow within a reasonable time: four months was acceptable in *Re Bogusz* [2013] EWHC 1449 (Ch) but too long in *King v Dubrey*, since the donor could have changed her will; similarly *Keeling v Keeling* [2017] WTLR 173 (donor ignored advice to make a will).
  2. **Delivery of the subject matter, or of dominion over it**, with intention to part with it on death (not for safekeeping). Delivery may precede the words (*Cain v Moon*). Symbolic delivery suffices: keys to a wardrobe containing safe keys (*Re Mustapha* (1891) 8 TLR 160); car keys even though the donor kept a spare (*Woodward v Woodward* [1995] 3 All ER 980). For choses in action, deliver "the essential indicia or evidence of title" (*Birch v Treasury Solicitor* [1951] Ch 298, 311: a Post Office Savings Bank book). Debts: mortgage deed (*Wilkes v Allington*) or receipt (*Moore v Darton*). Unregistered land: keys to a box of title deeds (*Sen v Headley*; followed in *Vallee v Birchwood* [2013] EWHC 1449). Registered land is an open question and arguably inconsistent with the Land Registration Act 2002. Shares: possible (*Staniland v Willott* (1852) 3 Mac & G 664; *Estate of Chen Soo King* [2013] HKEC 210). No delivery where the donor took back the key (*Bunn v Markham* (1816) 7 Taunt 224).
  3. **Conditional on death**: the property reverts if the donor recovers. May be express (*Dillon v Duffin* (1890) 44 Ch D 76) or inferred (*Re Lillingston* [1952] 2 All ER 184). A failed outright inter vivos gift is not a DMC.
- *Cain v Moon*: a daughter gave her mother a £50 deposit note in recognition of past nursing; later, gravely ill, she said "everything I possess and the bank-note is for you if I die"; valid DMC against the husband-administrator.

### 8. Covenants and other ways of vesting title (revision 1.5 and Wilde)
- Wilde ("The constitution of trusts: settlors constituting trusts by 'vesting' title without 'transferring' title", 2022) argues textbooks over-focus on the *Milroy* "own, declare, transfer" model. Settlors can constitute trusts by *vesting* property in trustees without owning or transferring it: creating property in the trustees' favour (*Fletcher v Fletcher* (1844) 4 Hare 67: a covenant by deed with trustees to pay £60,000 created a chose in action held by the trustees for the beneficiary, who recovered); buying property in the trustees' names (*Muggeridge v Stanton* (1859)); directing a transfer (*Vandervell v IRC*); or authorising trustees to collect property (*Re Bowden* [1936] Ch 71).

## Key authorities
- *Milroy v Lord* (1862) 4 De GF & J 264: three modes; everything necessary must be done; no conversion of failed gift into trust.
- *Re Fry* [1946] Ch 312: not everything done (consent outstanding).
- *Re Rose* [1952] Ch 499; *Mascall v Mascall* (1985) 49 P & CR 119: everything within the donor's power.
- *T Choithram International v Pagarani* [2001] 1 WLR 1 (PC): equity will not strive officiously to defeat a gift.
- *Pennington v Waine* [2002] EWCA Civ 227: unconscionability; *Curtis v Pulbrook*; *Khan v Mahmood* [2021] EWHC 597 (Ch).
- *Strong v Bird* (1874) LR 18 Eq 315; *Re James* [1935] Ch 449; *Re Gonin* [1979] Ch 16.
- *Cain v Moon* [1896] 2 QB 283; *King v Dubrey* [2015] EWCA Civ 581; *Sen v Headley* [1991] Ch 425; *Birch v Treasury Solicitor* [1951] Ch 298; *Wilkes v Allington* [1931] 2 Ch 104; *Woodward v Woodward* [1995].
- *Re Bowden* [1936] Ch 71: constituted trust irrevocable.
- *Fletcher v Fletcher* (1844) 4 Hare 67: trust of a covenant.
- LPA 1925 s 52(1), s 53(2); Companies Act 2006 s 770(1); Stock Transfer Act 1963.

## Debate and critique
- **Certainty versus conscience**: *Milroy*'s bright line gives predictability and protects donors from being bound before they have finished. *Re Rose* is a principled relaxation (the donor has done his part; only third-party steps remain). *Pennington* goes much further: the handout says it "seems to ride roughshod over several centuries of equity jurisprudence", and cites Halliwell ("Perfecting imperfect gifts and trusts: have we reached the end of the Chancellor's foot?" [2003] Conv 192): judges may use nebulous unconscionability to disapply *Milroy* at will. The split over detrimental reliance (*Curtis v Pulbrook* v *Khan v Mahmood*) shows the uncertainty.
- **What trust arises under Re Rose?** Garton ("The role of the trust mechanism in the rule in Re Rose" [2003] Conv 364) argues neither an express nor a constructive trust fits without a radical rethink of trust theory: an express trust lacks the necessary intention; a constructive trust imposes trustee duties and liability risks the transferor never chose. He notes Lowrie and Todd's proposal to drop the trust mechanism altogether (legal and equitable title might separate without a trust, by analogy with *Westdeutsche*). He sees *Pennington*'s unconscionability as a potentially flexible and theory-consistent mitigation of formalities, but warns courts must not create an unfettered, arbitrary discretion to perfect imperfect transactions. Other problems he flags: the register of members, and later transfers of legal title.
- **Choithram**: Rickett criticises it for blurring *Milroy*'s categories and stretching the donor's words; supporters see a sensible result where the donor was himself a trustee and plainly intended the gift. The slides invite you to consider whether you agree.
- **Are textbooks too narrow?** Wilde's "vesting" thesis (above) argues equity regularly assists volunteers where title is vested in trustees without a transfer by the settlor, which the *Milroy* orthodoxy obscures.
- **Comparative perspective**: Love ("Imperfect Gifts as Declarations of Trust: An Unapologetic Anomaly" (1978) 67 Ky LJ 309) examines, from a US perspective, the practice of treating imperfect gifts as declarations of trust, the very conversion *Milroy* forbids in England.
- **Policy of exceptions**: *Strong v Bird* and DMCs are criticised as anomalous (DMCs as a Roman survival that sidesteps Wills Act formalities; *King v Dubrey* signals a stricter modern approach), yet both have clear requirements, unlike *Pennington*.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Which *Milroy* mode was intended?** Outright gift, transfer to trustees, or self-declaration? If self-declaration, check certainty of intention (and do not convert a failed gift into a declaration).
2. **What formalities apply to that property?** Land: deed plus registration. Shares: form, certificate, company registration (check articles, directors' discretion). Chattels: delivery. Equitable interests: s 53(1)(c) writing.
3. **Was the transfer complete?** If yes, constituted and irrevocable. If no:
4. **Re Rose**: had the donor done everything *he* had to do, leaving only third-party steps (registration)? Agents' failures count against the donor. Any outstanding consent (*Re Fry*)?
5. **Choithram**: is the donor one of several trustees to whom he purported to give the property?
6. **Pennington**: would it be unconscionable for the donor to resile (e.g. was the donee told of the gift, did he act on it?)? Discuss whether detrimental reliance is needed (*Curtis* v *Khan*) and the criticism.
7. **Strong v Bird**: did the donee become executor or administrator? Was there an intention of an *immediate* gift of *specific* property, continuing to death (*Re Gonin* failure points)?
8. **DMC**: contemplation of death, death within a reasonable time, delivery of dominion (actual, symbolic, indicia of title), conditional on death. Registered land is doubtful.
9. **Outcome**: if all fail, the property stays with the donor (or estate); intended beneficiaries, as volunteers, have no claim.
10. **Traps**: confusing declaration formalities with transfer formalities; applying *Re Rose* where the donor's agent had not finished; forgetting that DMCs need no Wills Act compliance but do need delivery.
