# The trust in an international context

## Overview
English trust law applies only in England and Wales, yet the trust is now used worldwide: in other UK jurisdictions, Crown dependencies and overseas territories, former colonies, the United States, offshore financial centres, civil law and mixed systems and even Islamic law jurisdictions. This topic (week 20) looks at how the trust spread, the statute-based "international" or "offshore" model, the Hague Convention 1985 on recognition, the Italian *trust interno*, trust-like tools in civil law systems, and the debate over whether a trust needs equity at all. It is mainly essay material, but it also explains why English courts increasingly meet foreign-law trusts and why offshore case law influences English law.

## The law

### 1. Where English trust law applies, and how it spread
1. English trust law governs England and Wales. **Scotland and Northern Ireland** have their own trust law.
2. **Crown dependencies**: the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands (Jersey, Guernsey) have their own legal systems. The Channel Islands were part of the Duchy of Normandy until the French Crown took the mainland in 1204; they kept customary law (different in Jersey and Guernsey). The monarch is head of state as Lord of Mann or Duke of Normandy.
3. **Colonies** inherited English law as it stood on settlement and then developed separately unless Westminster legislated for them. Hence abolished English statutes can survive abroad: the Statute of Uses 1535 remains in force in some US states. Fourteen remaining colonies are now British Overseas Territories (e.g. Anguilla, Bermuda, Cayman Islands, Gibraltar, Turks and Caicos).
4. The **Privy Council** (Judicial Committee) was the single final court for these jurisdictions, applying the law of the place of origin; it kept that role after independence for a time (Canada until 1949, Australia until 1986, New Zealand until 2003). Since 2005 members of the Caribbean Community have their own final court in civil matters.

### 2. The "English trust" v the "international" or "offshore" trust
- The English trust is mainly case-law based. The **international model** is mainly statutory, emerged in the last two decades of the twentieth century in colonies, former colonies and Crown territories engaged in offshore finance, and has spread to civil law (Malta, San Marino), Islamic (Dubai, Bahrain) and mixed systems.
- **Why offshore jurisdictions legislated**: unclear principles in English case law; gaps in English law; English rules outdated for modern uses and an international clientele.
- **Common features** (handout): limiting trustee liability to the trust fund; powers reserved to the settlor or given to non-trustees; extending protective trusts to beneficiaries' rights over capital; validating non-charitable purpose trusts; limiting foreign succession and creditor laws; **protectors**; registration; longer duration; confidentiality; a resident trustee; licensing of professional trustees.
- English law has borrowed some solutions: trustee delegation, remuneration, investment powers, duration and the accumulation period.
- There is now competition between offshore centres, so the "unity" of the international model is doubtful. There is a judicial **dialogue**: Jersey and Cayman judgments are adopted by English courts and English rules are adopted offshore; small jurisdictions often appoint English judges temporarily. Yet the same dispute need not be decided the same way: Jersey judges have stressed differences from England.

### 3. Examples of offshore statutory innovation (revision 13, drawing on Hayton and Hargreaves)
- **Jersey**: its trust centre dates from the Victorian era (retiring imperialists and wealthy English settlors attracted by lower taxes and climate). The Trusts (Jersey) Law 1984 "recognized and clarified, but did not codify" existing practice (Hargreaves). The Jersey trust is in substance the English Chancery trust set within Norman-French property law, and Jersey's trust law does not use the words "equity" or "equitable". *West v Lazards* [1993] JLR 165 is cited as showing Jersey courts' pro-beneficiary stance. Section 47H of the Jersey law (as revision 13 cites it) is described as keeping a wider version of the rule in *Hastings-Bass* than English law after *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26.
- **Cayman Islands**: exempted trusts from the 1960s (Trusts Law 2011 Revision s 83 described as vesting beneficiaries' rights in the Registrar); s 90 permitting retrospective rectification, applied in *Re Goldentrust* [2012] (Grand Court).
- **Bermuda**: Trustee Act s 47 described as letting the court confer powers on trustees for expedient transactions, contrasted with the consent-based Variation of Trusts Act 1958 in England.
- Hayton reports a 2015 study (Hofri-Winogradow) that 36 per cent of trusts used a foreign governing law, and predicts more use of, and changes to, more flexible foreign governing laws.

### 4. The United States
- Trusts are not federal: each state (as each Canadian province and Australian state) has its own trust law, which has diverged from English law. Most current states were not English colonies.
- Unification efforts: the Uniform Trust Code and American Law Institute Restatements (Trusts; Restitution), often used by judges to fill gaps.
- State competition for clients: "dynasty trusts" of unlimited duration and self-settled protective trusts. Active states include Delaware, South Dakota, Alaska and Wisconsin.

### 5. Trusts in jurisdictions without equity
- The handout rejects the "widely held" view that trusts are incompatible with civil law as based on misunderstandings since the late nineteenth century. From the twelfth century European civil law developed testamentary tools that helped Chancery build the trust; that civilian influence makes trusts intelligible in Romanist and mixed systems.
- Lack of equity is a real but surmountable obstacle: Jersey, Scotland, Cyprus (which imported equitable principles by statute) and San Marino all operate trusts. San Marino, with no link to English law, introduced the trust while keeping its civil law foundations.

### 6. The Hague Convention 1985
- *Convention on the Law Applicable to Trusts and on their Recognition*, signed 1 July 1985.
- **First aim**: choice of law. Revision 13 (citing Hayton) notes the settlor may choose the governing law (Art 6), failing which the law with the closest connection applies (Art 7). This mattered to common law systems that lacked rules on these issues.
- **Second aim**: **recognition**. Contracting states must recognise trusts governed by a foreign law (Art 11), even if they have no domestic trust law. So England can have trusts governed by Jersey law, and Italy, with no domestic trust law, can have trusts governed by foreign law.
- Revision 13 notes UK implementation by the Recognition of Trusts Act 1987 and the public policy exception in Art 18 (provisions may be disregarded if manifestly incompatible with public policy).
- Gretton criticises the preamble's description of the trust as developed in courts of equity, but approves the operative definition based on assets placed under a trustee's control as a separate fund, which suits non-equity systems.

### 7. The Italian *trust interno*
- Italy ratified the Convention in 1989 (in force 1992, per Lupoi). The unexpected result: trusts with Italian settlors, Italian assets and Italian beneficiaries, governed by a foreign law. Within a few years the trust became common in Italian practice; Lupoi reports almost 200 Italian court decisions in a decade, including replacing trustees and approving variations under English or Jersey law. Initial academic reaction was mostly negative.
- Italians often prefer the trust laws of civil or mixed jurisdictions (San Marino, Jersey) that fit their own tradition. The phenomenon is a "living comparative law laboratory" (Lupoi).

### 8. Trust-like tools
- **Civil law**: pre-codification tools surviving today, e.g. *hereus* or *legatari de confianza* in Catalan succession law (Law No 40 of 30 December 1991), the *Compilación de Navarra*, *fiducia successoria* in Aragón (Law No 1 of 1999), *fideicomiso* in Latin America, and recent laws in Argentina, Uruguay, Catalonia, Luxembourg, Romania, Hungary and the Czech Republic.
- **France – fiducie** (Civil Code reform 2007): a *constituant* transfers assets to a *fiduciaire* for a *bénéficiaire*; the fiduciary has legal ownership and acts on the constituant's instructions. Similar to, but structurally distinct from, a trust.
- **Québec** (mixed system, French-based civil code): treats the trust fund as an autonomous estate (a kind of legal person) rather than assets owned by the trustee.
- **Waqf** (Islamic law): property permanently dedicated to religious, charitable or educational purposes, managed by a custodian. The handout suggests the waqf probably influenced the English trust after the Crusades, though it focuses on perpetual charity rather than individual beneficiaries.

## Key authorities
- Hague Convention on the Law Applicable to Trusts and on their Recognition (1 July 1985): Arts 6, 7 (applicable law), 11 (recognition), 18 (public policy), as reported in revision 13.
- Recognition of Trusts Act 1987: UK implementation (revision 13).
- Trusts (Jersey) Law 1984; Cayman Trusts Law (2011 Revision) ss 83, 90; Bermuda Trustee Act s 47 (as described by Hayton in revision 13).
- *West v Lazards* [1993] JLR 165 (Jersey): pro-beneficiary approach.
- *Re Goldentrust* [2012] (Cayman Grand Court): retrospective rectification under s 90.
- *Webb v Webb* (Case C-294/92) [1994] ECR I-1717: per revision 13 (citing Gretton), the Court of Justice held that an English trust dispute over French property was not an action *in rem* under Art 16 of the Brussels Convention, treating the beneficial interest as a personal right.
- *Pitt v Holt* [2013] UKSC 26: English restriction of *Hastings-Bass*, contrasted with Jersey (see variation-of-trusts.md).
- *Zhang Hong Li v DBS Bank (Hong Kong) Ltd* (HKCFA): upheld anti-*Bartlett* clauses (Fee).
- *Byers v Saudi National Bank* (CA 2022): knowing receipt needs actual beneficial receipt; discussed by Ashdown in the context of non-trust jurisdictions (see knowing-receipt.md).

## Debate and critique
- **Does a trust need equity?** Gretton ("Trusts without Equity", 2000) argues not. Scots law has known the trust since the seventeenth century and fitted it into an almost pure *ius commune* property system with no legal/equitable split: the trustee owns, and the beneficiary has personal rights enforceable against a **special patrimony** (Stair's *Institutions* (1681): the property is "in the person of the intrusted"). He also points to Roman *fiducia cum amico*. The trust as segregated fund is the core, not divided ownership.
- **Civilian roots**: Lupoi ("Trust and confidence" (2009) LQR) argues that the *ius commune* theory of *confidentia* enriched English practice in the second half of the fifteenth century, supplying principles on which the English built the modern trust. He notes the Statute of Uses' wording "use, confidence or trust" and Coke's maxim that a court of conscience judges "covin, accident and breach of confidence". This undercuts Gierke's "I cannot understand your trust".
- **Competition and regulatory arbitrage**: offshore liberalisation (protectors, reserved powers, purpose trusts, limited liability, confidentiality) serves international clients and tax planning. Critics may ask whether a trust so heavily settlor-controlled remains a trust. Fee ("Trust-owned companies and the irreducible core") argues for an *irreducible core* of minimum duties needed to keep the concept intact, distinguishing core from merely mandatory duties after anti-*Bartlett* clauses were upheld in Hong Kong.
- **Commercial evolution**: Zhang ("The new role trusts play in modern financial markets") traces the trust from family guardian to commercial "entrepreneur", especially in China, attributing its appeal to flexibility, independent trust property, separation of control and benefit, the ability to get round legal obstacles and advantages over companies. Compare Hansmann and Mattei on asset partitioning (introduction-to-the-trust.md).
- **Litigation across borders**: Ashdown ("Attacks on trusts in civil law jurisdictions", 2023) explains that when trust assets end up in a non-trust jurisdiction, tracing and third-party claims raise jurisdiction, choice of law and, more fundamentally, property questions: whether the defendant took an unimpeachable title under the local property law.
- **Shift in authority**: the handout concludes that England's primacy is no longer absolute; Jersey, Cayman and others are hubs of complex trust litigation shaping trust jurisprudence.

## Applying it to a problem question
Problem questions are unlikely to be purely international, but if facts involve foreign elements:
1. **Which law governs the trust?** Look for an express choice; otherwise the closest connection (Hague Convention Arts 6–7 as summarised in revision 13).
2. **Will the forum recognise it?** Contracting states must recognise foreign-law trusts (Art 11), subject to public policy (Art 18).
3. **Are offshore features valid?** Protectors, reserved settlor powers, purpose trusts or wide exemption clauses may be valid under the governing law yet raise questions about the irreducible core of trusteeship.
4. **Third-party and tracing claims abroad**: consider whether the local property law gives the recipient a clean title (Ashdown).
5. **Essay use**: compatibility of trusts with civil law; whether equity is essential; whether offshore competition is desirable; the influence of offshore courts on English law.
