# Remedies for breach of trust (non-fiduciary duties)

## Overview
A "breach of trust" is a failure to perform the duties imposed by law or the trust instrument, or acting beyond authority, whether deliberate or inadvertent, by act or omission. (Breach of fiduciary duty, with its account of profits, is covered in fiduciary-duties.md.) Remedies fall into three groups: personal claims against the trustee, proprietary claims, and personal claims against third parties. This note concerns the first group, plus the defences, relief and limitation rules that apply. The central modern issue is how far causation and common law style compensation principles apply to equitable compensation.

## The law
**1. Two types of breach and two forms of compensation.**
- Unauthorised action (outside powers, for example paying away trust money or an unauthorised investment): the trustee must restore the fund as if the transaction had not occurred ("substitutive compensation" or "falsifying the account"). Where an unauthorised act produces a profit, there is no financial remedy for the beneficiaries to claim loss.
- Failure of skill and care (acting within powers but carelessly): the trustee compensates the difference between the actual result and what proper performance would have achieved ("reparative compensation" or "surcharging the account").

**2. Loss is required.** A breach without loss gives no remedy, nor does a loss not attributable to the breach (for example ordinary investment risk). Liability is to the trust fund, not to individual beneficiaries, so beneficiaries' tax effects are ignored (*Bartlett v Barclays Bank Trust Co (No 2)* [1980] 2 All ER 92). Proving loss from careless investment needs a firm comparator. Losses and gains offset only if they arise from the same unauthorised transaction, and the portfolio as a whole is likely to be viewed as a whole.

**3. Practical point.** Personal liability is only worth pursuing if the trustee has funds or insurance; otherwise consider proprietary claims or third-party claims (see tracing-and-proprietary-claims.md, dishonest-assistance.md and knowing-receipt.md). Corporate trustees with limited liability are common.

**4. Substitutive compensation and causation.**
- Traditionally causation and remoteness were not applied to misapplication of trust property, and the date of breach fixed the measure.
- *Target Holdings v Redferns* [1995] 3 All ER 785: solicitors released mortgage funds held on bare trust before the necessary documents were executed; the property's value had been inflated. The Court of Appeal treated it as straightforward payment away in breach. The House of Lords (Lord Browne-Wilkinson) disagreed: for a trust that is a bare trust in a commercial transaction, causation applies; the claimant had to show loss that would not have happened but for the breach, and Target got what it bargained for (a first charge). Quantum is assessed at the date of judgment, not breach ("stopping the clock" is unjustified), and once causation is established remoteness rules do not apply: losses must be those which on a common-sense view of causation were caused by the breach (adopting *Canson Enterprises v Broughton* (1991) 85 DLR (4th) 129, McLachlin J). Reconstitution of the fund is required only where the trust is continuing with several beneficiaries.
- *AIB Group (UK) plc v Mark Redler & Co* [2014] UKSC 58: solicitors failed to redeem the whole of the prior mortgage; the bank obtained only a second charge. The Supreme Court applied *Target*: compensation is the loss to the trust fund caused by the breach on a but-for basis (the bank recovered only the amount that had to be paid to the first mortgagee). A solicitor who holds money on bare trust as part of a commercial transaction can be required to restore it until the transaction completes, but not after. Hui Jing and Han Xiao (Conv 2024) say the *AIB* rule means equitable compensation is unavailable where the breach caused no loss to the fund, and argue that Jackson LJ in *Various Claimants v Giambrone & Law* [2017] EWCA Civ 1193 departed from the *AIB* causation test despite saying he followed it; the materials-era literature on *Giambrone* had focused on his active/passive duty distinction.

**5. Reparative compensation.** Assessed at the time of trial. But-for causation is needed, then the question is whether remoteness limits recovery. Millett LJ in *Bristol & West v Mothew* [1998] Ch 1, 17: equitable compensation for breach of the duty of skill and care resembles damages and common law rules of causation, remoteness and measure may be applied by analogy; this should be distinguished from compensation for breach of fiduciary duty, which may be awarded in lieu of rescission or specific restitution.

**6. Several trustees.** Trustees are in principle liable only for their own breaches (*Townley v Sherborn* (1634) J Bridg 35), but equity demands unanimity and does not tolerate a sleeping trustee. A passive trustee may breach the duty of care by leaving matters with a co-trustee without inquiry, doing nothing despite knowing of a breach, letting funds be in a co-trustee's sole control, or failing to seek redress. Indemnity from a co-trustee is possible in limited cases, such as a solicitor-trustee who misled a lay trustee (*Re Partington* (1887) 57 LT 654), but only if the claimant acted merely on the other's advice and control (*Head v Gould* [1898] 2 Ch 250). Co-trustees liable for the same breach are jointly and severally liable (*Bishopsgate Investment Management v Maxwell (No 2)* [1994] 1 All ER 261), with contribution under the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978 s 2: what is "just and equitable" having regard to responsibility, with power to exempt or order a complete indemnity.

**7. Consent and acquiescence.** A beneficiary who is an adult with capacity, and gives informed consent, exercising independent judgment, before or after the breach, cannot sue and may indemnify the trustee. If the trustees themselves did not know they were committing a breach, the beneficiary need not know either (*Holder v Holder* [1968] 1 All ER 665). Consent is ineffective if it should have been obvious the beneficiary was not exercising independent judgment: *Re Pauling's Settlement Trusts* [1964] Ch 303 (bank advanced capital to adult children knowing the parents used it for their own purposes; children under parental influence). Where only some beneficiaries consented, the court may impound the consenting beneficiary's interest to indemnify the trustee (Trustee Act 1925 s 62(1)).

**8. Exemption clauses.** *Armitage v Nurse* [1998] Ch 241: a clause excusing trustees from liability for loss unless caused by their own "actual fraud" was valid. Millett LJ: there is an "irreducible core" of obligations fundamental to the trust, but it consists of honesty and good faith, not skill, care or prudence. He noted that views were that such clauses had gone too far but left reform to Parliament. The Law Commission's 2006 report (Report No 301) decided against legislating, and clauses excluding even gross negligence remain valid; the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 does not invalidate them. Solicitor-trustees cannot rely on a belief in beneficiaries' interests that no reasonable solicitor-trustee could hold (*Walker v Stones* [2001] QB 902). "Anti-Bartlett" clauses exclude the duty from *Bartlett v Barclays Bank Trust Co* [1980] Ch 515 to engage in the running of a company the trustees control.

**9. Statutory relief (Trustee Act 1925 s 61).** The court may relieve a trustee wholly or partly if he acted honestly and reasonably and ought fairly to be excused, including for not seeking the court's directions. Reasonableness is objective; for professional trustees it may include following industry practice, a higher threshold than for lay trustees (*Perrins v Bellamy* [1898] 2 Ch 521; *Lloyds TSB Bank v Markandan & Uddin* [2012] EWCA Civ 65).

**10. Limitation.** Limitation Act 1980 s 21(3): six years from breach. No time limit for fraudulent breach or recovery of trust property or its proceeds from the trustee (s 21(1)). Time does not run for future interests until they fall into possession (s 23(1)); concealment postpones time until discovery (s 32(1)(b)). Laches applies only where no statutory period governs.

## Key authorities
- *Target Holdings v Redferns* [1995]; *AIB v Redler* [2014] UKSC 58: causation and quantum.
- *Bristol & West v Mothew* [1998] Ch 1: negligence compensation analogous to damages.
- *Armitage v Nurse* [1998] Ch 241: exemption clauses and the irreducible core.
- *Re Pauling's Settlement Trusts* [1964]: consent must be independent.
- *Bartlett v Barclays* [1980]: duty to supervise controlled company; compensation and tax.
- *Bishopsgate v Maxwell (No 2)*: joint and several liability.
- Trustee Act 1925 ss 61, 62; Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978 s 2; Limitation Act 1980 ss 21, 23, 32.

## Debate and critique
- **Has equity assimilated into tort?** *Target* and *AIB* import but-for causation and a common sense approach into equitable compensation, replacing the strict restoration of the fund. the materials questions whether this applies to all trusts or only to commercial bare trusts, and *Giambrone* commentary suggests lower courts apply the *AIB* rule inconsistently.
- **Exemption clauses.** Critics say clauses that exclude gross negligence defeat the point of paid professional trusteeship; Millett LJ accepted the criticism but left it to Parliament; the Law Commission declined reform. the materials asks whether the higher standard for solicitor-trustees should apply to all professionals, and warns that anti-Bartlett clauses may reduce the trust to a shell.
- **Date of assessment.** Date of trial (not breach) avoids compensating a loss never actually suffered, but weakens the deterrent function of strict restoration.
- **Section 61.** Wide discretion mostly used for reasonable mistakes, often following wrong professional advice; professionals face a stricter reasonableness test.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the breach: unauthorised act or negligent performance? Identify the duty (investment, care, supervision).
2. Show loss to the fund and identify a comparator (what a proper investor would have achieved).
3. Causation: but-for (*Target*, *AIB*); distinguish a continuing multi-beneficiary trust (restore the fund) from a completed commercial bare trust.
4. Identify defendants: each trustee, passive trustees, indemnity and contribution (1978 Act).
5. Defences: consent or acquiescence (capacity, independence, information); exemption clause (scope; dishonesty; solicitor-trustee); s 61; limitation (fraud and recovery of trust property exceptions); laches.
6. If trustees are insolvent, move to proprietary claims or third-party liability.
7. Trap: do not apply the strict profit rules from fiduciary breach to a mere negligence breach, and do not forget that liability runs to the fund.
