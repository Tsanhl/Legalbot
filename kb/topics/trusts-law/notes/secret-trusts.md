# Secret trusts

## Overview
A secret trust arises where a testator leaves property by will to a legatee who has agreed, outside the will, to hold it for someone else. In a **fully secret trust (FST)** the will shows an absolute gift; in a **half-secret trust (HST)** the will says the legatee takes as trustee but does not name the beneficiaries. Neither complies with Wills Act 1837 s 9 (read with s 1, which brings every testamentary disposition within the Act), yet both are enforced. Secret trusts began as a way to keep dispositions private, for example for illegitimate children or to avoid religious persecution, and are still used for privacy or by indecisive testators. They are a favourite topic because the requirements differ between FSTs and HSTs and because the theoretical basis is weak and much criticised.

## The law

### 1. Why they are enforced (two orthodox justifications)
1. **Fraud**: equity will not allow a statute to be used as an instrument of fraud; a legatee who agreed to hold on trust cannot rely on the Wills Act to keep the property.
2. **Dehors the will**: the trust is said to arise outside the will, from communication and acceptance during the testator's life, so the Wills Act does not apply.
- *Blackwell v Blackwell* [1929] AC 318, Viscount Sumner (334): a court of conscience finds a legal owner under a valid will and, on proof of the testator's intention, communication and the legatee's acquiescence, will not let him do what he likes with his own; equity "fastens on the conscience of the legatee a trust". The will operates first according to its terms; equity then makes the legatee apply the property as directed.

### 2. Fully secret trusts – requirements
**Ottaway v Norman** [1971] 3 All ER 1325, Brightman J (1332): the essential elements are (i) the testator's intention to subject the primary donee to an obligation in favour of the secondary donee; (ii) communication of that intention to the primary donee; (iii) acceptance by the primary donee, expressly or by acquiescence. It does not matter whether these come before or after the will. Plus the property must actually pass to the legatee (usually by the will); until then the trust is not constituted and is revocable. (Revision 2 records the facts: a bungalow left to the testator's housekeeper, who had agreed to leave it to his son; the trust was enforced.)

**(a) Intention**: there must be an intention to impose a *legally binding* obligation, not a moral one (essentially certainty of intention).
- *Re Snowden* [1979] Ch 528: Ethel left residue to her brother Bert, telling him he would "know what to do" and be "fair to everybody" among nephews and nieces; Bert died six days later leaving everything to his son. Held: only a moral obligation, so no secret trust; Bert (and then his son) took free of trust. Penner notes Megarry VC held that where there is no actual fraud the ordinary civil standard (balance of probabilities) applies, and refers to *Titcombe v Ison* (2021) as a recent case finding at most a moral obligation.
- FSTs are hard to prove without evidence (*Re Mattingley* [2021] EWHC 3353 (Ch)).

**(b) Communication**: must happen in the testator's lifetime.
- A note found after death is not communication: *Wallgrave v Tebbs* (1855) 2 K & J 313; the legatee takes absolutely.
- If the legatee is told he is to be a trustee but not told the terms or beneficiaries, communication fails, but his conscience is affected: he holds on resulting trust for the estate (*Re Boyes* (1884) 26 Ch D 531).
- A sealed letter handed over in life, marked to be opened after death, is enough if the recipient knows it contains the trust terms (*Re Keen* [1937] Ch 236).
- **Several legatees** (*Re Stead* [1900] 1 Ch 237):
  - tenants in common: only those to whom the trust was communicated are bound, whenever communicated;
  - joint tenants, communication after the will: only those told are bound;
  - joint tenants, communication before or at execution to one of them: all are bound.
  Penner notes Farwell J in *Re Stead* doubted any good reason for these rules but accepted them.

**(c) Acceptance**: may be informal; silent acquiescence suffices (*Moss v Cooper* (1861) 1 J & H 352). If the legatee refuses, or accepts and then withdraws in the testator's lifetime with the withdrawal communicated, he takes absolutely. Penner adds that the imposition of a merely moral obligation is insufficient (*McCormick v Grogan*; *Re Snowden*).

**(d) Failure of an FST**
- Trust not proved: legatee takes beneficially.
- Only the terms were not communicated: resulting trust for the residuary estate (*Re Boyes*).
- Secret trustee dies before the testator: the gift lapses, so the FST fails (*Re Maddock* [1902] 2 Ch 220); the property falls into residue. If the trustee dies after the testator the trust survives (equity will not let a trust fail for want of a trustee).

**(e) Land**: whether an FST of land must satisfy LPA 1925 s 53(1)(b), or is a constructive trust within s 53(2), is unresolved by *Ottaway v Norman* (Penner notes the point was not argued there; *Re Baillie* (1886) accepted obiter that formalities would apply to an HST of land).

### 3. Half-secret trusts – requirements
The will shows the legatee is a trustee but gives no details. Intention is clear from the will; the key requirement is communication (and acceptance).
- **Timing**: communication must be before or at execution of the will; later communication is void (*Blackwell v Blackwell*). Viscount Sumner (339): a testator "cannot reserve to himself a power of making future unwitnessed dispositions by merely naming a trustee and leaving the purposes of the trust to be supplied afterwards"; otherwise the Wills Act would be given "the go-by". (The gift was £12,000 to trustees for "the purposes indicated by me to them"; revision 2 notes it benefited a mistress and child.)
- *Re Bateman's WT* [1970] 3 All ER 817: a gift to persons "as shall be stated by me in a sealed letter" (i.e. in future) failed. Penner notes the post-execution rule is "generally regarded as settled, if wrong".
- **Consistency with the will**: *Re Keen* [1937] Ch 236 (CA): £10,000 to two people on trust for those "as may be notified by me to them or either of them during my lifetime". Although the testator had in fact told one trustee before execution, the will contemplated future notification and so conflicted with the evidence; the trust failed.
- Evidence cannot contradict the will: in *Re Rees* (1950) evidence that named trustees were to keep the surplus conflicted with their description as trustees, and the surplus went on intestacy (Penner). Pennycuick J doubted this reasoning obiter in *Re Tyler's Fund Trusts* (1967); *Rawstron v Freud* (2014) rejected the "public interest" objection in *Re Rees* to solicitors taking beneficially and admitted the solicitor's evidence of an FST.
- **Several trustees**: *Re Stead* applies in theory, but trustees named in a will take as joint tenants and communication must be before execution, so in practice communication to one binds all.
- **Failure of an HST**: resulting trust for the residuary estate, because the will shows the legatee was never meant to take beneficially. If the trustee predeceases the testator the HST does **not** fail: the will names him trustee and equity will not let a trust fail for want of a trustee.

### 4. Key differences
| Issue | Fully secret | Half-secret |
|---|---|---|
| Face of will | absolute gift | gift on trust |
| Intention | must show legal not moral obligation (*Re Snowden*) | apparent from will |
| Communication | any time before death | before or at execution (*Blackwell*), consistent with will (*Re Keen*) |
| Failure | legatee takes beneficially (unless terms alone uncommunicated: resulting trust) | resulting trust for residue |
| Trustee predeceases testator | fails; property to residue | trust upheld |

### 5. Consequences of the dehors theory in the cases
- *Re Young* (1951): a chauffeur who witnessed the will could still take under an HST, despite Wills Act s 15 (gifts to attesting witnesses fail), because he did not take "under the will" (Danckwerts J).
- *Re Gardner (No 2)* [1923] 2 Ch 230: a secret beneficiary who predeceased the testatrix did not lapse; her interest passed to her estate (Romer J), on the view that the trust was inter vivos. Penner and Siu say it was wrongly decided.

## Key authorities
- Wills Act 1837 ss 1, 9, 15.
- *Blackwell v Blackwell* [1929] AC 318 (HL): HSTs enforced; communication before or at execution; the classic statement of the fraud and dehors reasoning.
- *Ottaway v Norman* [1971] 3 All ER 1325: the three elements for FSTs.
- *Re Snowden* [1979] Ch 528: moral v legal obligation; civil standard of proof absent fraud.
- *McCormick v Grogan* (1869) (HL): early fraud theory; ambiguous as to what fraud is required (Penner).
- *Re Keen* [1937] Ch 236: sealed letter; inconsistency with the will.
- *Re Boyes* (1884); *Wallgrave v Tebbs* (1855); *Moss v Cooper* (1861); *Re Stead* [1900]; *Re Maddock* [1902]; *Re Bateman's WT* [1970]; *Re Young* (1951); *Re Gardner (No 2)* [1923]; *Re Rees* (1950); *Rawstron v Freud* (2014).
- Historical: *Reech v Kennegal* (1748) and *Re Snowden* cited for secret trusts read into situations where a legatee fails to carry out the testator's wishes.

## Debate and critique
- **The fraud theory**
  - Narrow version: equity intervenes only if the legatee fraudulently induced the gift (Penner's reading of one strand of *McCormick v Grogan*). Wide version: any refusal to perform the agreement is "fraud". The law has followed the wide version: Lord Sterndale MR in *Re Gardner* (1920) said it does not matter whether one calls it breach of trust or fraud.
  - **Fits FSTs, not HSTs**: an HST trustee cannot keep the property (resulting trust), so there is no risk of personal enrichment. *Blackwell* relocated the fraud to the beneficiaries. Penner (citing Sheridan) calls this **circular**: there is only a fraud on the beneficiaries if they are already validly entitled, which is the question. Siu agrees the orthodox reading cannot justify HSTs.
  - Challinor ("Debunking the myth of secret trusts?" [2005] Conv 492): the extended notion of fraud is too mild to justify overriding strict statutory formality in the way real bad faith would.
  - Leung ("Equity as a nest") defends the fraud theory on a "principle-based" approach: prevention of fraud is a legal principle that can justify and reconcile FSTs and HSTs, making the dehors theory unnecessary.
- **The dehors theory**
  - Penner: it should be called the "dehors the Wills Act" theory, and it is "fundamentally unsound". Secret trusts *are* testamentary: they are revocable and ambulatory (the testator can change his will so the property never arrives), and an inter vivos declaration would be a trust of future property, which is invalid. A pecuniary legacy cannot be segregated in advance. Challinor and Siu make similar points; Siu notes Critchley's criticism. The theory is "an attempt to cloak the embarrassing jam equity has got itself into" (Penner).
  - Penner suggests the true reason for *Blackwell* is pragmatic: Viscount Sumner asked why equity should give effect to the testator's wishes in one case and frustrate them in the other "over a mere matter of words".
- **Constructive trust and "loss of opportunity"**: Siu ("Keeping Secrets" (2015) 3 NE L Rev 75) argues secret trusts are best explained as constructive trusts imposed because of unconscionability: the testator relied on the legatee's promise and, being dead, lost the opportunity to make other arrangements (an idea he links to Gardner and Waters). This fits s 53(2) for land. Siu himself warns it could spread beyond secret trusts, since most formal failures are discovered after death.
- **Abolition**: Griffin ("The need for the abolition of secret trusts", 2017) argues both constitutional theories fail and the pragmatic reasons (usage, fraud prevention, certainty) do not justify enforcement; enforcement even *increases* fraud risk by encouraging informal arrangements. Challinor argues the original reasons for secrecy are obsolete and other private devices exist. Penner would enforce them only where a testator was genuinely fraudulently induced; inter vivos trusts, joint tenancy and insurance nominations already allow secret post-death gifts.
- **Policy point**: Penner argues equity lets a testator make an informal will merely by using a human "vessel", whereas an unattested paper cannot "commit fraud" though it serves the same function.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Read the will**: absolute gift (FST) or gift on trust without beneficiaries (HST)?
2. **Intention**: legal obligation or mere hope or moral wish ("you'll know what to do")? Cite *Re Snowden* for FSTs.
3. **Communication**: what was communicated, when, to whom? FST: any time before death, including a sealed letter handed over (*Re Keen*). HST: before or at execution and consistent with the will's wording (*Blackwell*, *Re Keen*, *Re Bateman*). Were both the existence *and* the terms communicated (*Re Boyes*)?
4. **Several trustees**: tenants in common or joint tenants? Before or after execution (*Re Stead*)?
5. **Acceptance**: express or silent (*Moss v Cooper*)? Any withdrawal communicated in life?
6. **Events after communication**: trustee predeceases (FST fails, HST survives); beneficiary predeceases (*Re Gardner (No 2)*, criticised); beneficiary or spouse witnessed the will (*Re Young*); trustee tries to keep the property (FST v HST outcome).
7. **Land**: flag s 53(1)(b) v s 53(2) and the express/constructive debate.
8. **Remedy on failure**: FST: legatee keeps it, or resulting trust if only terms missing; HST: resulting trust to residue (who takes residue?).
9. **Essay hook**: evaluate the fraud, dehors and constructive trust (loss of opportunity) explanations and whether secret trusts should be abolished.
