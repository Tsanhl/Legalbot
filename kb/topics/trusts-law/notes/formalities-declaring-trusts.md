# Formalities for declaring express trusts

## Overview
An express trust must be validly *declared*, and (unless the settlor declares himself trustee) the property must also be vested in the trustee (constitution, see constitution-of-trusts.md). This note covers the formalities for the declaration itself: none for personalty; written evidence for land under LPA 1925 s 53(1)(b); and full will formalities under Wills Act 1837 s 9 for trusts taking effect on death. It also covers the equitable principle that a statute may not be used as an instrument of fraud (*Rochefoucauld v Boustead*), which lets courts enforce some oral trusts of land, and the debate over whether such trusts are express or constructive. Secret trusts, the main exception to the Wills Act, have their own note (secret-trusts.md); dispositions of existing equitable interests (s 53(1)(c)) are in dispositions-of-equitable-interests.md.

## The law

### 1. Where formalities sit among the requirements for an express trust
The requirements are: capacity; formalities for creation; public policy; formalities for disposing of equitable interests; the three certainties; constitution; perpetuity rules.

**Capacity**: a minor can create a trust (but not of land, since a minor cannot hold legal title to land); the trust is voidable if repudiated on reaching majority. A person lacking mental capacity cannot create a trust.

**Who can be settlor, trustee and beneficiary**: X can transfer to Y on trust for Y and Z, or on trust for X; X can declare herself trustee for Z, or for X and Z. But X cannot declare herself trustee for X alone, since there would be no split between legal and beneficial title.

### 2. Inter vivos trusts of personal property
- **No formalities**. A trust of personalty can be declared orally ("by parol"), whether by self-declaration or by transfer to a separate trustee.
- If a separate trustee is intended, legal title must be transferred, and that transfer must satisfy whatever formality applies to that kind of property (e.g. share transfer rules). Those are transfer rules, not trust-creation rules. A self-declaring settlor already holds title, so no transfer is needed.

### 3. Inter vivos trusts of land: LPA 1925 s 53(1)(b) and s 53(2)
- **s 53(1)(b)**: "a declaration of trust respecting any land or any interest therein must be manifested and proved by some writing signed by some person who is able to declare such trust or by his will".
- **s 53(2)**: "This section does not affect the creation or operation of resulting, implied or constructive trusts."
- The rule descends from the Statute of Frauds 1677 (s 7).
- **Evidential, not constitutive**: the trust need not be *declared* in writing, only *evidenced* in signed writing, and the date of the writing does not matter (*Rochefoucauld v Boustead* [1897] 1 Ch 196, Lindley LJ at 206). So an oral trust followed by a later signed letter referring to it satisfies the section.
- **Email** can satisfy the writing requirement (*Hudson v Hathway* [2022] EWCA Civ 1648). The PQ guide in the secondary folder adds that a signature needs an intention to authenticate, and that typing one's name at the end of an email can be a signature (it attributes both points to *Hudson v Hathway*).
- **Effect of non-compliance**: the trust is not void but unenforceable (*Gardner v Rowe* (1828) 5 Russ 258).

### 4. "Equity will not allow a statute to be used as an instrument of fraud"
Where land is conveyed to someone on an oral trust and the transferee later denies the trust, equity will not let the transferee rely on the lack of writing; the court declares that they hold on trust for the intended beneficiary.

**Rochefoucauld v Boustead [1897] 1 Ch 196 (CA)**: the Comtesse owned mortgaged coffee estates in Ceylon; the Dutch mortgagee planned to sell. Boustead orally agreed to buy the estates and hold them on trust for her, subject to repayment. He later treated them as his own and sold them. Held: he could not rely on Statute of Frauds s 7. Lindley LJ (205–206): it is "a fraud on the part of a person to whom land is conveyed as a trustee, and who knows it was so conveyed, to deny the trust and claim the land himself"; parol evidence may prove the trust. The estates were held on trust for her, subject to a charge for his advances.

**Bannister v Bannister [1948] 2 All ER 133 (CA)**: an elderly widow sold cottages cheaply to her brother-in-law on an oral understanding that she could live in one for life; he tried to evict her. Held: a constructive trust gave her a life interest. Scott LJ (136): the principle is not confined to conveyances obtained by fraud; the fraud arises as soon as the absolute character of the conveyance is set up to defeat the beneficial interest that the true bargain gave another.

**What "fraud" means here**: equitable fraud is wider than common law deceit. No intention to deceive at the time of the agreement is needed: Boustead probably meant at first to keep his word; Bannister denied any dishonest intention. Acting inconsistently with the agreement is enough. *De Bruyne v De Bruyne* [2010] EWCA Civ 519 (Patten LJ at [51]): fraud in equity extends to unconscionable conduct such as denying or refusing to carry out the agreement on which the property was transferred.

**Limit: property acquired from a third party**. In *Re Smith* [2023] EWHC 255 (Comm), Foxton J (at [7]–[8]) held the principle applies only where the *beneficiary* transferred the property to the putative trustee on the basis of an oral trust and the trustee then relies on the lack of writing. It does not apply where the defendant bought from a third party and then orally declared a trust; extending it would "drive a coach and four" through the writing requirement.

### 5. Express or constructive trust? (*Rochefoucauld* debate)
- If the trust is *express*, the court seems to enforce an express trust of land in defiance of s 53(1)(b), which raises parliamentary sovereignty concerns. If it is *constructive*, s 53(2) exempts it from writing.
- In *Rochefoucauld* the question mattered for limitation: limitation periods did not then apply to express trustees, and the CA treated the trust as express, so the claim was not time-barred.
- But a long nineteenth-century line of cases treated some non-express trusts as express *for limitation purposes*, and *Taylor v Davies* [1920] AC 636 confirmed *Rochefoucauld* falls in that category, implying the trust was really constructive. Later cases (*Bannister*) favour a constructive trust analysis.
- The secondary PQ guide reports competing academic views: Swadling (express trust; the rule effectively waives the writing requirement), McFarlane (constructive trust imposed in response to the fraud), and *Solomon v McCarthy* (a county court decision suggesting a resulting trust for the settlor). That guide prefers the express trust view; the discussion leans to the constructive view. Present both.

### 6. Testamentary trusts: Wills Act 1837 s 9
- **s 9**: no will is valid unless (a) it is in writing and signed by the testator (or by someone in his presence and at his direction); (b) it appears the testator intended by the signature to give effect to the will; (c) the signature is made or acknowledged in the presence of two or more witnesses present at the same time; and (d) each witness attests and signs, or acknowledges his signature, in the testator's presence (not necessarily in each other's); no form of attestation is required.
- **s 1**: "will" extends to a testament, a codicil and *any other testamentary disposition*. So any attempt to direct where property goes on death, including by trust, is void unless in a duly executed will.
- Before 1837 the Statute of Frauds 1677 governed testamentary formalities (writing for land; restricting oral wills of personalty).
- A will is "executed" when signed and attested but takes effect only on death. On death the personal representative (executor under a will; administrator on intestacy) obtains probate; a proved will becomes a public document, which is why secrecy can matter.
- **Exceptions**: fully secret and half-secret trusts (see secret-trusts.md).

### 7. Why formalities matter (functions)
From the discussion and Penner: writing provides reliable evidence, protects against fraudulent claims, and (for wills) channels testamentary wishes into a checked, public form. Penner (Law Trove ch 9) notes that the policy of the Wills Act, to invalidate informal testamentary gifts, applies just as much to gifts on trust.

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 s 53(1)(b), s 53(2): writing to evidence trusts of land; exemption for resulting, implied and constructive trusts.
- Wills Act 1837 ss 1, 9: will formalities apply to every testamentary disposition.
- *Rochefoucauld v Boustead* [1897] 1 Ch 196: writing may post-date the declaration; statute cannot be used as an instrument of fraud.
- *Bannister v Bannister* [1948] 2 All ER 133: constructive trust where an absolute conveyance is set up to defeat an agreed interest.
- *De Bruyne v De Bruyne* [2010] EWCA Civ 519: wide meaning of equitable fraud.
- *Taylor v Davies* [1920] AC 636: *Rochefoucauld* trust treated as express only for limitation.
- *Gardner v Rowe* (1828) 5 Russ 258: non-compliant trust of land unenforceable, not void.
- *Hudson v Hathway* [2022] EWCA Civ 1648: email can satisfy writing.
- *Re Smith* [2023] EWHC 255 (Comm): the fraud principle does not cover land bought from a third party then orally declared on trust.

## Debate and critique
- **Sovereignty versus conscience**: enforcing an oral express trust of land looks like judges overriding Parliament. The constructive trust reading (s 53(2)) avoids this but arguably relabels the same result. Watt (ch 16, maxims) argues the maxim protects a statute's integrity rather than defeating it, since the statute was passed to prevent fraud.
- **How wide is "fraud"?** *Bannister* and *De Bruyne* stretch fraud to any unconscionable denial. Critics (e.g. Challiner and Griffin, in secret-trusts.md) argue such a mild notion of fraud cannot justify overriding statutory formality; *Re Smith* shows courts do draw a line so the requirement is not emptied of content.
- **Express, constructive or resulting?** Swadling, McFarlane and *Solomon v McCarthy* (above). The answer matters for limitation, for whether s 53(2) applies, and for the remedy (enforcing the bargain v returning the property to the transferor).
- **Overlap with common intention constructive trusts of the family home**: *Hudson v Hathway* also concerned cohabitants varying their shares; Watt reports the CA required detrimental reliance on the new common intention (see constructive-trusts.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the property**: personalty (no formality for declaration) or land (s 53(1)(b))? Is the trust inter vivos or meant to operate on death (Wills Act s 9)?
2. **Who is trustee?** If a third party, has legal title been properly transferred (constitution)? If self-declaration, no transfer needed.
3. **Land: is there signed writing?** It can be later than the declaration and can be an email. Who signed? It must be someone able to declare the trust.
4. **No writing?** Is the defendant the transferee who received the land from the claimant on an oral trust (*Rochefoucauld*, *Bannister*)? If so, the defendant cannot rely on s 53(1)(b). If the defendant bought from a third party and merely declared a trust orally, *Re Smith* suggests the claim fails.
5. **Classify the resulting trust** (express v constructive) and say why it matters (s 53(2), limitation).
6. **Testamentary wishes outside a valid will** fail unless a secret trust can be shown.
7. **Traps**: treating s 53(1)(b) as making oral trusts void (they are unenforceable); forgetting that personalty needs no writing; confusing declaration (s 53(1)(b)) with disposal of an existing equitable interest (s 53(1)(c)); applying *Rochefoucauld* where the property did not come from the claimant.
