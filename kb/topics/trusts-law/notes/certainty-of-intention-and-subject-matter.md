# Certainty of intention and certainty of subject matter

## Overview
An express trust needs three certainties: intention, subject matter and objects (*Knight v Knight*). This note covers the first two; objects are in certainty-of-objects-trusts-and-powers.md. Certainty matters for two reasons stressed: (1) the court must be able to administer the trust if the trustee fails to, and (2) whether a trust exists often decides who wins in an insolvency, since trust assets are taken out of the insolvent's estate. Many leading cases (*Kayford*, *London Wine*, *Goldcorp*, *Hunter v Moss*) are insolvency disputes, and the courts may be influenced by the effect on other creditors. The certainties overlap: uncertainty of subject matter can cast doubt on intention (*Mussoorie Bank v Raynor*). The consequences of each failure differ, so analyse each separately.

## The law

### 1. The three certainties
- *Knight v Knight* (1840) 3 Beav 148, 172–173 (Lord Langdale MR): a recommendation or wish attached to a gift creates a trust (1) if the words, read as a whole, are imperative; (2) if the subject of the wish is certain; and (3) if the objects are certain.
- Do not confuse **subject matter** (the property) with **objects** (beneficiaries or purposes).
- *Mussoorie Bank v Raynor* (1882) 7 App Cas 321, 331 (Sir Arthur Hobhouse): uncertainty in the property has a "reflex action" on the words, suggesting the testator did not mean them to be imperative.

### 2. Certainty of intention
**(a) Objective test, substance over form**
1. The question is whether the words or conduct objectively show an intention to create a trust. Lord Millett in *Twinsectra v Yardley* [2002] 2 AC 164, 185: subjective intentions are irrelevant; if the settlor enters into arrangements that have the effect of a trust, he need not realise it.
2. No special words are needed, only "expressions which have that meaning" (Jessel MR, *Richards v Mayor of Dellbridge* (1874) LR 18 Eq 11, 14). The word "trust" or "trustees" is neither required nor decisive (*Singha v Heer* [2016] EWCA Civ 424; *Rawstron v Freud* [2014] EWHC 2577 (Ch)). Intention may be inferred from conduct.
3. **Re Kayford Ltd** [1975] 1 All ER 604: a mail order company, on its accountants' advice, paid customers' prepayments into a separate account (later renamed a "Customers' Trust Deposit Account") so they could be refunded if the company failed. Held: a trust for the customers. Megarry J (607): a trust can be created without the words "trust" or "confidence"; the question is whether in substance a sufficient intention has been shown.
4. The court generally stays within the "four corners" of the instrument. But reproducing a formula previously held to create a trust indicates intention (*Re Steele's Will Trusts* [1948] Ch 603).
5. **Sham trusts** are an exception where actual intention matters: a trust with no real intention to benefit the beneficiary is void (*Midland Bank plc v Wyatt* [1995] 1 FLR 697). See public-policy-limits-on-trusts.md.
6. *Byrnes v Kendle* [2011] HCA 26 (Australia) shows the objective approach at its starkest: a trust enforced although the settlor subjectively had no intention to create one.

**(b) Precatory words**
1. Older cases treated words of wish, hope, desire or confidence as automatically creating a trust.
2. Rejected in the later nineteenth century. **Re Adams and Kensington Vestry** (1884) 27 Ch D 394: estate to the testator's wife absolutely "in full confidence that she will do what is right as to the disposal thereof between my children". Held: no trust; the wife took absolutely. Cotton LJ (409): older authorities "went a great deal too far"; one must construe the whole will.
3. **Comiskey v Bowring-Hanbury** [1905] AC 84: estate to wife absolutely "in full confidence" that she would leave it to one or more of the nieces as she chose, with a gift over to the nieces equally in default. Held: a trust. Lord Davey: "in full confidence" is neutral; the context decides. The specific default provision showed an imperative intention.
4. The settlor must intend a legally enforceable, not merely moral, obligation (*Re Diggles* (1888) 39 Ch D 253). Unless the word "trust" is used (usually conclusive), intention is a matter of construction.

**(c) Self-declarations of trust**
1. No particular words or single moment of declaration are required. **Paul v Constance** [1977] 1 WLR 527 (CA): Mr Constance repeatedly told his partner, Ms Paul, that money in his bank account (£950 damages) was "as much yours as mine"; the account was in his name but used for both. On his intestacy his estranged wife would otherwise take all. Held: an express declaration of trust for both. Scarman LJ (532) agreed with the judge that the words used on numerous occasions amounted to a declaration.
2. But loose conversation, or an attempt to rescue an imperfect gift, is not a declaration. **Jones v Lock** (1865) LR 1 Ch App 25: a father put a £900 cheque payable to himself into his baby's hand saying "I give this to baby... I am going to put it away for him". Held: no effective declaration; there was an intention to make a gift, not to create a trust, and these "loose words" were not enough. The discussion adds the general point that a declaration will not be found where it would merely serve to perfect an imperfect gift (see constitution-of-trusts.md).

**(d) Consequences of no certainty of intention**
- Where property was transferred (inter vivos or by will): the transferee takes absolutely as a gift (e.g. the widow in *Re Adams*).
- Where an alleged self-declaration fails: the "settlor" remains outright owner.

### 3. Certainty of subject matter
The property must be identifiable. This is rarely a problem where property is transferred to trustees (it must be identified to be transferred), but critical for self-declarations and trusts of part of property already held.

**(a) Uncertain portions**
- *Sprange v Barnard* (1789) 2 Bro CC 585: property to the husband "for his sole use" and at his death "the remaining part of what is left, that he does not want for his own wants and use" to be divided among siblings. No trust; husband took absolutely.
- *Palmer v Simmonds* (1854) 2 Drew 221: "the bulk of my residuary estate" too uncertain.
- "Everything" or "my entire wealth" is certain (*T Choithram International SA v Pagarani* [2001] 1 WLR 1, which the discussion callss "a bit controversial").
- **Rule in Hancock v Watson** [1902] AC 14 (Lord Davey, 22): where an absolute gift has trusts engrafted on it that fail, the absolute gift takes effect.

**(b) Tangible property in a homogeneous mass: segregation required**
- **Re London Wine Co (Shippers) Ltd** (1986) PCC 121: customers bought wine stored by the merchant and received certificates calling them sole beneficial owners, but their wine was never separated from general stock. On the company's insolvency, no trust: no customer could point to particular bottles. A trust of the *whole* or of a *fraction* (e.g. 50%) of a mass is valid.
- **Re Goldcorp Exchange Ltd** [1995] 1 AC 74 (PC): a bullion dealer sold bullion to be held for customers, issued certificates of ownership, but failed to buy and hold enough; stock fluctuated and mixed sold and unsold bullion. No trust could be identified. Penner records Lord Mustill's view that a proprietary right cannot exist "in the air" over an undifferentiated mass.

**(c) Intangible property in a homogeneous mass: segregation not required**
- **Hunter v Moss** [1994] 1 WLR 452 (CA): Moss, owning 950 of 1,000 issued shares in a private company, orally declared himself trustee of 5% of the issued capital (50 shares) for Hunter, without segregation. Held: a valid trust. At first instance Colin Rimer QC distinguished *London Wine*: tangible items in a mass are physically separate and may differ (a corked case of wine), whereas identical intangibles (shares of one class, money in an account) need not be treated the same way.
- Followed in *Re Harvard Securities Ltd* [1997] 2 BCLC 369. The discussion suggests courts may in future reason by analogy with Sale of Goods Act 1979 s 20A (buyers of part of a bulk become tenants in common in proportion to their share).
- *Goldcorp* was decided by the PC before the CA's judgment in *Hunter v Moss* was available to it.

**(d) Residue, identifiable property and future property**
- "Residue" has a clear legal meaning (what is left after debts and legacies), so is certain (*Re Estate of Last* [1958] P 137).
- A trust must attach to specific property: *Hemmens v Wilson Browne* [1995] Ch 223 (a document giving a right to £110,000 identified no fund; no trust).
- Future property (e.g. an expected inheritance) cannot be held on trust; a beneficiary's existing rights can, but not future property expected from them (*Williams v IRC* [1965] NZLR 395).
- A "floating" or "suspended" trust over whatever is left on X's death has been suggested as a solution to *Sprange*-type gifts, but the discussion notes no supporting authority.

**(e) Certainty of beneficial entitlement**
- **Boyce v Boyce** (1849) 16 Sim 476: two houses on trust; Maria to choose one, the other to go to Charlotte. Maria died before the testator and never chose, so Charlotte's share could not be identified; the trust failed. Result: a **resulting trust** for the settlor/estate, because the executors clearly took as trustees.

**(f) Objective yardsticks**
- *Re Golay's Will Trusts* [1965] 1 WLR 969: "a reasonable income" from properties was certain, since the court could assess it objectively (Ungoed-Thomas J, 972). A subjective standard ("my favourite dress") fails.
- Penner adds *North v Wilkinson*: a sole trader's "business assets" can be held on trust; courts can resolve which assets qualify.

**(g) Consequences of no certainty of subject matter**
- Property already transferred with a direction to hold an undefined part on trust: recipient takes absolutely (*Sprange*; *Hancock v Watson*).
- Not yet transferred (self-declaration; will being administered): settlor stays owner, or executors hold on resulting trust for the estate.
- Property certain but shares uncertain: resulting trust for the settlor or estate (*Boyce*).

## Debate and critique
- **Hunter v Moss**: Penner argues there is no good reason to distinguish *London Wine*; the CA drew a goods/intangibles line "without further reasoned argument". Dillon LJ's analogy with a will (a testator can leave 50 of 950 shares) fails because, as Hayton points out, a legatee's proprietary interest arises only when shares are appropriated; the true analogy is a transfer of all 950 to a trustee. Practical problem: if Moss sells 100 shares, are they Hunter's? Martin's tracing solution treats the settlor as having made himself a trustee in breach at the moment of declaration, which Penner calls conceptually confused. Despite criticism, the case is accepted on the basis that the declaration is construed as a trust of a 1/19th share of the whole holding (Goode; *White v Shortall* (2006); *Re Lehman Brothers International (Europe)* (2010, 2011)). Penner also discusses the "floating trust" in *Lehman*.
- **Fungibility, not tangibility**: Chung ("Challenging the orthodoxy: a critique of Re Goldcorp", Trusts & Trustees) argues *Goldcorp* was wrong. Bullion bars are made identical in weight and purity, and the market trades on an *unallocated* basis, so investors care about quantity not particular bars. The real test should be fungible v non-fungible (drawing on Goode's test of divisibility without loss of identity): wine can deteriorate and is non-fungible, bullion is chemically stable and fungible. He points to *Re Lehman Brothers* and the Hong Kong case *Yang Foo Oi v Wai Wai Chen* (2018) as signs of a judicial shift towards fungibility.
- **Distributional certainty**: Wilde ("The three certainties required to declare a trust – or is it four?" (2020) 79 CLJ 349) argues for a fourth certainty: where property is divided among beneficiaries their *shares* must be clear (the *Boyce* problem). This is usually treated as uncertainty of subject matter, but it has different consequences and special resolution techniques, may explain administrative unworkability in discretionary trusts, and is not required for charitable trusts.
- **Self-declaration and intention**: Agnew and Douglas ("Self-declarations of trust" (2019) LQR 67) argue the "declaration" is not a mere formality but proof of a *double intention*: (1) to hold rights for another, which combines conferring the beneficial interest and assuming trustee obligations; and (2) to constitute the trust by the very words or acts. Merely forming and expressing an intention to hold for another is not enough. They suggest the "will theory" may explain why the law recognises self-declared trusts: divesting oneself of an interest in favour of another is an inherent prerogative of ownership. Contrast the generous reading of informal words in *Paul v Constance* with *Jones v Lock*.
- **Insolvency policy**: The discussion notes the courts may in practice be influenced by context, e.g. whether finding a trust disadvantages other creditors (*Kayford* protected customers at the expense of general creditors).
- **Objective v subjective intention**: *Twinsectra* and *Byrnes v Kendle* make intention objective, yet shams turn on actual intention; this tension is worth discussing.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Intention**: quote the words. Imperative ("on trust", "shall") or precatory ("hope", "in full confidence")? Read the whole instrument (*Re Adams* v *Comiskey*). Any conduct suggesting a trust (separate account: *Kayford*; repeated statements: *Paul v Constance*)? Or only an intention to give (*Jones v Lock*)?
2. **Consequence if intention fails**: gift to the recipient, or the owner keeps the property.
3. **Subject matter**: is the property defined ("my house", "residue", "all my shares in X") or vague ("the bulk", "whatever is left")? Is it part of a mass? Tangible and unsegregated (*London Wine*, *Goldcorp*: fail) or intangible (*Hunter v Moss*: valid, perhaps as a fractional share)? Consider the fungibility critique.
4. **Objective yardstick?** "Reasonable income" is fine (*Re Golay*); a subjective test is not.
5. **Shares among beneficiaries**: clear? If not, resulting trust (*Boyce*).
6. **Consequence if subject matter fails**: absolute gift (*Hancock v Watson*) if property passed and only a trust over part failed; otherwise no trust or resulting trust.
7. **Insolvency facts**: identify the competing creditors and explain why a trust matters to them.
8. **Traps**: treating "trust" as essential (*Kayford*); assuming precatory words always fail (*Comiskey*); ignoring that *Goldcorp* (PC) is only persuasive while *Hunter v Moss* is CA authority; forgetting that a percentage share of a whole is fine even for tangibles.
