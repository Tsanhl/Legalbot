# Tracing and proprietary claims

## Overview
When trust property has been misappropriated, a beneficiary may prefer a proprietary claim, which asserts ownership of a specific asset in someone else's hands, over a personal claim against a trustee who may be insolvent. Tracing is the evidential process that identifies the original asset, or its substitute, so that a proprietary claim can be made. The course structures the topic as four steps: proprietary base, following or tracing, claiming and remedy, defences. English law has separate common law and equitable tracing rules, with equity the more generous but conditional on a fiduciary relationship.

## The law (England and Wales)
**1. Why a proprietary claim.** Advantages listed in the materials: no wrongdoing by the defendant need be shown (innocent recipients can be reached); better prospects of recovery; the specific asset can be returned; freezing injunctions are available; priority over general creditors in the defendant's insolvency; and any increase in value or profit can be claimed.

**2. Step 1: proprietary base.** The claimant must start with a right that binds the world: legal title at common law, or a beneficial interest in equity.

**3. Step 2: following versus tracing.** Following is tracking the same asset from hand to hand; tracing identifies a new asset as the substitute for the old (Millett LJ in *Foskett v McKeown* [2001] 1 AC 102, 127; the claimant may elect between following the original and tracing into the substitute). Tracing is evidence, not a claim: "tracing is a process of identifying assets: it belongs to the realm of evidence" (Steyn LJ in *Foskett*). Tracing does not itself create rights; the claimant must still show how a proprietary right subsists in the new asset.

**4. Common law tracing.**
- Claims: conversion (damages for chattels) and money had and received (restitutionary). Remedies are usually personal. Where a chattel (other than money) is taken, title does not pass under nemo dat, so the owner can follow it.
- Clean substitutions. *Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale Ltd* [1991] 2 AC 548: a solicitor partner took firm money to gamble. The firm traced its chose in action against the bank into cash and into the casino's hands; since the partner got title to the cash, the court treated his title as defeasible. The conversion claim failed (a substitute cannot make an innocent recipient a wrongdoer), but money had and received succeeded; change of position was available to the casino for winnings already paid. Some commentators see this as unjust enrichment, not vindication of property.
- Profits. *FC Jones & Sons (Trustee) v Jones* [1997] Ch 159: trustee in bankruptcy traced money paid to a wife into a profitable futures investment and recovered the whole balance, including profits, by an action in debt, because legal title never passed.
- Bank accounts. *Banque Belge pour l'Etranger v Hambrouck* [1921] 1 KB 321: fraudulently obtained money was the only money in the cashier's and mistress's accounts, so was traceable.
- Mixed funds. Common law can trace into a mixed account where the defendant did the mixing (*Agip (Africa) Ltd v Jackson* [1990] 1 Ch 265), but not through the mix into new assets. In *Agip* the tracing was allowed only in equity because the money had been mixed in the bank clearing system, a result the materials finds hard to reconcile with *Banque Belge*.

**5. Equitable tracing.**
- Needs a proprietary base (beneficial interest) and a fiduciary relationship (*Agip* [1990] 1 Ch 265; Fox LJ in the Court of Appeal [1991] 1 Ch 547, 566-7: fiduciary relationship "a prerequisite"). Beneficiaries under a trust satisfy it easily; legal owners find it harder.
- *Re Diplock* [1948] Ch 465: next of kin could trace in equity into charities' hands because the executors had stood in a fiduciary position to the estate. the materials it can be read as supplying a proprietary base rather than an extra requirement, but later cases treat it as a further requirement. (Pawlowski's summary in the readings describes *Diplock* as insisting on a pre-existing fiduciary relationship.)
- *Chase Manhattan Bank v Israel-British Bank* [1981] Ch 105: payment made twice by mistake; Goulding J found a retained equitable property and fiduciary duty arising on receipt. Criticised in *Westdeutsche Landesbank v Islington LBC* [1996] AC 669, though a constructive trust can arise once the recipient's conscience is affected.
- Equitable remedies: constructive or resulting trust over the asset or a proportionate share; an equitable charge or lien; subrogation (stepping into the shoes of a lender whose secured debt was paid off with trust money: *Boscawen v Bajwa* [1996] 1 WLR 328).
- *Foskett v McKeown*: £20,440 of investors' money paid two life policy premiums; the policy paid £1m to the trustee's children. The House of Lords (3:2) allowed tracing into a 40 per cent share (about £400,000), rather than only a lien for the premiums; Millett LJ disapproved dicta in *Re Hallett's Estate* limiting claimants to a lien, because trustees must not profit from breach. A lien is better where the asset has fallen in value; a proportionate share where it has risen. A lien is not available where two trusts' money or an innocent volunteer's money is mixed and the asset falls, since one innocent party would get priority; losses are shared pari passu.

**6. Mixed funds: allocation rules.**
- Presumption of honesty (*Re Hallett's Estate* (1880) 13 Ch D 696): a trustee mixing trust and personal money in a bank account is presumed to spend his own first, so the trust money survives.
- Investment first: *Re Oatway* [1903] 2 Ch 356: where the trustee bought shares from the mixed account and then dissipated the rest, the shares are taken to be bought with trust money; the presumption must work against the wrongdoer.
- Cherry-picking: *Shalson v Russo* [2005] Ch 281 (Rimer J) lets the beneficiary choose between the surviving balance and the profitable investment; *Turner v Jacob* [2006] EWHC 1317 (Patten J) disagreed without considering *Shalson*; Rimer J's view is generally preferred.
- Lowest intermediate balance: the claim to the account cannot exceed the lowest balance reached after the mixing, since later deposits come from the trustee (*James Roscoe (Bolton) Ltd v Winder* [1915] 1 Ch 62: claim limited to the £25 18s left, though the account later rose to £358). It is rebuttable only by clear evidence that later credits were meant to restore the trust fund.
- Competing innocent claimants: the rule in *Clayton's Case* (1817) 1 Mer 572 (first in, first out). It is a default rule that can be displaced where it contradicts the claimants' express or presumed intentions or produces injustice or excessive cost (*Barlow Clowes International v Vaughan* [1992] 4 All ER 22, adopting pari passu; *Commerzbank AG v IMB Morgan plc* [2004] EWHC 2771; *Charity Commission v Framjee* [2014] EWHC 2507 (Ch), Henderson J: probably still the default but "may be displaced with relative ease").
- Rolling charge (North American approach): each wrongful withdrawal reduces each contributor's share pro rata. Fairer, but costly and record-dependent; rejected as impractical in *Barlow Clowes*.

**7. Defences and limits.**
- Dissipation or destruction: in equity, the asset or its substitute must still exist at judgment; at common law a personal claim can survive.
- Debts and unsecured loans: no asset to trace into; banks and creditors who took without notice are protected.
- Overdrawn accounts: generally cannot trace through (*Bishopsgate Investment Management v Homan* [1995] Ch 211). "Backwards tracing", obiter in *Homan* (Dillon LJ), was accepted in *Relfo Ltd v Varsani* [2014] EWCA Civ 360 and *Federal Republic of Brazil v Durant International* [2015] UKPC 35, where coordination between depleting the fund and acquiring the asset is shown.
- Bona fide purchaser for value without notice ("equity's darling"): extinguishes the equitable interest. At common law applies only to money and negotiable instruments. A bank taking cash is a purchaser of the cash, not of the account balance.
- Change of position (*Lipkin Gorman*, Lord Goff: injustice of repayment outweighs injustice of denying restitution). Unclear whether it applies to equitable proprietary claims; before it, "inequitability" served this role, as in *Re Diplock* (no charge over innocent charities' improved land).

## Key authorities
- *Foskett v McKeown* [2001] 1 AC 102: tracing as evidence; proportionate shares; criticism of duality.
- *Agip (Africa) v Jackson*: no common law tracing through mixes; fiduciary requirement.
- *Re Diplock*; *Chase Manhattan*: fiduciary requirement and its extension.
- *Lipkin Gorman*; *FC Jones v Jones*; *Banque Belge*: common law tracing.
- *Re Hallett*, *Re Oatway*, *Roscoe v Winder*, *Clayton's Case*, *Barlow Clowes*: mixed funds.
- *Homan*, *Relfo*, *Brazil v Durant*: overdrafts and backwards tracing.

## Debate and critique
- **One set of rules?** Millett LJ in *Foskett* (obiter): nothing inherently legal or equitable about tracing; there is no logic in the fiduciary precondition, which may be relevant to the nature of the claim but not to tracing; the US has no such duality. Birks ("The Necessity of a Unitary Law of Tracing") builds on Millett LJ's remarks in arguing for a single law. For now the distinction remains.
- **Fiduciary requirement.** Seen as arbitrary: it denies equitable tracing to legal owners such as defrauded payers, and is stretched artificially in *Chase*.
- **Clayton's Case.** Arbitrary and chronology-based, so one innocent victim recovers fully and another nothing; courts displace it readily.
- **Nature of *Lipkin Gorman*.** Property or unjust enrichment, with consequences for defences.
- **Proprietary priority.** Gives claimants advantage over unsecured creditors, a policy question that recurs in constructive trust debates.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify what was taken, by whom, and where it went. Is the claimant a legal owner, beneficiary, or neither?
2. Proprietary base: legal title or beneficial interest at the time of the misappropriation?
3. Following or tracing? Is there mixing? If none, common law may suffice (money had and received, conversion). If mixed through bank accounts or into new property, move to equity and prove a fiduciary relationship (trustee, agent, solicitor, company director; or a recipient whose conscience is affected).
4. Apply the mixed fund rules in order: honesty presumption (trustee with own money); lowest intermediate balance; investment bought first (*Oatway*) and cherry-picking; competing innocent contributors (pari passu or *Clayton*).
5. Choose a remedy: proportionate share (asset rose), lien (asset fell), subrogation (debt discharged), personal money-had-and-received claim.
6. Check defences: BFP, change of position, dissipation, overdraft, laches.
7. Link back: if no tracing is possible or the recipient is solvent, consider personal liability (knowing receipt, dishonest assistance).
