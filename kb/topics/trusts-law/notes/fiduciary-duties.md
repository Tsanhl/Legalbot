# Fiduciary duties of trustees and remedies for their breach

## Overview
Trustees owe two families of duty. Non-fiduciary duties (from the trust instrument, common law and statute, such as investment and care) are dealt with elsewhere; breach is a "breach of trust". Fiduciary duties arise because the trustee is also a fiduciary: someone who must not let personal interest interfere with dealings for the principal (here the beneficiary). Breach is a "breach of fiduciary duty", which attracts strict, deterrent rules and a distinctive set of equitable remedies. Not all trustees are fiduciaries in every respect, and the fiduciary label is itself poorly defined.

## The law (England and Wales)
**1. Who is a fiduciary.** The term is not well defined. Relationships described in the materials as fiduciary per se include trustee and beneficiary, solicitor and client (*Re Hallett's Estate* (1880) 13 Ch D 696), partners (*Bentley v Craven* (1853) 18 Beav 75), mortgagee and mortgagor (*Farrars v Farrars Ltd* (1888) 40 Ch D 395), principal and agent (*Kirkham v Peel* (1880) 43 LT 171) and a government employee and the Crown (*AG for Hong Kong v Reid* [1994] 1 AC 324). The categories are not closed, and the content of the duties can be shaped by associated contracts.

**2. Essence: loyalty and good faith.** Millett LJ in *Bristol and West BS v Mothew* [1998] 1 Ch 1, 18: a fiduciary has undertaken to act for another in circumstances giving rise to trust and confidence, and "the distinguishing obligation of a fiduciary is the obligation of loyalty". the materials a contrary emphasis (James Penner): the relationship arises where one person exercises judgment for another, and the no-conflict rule keeps the decision-taker's interest from interfering with that judgment, a matter of fact rather than emotional attachment. Fiduciaries must also act in good faith, which concerns their state of mind when deciding, not the outcome.

**3. Two main rules.** The no conflict rule and the no unauthorised profit rule. Lord Upjohn in *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] 2 AC 46 treated the profit rule as part of the wider conflict rule; Lionel Smith (as reported in the materials) argues they are distinct, since the conflict rule sets aside tainted judgments whereas the profit rule recovers profits regardless of taint.

**4. No conflict rule.** *Bray v Ford* [1896] AC 44, 51 (Lord Herschell): an "inflexible rule" that a fiduciary must not put himself in a position where interest and duty conflict. It is breached even if: the fiduciary acted in perfect good faith; the principal suffered no loss or even benefited; the principal would have consented if asked; the conflict was only a possibility. It also covers conflicts between duties to different principals, past or present (the undivided loyalty rule). In professional firms conflicts are managed through "Chinese walls" (*Jefri Bolkiah v KPMG* [1999] 2 AC 222).

**5. Self-dealing.** *Tito v Waddell (No 2)* [1977] Ch 106 (Megarry V-C): if a trustee sells trust property to himself, the sale is voidable by any beneficiary ex debito justitiae however fair. Same if all trustees sell to one of them. Exceptions: authorisation in the trust instrument (*Sargeant v National Westminster Bank* (1990) 61 P & CR 518), informed consent of all beneficiaries, or court approval. The rule applies despite public auction or independent valuation (*Wright v Morgan* [1926] AC 788; *Ex p James* (1803) 8 Ves Jr 337, Lord Eldon). *Holder v Holder* [1968] 1 All ER 665: strictness relaxed where an executor who had barely acted and renounced bought a farm at auction on knowledge gained as tenant, the beneficiaries knew he acted as purchaser, and the proving executors were uninfluenced.

**6. Fair-dealing.** Where a trustee buys a beneficiary's beneficial interest, the transaction is not voidable as of right but may be set aside unless the trustee shows he took no advantage of his position, made full disclosure, and the deal was fair and honest (*Tito v Waddell (No 2)*). The trustee must show arm's length dealing, competent independent advice for the beneficiary and full disclosure of material facts.

**7. No unauthorised profit.** *Bray v Ford*: a fiduciary is not entitled to profit unless expressly provided. The rule is about using office or resources for private gain, not about loss to the principal.
- *Keech v Sandford* (1726) Sel Cas Ch 61: a trustee renewed a market lease for himself after the landlord refused to renew for the infant beneficiary; had to assign the lease and account.
- *Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver* [1967] 2 AC 134: directors took shares in a subsidiary when the company lacked funds; liable to account since the profit arose by reason of office and in the course of its execution; liability does not depend on fraud, bad faith or loss (Lord Russell). They had not obtained informed shareholder consent or ratification.
- *Boardman v Phipps* [1967] 2 AC 46: solicitor to a trust, using information from attending a company AGM as proxy for the trustees, bought control of the company with a beneficiary; the trust also profited; majority held he must account because the opportunity and information came through his fiduciary position and he lacked fully informed consent of all beneficiaries. Lords Guest and Hodson relied on *Keech*; Lord Cohen on the possibility of conflict. Lord Upjohn and Viscount Dilhorne dissented, requiring a "real sensible possibility" of conflict. Boardman was awarded remuneration on a quantum meruit "on a liberal scale"; no such allowance where there is dishonesty such as bribes.

**8. Defences / avoiding liability.** (a) Fully informed consent of the principal (all beneficiaries) before or after breach (ratification). (b) Authorisation in the trust instrument or, for remuneration, the Trustee Act 2000. (c) Conflict inherent in the structure (for example a trustee who is also a beneficiary). (d) A principal who delays in order to speculate on a rise in value is taken to acquiesce.

**9. Remedies.** The full range of equitable remedies: injunction, equitable compensation, equitable lien, account of profits, and constructive trust.
- Self-dealing: beneficiary elects to avoid the sale (property returned or resold as trust property) or affirm it (the proceeds are held on trust); any onward profit can be claimed.
- Fair-dealing: set aside if the trustee cannot show the three matters above.
- Unauthorised profits: account. A trustee has a proprietary liability; for other fiduciaries the account is personal, but a constructive trust is usually found. The proprietary route allows tracing, does not depend on the fiduciary's solvency, captures subsequent gains (unlike a lien), but subordinates the fiduciary's unsecured creditors.

**10. Bribes and secret commissions.** *Lister v Stubbs* (1890) 45 Ch D 1: a purchasing agent who invested bribes in land owed only a personal duty to account, not a trust. *AG for Hong Kong v Reid* [1994] 1 AC 324 (Privy Council): bribes and their proceeds are held on constructive trust (equity regards as done what ought to be done). *Sinclair Investments v Versailles Trade Finance* [2011] EWCA Civ 347 overruled *Reid*, requiring a pre-existing beneficial interest. *FHR European Ventures LLP v Cedar Capital Partners LLC* [2015] AC 250 (Supreme Court) overruled *Sinclair*: an agent's secret EUR 10m commission was held on constructive trust because the property should never have been in the agent's hands and diminished the principal's benefit.

**11. Directors and illegality.** *CPS v Aquila Advisory Ltd* [2021] UKSC 49: directors' profits from a fraudulent tax scheme; their acts are not attributed to the company so as to bar its proprietary claim by illegality (applying *Bilta (UK) Ltd v Nazir* [2015] UKSC 23), because that would defeat the purpose of fiduciary duties.

## Key authorities
- *Keech v Sandford*; *Regal v Gulliver*; *Boardman v Phipps*: strict profit rule.
- *Bray v Ford*; *Tito v Waddell (No 2)*: conflict, self-dealing and fair-dealing formulations.
- *Holder v Holder*: relaxed self-dealing.
- *Jefri Bolkiah v KPMG*: Chinese walls.
- *Lister v Stubbs*, *Reid*, *Sinclair*, *FHR v Cedar*: proprietary claims over bribes.
- *Aquila*: attribution and illegality.

## Debate and critique
- **What is loyalty?** Conaglen (LQR 2005) argues that fiduciary loyalty is a "subsidiary and prophylactic" protection of the proper performance of non-fiduciary duties, so that the prophylactic aspect is more than mere strictness; he rejects viewing fiduciary duties as simply default contract terms or torts. the materials contrasts Penner's judgment-based view.
- **Why so strict?** Samet (OJLS 2008) justifies stringent profit-stripping through moral psychology: because of the knowledge gap between principal and fiduciary, even virtuous fiduciaries may self-deceive and approve manipulative transactions as harmless, so the rule guards the fiduciary's conscience. the materials frames the rules as deterrent rather than compensatory.
- **Possibility of conflict.** *Boardman*: majority treats any possibility as enough; Lord Upjohn's "real sensible possibility" test is the milder alternative. Professional conflicts and the quantum meruit allowance soften the rule.
- **Proprietary remedies for bribes.** Pro-constructive trust: prevents the fiduciary benefiting from breach, allows tracing, gives priority over creditors. Against (the materials): bribes are not the principal's property, and priority harms unsecured creditors. *FHR* resolved the English position in favour of a trust.
- **One rule or two?** Whether the conflict and profit rules are separate affects how far an actual conflict must be shown.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the defendant a fiduciary (trustee, solicitor, agent, director, partner)? State the category.
2. Identify the type of act: sale of trust property to self (self-dealing: voidable as of right unless authorised); purchase of a beneficiary's interest (fair-dealing: onus on trustee); use of position or information to make a gain (profit rule); acting for two principals (conflict).
3. Ask: did the trust instrument authorise it? Did all adult, sui juris beneficiaries give fully informed consent? Was there ratification? Was the conflict inherent?
4. Disregard good faith, absence of loss, and "the trust could not have made the profit" as defences (*Keech*, *Regal*, *Boardman*), but consider an allowance for skill and effort unless dishonesty.
5. Remedy: avoid or affirm, account, constructive trust (tracing and priority in insolvency), equitable compensation. For bribes, apply *FHR*.
6. Traps: confusing breach of fiduciary duty with breach of non-fiduciary duty (compensation and causation rules differ); overlooking that consent must be fully informed from all beneficiaries.
