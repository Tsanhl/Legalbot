# Certainty of objects: fixed trusts, discretionary trusts and powers

## Overview
The third certainty requires that the beneficiaries (objects) be identifiable from the trust instrument. Trustees must know whom they distribute to and owe duties to; a trust can be enforced only by its beneficiaries, and an unenforceable trust is a contradiction because trusts are mandatory; and the court may need to identify beneficiaries in litigation. If objects are uncertain the property goes back to the settlor (or estate) on resulting trust. The test depends on the type of disposition: **fixed trusts** need a complete list; **discretionary trusts** and **powers of appointment** use the "is or is not" test (*McPhail v Doulton*). Discretionary trusts can additionally fail for **administrative unworkability**. Distinguishing trusts from powers, and conceptual from evidential uncertainty, is central to answering problem questions.

## The law

### 1. Trusts versus powers of appointment
1. **Trusts are mandatory**: trustees must distribute. **Powers are permissive**: the holder may, but need not, appoint.
2. **Fiduciary powers** are held by trustees; **bare (personal, "mere") powers** by others.
3. A fiduciary power-holder must from time to time *consider* whether to exercise the power (no duty to exercise) and must survey the range of objects before exercising it (*Re Hay's Settlement Trusts* [1981] 3 All ER 786). A bare power-holder has no such duties.
4. All power-holders must stay within the power: no excessive exercise and no appointments outside the class.
5. One arrangement may contain trusts, fiduciary powers and bare powers.
6. Discretionary trust v fixed trust (week 1): under a discretionary trust a beneficiary can compel the trustees to perform but cannot claim any particular share.

### 2. Conceptual and evidential certainty
- **Conceptual (linguistic, semantic) certainty**: are the class's boundaries clear in the abstract? "My first cousins" is clear; "my friends" is not.
- **Evidential certainty**: can members actually be found? *Re Sayer* [1957] Ch 423: a trust for employees and ex-employees of a confectionery firm with 70 shops and high staff turnover was conceptually clear but failed because no accurate list of ex-employees could be made (it was decided under the list test).
- Distinguish **ascertainability** (whether a known member can be located or is alive) from both; the handout does not develop this separately.

### 3. Historical basis: the court must be able to execute
*Morice v Bishop of Durham* (1805) 10 Ves Jr 522, 539–540 (Lord Eldon): because execution of a trust is under the court's control, it must be capable of review and, if the trustee dies, of execution by the court; unless subject and objects can be ascertained, the court can neither correct maladministration nor direct due administration. It was also thought the court could not exercise the trustees' discretion and could only divide equally ("equality is equity").

### 4. Fixed trusts: the complete list test
- The trustees must be able to draw up a **complete list** of beneficiaries, which requires both conceptual and evidential certainty. Pumfrey J in *OT Computers Ltd v First National Tricity Finance Ltd* [2003] EWHC 1010 (Ch) at [21]: it must be possible to identify each member of the class.
- *OT Computers*: a company facing insolvency set up two trust accounts. The trust for customers' deposits was valid (all customers were known or discoverable). The fixed trust for "urgent suppliers" failed: "urgent" was too imprecise to identify every supplier.

### 5. Discretionary trusts and powers: the "is or is not" test
1. **Powers**: *Re Gulbenkian's Settlement Trusts* [1970] AC 508 (the week 6 key case list gives it as [1968] 3 All ER 785). Lord Upjohn (521): a power among a class is valid "if you can say with certainty whether any given individual is or is not a member of the class; you do not have to be able to ascertain every member".
2. **Discretionary trusts before 1971**: the list test applied (*IRC v Broadway Cottages Trust* [1955] Ch 20), because the court, if trustees failed to act, was thought bound to divide equally.
3. **McPhail v Doulton** [1971] AC 424 (also called *Re Baden's Deed Trusts (No 1)*). Clause 9(a) directed trustees to "apply the net income... in making at their absolute discretion grants" to officers, employees, ex-officers and ex-employees of a company and their relatives or dependants.
   - Unanimously: the clause was a **discretionary trust** ("trust power"), not a power ("mere power"), because the trustees were *required* to exercise their discretion.
   - By 3:2: the test for discretionary trusts is the **is or is not** test.
   - Lord Wilberforce (451): equal division is "surely the last thing the settlor ever intended"; the court need not execute by equal division.
   - (456–457): the court will not normally compel exercise of a power, but will intervene if trustees exceed it, and possibly if they exercise it capriciously. If discretionary trustees fail to act, the court will execute the trust in the manner best calculated to give effect to the settlor's intention: by appointing new trustees, by authorising representatives of the beneficiaries to prepare a scheme of distribution, or by directing the trustees to distribute.
   - Trustees must survey the range of objects; a wider and more comprehensive inquiry is needed for discretionary trusts than for powers. A trustee would never need a complete list, "which anyhow would tell him little that he needs to know".
4. **Re Baden's Deed Trusts (No 2)** [1973] Ch 9 (CA) applied the test to "relatives" and "dependants" (both held conceptually certain). Three different approaches:
   - **Sachs LJ** (with Megaw LJ forming the majority): the test does not require proof that any given individual is *not* in the class, because it is a test of conceptual rather than evidential certainty.
   - **Megaw LJ**: valid if it can be said of a "substantial number" of people that they are in the class, even if for many it cannot be proved whether they are in or out (Sachs LJ appeared not to agree with this extension).
   - **Stamp LJ**: it must be possible to say of *any* individual that he is or is not in the class; on a wide reading of "relatives" (descendants of a common ancestor) the trust would fail, so he read "relatives" narrowly as "nearest blood relatives".
   - Result: "relatives" passes, but the court was inconsistent on how to apply the test.
5. Other examples: "old friends" conceptually uncertain (*Brown v Gould* [1972] Ch 53); "customers" uncertain (*Sparfax v Dommett* (1972) The Times, 14 July).

### 6. Administrative unworkability
- Lord Wilberforce in *McPhail* (457): even if the words are clear, the class may be "so hopelessly wide as not to form 'anything like a class'", making the trust administratively unworkable; tentatively, "all the residents of Greater London". A discretionary trust for "relatives" even of a living person is not in that category.
- *R v District Auditor, ex p West Yorkshire Metropolitan County Council* [1986] RVR 24: a discretionary trust for the "inhabitants of the County of West Yorkshire" (about 2.5 million people) was administratively unworkable and void. The handout calls the decision unsatisfactory: unlike Lord Wilberforce's hypothetical, there was a real connection between the council and the inhabitants.
- **Powers are different**: *Re Manisty's Settlement* [1974] Ch 17: a power is never void merely because of the size of the class. Megarry V-C in *Re Hay's ST* suggested obiter that fiduciary powers could be void for administrative unworkability (first instance, not conclusive). Brown and Pawlowski note Megarry V-C also considered that a discretionary trust for everyone in the world except an excluded class would be unworkable.
- *McPhail* also mentions possible intervention for **capricious** exercise of powers (Lord Wilberforce, 456).

### 7. Avoiding uncertainty by construction: gifts subject to a condition precedent
- **Re Barlow's Will Trusts** [1979] 1 WLR 278: executors were to allow "any members of my family and any friends of mine who may wish to do so" to buy paintings at a reduced price. "Friends" would fail the is or is not test if this were a discretionary trust, but it was construed as a **series of individual gifts subject to a condition precedent**. Such a gift is valid if it can be said that at least one person qualifies (the handout cites *Re Allen*). Whether an instrument creates conditional gifts or a discretionary trust is a matter of construction.

### 8. Arbiter (opinion) clauses
Can a settlor cure conceptual uncertainty by letting a trustee or third party decide? Authority (mostly on conditions, not discretionary trusts) is inconsistent:
- **Re Tuck's Settlement Trusts** [1978] Ch 49 (CA): income to male issue marrying an "approved wife" of Jewish blood and faith, with disputes to be decided conclusively by the Chief Rabbi. Valid, but reasoning differed: Russell LJ thought it certain even without the clause; Lord Denning MR said a settlor may provide that any dispute or doubt be resolved by executors, trustees or a third person; Eveleigh LJ held the Chief Rabbi's view was part of the definition of the class (on that reasoning a clause merely letting him decide what the settlor meant would not work).
- **Re Coxen** [1948] Ch 747: a gift over if, in the trustees' opinion, the widow had ceased permanently to reside in the house. Valid: the trustees decide a question of fact, and the "double event" was sufficiently defined. But Jenkins J said that if the state of affairs were insufficiently defined, making the trustees' opinion the criterion would not save it. Supported by *Re Wright's WT* [1981] LS Gaz 841 (people and institutions who "they think have helped me or my late husband": uncertain) and *Re Jones* [1953] Ch 125 (forfeiture if, in the "uncontrolled opinion" of the company, the daughter had "social or other relationship" with a named person: uncertain).
- **Re Leek** [1969] 1 Ch 563: a discretionary trust for "such persons as the company may consider to have a moral claim" failed the then list test, but the CA suggested it would pass the is or is not test because the company had authority to resolve uncertainty. Virgo: *Leek* is right because the trustees' opinion was an essential part of the class definition; if the trust were for "those who have a moral claim" with trustees empowered to decide, it should fail.
- Three possible positions (handout): (1) Denning/*Leek*: an arbiter can cure any uncertainty; (2) *Coxen*/majority in *Tuck*: an arbiter can decide questions of fact if the criteria are sufficiently defined, but not conceptual uncertainty; (3) Eveleigh LJ/Virgo: an arbiter can cure conceptual uncertainty only if the arbiter's opinion forms an essential part of the definition.

### 9. Consequence of uncertain objects
Resulting trust for the settlor or the testator's estate.

## Key authorities
- *Morice v Bishop of Durham* (1805): the court must be able to control and execute a trust.
- *IRC v Broadway Cottages Trust* [1955] Ch 20: old list test for discretionary trusts (overruled on this point by *McPhail*).
- *Re Gulbenkian's ST* [1970] AC 508: is or is not test for powers.
- *McPhail v Doulton* [1971] AC 424: is or is not test for discretionary trusts; court's methods of execution; administrative unworkability.
- *Re Baden (No 2)* [1973] Ch 9: applying the test to "relatives" and "dependants".
- *OT Computers v First National Tricity Finance* [2003] EWHC 1010 (Ch): complete list test for fixed trusts.
- *Re Sayer* [1957] Ch 423: evidential uncertainty.
- *R v District Auditor, ex p West Yorkshire MCC* [1986] RVR 24: administrative unworkability.
- *Re Manisty's Settlement* [1974] Ch 17; *Re Hay's ST* [1981] 3 All ER 786: powers and duties of fiduciary power-holders.
- *Re Barlow's WT* [1979] 1 WLR 278: gifts subject to condition precedent.
- *Re Tuck's ST* [1978] Ch 49; *Re Coxen* [1948] Ch 747; *Re Leek* [1969] 1 Ch 563; *Re Wright's WT* [1981]; *Re Jones* [1953]: arbiter clauses.
- *Brown v Gould* [1972] Ch 53; *Sparfax v Dommett* (1972): uncertain terms.

## Debate and critique
- **Which version of Baden (No 2)?** Sachs LJ's purely conceptual approach is the most generous; Stamp LJ's strict approach is truest to the words "is *or is not*" but required a strained narrow meaning of "relatives"; Megaw LJ's "substantial number" test arguably reintroduces a quantitative element into a test meant to be about definitions. There is no single ratio on application.
- **Administrative unworkability: certainty or practicality?** Hardcastle ("Administrative unworkability – a reassessment of an abiding problem" [1990] Conv 24) finds its juridical basis "nebulous" but useful. He treats it as a logical extension of *evidential* certainty: performance becomes futile where a potentially innumerable class is combined with a total lack of provable definitional criteria. He rejects the view that it is an unwarranted threat to freedom of disposition, seeing it as a curb on whimsical, unworkable settlements so the court can spend scarce resources on worthwhile ones.
- **Reframe as economic viability**: Brown and Pawlowski ("Re-thinking administrative unworkability in discretionary trusts", Trusts & Trustees 2021) argue modern information technology can make distribution to huge classes cheap, so the doctrine should become a cost/benefit test of administration costs against the fund. They suggest that, in most cases, administration costs exceeding about 50% of the fund could justify treating the trust as economically unworkable.
- **West Yorkshire criticised**: the inhabitants of a county do share a connection with the council-settlor, so the class arguably *was* "something like a class".
- **Trust/power asymmetry**: powers survive administrative unworkability (*Manisty*) while discretionary trusts do not, yet *McPhail* assimilated their certainty tests. Is the remaining difference justified by the court's need to execute a trust but not a power? *Re Hay's* hints at closing the gap.
- **Distributional certainty**: Wilde (2020) suggests administrative unworkability may be better explained as a failure of "distributional certainty" (see certainty-of-intention-and-subject-matter.md).
- **Arbiter clauses**: the line between an arbiter deciding facts and an arbiter supplying the definition (Virgo) may be "too fine a distinction" (handout). Freedom of disposition argues for respecting settlors' choice of arbiter; the court's supervisory role argues against letting a third party define the class.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Classify each disposition**: fixed trust (shares fixed), discretionary trust (trustees *must* distribute but choose how), fiduciary power (trustees *may* appoint), bare power (non-trustee may appoint), or conditional gifts (*Barlow*)? Construe the words: in *McPhail* the direction that trustees "shall apply" income made the clause a trust because they were required to exercise their discretion.
2. **Apply the right test**:
   - fixed trust: complete list; conceptual *and* evidential certainty (*OT Computers*; *Re Sayer*);
   - discretionary trust or power: is or is not, conceptual certainty (*McPhail*; *Gulbenkian*), then *Baden (No 2)* on evidential gaps;
   - conditional gift: can at least one person clearly qualify (*Barlow*)?
3. **Test the words**: "relatives", "dependants", "employees" pass; "friends", "old friends", "customers", "people who helped me" are doubtful (but "friends" may survive as a condition precedent).
4. **Class size**: for discretionary trusts, is it "hopelessly wide" (*McPhail*; *West Yorkshire*)? For powers, size alone is not fatal (*Manisty*), subject to *Re Hay's* obiter.
5. **Arbiter clause?** Does it resolve facts with defined criteria (*Coxen*) or attempt to supply a definition (*Re Wright*; *Re Jones*)? Present the three views.
6. **Duties**: fiduciary power-holders must consider and survey (*Re Hay's*); discretionary trustees must distribute and survey more widely; capricious exercise may be challenged.
7. **Consequence of failure**: resulting trust to settlor or estate. If a power fails, consider who takes in default.
8. **Traps**: applying the list test to a discretionary trust; forgetting that evidential uncertainty does not defeat the is or is not test; treating administrative unworkability as applying equally to powers.
