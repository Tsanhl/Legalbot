# Trustees' non-fiduciary duties and investment

## Overview
Alongside their fiduciary duties (loyalty), trustees owe non-fiduciary duties whose breach is a "breach of trust". The main sources are the trust instrument, equity ("common law" in the handout's loose sense) and statute. The trust instrument trumps the general law, which is the default where the instrument is silent. Whether some duties are so fundamental they cannot be excluded is hotly debated (dealt with under exemption clauses). This note concentrates on investment: the Trustee Act 2000 (TA 2000) gave trustees a wide power but regulates it with statutory duties, alongside two common law duties (best interests and impartiality).

## The law
**1. The power.** Trustees must invest trust funds, but the power was long narrow. TA 2000 s 3(1): "a trustee may make any kind of investment that he could make if he were absolutely entitled to the assets of the trust" (the general power of investment), which the instrument may restrict or exclude. The Act aims to fit modern usage and modern portfolio theory.

**2. Statutory duty of care (s 1).** A trustee must exercise "such care and skill as is reasonable in the circumstances", having regard to (a) any special knowledge or experience held or claimed, and (b) if acting in the course of a business or profession, the knowledge expected of that kind of person. It applies to the general power and any other investment power, and the duties relating to the standard investment criteria (s 4) and advice (s 5), but not to dispositive powers. It can be excluded by the instrument (Sch 1 para 7). The standard is variable: higher for professionals, though professional trustees commonly exclude the duty so lay trustees may in practice face a higher standard.

**3. Standard of prudence.** Earlier case law on the "prudent man of business" remains relevant because the TA 2000 explanatory notes say s 1 makes the existing standard explicit (*Speight v Gaunt* (1883)). *Learoyd v Whiteley* (1886) 33 Ch D 347 (Lindley LJ): the trustee must take the care an ordinary prudent person would take investing for others for whom he felt morally bound to provide; the House of Lords ((1887) 12 App Cas 727) added that trustees should avoid investments attended with hazard. This sits uneasily with modern portfolio theory, which judges investments as a portfolio and accepts risk. Movement towards it: *Bartlett v Barclays Bank Trust Co* [1980] Ch 515 (Brightman J: the distinction is between prudent risk and hazard; the trustee is not an insurer); *Nestle v National Westminster Bank* [1988] (Hoffmann J: an investment too risky in isolation may be justified within a portfolio); *Trustees of the British Museum v AG* [1984] 1 WLR 418 (Megarry V-C: a large fund may justify more latitude).

**4. Standard investment criteria and review (s 4).** Trustees must have regard to the standard investment criteria whenever exercising any investment power and must review investments from time to time, considering whether to vary them. Criteria: (a) suitability to the trust of investments of that kind and of that particular investment; (b) the need for diversification, so far as appropriate to the circumstances. Suitability may cover the type and size of fund, purpose, duration, split between life tenants and remaindermen, needs for income and tax position; its precise meaning is debated.

**5. Advice (s 5).** Before exercising an investment power and when reviewing, trustees must obtain and consider "proper advice" (from someone reasonably believed qualified by ability and practical experience), unless they reasonably conclude it is unnecessary or inappropriate (e.g. their own experience, or small sums where cost would be disproportionate). Advice must not be followed blindly; if they act against advice, they must show they acted prudently (*Cowan v Scargill* [1985] Ch 270).

**6. Best interests of beneficiaries.** The paramount duty is to exercise powers in the beneficiaries' best interests. *Cowan v Scargill*: National Coal Board pension trust; the union-appointed trustees refused a plan involving overseas investment and competing industries. Megarry V-C: where the trust's purpose is financial benefit, best interests are normally best financial interests, regardless of trustees' own moral views; exceptions (e.g. adult beneficiaries with strict moral views) will be very rare and the burden on the person asserting it very heavy. *Merchant Navy Ratings Pension Fund Trustees v Stena Line* [2015] EWHC 448 (Ch) (Asplin J): best interests is not a separate stand-alone duty; the purpose of the trust defines the best interests ("opposite sides of the same coin"; also *Harries v Church Commissioners*).

**7. Ethical investment.** TA 2000 is silent, but the explanatory notes say "suitability" includes relevant ethical considerations. Charities Act 2011 Part 14A expressly empowers social investment. *Butler-Sloss v Charity Commission* [2022] EWHC 974 (Ch): trustees of environmental charities may exclude investments incompatible with the Paris Agreement, relaxing *Harries v Church Commissioners* [1992] 1 WLR 1241 (non-financial considerations only to the extent they do not significantly jeopardise accepted investment criteria). For non-charitable trusts, trustees must still show ethical restrictions better serve beneficiaries' financial interests.

**8. Impartiality.** Where beneficiaries' interests compete (typically life tenant versus remainderman), trustees must maintain a fair balance (*Raby v Ridehalgh* (1855)), but fairness is not equality: Staughton LJ in *Nestle v NatWest* [1993] 1 WLR 1260 said a trustee may favour a life tenant in penury over a wealthy remainderman; Hoffmann J at first instance allowed regard to the life tenant's closeness to the settlor. There is no duty to convert property against the settlor's intention (e.g. to preserve works of art for the next generation). The duty does not apply where the trustees have a discretion to choose between beneficiaries, subject to avoiding "irrelevant, irrational or improper factors" (*Edge v Pensions Ombudsman* [1998] Ch 512).

**9. Practical limits on liability.** Breach and loss must both be shown and linked. *Re Chapman* [1896] 2 Ch 763: honest, ordinarily prudent trustees are not liable for mere errors of judgment. *Nestle v NatWest* [1993]: a fund allegedly worth more than £1m with inflation was worth £269,000, but the claimant could not prove causation of loss. Exclusion clauses further limit claims. Trustees holding shares in a company may need to act: *Bartlett* (Barclays stood back while the board embarked on risky developments; the prudent man of business would inquire and, if necessary, act, so it was in breach). It could set off the loss against gains from the same transaction (the rule against setting off gains and losses does not apply to a single transaction).

**10. General duties** (not elaborated in the handout): know the trust's terms; inquire into the trust's business; personal service subject to delegation under TA 2000; exercise discretion properly; act unanimously (*Boardman v Phipps* [1967] 2 AC 46, with exceptions for pension, charitable trusts and where the instrument allows majority decisions); keep accounts; provide information to beneficiaries.

## Key authorities
- TA 2000 ss 1, 3, 4, 5, Sch 1 para 7.
- *Speight v Gaunt*; *Learoyd v Whiteley*; *Bartlett*; *Nestle v NatWest*; *Re Chapman*.
- *Cowan v Scargill*; *Harries v Church Commissioners*; *Butler-Sloss*; *Merchant Navy Ratings v Stena Line*.

## Debate and critique
- Does "prudent man of business" still fit modern portfolio theory, given its individual-asset, hazard-avoiding focus?
- What do suitability and diversification mean; how far do the criteria allow for ethical or ESG investing, and should the financial-interests rule in *Cowan* yield to beneficiaries' values?
- Professional trustees owe a higher standard but exclude it, so lay trustees may in practice be held to more; the difficulty of proving loss and causation (*Nestle*) makes enforcement weak.
- Best interests as purpose-defined (Asplin J) rather than a free-standing duty.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Check the trust instrument first: does it widen, restrict or exclude investment powers or the duty of care?
2. Power: TA 2000 s 3 general power unless restricted.
3. Duties: s 1 care (professional or lay?), s 4 criteria and periodic review, s 5 advice (exceptions?).
4. Best interests: is a non-financial factor (ethics) involved? Apply *Cowan*, *Harries*, *Butler-Sloss* for charities.
5. Impartiality: competing classes; consider each class's needs; discretionary choice excluded.
6. Remedy realities: breach, loss, causation (*Nestle*), exemption clauses; set-off where gains and losses come from one transaction (*Bartlett*).
