# The beneficiary principle and private purpose trusts

## Overview
As a general rule, a trust for a non-charitable purpose is void. Three reasons are given: (1) the **beneficiary principle**: someone must be able to enforce the trust so the court can control it; (2) the **rules against perpetuities**: a purpose trust must not tie up capital indefinitely; and (3) **certainty**: the purpose must be clear enough to administer. Charitable trusts are the main exception (enforced by the Attorney-General/Charity Commission). There is also a closed list of "anomalous exceptions" (trusts of imperfect obligation: monuments and graves, specific animals, possibly private masses, fox-hunting). Courts can sometimes save a gift by construing it as a trust for *people* (*Re Denley*) or, for unincorporated associations, as a gift to members on contract-holding terms (*Neville Estates v Madden*; *Re Lipinski*). 

## The law

### 1. The beneficiary principle
- *Morice v Bishop of Durham* (1804) 9 Ves 399, 405 (Sir William Grant MR): "Every... trust must have a definite object. There must be someone in whose favour the court can decree performance." A bequest for "such objects of benevolence and liberality" as the Bishop approved failed; the property was undisposed of and resulted to those entitled in default.
- *Bowman v Secular Society* [1917] AC 406, 441 (Lord Parker): a valid trust must be for the benefit of individuals.
- *Leahy v A-G for New South Wales* [1959] AC 457, 478–479 (PC, Viscount Simonds): a gift can be made to persons (including a corporation) but not to a purpose; a trust may be for persons but not for a purpose unless charitable, because "a purpose or object cannot sue", whereas the Attorney-General can sue to enforce a charity.
- **Rationale**: without a legal person interested in proper performance, administration cannot be policed.
- **Re Astor's Settlement Trusts** [1952] Ch 534 (Roxburgh J): income for the maintenance of good understanding between nations and the preservation of the independence and integrity of newspapers (accepted as non-charitable) failed on two grounds: (a) the beneficiary principle (541–542: it is hard to see equitable obligations that nobody can enforce, and one cannot contemplate "with equanimity" large funds for non-charitable purposes that no court or department of state can control); and (b) uncertainty (547: purposes must embody definite concepts and the means of attaining them must be prescribed with sufficient certainty). Both stem from one principle (549): equity does not recognise a trust "which it cannot both enforce and control".

### 2. Anomalous exceptions: trusts of imperfect obligation
Described by Underhill (approved in *Re Astor*) as "concessions to human weakness or sentiment"; there is no principled basis for them.
1. **Tombs, graves and monuments**: *Trimmer v Danby* (1856) 25 LJ Ch 424 (£1,000 for a monument in St Paul's: valid); *Mussett v Bingle* [1876] WN 86 (£300 to erect a monument valid; £200 for its upkeep void for perpetuity); *Pirbright v Salwey* [1896] WN 86 (upkeep of a burial enclosure "as long as the law permitted": valid for 21 years); *Re Hooper* [1932] 1 Ch 38 (upkeep "so far as they legally can": valid for 21 years).
2. **Specific animals** (animals generally can be charitable: *Re Wedgwood* [1915] 1 Ch 113): *Pettingall v Pettingall* (1842) 11 LJ Ch 176 (favourite black mare); *Mitford v Reynolds* (1848) 16 Sim 105 (horses); *Re Dean* (1889) 41 Ch D 552 (North J upheld £750 a year for 50 years for horses and hounds if they lived so long, rejecting the objection that no one could enforce it). *Re Dean* is seen as problematic: it seems to reject the beneficiary principle; the analogy with charitable animal trusts ignores the Attorney-General's enforcement role; and 50 years breaches the perpetuity rule. **Correct approach**: *Re Kelly* [1932] IR 255 (Ireland): valid for 21 years from the death provided a dog lived so long; Meredith J: "lives" means human lives, not dogs, carp, tortoises "or trees in California". But courts have sometimes taken notice of an animal's life expectancy (*Re Haines* (1952) The Times, 7 November).
3. **Masses for the dead**: once void as superstitious uses (Statute of Chantries 1547); held capable of validity in *Bourne v Keane* [1919] AC 815, which did not decide whether they were charitable; *Re Hetherington* [1990] Ch 1 held them charitable if celebrated in public. Sir Nicholas Browne-Wilkinson V-C (at 9) left open whether they could alternatively be valid as an anomalous purpose trust, in which case private masses might be valid though not charitable.
4. **Fox-hunting**: *Re Thompson* [1934] Ch 342 (money to a friend to promote fox-hunting: upheld with a *Pettingall* order). The discussion notes the Hunting Act 2004 ban has exemptions (Sch 1), so the category may still matter.
- **Closed class**: *Re Endacott* [1960] Ch 232 (CA): about £2,000 to a parish council for "some useful memorial to myself" failed. Lord Evershed MR (246): extending the exceptions would validate "almost limitless heads of non-charitable trusts". Harman LJ (250–251): these cases are perhaps "occasions when Homer has nodded" and should not be increased or followed except where exactly alike.
- **Other invalid purpose trusts**: *Re Shaw* [1957] 1 WLR 729 (research into a 40-letter alphabet and transliterating a play).
- **Juridical status**: valid but unenforceable ("imperfect obligation"); trustees cannot be compelled. The court can make a **Pettingall order**: trustees undertake to apply the fund for the purpose, and those entitled on failure or to any surplus get liberty to apply if it is misapplied.
- Still subject to the rules against **capriciousness** and **perpetuity**.

### 3. Capricious and useless purposes
- *M'Caig v University of Glasgow* 1907 SC 231 and *M'Caig's Trustees v Kirk-Session of United Free Church of Lismore* 1915 SC 426 (Scotland): trusts to build towers and statues of the testator and relatives failed as of no utility; *Brown v Burdett* [1882] (blocking up windows and keeping a house empty for 20 years). The discussion asks whether there is a tension between avoiding useless dispositions and preserving testamentary freedom (Pawlowski and Brown, "Testamentary Trusts and the Rule Against Capricious Purposes" [2012] Trust Law International 109).

### 4. Trust for people or for a purpose? (construction)
- *Re Abbott Fund Trusts* [1900] 2 Ch 326: a fund for two elderly deaf and dumb ladies, with wide discretion, construed as a trust for the *purpose* of benefiting them (valid, as they could enforce); on their deaths the surplus went back to the donors on resulting trust.
- *Re Andrew's Trust* [1905] 2 Ch 48: a fund for the education of a clergyman's children; after their education the children took the surplus in equal shares.
- *Re Osoba* [1979] 2 All ER 393 (CA): residue "for the training of my daughter up to University grade": an absolute gift, the purpose being mere motive.
- *Re Bowes* [1896] 1 Ch 507: money to plant trees on a settled estate belonged to the estate owners absolutely.
- **Re Denley's Trust Deed** [1969] 1 Ch 673 (Goff J): land on trust as a sports ground primarily for a company's employees (and, at the trustees' discretion, others) for a perpetuity period. Valid: although expressed as a purpose, it directly or indirectly benefited ascertainable individuals with standing to enforce. Goff J: the beneficiary principle is confined to purpose trusts that are "abstract or impersonal"; but a purpose whose benefit to individuals is so indirect or intangible as to give them no locus standi would still fail. Under *Denley* the trustees must carry out the purpose.

### 5. Perpetuity and certainty
- **Remoteness of vesting**: rarely fatal to a *Denley* trust, because the Perpetuities and Accumulations Act 2009 allows wait and see and class-closing at the end of the 125-year period.
- **Inalienability**: a private purpose trust is void if income is tied up beyond the perpetuity period (lives in being plus 21 years); the 2009 Act cannot save it (see public-policy-limits-on-trusts.md).
- **Certainty**: "benevolence", "liberality" (*Morice*); "integrity of the press" (*Astor*); "useful" memorial (*Endacott*).

### 6. Unincorporated associations
- An unincorporated association (a non-commercial club or society) is not a legal person and cannot hold property; a gift to it risks being a non-charitable purpose trust. People nonetheless think of clubs as continuing entities (Viscount Simonds in *Leahy*).
- **Definition**: *Conservative and Unionist Central Office v Burrell* [1982] 1 WLR 522, 525 (Lawton LJ): two or more persons bound together for common non-business purposes by mutual undertakings, with rules identifying who controls the organisation and its funds, which can be joined or left at will; the bond must be contractual.
- **Neville Estates Ltd v Madden** [1962] Ch 832, 849 (Cross J), building on *Leahy*: a gift may take effect (1) to the current members as joint tenants (each can sever); (2) to the current members subject to their contractual rights and liabilities as members (no severance; shares accrue to remaining and future members; no perpetuity or uncertainty problem unless the rules stop members dividing the property); or (3) on trust for the purposes of the association as a quasi-corporate entity (fails unless charitable). A gift within (2) is not charitable even if the association's objects are.
- **Five possible constructions**: charitable trust; non-charitable purpose trust (usually void: *Leahy* (trust for an order of nuns, dispersed worldwide, void); *Denley* might save some); gift to members at the time as joint tenants (rare; suits small groups using the association as a "convenient label": Vinelott J in *Re Grant's WT*); trust for present and future members (traditionally perpetuity problems, maybe not after 2009; leavers keep an interest); **contract-holding** (preferred): members hold subject to the rules, leavers lose and joiners gain, no severance at will.
- **Re Lipinski's Will Trusts** [1977] 1 All ER 33 (Oliver J): a legacy to a Jewish youth sports and social association, in memory of the testator's wife, to be used "solely" for constructing or improving buildings. Valid: a purpose within the association's powers of which the members are the beneficiaries; the members could enforce or, by their contractual rights, end the trust and take the property, since trustees and beneficiaries were the same persons.
- **Limit**: the contract-holding analysis needs rules allowing members to deal with the property (or to change the rules). *Re Grant's WT* [1979] 3 All ER 359: a gift to a local Labour Party for a constituency party failed because its rules could be changed only with national bodies' approval and property could not be dealt with without the National Executive Committee's consent. Similarly *St Andrew's (Cheam) Lawn Tennis Club Trust* [2012] EWHC 1040 at [62] (members could not divide the land or change the rules without the Church's consent). Contrast *Re Horley Town Football Club* [2006] EWHC 2386 (membership extended to temporary members from 1990).
- **Other constructions**: an inter vivos gift may be a transfer to an agent (*Burrell*); a *power* to appoint for a purpose is valid if certain and within the perpetuity period, but courts refuse to treat failed trusts as powers to evade the beneficiary principle (*Re Shaw*; *Re Endacott*).

## Debate and critique
- **Is the beneficiary principle sound?** Roxburgh J's "enforce and control" rationale is persuasive for large funds, but the anomalous exceptions show equity tolerating unenforceable trusts. Harman LJ's "Homer has nodded" concedes they are unprincipled.
- **Pettingall orders and enforcement**: Wilde ("Trusts of imperfect obligation", Trusts & Trustees) doubts that the Pettingall order is a true enforcement mechanism. The court never ordered performance; those entitled on failure might want either performance or the property on resulting trust, and the cases do not say which they can apply for. The likely result of breach is merely admonishing the trustee, with the property then held on resulting trust. (He quips that the best answer to "who enforces?" may be "the police".) He also rejects the view (Megarry, 1952 LQR note on *Astor*) that these are really *powers* misclassified as trusts: fiduciary powers carry duties, and for purpose powers no one could enforce those duties either (citing Lionel Smith).
- **Re Denley**: is it a genuine exception or a trust for beneficiaries? Goff J treats it as outside the mischief of the principle, but his own reservation (benefit "so indirect or intangible" as to give no standing) leaves its reach uncertain.
- **Contract-holding**: it depends entirely on the association's rules and fails where outside bodies control them (*Re Grant*; *St Andrew's (Cheam)*), so similar gifts can succeed or fail on drafting details. Jaffey (footnote in "Remedial consistency") compares the content of a member's right to the contract between members (*Neville Estates*; *Re Recher's WT* [1972] Ch 526).
- **Reform: legalise private purpose trusts with an enforcer**: Pawlowski and Summers ("Private purpose trusts – a reform proposal" [2007] Conv 440) note probate practitioners still frequently use imperfect obligation trusts (pets, graves, private religious ceremonies), drawing on a survey by J Brown. They see no clear policy reason against non-charitable purpose trusts and, drawing on offshore models (Trusts (Jersey) Law 1984; Cayman STAR 1997; BVI VISTA 2003), propose legislation: creation by deed or will; opt-in designation; an 80-year perpetuity period with no accumulation limit; determination events (purpose fulfilled or impossible) policed by an *independent enforcer*; purposes certain and not against public policy; default beneficiaries specified. Watt notes the Law Commission left inalienability reform to a wider purpose trust review.
- **Crowdfunding**: Wilde (2024) asks whether crowdfunded litigation money is held on purpose trusts (void) or beneficiary trusts for the litigant limited to costs (valid), preferring the latter, and links it to *Quistclose* (see quistclose-trusts.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Construe the gift**: absolute gift with motive (*Osoba*), trust for people (*Abbott*, *Andrew's*), *Denley*-type purpose benefiting ascertainable individuals, or abstract purpose (*Astor*, *Endacott*)?
2. **Is the purpose charitable?** If so, the beneficiary principle does not apply (charitable-trusts.md).
3. **Anomalous exception?** Monument/grave, specific animal, private masses, fox-hunting. Do not extend (*Endacott*). Check perpetuity (21 years unless expressly limited to a valid period: *Re Hooper*, *Re Kelly*; not 50 years as in *Re Dean*), certainty, capriciousness (*M'Caig*). Suggest a Pettingall order.
4. **Denley**: are beneficiaries ascertainable (certainty of objects: is or is not) and is their benefit direct enough for standing? Perpetuity period respected?
5. **Unincorporated association**: is it an association (*Burrell*)? Choose a *Neville Estates* construction; prefer contract-holding if the rules let members control and divide the property (*Lipinski*); fails if outside control (*Re Grant*). A specified purpose within the association's powers that benefits members can survive (*Lipinski*).
6. **If void**: resulting trust to settlor/estate (*Morice*; *Abbott*).
7. **Traps**: treating every purpose wording as fatal; forgetting perpetuity and capriciousness for valid exceptions; using *Re Dean*'s 50-year period; ignoring *Re Grant*-type rule restrictions.
