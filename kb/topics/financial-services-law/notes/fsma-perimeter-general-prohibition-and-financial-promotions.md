# The FSMA 2000 perimeter: general prohibition, financial promotion, unenforceability and s 138D actions

## Overview
FSMA 2000 draws a "perimeter" around regulated financial activity. No one may carry on a regulated activity by way of business in the UK unless authorised or exempt (s 19); no one may communicate a financial promotion in the course of business unless authorised or the promotion is approved by an authorised person (s 21). Breaches are criminal offences, make resulting agreements unenforceable against the customer with a right to recover money (ss 26-28), and authorised firms that break FCA rules can be sued for damages by private persons (s 138D).

## The law
**General prohibition (s 19).** No person may carry on a regulated activity in the United Kingdom, or purport to do so, unless he is an authorised person or an exempt person (s 19(1)). The prohibition covers activities that are "specified" in the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001 (SI 2001/544) (the RAO), made under s 22 and Schedule 2, and carried on **by way of business**. The principal regulated activities include: accepting deposits; effecting and carrying out contracts of insurance; dealing in investments as principal or agent; arranging deals in investments; managing investments; advising on investments; operating a collective investment scheme; establishing and operating pensions schemes; regulated mortgage activities; and consumer credit activities (within the RAO since 1 April 2014). Payment services and e-money are regulated under separate regimes (the Payment Services Regulations 2017 and Electronic Money Regulations 2011). The investments covered (shares, debentures, units in collective investment schemes, derivatives and so on) are listed in Part III of the RAO. The FCA's Perimeter Guidance manual (PERG) explains how these are applied.
- Offence: contravention of the general prohibition is an offence punishable on summary conviction by up to six months' imprisonment or a fine, and on indictment by up to two years or a fine (s 23(1)).
- Exempt persons include appointed representatives of authorised firms (s 39) and persons exempt by order (for example, some recognised bodies). An authorised firm acting outside its permission contravenes s 20.
- **Cryptoassets**: until the new regime starts, most cryptoasset activities are outside the perimeter (apart from the financial promotion rules and anti-money-laundering registration). The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026 create new regulated activities for cryptoassets from 25 October 2027. The FCA's authorisation application window runs from 30 September 2026 to 28 February 2027; firms that apply in the window can rely on savings provisions while their applications are decided.

**Financial promotion (s 21).** A person must not, in the course of business, communicate an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity, or to engage in claims management activity (s 21(1)). It is not an offence if the person is authorised, or the content has been approved by an authorised person (s 21(2)); since 7 February 2024, an authorised person can approve another person's promotion only if it has FCA permission to do so (the "approver gateway", s 55NA, inserted by FSMA 2023). The communication must be capable of having an effect in the UK if it originates abroad (s 21(3)). The Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Financial Promotion) Order 2005 (SI 2005/1529) (FPO) provides many exemptions (for example, communications to investment professionals, certified high net worth individuals, sophisticated investors, and one-off or real-time communications in certain circumstances). Since 8 October 2023 qualifying cryptoassets are within the regime, which has led to a run of cases and the FCA's alerts about unauthorised crypto promotions.
- Offence: s 25(1) (the same penalties as s 23). Defences: reasonable belief that the content was prepared or approved by an authorised person; all reasonable precautions and due diligence (s 25(2)).
- Civil consequence: an agreement entered into as a consequence of an unlawful communication is unenforceable against the other party, who may recover money paid and compensation (s 30), subject to the court's discretion to allow enforcement if it is just and equitable (s 30(4)).

**Unenforceability of agreements (ss 26-28).**
- s 26(1): an agreement made by a person in the course of carrying on a regulated activity in contravention of the general prohibition is unenforceable against the other party. s 26(2): the other party is entitled to recover any money or property paid or transferred under the agreement and compensation for any loss sustained as a result of having parted with it. (s 26(4): not applicable to deposit-taking, which has its own provision in s 29.)
- s 27: an agreement made by an authorised person *in consequence of* something said or done by an unauthorised third party in the course of a regulated activity in contravention of the general prohibition (the "unauthorised introducer") is unenforceable against the other party, with the same recovery rights.
- s 28(2)-(3): compensation is the amount agreed or determined by the court; the court may allow the agreement to be enforced or money retained if it is just and equitable. The court must have regard, for s 26, to whether the person carrying on the activity reasonably believed he was not contravening the general prohibition (s 28(5)), and for s 27, whether the authorised person knew the third party was contravening it (s 28(6)). s 28(7): a person who elects not to perform or recovers money must repay money and return property received under the agreement. s 28(9): the commission of an authorisation offence does not make the agreement illegal or invalid beyond what ss 26-27 provide. Credit-related agreements have their own provisions (ss 26A, 28A).

**Private actions: s 138D.** A contravention by an authorised person of an FCA rule is actionable at the suit of a *private person* who suffers loss as a result, subject to the defences and incidents of an action for breach of statutory duty (s 138D(2)). The FCA may by rule specify that a particular rule is not actionable (s 138D(3)); in practice the Principles for Businesses, and certain other rules, are expressly excluded. s 138D(5) excludes certain categories of rules altogether (including Part 6 rules, the threshold condition code and rules requiring financial resources). "Private person" has the meaning prescribed by the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Rights of Action) Regulations 2001: individuals acting otherwise than in the course of business, and some others. s 138E: a contravention of an FCA rule does not make a transaction void or unenforceable and is not itself an offence, with limited exceptions. The Consumer Duty did not create a new s 138D claim.

## Key authorities
- *Rubenstein v HSBC Bank plc* [2012] EWCA Civ 1184: a bank advised a customer, who wanted no risk to his capital, to invest in a bond fund it described as equivalent to a cash deposit; it was liable in negligence and for breach of COBS rules (under the predecessor of s 138D). The loss suffered when the fund fell in the 2008 market turmoil was not too remote, because protecting against that very risk was the point of the advice.
- *Philipp v Barclays Bank UK plc* [2023] UKSC 25 and *Larsson v Revolut Ltd* [2024] EWHC 1287 (Ch): see the note on authorised push payment fraud; they show that bank duties are tied to the contractual relationship and do not expand with regulatory requirements.
The perimeter provisions are mainly statutory and there is little leading case law in this knowledge base; see PERG for the FCA's guidance on where the perimeter lies.

## Applying it
1. Is there a "regulated activity" under the RAO (arts. for dealing, arranging, advising, managing; deposits; insurance), involving a specified investment, carried on **by way of business** in the UK? If not, no authorisation is needed (but consider other regimes such as consumer credit, payment services, and the crypto promotion rules).
2. Is the person authorised, exempt (appointed representative), or relying on an RAO exclusion? If none, there is a general prohibition contravention, a criminal offence and unenforceability under s 26.
3. Was there a communication of an invitation or inducement to engage in investment activity, in the course of business, by an unauthorised person without approval by an authorised person? If so, s 21 and s 25 apply, subject to FPO exemptions.
4. Consequences: criminal liability; the customer may rely on ss 26-28 to get their money back (the court may allow enforcement or retention only if just and equitable, having regard to the firm's reasonable belief or knowledge under s 28(5)-(6)); the FCA can seek injunctions and restitution (ss 380-382).
5. For a private claim against an authorised firm for breach of FCA rules, check s 138D, whether the claimant is a "private person", whether the rule is excluded, and causation and loss.
6. Common traps: assuming that authorisation is required for any financial service (it is not: perimeter is defined by the RAO); forgetting "by way of business"; confusing ss 26 and 27; assuming s 138D gives a claim for breach of the Principles.
Everyday version: unauthorised traders offering investments, or people running a trading scheme without FCA authorisation, can be prosecuted and any contract can be undone; a customer who dealt with them can usually get their money back through the courts or the regulator.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the perimeter is clear enough (PERG exists because it is not), the "by way of business" test, and calls for a "smarter" approach to regulation.
- Section 138D: whether private rights of action should extend to firm-level duties such as the Consumer Duty (the FCA concluded not), as against the FOS route.
- Regulating cryptoassets and the use of social media "finfluencers" (financial promotion enforcement in the digital sphere).

## Recent changes and points to check
- Approver gateway for financial promotions: from 7 February 2024.
- Qualifying cryptoasset promotions regime: from 8 October 2023.
- New regulated activities for cryptoassets (Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026): the regime commences on 25 October 2027; the FCA application window runs from 30 September 2026 to 28 February 2027.
- FSMA 2023 is replacing assimilated (formerly retained) EU law in financial services with FCA and PRA rules over time (the Smarter Regulatory Framework).
- The Financial Services and Markets Bill [HL] (introduced May 2026; completed its Lords stages on 15 September 2026; in Commons committee in November 2026) would, among other things, abolish the Payment Systems Regulator and transfer its functions to the FCA, reform the Financial Ombudsman Service, cut the administrative burden of the Senior Managers and Certification Regime and update the ring-fencing framework. It was not law as of November 2026.
