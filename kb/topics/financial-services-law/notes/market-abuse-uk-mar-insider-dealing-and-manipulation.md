# Market abuse: UK MAR, criminal insider dealing and misleading statements

## Overview
UK market abuse law has a civil/administrative regime (the UK Market Abuse Regulation, "UK MAR", enforced by the FCA with unlimited financial penalties on a balance-of-probabilities standard) and criminal regimes: insider dealing under Part V of the Criminal Justice Act 1993 (CJA) and misleading statements and impressions under ss 89-91 of the Financial Services Act 2012 (FSA 2012), each punishable by up to 10 years' imprisonment on indictment. The civil regime is wider than the criminal one and does not need a mental element of dishonesty.

## The law
**UK MAR (Regulation (EU) 596/2014 as it forms part of UK law).** Applies to financial instruments admitted to trading (or requested to be admitted) on a UK trading venue and related instruments, wherever the conduct occurs, within the territorial reach of the Regulation (Article 2).
- **Inside information** (Art 7(1)(a)): information of a precise nature, which has not been made public, relating directly or indirectly to one or more issuers or financial instruments, and which, if made public, would be likely to have a significant effect on the prices of the financial instruments (or related derivatives). It is "precise" if it indicates circumstances that exist or may reasonably be expected to come into existence or an event that has occurred or may reasonably be expected to occur, and is specific enough to enable a conclusion about the possible effect on prices (Art 7(2)); intermediate steps in protracted processes can themselves be inside information (Art 7(2)-(3)). Information is likely to have a significant price effect if a reasonable investor would be likely to use it as part of the basis of investment decisions (Art 7(4)).
- **Insider dealing** (Art 8): where a person possesses inside information and uses it by acquiring or disposing of, for its own account or a third party's, directly or indirectly, financial instruments to which the information relates; including cancelling or amending an order placed before possession. Recommending or inducing another to engage in insider dealing is also insider dealing where the person knows or ought to know it is based on inside information (Art 8(2)). Art 8(3) provides that using a recommendation or inducement is insider dealing where the person acting on it knows or ought to know it is based on inside information. Art 9 sets out legitimate behaviours (for example, market makers acting in the ordinary course; execution of orders on behalf of third parties; transactions made to satisfy a legal obligation).
- **Unlawful disclosure** (Art 10): disclosure of inside information to any other person except where made in the normal exercise of an employment, profession or duties; onward recommendation or inducement based on inside information (Art 10(2)). Market soundings that comply with Art 11 are lawful.
- **Market manipulation** (Art 12): (a) transactions, orders or behaviours that give, or are likely to give, false or misleading signals about supply, demand or price, or secure the price at an abnormal or artificial level (subject to legitimate reasons and accepted market practice); (b) transactions or orders employing a fictitious device or any other form of deception or contrivance; (c) disseminating information through media or otherwise which gives false or misleading signals or secures an abnormal price, where the person knew or ought to have known the information was false or misleading; (d) transmitting false or misleading information or inputs in relation to a benchmark. Annex I and the Delegated Regulation list indicators, including layering, spoofing, wash trades and "marking the close".
- **Prohibitions** (Art 14 and Art 15): a person shall not engage or attempt to engage in insider dealing, recommend or induce, unlawfully disclose inside information, or engage or attempt to engage in market manipulation.
- **Obligations on issuers and others**: public disclosure of inside information as soon as possible (Art 17), with the possibility of delay (Art 17(4)); insider lists (Art 18); notification of transactions by persons discharging managerial responsibilities (PDMRs) and closely associated persons within three business days of the transaction (Art 19); suspicious transaction and order reports by persons professionally arranging or executing transactions (Art 16(2)).
- **Safe harbours**: buy-back programmes and stabilisation (Art 5), market soundings (Art 11), and accepted market practices (Art 13).
- **Enforcement**: the FCA can impose an unlimited financial penalty or issue a public censure (FSMA s 123(1)-(3), for contravention of Art 14 or Art 15 or other provisions of UK MAR), prohibit individuals (s 123A), seek restitution or injunctions (ss 381-383), and impose requirements under Part 11 for information. Civil standard of proof; the Upper Tribunal reviews decisions.

**Criminal insider dealing: Criminal Justice Act 1993, Part V.**
- s 52(1): an individual who has information as an insider is guilty if he deals in securities that are price-affected in relation to the information, in the circumstances set out in s 52(3) (the acquisition or disposal occurs on a regulated market, or he relies on a professional intermediary or is acting as one).
- s 52(2)(a): encouraging another person to deal, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the dealing would take place in the s 52(3) circumstances; s 52(2)(b): disclosing the information to another person otherwise than in the proper performance of employment, office or profession.
- s 56: inside information is information which (a) relates to particular securities or a particular issuer, not securities or issuers generally; (b) is specific or precise; (c) has not been made public; and (d) if made public would be likely to have a significant effect on the price of any securities (price-sensitive).
- s 57: a person has information as an insider only if it is, and he knows it is, inside information, and he has it, and knows he has it, from an inside source: as a director, employee or shareholder of an issuer, or through employment, office or profession, or directly or indirectly from such a person. s 58: information is made public if published under regulated market rules, in public records, readily acquired by those likely to deal, or derived from public information; it can be public even if it takes diligence or expertise to find it.
- s 53 defences: for dealing, that he did not expect the dealing to result in a profit (or avoided loss) attributable to the information being price-sensitive; that he reasonably believed that the information had been disclosed widely enough that no one taking part would be prejudiced; or that he would have done what he did even if he had not had the information. Similar defences for encouraging (s 53(2)) and for disclosing (s 53(3)). Schedule 1 contains special defences (market makers; market information; price stabilisation).
- Penalty (s 61): on conviction on indictment, imprisonment of up to ten years (the maximum was increased from seven years from 1 November 2021 by the Financial Services Act 2021) or a fine, or both; on summary conviction up to six months or a fine. Proceedings are brought by the FCA (and the SFO or CPS in other cases).

**Misleading statements and impressions: Financial Services Act 2012.**
- s 89: offence where a person makes a statement which he knows to be false or misleading in a material respect, or is reckless as to that, or dishonestly conceals material facts, intending to induce (or being reckless as to whether it may induce) another person to enter or refrain from entering a relevant agreement or to exercise or refrain from exercising rights in a relevant investment (UK connection required). Defence in s 89(3) for conformity with price stabilising rules, control of information rules, or the Art 5 UK MAR exemption.
- s 90: a person who does an act or engages in a course of conduct that creates a false or misleading impression as to the market in, or the price or value of, relevant investments, with intent to create the impression and either (a) intending thereby to induce another to acquire, dispose of, subscribe for or underwrite investments or to exercise or refrain from exercising rights (s 90(2)), or (b) knowing or reckless that the impression is false or misleading and intending to make a gain or cause loss or exposure to loss (s 90(3)). Defence of reasonable belief for the inducement limb, and conformity with stabilisation rules and Art 5 (s 90(9)).
- s 91: misleading statements and impressions relating to benchmarks. s 92: penalty: up to 10 years' imprisonment or a fine, or both, on indictment.

## Key authorities
- *Spector Photo Group NV v Commissie voor het Bank-, Financie- en Assurantiewezen* (Case C-45/08) (CJEU, 2009): a person who holds inside information and trades is presumed to have used it, rebuttable by evidence; persuasive for the "use" element in Art 8 (assimilated EU case law, which UK courts may now depart from).
- *Hannam v Financial Conduct Authority* [2014] UKUT 233 (TCC): the Upper Tribunal upheld the FCA's finding that a senior banker committed market abuse by disclosing inside information in two emails to a prospective client, rejecting his argument that the disclosures were made in the proper course of his employment (penalty GBP 450,000). Its view that "precise" information must allow a conclusion about the direction of the price movement is doubtful after *Lafonta v AMF* (Case C-628/13, 2015), where the CJEU held that information can be precise without indicating whether the price will rise or fall.
- *Financial Conduct Authority v Da Vinci Invest Ltd* [2015] EWHC 2401 (Ch) (Snowden J): the first case in which the court used its powers (s 129 and s 381 FSMA) to impose financial penalties and permanent injunctions for market abuse: layering/spoofing in the CFD market was market manipulation under the then s 118(5) FSMA; the FCA argued, and the court accepted that market abuse is assessed objectively, without needing proof of intent.

## Applying it
1. **Inside information?** Is it precise, non-public, relating to an issuer or instrument, and price-sensitive (reasonable investor test under UK MAR; "significant effect on price" under CJA)? Remember intermediate steps in a protracted event (such as a bid being prepared).
2. **Who holds it and how?** For the criminal offence, the person must be an "insider" with information from an inside source and know it. For UK MAR, any person who possesses inside information (in some cases because of criminal activity or any other reason) can commit insider dealing; the persons listed in Art 8(4) are the main categories.
3. **Conduct**: dealing (acquiring or disposing, cancelling or amending orders); recommending or inducing; unlawful disclosure (outside the normal exercise of employment, profession or duties); or manipulation (false or misleading signals, abnormal price, deception, false information).
4. **Mental element**: UK MAR insider dealing needs possession and use (use may be presumed); manipulation by false signals needs no intent; criminal offences need the knowledge, recklessness or intent defined in FSA 2012 ss 89-90 or the insider's knowledge (CJA s 57). There is no statutory reasonable-belief defence to an FCA penalty under s 123 (the pre-2016 defence was not retained), though state of mind affects the penalty (DEPP).
5. **Defences and exemptions**: UK MAR Art 5 (buy-back and stabilisation), Art 9 (legitimate behaviour), Art 11 (market soundings), accepted market practices; CJA ss 53 and Schedule 1; FSA 2012 ss 89(3), 90(9).
6. **Consequences**: FCA civil penalty or censure and possible prohibition; criminal prosecution (up to 10 years); confiscation under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002; disqualification and loss of approval.
7. Common traps: confusing the criminal "price-sensitive information" test with the UK MAR reasonable investor test; treating tipping off as legal because the tipper did not trade; forgetting that disclosure in the proper course of employment is exempt; ignoring that the criminal offences need dealing on a regulated market or via a professional intermediary.
Everyday version: trading on, or tipping others off about, confidential price-moving information from inside a company is both a crime and civil market abuse, as is giving the market a false picture to move prices.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the dual civil and criminal regimes are effective, given the small number of criminal convictions compared with FCA civil enforcement; the criminal law's insistence on an "inside source".
- Whether the Treasury's replacement of assimilated UK MAR with FCA rules will lower or maintain standards (the aim stated is to retain substance while enabling a more flexible regime).
- The treatment of cryptoasset market abuse: the FCA's final rules for a separate market abuse regime for cryptoassets (PS26/9, 30 June 2026) prohibit insider dealing, unlawful disclosure and manipulation in cryptoassets from 25 October 2027, when the cryptoasset regime starts.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The criminal maximum for insider dealing and for FSA 2012 ss 89-91 is ten years' imprisonment (insider dealing raised from seven years from 1 November 2021).
- UK MAR remains in force as assimilated law. FSMA 2023 lists it for revocation and replacement by FCA rules and Treasury legislation under the Smarter Regulatory Framework, but no repeal date had been set by November 2026 (the UK Prospectus Regulation, by contrast, was replaced on 19 January 2026). The FCA's MAR sourcebook contains guidance and related rules; it is not the Regulation itself.
- The UK Listing Rules (UKLR) replaced the old Listing Rules from 29 July 2024; issuers' disclosure of inside information remains governed by UK MAR Art 17.
