# Payment Services Regulations 2017: authorised and unauthorised payments

## Overview
Part 7 of the Payment Services Regulations 2017 (SI 2017/752) (PSRs) sets out who bears the loss when a payment is made without the account holder's consent. If a payment is **unauthorised**, the payer's payment service provider (PSP) must refund it by the end of the next business day, and the customer's liability is capped (GBP 35) unless the customer acted fraudulently or, with intent or gross negligence, breached the duties to keep credentials safe and notify promptly. If the payer **authorised** the payment, even if tricked, the PSRs do not provide a refund (see the APP fraud note). The customer must notify within 13 months of the debit date.

## The law
**Authorisation (reg 67).** A payment transaction is regarded as authorised by the payer only if the payer has given its consent to the execution of the transaction or a series of transactions (reg 67(1)). Consent may be given before or, if agreed, after execution; must be given in the form and following the procedure agreed between payer and PSP (for example, entering a PIN, using biometric, approving in the banking app); and may be given via the payee or a payment initiation service provider (reg 67(2)). The payer can withdraw consent before the payment order can no longer be revoked (reg 67(3)); consent to a series (for example a direct debit or continuous payment authority) can be withdrawn so that future payments are not authorised (reg 67(4)). Consent is therefore judged by the agreed procedure and the payer's actual intention; use of the instrument alone does not prove it (reg 75(3)).

**User obligations (reg 72).** A payment service user to whom a payment instrument has been issued must use it in accordance with the terms and conditions governing its issue and use (which must be objective, non-discriminatory and proportionate), and notify the PSP in the agreed manner and without undue delay on becoming aware of the loss, theft, misappropriation or unauthorised use (reg 72(1)-(2)). The user must take all reasonable steps to keep safe personalised security credentials (reg 72(3)). The PSP's duties (reg 73) include making sure credentials are not accessible to others, providing means of notification at all times and free of charge, providing proof of notification for 18 months, and preventing use of the instrument after notification.

**Notification and time limit (reg 74).** A user is entitled to redress for unauthorised or incorrectly executed payments only if it notifies the PSP without undue delay, and in any event no later than 13 months after the debit date, on becoming aware of the problem (reg 74(1)). If the PSP failed to provide the information required by Part 6, the customer can still obtain redress notwithstanding late notice (reg 74(2)).

**Evidence (reg 75).** Where the customer denies authorising an executed payment, or claims it was not correctly executed, it is for the PSP to prove that the transaction was authenticated, accurately recorded, entered in the PSP's accounts and not affected by a technical breakdown or other deficiency (reg 75(1)); a payment initiation service provider has the same burden within its sphere (reg 75(2)). The use of a payment instrument recorded by the PSP is not in itself necessarily sufficient to prove either that the payer authorised it or that the payer acted fraudulently or with intent or gross negligence in breach of reg 72 (reg 75(3)). If the PSP alleges fraud or intentional or grossly negligent breach, it must provide supporting evidence to the payer (reg 75(4)).

**PSP's liability for unauthorised payments (reg 76).** Subject to regs 74 and 75, where an executed payment was not authorised in accordance with reg 67, the PSP must refund the amount to the payer and, where applicable, restore the debited account to the state it would have been in (reg 76(1)). The refund must be made as soon as practicable and in any event no later than the end of the business day following the day on which it becomes aware of the unauthorised transaction (reg 76(2)). That timing does not apply where the PSP has reasonable grounds to suspect fraudulent behaviour by the customer and notifies the National Crime Agency or other persons mentioned in the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 s 333A(2) in writing (reg 76(3)). The credit value date must be no later than the date of the debit (reg 76(4)). For payments initiated through a payment initiation service provider, the account servicing PSP refunds and can claim compensation from the initiating provider if it is liable (reg 76(5)).

**Payer's liability (reg 77).**
- A PSP liable under reg 76(1) may require the payer to bear losses up to a maximum of GBP 35 arising from use of a lost or stolen payment instrument, or misappropriation (reg 77(1)). This does not apply if the loss, theft or misappropriation was not detectable by the payer before payment (unless the payer acted fraudulently), or the loss was caused by acts or omissions of an employee, agent or branch of the PSP or an outsourced entity (reg 77(2)).
- The payer is liable for **all** losses if the payer has acted fraudulently, or has with intent or **gross negligence** failed to comply with reg 72 (reg 77(3)).
- Except where the payer acted fraudulently, the payer is **not** liable for losses (a) arising after notification under reg 72(1)(b); (b) where the PSP failed to provide appropriate means for notification at any time; (c) where strong customer authentication (SCA) was required by reg 100 but the payer's PSP did not require it; or (d) where the instrument was used in connection with a distance contract (other than an excepted contract) (reg 77(4)-(5)).
- Where SCA is required but the payee or its PSP does not accept it, the payee or its PSP must compensate the payer's PSP (reg 77(6)).

**Strong customer authentication (reg 100).** A PSP must apply SCA where the customer accesses its payment account online, initiates an electronic payment transaction, or carries out an action through a remote channel that may imply a risk of fraud (reg 100(1)). For electronic remote payments, SCA must dynamically link the transaction to a specific amount and payee (reg 100(2)). SCA means authentication using two or more independent elements from knowledge, possession and inherence (defined in the Regulations and technical standards), subject to exemptions in the technical standards (reg 100(5)).

**Gross negligence.** The PSRs do not define it. The FCA's guidance (the Approach Document) says it is a higher standard than negligence: in the FCA's words, the customer must have shown "a very significant degree of carelessness". The Ombudsman applies it case by case: the PSP bears the burden of proving it with evidence (reg 75(4)); sharing a PIN or one-time code with a caller who claimed to be from the bank may be considered, but the Ombudsman has often found that people tricked by sophisticated impersonation were not grossly negligent. There is little appellate authority on the point.

**Other payment errors (outline).** Regs 79-80 (refunds for payee-initiated payments, for example direct debits under a scheme guarantee and card-based payments where the amount was not known in advance); reg 90 (incorrect unique identifiers: the PSP is not liable for non-execution or defective execution if it executed in accordance with the unique identifier supplied, but must make reasonable efforts to recover the funds); regs 91-93 (non-execution or defective or late execution: liability of the payer's PSP unless it proves the payee's PSP received the amount); reg 94 (charges and interest). Since 30 October 2024 (Payment Services (Amendment) Regulations 2024, SI 2024/1013), reg 86(2A)-(2D) allows a payer's PSP to delay crediting the payee's PSP, where, by the end of the business day after receipt, it has established reasonable grounds to suspect that the payment order was placed subsequent to fraud or dishonesty by someone other than the payer, for the purpose of contacting the payer or others to make enquiries, for no longer than necessary and in any event no later than the end of the fourth business day following receipt of the order; the PSP must notify the payer of the delay, the reasons and any information or action needed from the payer, by the end of the business day after receipt (unless unlawful); payee-initiated card payments are outside these provisions; reg 94A makes the PSP liable for charges and interest caused by the delay.

## Key authorities
- *Philipp v Barclays Bank UK plc* [2023] UKSC 25: the Supreme Court explained that the PSRs' refund rights are largely concerned with unauthorised payments (paras 17-18); it also noted the reimbursement scheme enacted by FSMA 2023 s 72 (para 21). Authorised payments, even induced by fraud, are authorised.
- *Larsson v Revolut Ltd* [2024] EWHC 1287 (Ch): contract and negligence claims against a receiving payment institution in an APP fraud were struck out; the court declined to create a new duty to protect payers, treating the matter as one for the regulators and Parliament (a dishonest assistance claim was allowed to be re-pleaded).
- *Barclays Bank plc v Quincecare Ltd* [1992] 4 All ER 363: relevant at common law only where an agent's instruction is not authorised by the customer.
- FOS decisions applying regs 72-77 are not binding precedent but show how gross negligence and consent are assessed.

## Applying it
1. Was the payment **authorised** (reg 67)? Who initiated it, with what credentials, and did the payer consent in the agreed form? A payment made by the customer under a scammer's influence is authorised; a payment made by the fraudster using the customer's stolen or phished credentials, without the customer actually approving it, is generally unauthorised (but check how the transaction was approved; if the customer approved in the app it may be authorised).
2. If disputed, the PSP must prove authentication and proper recording (reg 75(1)); the logs alone are not sufficient to show authorisation or gross negligence (reg 75(3)).
3. If unauthorised: refund by end of next business day (reg 76), subject to the fraud-suspicion exception (reg 76(3)). Customer liability: up to GBP 35 (reg 77(1)) if the instrument was lost, stolen or misappropriated and the exceptions in reg 77(2) do not apply; nothing after notification; nothing if SCA was not applied; full liability if fraud, or intent or gross negligence in breach of reg 72 (reg 77(3)).
4. Notification: without undue delay and within 13 months of debit (reg 74).
5. If the PSP alleges gross negligence, require its evidence (reg 75(4)); consider what the customer did (disclosing a PIN or credentials, ignoring warnings, leaving a card and PIN together) and whether the PSP's own security (SCA) was sound.
6. Remedies: complain to the PSP, then to the Financial Ombudsman Service; a court claim in contract is also possible, and the FCA enforces the Regulations against PSPs.
7. Common traps: confusing unauthorised with authorised-but-induced-by-fraud; treating GBP 35 as the liability cap in every case; forgetting that the burden of proof is on the PSP; forgetting the 13-month limit; overlooking card chargeback or Consumer Credit Act s 75 as alternative remedies.
Everyday version: if someone else makes a payment from your account that you did not approve, tell your bank at once; it should refund you by the end of the next working day, unless it can show that you were grossly careless or acted dishonestly.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the unauthorised/authorised divide is still right when fraudsters manipulate customers into approving payments; the reimbursement scheme is a regulatory overlay, and the debate is whether the PSRs should themselves be reformed.
- The meaning of gross negligence and whether the burden on the PSP is workable in practice.
- Government plans to replace the PSRs (assimilated EU law) with FCA rules under the Smarter Regulatory Framework; until that happens the PSRs 2017 continue to apply.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Delay for suspected fraud (regs 86(2A)-(2D) and 94A): in force 30 October 2024 (SI 2024/1013).
- Termination of indefinite framework contracts by the PSP: for contracts entered into on or after 28 April 2026, at least 90 days' notice with sufficiently detailed reasons and information on complaining (including to the Ombudsman) (reg 51B); earlier contracts keep the two-month rule (reg 51A) (SI 2025/688).
- The reimbursement scheme for APP fraud from 7 October 2024 (see separate note) operates under PSR directions and Pay.UK rules, not reg 76.
- The Financial Services and Markets Bill [HL] would abolish the PSR and move its functions to the FCA; it was still before Parliament in November 2026.
