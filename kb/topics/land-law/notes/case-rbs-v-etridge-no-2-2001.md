# Royal Bank of Scotland plc v Etridge (No 2) [2001] UKHL 44 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. The detailed procedural guidelines for solicitors are
> not set out here because they were not confirmed from two sources. Check Lord Nicholls's speech for them. Do not quote
> paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 11 October 2001 (one commentary says 10 October; most sources say 11). Lord Nicholls gave the main
speech and Lord Bingham said its principles had the support of all the members of the House. The case is the
leading authority on a bank's position when a person (typically a wife) charges her home as security for a loan to
another (typically her husband or his company) and later alleges undue influence or misrepresentation.

## Facts
Eight joined appeals. In each, a wife had charged the family home to secure borrowing that benefited her husband or his
business, the business failed, and the wife argued that the security was not binding because of undue influence. In
one appeal the wife's claim was brought against her solicitor, not the bank.

## Principles
- A bank is "put on inquiry" when a person guarantees or charges property for a debt of someone with whom she has a
  non-commercial relationship, the classic case being a wife standing surety for her husband's or his company's debt.
  Wikipedia describes it as arising where the home is charged for a loan benefiting only one party.
- The bank then must take reasonable steps to bring home to the surety the risk she is running. In practice it
  does this by communicating with her directly and ensuring that she receives independent legal advice.
- The solicitor who advises her may also act for the bank or for the husband. A solicitor acting for her is not generally required
  to confirm that undue influence is absent, unless special facts exist.
- A bank that has ensured she took independent advice is not responsible if the advice turns out to be defective.
  Her remedy is then a claim against the solicitor, not setting aside the bank's security.
- Independent advice does not in itself prove that she was free from influence when she signed.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the relationship: is it a non-commercial relationship (spouse, partner, parent) that puts the bank on
   inquiry?
2. Decide whether the claimant can show actual undue influence or misrepresentation by the debtor, or a
   transaction calling for explanation.
3. Examine what the bank did: direct contact with the surety, independent legal advice, and who the solicitor acted for.
4. If the bank took these steps, it is usually safe even if the advice was poor, and the claimant's remedy is
   against the solicitor.
5. Remember that if the bank did nothing, it is fixed with constructive notice and the charge may be set aside.
