# Leasehold covenants, termination and forfeiture

## Overview
The landlord–tenant relationship is governed by covenants – express terms and terms implied by common law or statute. Problem questions typically ask whether a landlord is in breach (quiet enjoyment, non-derogation from grant, repair, fitness for habitation) and whether a landlord can end the lease for the tenant's breach by forfeiture, and on what terms the tenant can obtain relief. The law (England and Wales) is a patchwork of old common law and statute, and the Law Commission (Report No 303, 2006) recommended replacing forfeiture with a statutory scheme that has not been enacted. The module does not focus on the enforceability of covenants between successors in title.

## The law

### 1. Express and implied covenants
- **Express**: repair, maintenance of common parts, insurance, user, alienation, rent.
- **Implied**: quiet enjoyment, non-derogation from grant, statutory repair and fitness obligations.
- **Interdependence**: lecture asks whether a tenant may withhold rent because the landlord fails to repair (answer given in the materials theatre – the slide is omitted in the sources), so treat covenants as generally independent obligations and advise caution about self-help.

### 2. Landlord's covenants
**(a) Quiet enjoyment** (implied)
1. Protects the tenant's stable, uninterrupted possession – not freedom from noise as such (Kekewich J in *Jenkins*).
2. Covers acts of the landlord and those claiming under it/its agents; not the pre-existing state of the premises: *Southwark LBC* (as cited in the materials: failure to soundproof was not a breach where that was the state of affairs before the tenancy).
3. Breaches: *Lavender v Betts* [1942] (removing doors and windows); *Perera v Vandiyar* [1953] (repeatedly cutting off gas and electricity); *Kenny v Preen* [1963] (persistent harassment, intimidation and threats); *Timothy Taylor Ltd v Mayfair House Corp* [2016] EWHC 1075 (building works and scaffolding without steps to minimise disturbance).

**(b) Non-derogation from grant** (implied)
1. The landlord must not take away with one hand what it gave with the other, rendering the land less fit for the purpose for which it was let, where the landlord knew that purpose at the grant.
2. *Aldin v Latimer* [1894] 2 Ch 437: building that interrupted airflow to a timber merchant's drying sheds – breach.
3. *Kilvert*: protection covers ordinary, not specially sensitive, trade use – the tenant could have bargained for more.
4. *Chartered Trust plc v Davies*: no breach merely by letting neighbouring premises to a competitor (the materials frames it this way).

**(c) Repair and fitness for habitation**
1. Common law: furnished lettings must be fit for habitation at the start (*Smith*, as cited in the materials), but there is no continuing duty (*Sarson v Roberts* [1895] 2 QB 395).
2. *Liverpool City Council v Irwin*: in multi-occupancy premises the landlord must ensure that common parts and essential means of access (stairs, lifts, corridors) are maintained and repaired.
3. Orthodoxy: "Fraud apart, there is no law against letting a tumble-down house" – displaced by statute.
4. **s 11 Landlord and Tenant Act 1985** – residential leases of less than seven years: landlord must keep in repair the structure and exterior (drains, gutters, external pipes, walls, windows and frames) and keep in repair and proper working order installations for water, gas, electricity, sanitation and space/water heating. The standard depends on the age, character, prospective life and locality (s 11(3)). The landlord's duty arises on notice of the defect (*O'Brien*, as cited in the materials).
   - Not covered (repair versus improvement): condensation (*Quick v Taff Ely BC* [1986]); damp or mould from a design defect (*Lee v Leeds CC* [2002]); inherent-defect rising damp, though an ineffective remedy not followed up may still lead to liability (*Uddin v Islington LBC* [2015]); paths/means of access beyond the structure (*Edwards v Kumarasamy* [2016] UKSC 40).
   - Covered: walls (*Green v Eales* (1841)); floors (*Marlborough Park Services v Rowe* [2006]); windows and frames (*Grand v Gill* [2011]).
5. **Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018**: implied term that the dwelling is fit for human habitation at the start and throughout the tenancy. Fitness factors (s 10 LTA 1985): disrepair, instability, serious damp, unsafe layout, lack of natural light or ventilation, hot/cold water supply, drainage and lavatories, food preparation and washing up, and any of the 29 hazards in the Housing Health and Safety Rating System (England) Regulations 2005. Remedies: specific performance and damages (including for discomfort and distress).
6. **Human rights**: *Lee v Leeds CC*: Art 8 might in principle require social landlords to maintain housing stock, assessed case by case with regard to community resources; not triggered on those facts (damp and condensation).

### 3. Tenant's covenants
1. **Rent**: usually express; where rent is payable but unspecified, a term will be implied having regard to the circumstances (and the same for service charges).
2. **Repair**: keep premises in "good tenantable repair", allowing for fair wear and tear.
3. **Not to commit waste**: permanent alteration from the tenant's action or neglect – *Mancetter Developments v Garmanson* (holes left on removing fixtures); *Powys v Belgrave* (rotting foundations due to excavations – as cited in the materials).
4. **Tenant-like user**: Lord Denning, *Warren v Keen* [1953]: the tenant "must do those little jobs about the place which a reasonable tenant would do" (changing bulbs, unblocking drains, ventilating); express covenants against illegal or immoral use.

### 4. Termination of leases (eight ways)
1. Expiry of the term (*Barrett v Morgan* [2000] 2 AC 264, Lord Millett).
2. Break clause (*Mannai Investment v Eagle Star* [1997] AC 749).
3. Notice to quit for periodic tenancies (*Hammersmith and Fulham LBC v Monk* [1992] 1 AC 478; *Newlon Housing Trust v Alsulaimen* [1999] 1 AC 313).
4. Surrender (*Barrett v Morgan*; *Parker v Jones* [1910]).
5. Merger of leasehold and freehold.
6. Repudiatory breach.
7. Frustration.
8. Forfeiture.
Death of a tenant does not of itself end a lease (lecture question).

### 5. Forfeiture
**Availability**: only to the landlord, and only if the lease has an express forfeiture (re-entry) clause, the covenant is drafted as a condition, or a right is implied (e.g. tenant disclaims the landlord's title) – textbook.

**Waiver**: a landlord who, knowing of the breach, does an unequivocal act recognising the lease's continuance (classically demanding or accepting rent) waives that breach: *Matthews v Smallwood* (1910); *Segal Securities v Thoseby* (1963); intention to waive is unnecessary (*Cornillie v Saha* (1996)); waiver even by an agent's error is irrevocable (*Central Estates (Belgravia) v Woolgar (No 2)* (1972)); continuing breaches recur (*Greenwich LBC v Discreet Selling Estates* (1990)).

**Re-entry**: by peaceable physical re-entry (in practice commercial premises; residential re-entry against an occupier is an offence under s 1 Protection from Eviction Act 1977, and s 2 requires court proceedings for premises let as a dwelling; violence is barred by s 6 Criminal Law Act 1977) or by possession proceedings (*Billson v Residential Apartments* (1992)).

**(a) Non-payment of rent** – Common Law Procedure Act 1852 ss 210–212
1. Formal demand for rent required unless dispensed with by the lease or the tenant is six months in arrears; then possession proceedings.
2. Relief: proceedings stop if all arrears and costs are paid up to five days before the hearing (s 138(2) County Courts Act 1984); the court must postpone possession for at least four weeks, with an automatic right to relief on payment (s 138(3); *Golding v Martin* (2019) – an immediate, unconditional order was set aside); application up to six months after re-entry (s 138(9A)); inherent/equitable jurisdiction (*Bland v Ingram's Estates (No 2)* (2002); s 139(2); *Howard v Fanshawe*; *Gill v Lewis*), normally on full payment; delay beyond six months may bar relief (*Gibbs v Lakeside Developments* (2018)).

**(b) Other breaches – s 146 LPA 1925**
1. Landlord's remedies for breach generally: damages, injunction, specific performance, forfeiture.
2. Before forfeiting for breach of a covenant other than rent, the landlord must serve a **s 146 notice** specifying the breach, requiring remedy (if capable of remedy) within a reasonable time, and requiring compensation (if desired). The breach must be identified precisely (textbook: *Akici v LR Butlin* (2005)).
3. **Remediability** – Slade LJ, *Expert Clothing v Hillgate House* [1986] Ch 340: can the harm caused by the breach be removed? *Savva v Hussein* (1997): no positive/negative distinction. Neuberger LJ in *Akici*: most breaches are remediable; two are not – sub-letting in breach of covenant (*Scala House v Forbes* [1974] QB 575 – one cannot "uncreate" an underlease) and illegal or immoral use (*Rugby School v Tannahill* [1935] 1 KB 87 – irremovable stigma).
4. **Relief – s 146(2)**: the court "may grant or refuse relief, as the court, having regard to the proceedings and conduct of the parties… and to all the other circumstances, thinks fit" and on such terms as it thinks fit. *Ropemaker Properties v Noonhaven* [1989] 2 EGLR 50 factors: severity of breach; conduct; past performance; intentions and motivation of the party in breach; remediability; the draconian nature of forfeiture; harm caused weighed against the value of the lease.

## Debate and critique
- **Law Commission Report No 303, *Termination of Tenancies for Tenant Default* (2006)**: forfeiture "is complex, it lacks coherence, and it can lead to injustice". Recommended abolition and replacement by a statutory scheme: a concept of "tenant default" (no need for a re-entry clause); a tenant default notice (also served on mortgagees and sub-tenants) with time to remedy; abolition of waiver; the possibility of termination even if a breach has been remedied; proportionality at the heart of the court's choice of order (termination, remedial order, order for sale where a termination order would give the landlord a disproportionate windfall, and orders available to mortgagees/sub-tenants) Not enacted.
- **Gravells, "Forfeiture of Leases for Breach of Covenant" [2006] JBL 830**: forfeiture is an essential management tool for long commercial and residential leases, but can be catastrophic for tenants (loss of premises, relocation costs) and yield windfalls to landlords who re-let at higher rents; the law must balance these interests and is ripe for reform.
- **Waiver's harshness** (Smith, per textbook): accidental rent demands irrevocably waive forfeiture, even where the tenant knows the landlord wants to forfeit (*Woolgar*) – though strictness prevents landlords taking rent and forfeiting ("double gain").
- **Repair and housing conditions**: the materials's news example (an elderly couple in unsafe council housing) illustrates the gap between legal duties and lived reality; s 11 excludes design defects (*Quick*; *Lee*), which the 2018 Act partly addresses. The revision lecture lists repair and HHSRS as live issues (Grenfell and housing policy themselves are non-examinable).
- **Lease/licence link**: statutory repair duties apply only to tenants, so classification matters (see leases-essential-characteristics; Whayman on property guardians).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there a lease (and of what length – s 11 applies to residential leases under seven years)?
2. Landlord's breaches:
   - Noise/disturbance: from the landlord or those claiming under it (quiet enjoyment) or from other tenants? Pre-existing condition (*Southwark*)? Harassment, cutting utilities (*Kenny*; *Perera*)?
   - Use of neighbouring land by the landlord frustrating the letting purpose (*Aldin*)?
   - Disrepair: structure/exterior or installations (s 11)? Has notice been given? Condensation/design defect (*Quick*; *Lee*) versus disrepair (windows – *Grand v Gill*)? Common parts such as a broken staircase (*Liverpool CC v Irwin*)?
   - Fitness: infestation, damp, HHSRS hazards – 2018 Act; remedies of specific performance/damages.
3. Tenant's breaches: rent, repair, waste, tenant-like user, user covenants.
4. Landlord wants to end the lease: is there a forfeiture clause/condition? Has the breach been waived? Rent – CLPA 1852 demand, CCA 1984 s 138 relief. Other breach – s 146 notice; remediable (*Akici*; *Expert Clothing*) or not (sub-letting; immoral use)? Relief under s 146(2) (*Ropemaker* factors). Residential – court order needed (PEA 1977).
5. Other termination routes: notice to quit, surrender, expiry, break clause.
6. Mention reform (Law Com 303) in essays and for evaluation.
