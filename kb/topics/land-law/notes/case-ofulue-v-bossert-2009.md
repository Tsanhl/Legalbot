# Ofulue v Bossert [2009] UKHL 16 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. One commentary describes the decision as unanimous,
> while two others record a 4-1 split with Lord Scott dissenting; the note follows the two. Do not quote paragraph
> numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 11 March 2009 (Lords Hope, Scott, Rodger, Walker and Neuberger). The case concerns adverse possession
under the **Limitation Act 1980** and whether a statement made in a "without
prejudice" letter could be used as an **acknowledgment of title** under section 29, restarting the 12-year period.

## Facts
Mr and Mrs Ofulue were the registered freehold owners of 61 Coborn Road, Bow, London. They let it and went to Nigeria; in
1981 a tenant allowed Mr Bossert and his daughter to occupy. The owners began possession proceedings in 1989. The
defence served in 1990 admitted the owners' title but denied a right to possession. A without prejudice letter
written on the occupiers' side in January 1992 (CaseMine and Nearly Legal agree on the month; another summary gives
1991) offered to buy the property. The first proceedings were struck out in 2002. The owners issued new
proceedings in 2003; the occupier (Ms Bossert, by then the respondent) pleaded adverse possession for over 12 years.
The owners argued that the earlier defence and the without prejudice offer were acknowledgments of their title.

## Decision
The appeal was **dismissed** by a majority of four to one (Lord Scott dissenting) and the adverse possession defence
succeeded.
- **Without prejudice letter:** the majority held it could not be relied on as an acknowledgment. The admission in it
  was bound up with the settlement offer, and the House saw the fact/offer distinction as too subtle to apply in practice
  and did not accept that section 29 contains a public policy that overrides the without prejudice rule.
- **Defence in the earlier action:** a statement in a pleading can be an acknowledgment of title, but it operates as at
  the date it is made and did not continue for the life of the proceedings. The Court of Appeal's contrary view was
  wrong on this point.

## Why it matters
It confirms that the without prejudice rule protects negotiations even when a later limitation dispute would otherwise
benefit from the admissions made in them. The House left room for further exceptions to the rule but found none here.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. In an adverse possession problem, check whether any acknowledgment of the paper owner's title in writing and signed
   could restart time (Limitation Act 1980, s 29). Treat pleadings as acknowledgments only as at their date.
2. If the only acknowledgment sits in a without prejudice offer to buy, the squatter's admissions cannot be used
   against them; say so and apply *Ofulue*.
3. Distinguish the limited exceptions to the without prejudice rule and conclude whether the case falls within one.
4. Remember the registered-land regime (Land Registration Act 2002, Schedule 6) is different; see the linked notes.
