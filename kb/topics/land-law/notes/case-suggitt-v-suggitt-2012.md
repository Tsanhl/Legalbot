# Suggitt v Suggitt [2012] EWCA Civ 1140 (case note)

> Built from the first-instance judgment (read on the National Archives) and secondary accounts of the appeal; the
> Court of Appeal judgment itself was not read. The full Court of Appeal panel and the exact date of judgment are not
> confirmed, so they are omitted. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Civil Division), 2012; the reasoning reported by commentators is that of Arden LJ. It is an appeal against
the Chancery decision of HHJ Roger Kaye QC ([2011] EWHC 903 (Ch), 20 April 2011). The case is cited for the way
detriment is assessed in an inheritance-type proprietary estoppel claim and for the approach to proportionality in the
remedy.

## Facts
Frank Suggitt (who died in October 2009) owned about 413 acres of farmland near Wenby in Yorkshire, across three
farms. His son John said Frank repeatedly promised him that he would have the
farmland, and somewhere to live, after Frank's death. The trial judge found that, although the evidence about the
precise words was weak, it was more likely than not that Frank made repeated promises that John would definitely
receive the farmland, and that the promise was not conditional. Frank's will left everything to his daughter Caroline,
with a wish (not a binding trust) that she transfer the farmland to John if she thought him capable of farming it.
The net estate was valued at about £4 million, most of it (about £3.8 million) the farmland and buildings.

John worked on the farm without a regular wage and arranged much of his life around the expectation of inheriting
it, but he had also been housed and supported by his father and had received substantial sums from family trusts and
estates over the years.

## First instance
HHJ Roger Kaye QC found reliance and real and substantial detriment (while taking account of the benefits John
had received), held that it would be unconscionable to leave
John without what he had been promised, and awarded him the farmland and the farm buildings, plus a house to live in;
Caroline kept the rest of the estate. No money award was made in place of the land.

## Court of Appeal
Caroline's appeal was **dismissed**. As reported by commentators:
- Detriment was assessed realistically, taking into account the benefits John had received, not on the footing that
  he had worked as an unpaid labourer. The finding of real and substantial detriment was not overturned.
- The court rejected a requirement of exact financial equivalence between the detriment and the remedy. The question
  was whether the award was out of all proportion to the detriment.
- Because the promise was that John would have the land unconditionally, giving him the land was not out of all
  proportion. A money award alone would have been the minimum and would not have done justice.
- Arden LJ's treatment of proportionality was later analysed by the Court of Appeal in *Habberfield v Habberfield*,
  and the Supreme Court has since restated the approach to remedy in *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27, so read
  *Suggitt* on remedy in the light of those cases.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Show a clear, unconditional assurance about land, repeated over time (promises about inheritance can ground an
   estoppel).
2. Identify detriment broadly (unpaid work, shaping one's life around the farm) and set it against benefits received.
3. On remedy, argue that the court starts with the expectation but tests it for proportionality; where the assurance
   was unconditional and the detriment real, the court may award the promised property.
4. Counter-arguments: benefits received by the claimant, conditions on the promise, and the interests of other family
   members.
