# Leases – types, formalities and effect on third parties

## Overview
Once an arrangement has the hallmarks of a lease (see leases-essential-characteristics), two practical questions follow: what *type* of lease is it (fixed term, periodic, at will, at sufferance, by estoppel, *Bruton*), and was it created with the formalities needed for a *legal* lease – or only an equitable one? The answers determine how the lease ends and whether it binds a purchaser of the landlord's land under the registered or unregistered systems (England and Wales). The revision lecture asks: when is a deed required, when is no writing needed, and when is a lease equitable?

## The law

### 1. Types of lease
1. **Fixed-term lease**: for a defined period (e.g. one year, 99 years); normally ends by expiry.
2. **Periodic tenancy**: runs from period to period (weekly, monthly, yearly) until ended by notice matching the period. Can be express or implied – classically from going into possession and paying rent at regular intervals. But:
   - *Javad v Mohammed Aqil* [1991] 1 WLR 1007: no inference of a periodic tenancy from rent where parties are still negotiating a lease; Nicholls LJ warned against inferring a greater interest than the parties contemplated.
   - *Barclays Wealth Trustees v Erimus Housing* [2014] EWCA Civ 303: payment of rent gives no presumption of a periodic tenancy; intention is assessed objectively; during subject-to-contract negotiations the occupier will usually be a tenant at will (Patten LJ).
   - *Smoke Club Ltd v Network Rail Infrastructure* [2021] UKUT 0078: nightclub operators who had expressly refused to pay rent on a tenancy-at-will basis were held to have an implied periodic tenancy (relevant to compensation on compulsory acquisition).
3. **Tenancy at will**: exclusive possession with consent, terminable by either party at any time – commonly during negotiations (*Javad*) or where a tenant holds over with consent.
4. **Tenancy at sufferance**: tenant holds over without the landlord's consent; becomes a tenancy at will if consent is given, or a trespasser if the landlord objects.
5. **Tenancy by estoppel**: the grantor is estopped from denying the lease.
6. ***Bruton* tenancy**: contractual, non-proprietary lease granted by someone without an estate (*Bruton v London & Quadrant HT* [1999]); binds only the parties.
7. **Lease for life / uncertain term for an individual**: converted to 90 years by s 149(6) LPA 1925 (*Berrisford v Mexfield* [2011]).

### 2. Formalities for a legal lease
1. **Three years or less** – no deed or writing needed; may be oral, provided it takes effect in possession and is at the best rent reasonably obtainable (the materials's summary of the s 54(2) LPA 1925 exception to s 52).
2. **More than three years** – must be by deed (s 52 LPA 1925; deed requirements in s 1 LP(MP)A 1989).
3. **More than seven years** – registered land: the grant is a registrable disposition that must be substantively registered (own title) to operate at law (s 27(2) LRA 2002); otherwise equitable at best. Unregistered land: grant of a lease of more than seven years triggers compulsory first registration (s 4 LRA 2002).

### 3. Equitable leases – *Walsh v Lonsdale* (1882)
If the legal formalities fail, equity treats an agreement for a lease as a lease on the same terms where:
1. There is a valid contract for a lease complying with **s 2 LP(MP)A 1989** – in writing, containing all expressly agreed terms, signed by or on behalf of both parties; and
2. **Specific performance is available** – consideration given, damages inadequate, and no inequitable conduct by the claimant. *Rouf v Tragus Holdings and Café Rouge* [2009] EWHC 96: no *Walsh v Lonsdale* lease where the tenant was in rent arrears.
Where there is no valid contract, consider whether possession plus regular rent supports an implied periodic tenancy, subject to *Javad* and *Erimus* (above).

### 4. Effect on third parties

**Legal leases – registered land**
- 3 years or less: cannot be the subject of a notice (s 33(b) LRA 2002) but override first registration and registered dispositions (Sch 1 para 1; Sch 3 para 1 – legal leases of seven years or less).
- More than 3 but not more than 7 years: can be protected by notice; if not, still override under Sch 1/Sch 3 para 1.
- More than 7 years: must be registered to be legal and then bind (s 27(2)); if unregistered, equitable at best.
- Short-lease tenants receive s 29 protection via s 29(4).

**Legal leases – unregistered land**: legal rights bind the world; long grants trigger first registration.

**Equitable leases – registered land**: protect by notice on the Charges Register; if not protected, the tenant may still have an overriding interest through actual occupation (Sch 1 para 2 / Sch 3 para 2), since equitable leases are outside para 1.

**Equitable leases – unregistered land**: a *Walsh v Lonsdale* lease is an estate contract registrable as a **Class C(iv)** land charge. If registered it binds third parties; if not, it is void against a purchaser of a legal estate for money or money's worth (s 4(6) LCA 1972) but binds non-purchasers such as donees.

### 5. Why legal versus equitable matters
- Legal leases bind more readily; equitable leases depend on registration/notice or actual occupation (registered) or land charges registration (unregistered).
- An equitable lease depends on the discretionary remedy of specific performance (*Rouf*).

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 ss 52, 54(2), 149(6); LP(MP)A 1989 ss 1, 2.
- LRA 2002 ss 4, 27(2), 29(4), 33(b); Sch 1 paras 1–2; Sch 3 paras 1–2.
- LCA 1972 s 2 (Class C(iv)), s 4(6).
- *Walsh v Lonsdale* (1882) 21 Ch D 9: agreement for a lease as good as a lease in equity.
- *Rouf v Tragus Holdings* [2009] EWHC 96: no equitable lease where specific performance unavailable.
- *Javad v Aqil* [1991] 1 WLR 1007; *Barclays Wealth v Erimus* [2014] EWCA Civ 303; *Smoke Club v Network Rail* [2021] UKUT 0078: implied periodic tenancies versus tenancy at will.
- *Bruton v London & Quadrant* [1999]; *Berrisford v Mexfield* [2011].

## Debate and critique
- **Informality and short leases**: the s 54(2) exception and overriding status of short legal leases reflect the policy that it is unreasonable to require registration of short, common arrangements; the 2002 Act reduced the overriding threshold from 21 to 7 years.
- ***Walsh v Lonsdale* and fusion**: the doctrine shows equity treating a specifically enforceable contract as creating a lease, but an equitable lease is weaker against third parties and contingent on equitable discretion.
- **Implied periodic tenancies**: *Erimus* moves from presumption to objective intention, giving more weight to negotiations and less to rent payments; this protects landlords from unintended security of tenure but can leave occupiers as tenants at will.
- **Certainty and type**: *Prudential* and *Mexfield* show courts rescuing defective arrangements by recharacterising them as periodic tenancies or 90-year leases – arguably producing results the parties never intended.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm it is a lease (exclusive possession, term, rent).
2. Identify the type (fixed, periodic – express or implied, at will during negotiations, sufferance, *Bruton*).
3. Formalities by duration: ≤3 years (oral OK if in possession at best rent); >3 years (deed); >7 years (registration in registered land; triggers first registration in unregistered land).
4. If formalities failed: is there a s 2-compliant contract and is specific performance available (*Walsh v Lonsdale*; *Rouf*)? Or an implied periodic tenancy from possession and rent?
5. Does it bind a purchaser?
   - Registered: legal ≤7 years – overriding (Sch 3 para 1); >7 years – registered?; equitable – notice? actual occupation (Sch 3 para 2) and its exceptions?
   - Unregistered: legal – binds; equitable – Class C(iv) registered? If not, void against purchaser for money or money's worth of a legal estate.
6. Then consider covenants, breach and termination.
