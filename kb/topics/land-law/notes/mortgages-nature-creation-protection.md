# Mortgages – nature, creation and protection of the mortgagor

## Overview
A mortgage is the grant of an interest in land as security for a loan – "a contract that creates property rights". Mortgages are the main source of personal credit in the UK and the way most people buy homes (the materials cites Bank of England figures of over £1,675 billion of residential loans in 2023). The law (England and Wales) must balance the lender's commercial need for effective security against the borrower's interest in keeping their home – a balance the revision lecture frames as economic versus social factors. This note covers terminology, creation of legal and equitable mortgages, and the mortgagor's protections (equity of redemption, the rule against clogs and fetters, collateral advantages, unconscionable terms and statutory regulation). Mortgagees' remedies and undue influence are in mortgagee-remedies-undue-influence.

## The law

### 1. Terminology
- **Mortgagor**: the borrower who grants the mortgage. **Mortgagee**: the lender. Mortgages are also called "charges".
- **Equity of redemption**: the sum of the mortgagor's rights less obligations – reflecting that the mortgagor remains owner.
- Most modern mortgages are instalment mortgages, either with a nominal early redemption date plus instalment clauses, or with no date and an acceleration clause making the whole debt payable on default.

### 2. Creation of legal mortgages
1. **Deed**: a legal mortgage is a conveyance (s 205(1)(ii) LPA 1925), so must be by deed (s 52 LPA 1925; s 1 LP(MP)A 1989).
2. **Unregistered land** – s 85 LPA 1925 allows (a) mortgage by demise (a 3,000-year lease to the lender, ending on redemption – "cesser on redemption", s 85(1)) – now almost obsolete; or (b) **charge by way of legal mortgage**, under which the lender has "the same protections, powers and remedies" as if it held a 3,000-year lease (s 87(1)). Since 1926 the legal estate stays with the mortgagor. Grant of a legal mortgage over unregistered land now triggers first registration.
3. **Registered land** – the only method is the registered charge (s 23(1) LRA 2002; s 87 LPA 1925). It is a registrable disposition (s 27(2)(f)) that operates at law only when registered; it then takes effect as a charge by way of legal mortgage (s 51) and is entered on the Charges Register, securing priority. Land Registry's Digital Mortgage Service (2018) allows electronic creation/discharge of some charges – not yet full e-conveyancing.

### 3. Equitable mortgages
Most commonly: (a) a mortgage (even by deed) of an interest that is itself only equitable; or (b) a failed legal mortgage (no deed) where there is a specifically enforceable contract to grant a mortgage complying with s 2 LP(MP)A 1989. The textbook also discusses the equitable charge.

### 4. Equitable right to redeem and the rule against clogs and fetters
1. At common law, failure to repay on the contractual redemption date forfeited the land and left the borrower liable for the debt. Equity, treating the mortgage as mere security, allowed redemption after that date – the **equitable right to redeem**, which arises once the contractual date has passed and is what borrowers rely on today.
2. Lindley MR, *Santley v Wilde* (1899): a clog or fetter is "something which is inconsistent with the idea of security… in the nature of a repugnant condition".
3. Five strands (Bevan, Figure 13.5):
   1. **Terms excluding or restricting redemption are void**: *Jones v Morgan* (2001) (irredeemability); *Re Sir Thomas Spencer Wells* (1933) (restricting who/when may redeem); postponement making redemption illusory – *Fairclough v Swan Brewery* (1912): a mortgage of a lease with 17½ years left could not be redeemed until six weeks before the lease expired – void. But freely negotiated postponement in a commercial context, with legal advice and no oppression, is valid: *Knightsbridge Estates Trust v Byrne* (1939) (40-year repayment). Early-redemption charges in fixed-rate deals are valid if explained.
   2. **Restraint of trade**: *Noakes v Rice* (1901) – a pub tie lasting beyond redemption was struck out; courts now allow more latitude for business deals.
   3. **Option for the mortgagee to buy the property** in the mortgage is void without any need to show unconscionability: *Samuel v Jarrah Timber* (1904). A truly separate, independent option may be valid: *Reeve v Lisle* (1902); substance not form (*Jones v Morgan*).
   4. **Collateral advantages** may be void (*Noakes v Rice*; *Bradley v Carritt* (1903)) but are valid unless (1) unfair and unconscionable, (2) a penalty clogging the equity, or (3) inconsistent with or repugnant to the right to redeem: Lord Parker, *Kreglinger v New Patagonia Meat & Cold Storage* (1914) – a five-year right of first refusal over sheepskins survived early repayment, being a collateral bargain negotiated at arm's length.
   5. **Oppressive or unconscionable terms** may be struck out (clause or whole mortgage). The test is strict – mere unreasonableness is not enough: *Multiservice Bookbinding v Marden* (1979) – a Swiss franc-linked repayment clause that trebled the sum due was valid; Browne-Wilkinson J: a bargain is unconscionable only if a party "imposed the objectionable terms in a morally reprehensible manner". Contrast *Cityland and Property Holdings v Dabrah* (1968) (punitive interest without equality of arms reduced) and *Paragon Finance v Nash* (2001) (implied term that a discretionary interest rate is not set dishonestly, for an improper purpose, capriciously or arbitrarily).

### 5. Statutory and regulatory protection
- **s 140A Consumer Credit Act 1974** (inserted by the Consumer Credit Act 2006): the court may intervene in unfair creditor–debtor relationships.
- **Financial Services and Markets Act 2000**: mortgages from 31 October 2004 are generally "regulated mortgage contracts"; lenders must treat consumers fairly and transparently; breach of conduct rules can lead to compensation.
- **Mortgage Market Review** (FSA, post-2008): rules on responsible lending (affordability) – see Nield below.
- **Pre-Action Protocol** for possession claims based on mortgage arrears (2008, updated 2011) – see the remedies note.
- **Mortgagor's powers**: to claim possession if the mortgagee has not (s 98 LPA 1925); to grant leases consistent with the mortgage (s 99); to seek a court-ordered sale (s 91).
- Covid-19 measures: payment deferrals up to three months, payment holidays and a moratorium on repossessions until mid-2021.

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 ss 52, 85, 87, 91, 98, 99, 205(1)(ii); LP(MP)A 1989 ss 1, 2; LRA 2002 ss 23(1), 27(2)(f), 51.
- *Santley v Wilde* (1899): definition of a clog.
- *Fairclough v Swan Brewery* (1912): postponement making redemption illusory is void.
- *Knightsbridge Estates Trust v Byrne* (1939): negotiated commercial postponement valid.
- *Samuel v Jarrah Timber* (1904); *Reeve v Lisle* (1902): options to purchase.
- *Noakes v Rice* (1901); *Bradley v Carritt* (1903); *Kreglinger v New Patagonia* (1914): collateral advantages.
- *Multiservice Bookbinding v Marden* (1979); *Cityland v Dabrah* (1968); *Paragon Finance v Nash* (2001): unconscionable terms.
- *Jones v Morgan* (2001): Lord Phillips on clogs.

## Debate and critique
- **Do we still need clogs?** Lord Mersey in *Kreglinger* called the doctrine "an unruly dog, which, if not securely chained to its own kennel, is prone to wander into places where it should not be". Lord Phillips in *Jones v Morgan*: an "appendix to our law which no longer serves a useful purpose and would be better excised". Thompson ("Do We Really Need Clogs?" [2001] Conv 502) and others argue it has caused more problems than it solves.
- **Law Commission Report No 204, *Transfer of Land: Land Mortgages* (1991)**: noted uncertainty about which terms offend and that rules formed in a different commercial era are not always suitable; recommended replacing the clogs doctrine with a single statutory jurisdiction to vary terms giving the mortgagee rights "substantially greater than or different from those necessary to make the property available as security" or otherwise unconscionable. Not enacted. (The Commission also recommended abolishing foreclosure.)
- **Watt, "The Lie of the Land: Mortgage Law as a Legal Fiction"** (in Cooke (ed), *Modern Studies in Property Law* Vol 4, 2007): Parliament abolished mortgage by conveyance and reconveyance, but courts still talk of an equity of redemption; land subject to a registered charge is not "redeemed" but simply discharged – so the concept should be abandoned.
- **Commercial versus consumer**: modern courts respect freedom of contract between equals (*Knightsbridge*; *Kreglinger*; *Marden*), with consumer protection increasingly supplied by statute and regulation (CCA s 140A; FSMA 2000).
- **Nield, "Mortgage Market Review: Hard-Wired Common Sense?" (2015)**: the MMR targets irresponsible lending after the 2008 crisis but is criticised for failing to align with responses to default and wider housing policy; responsible-lending rules alone are insufficient without addressing borrowers' vulnerability (drawing on McVea's critique of neoliberal market regulation).
- **Whitehouse** (longitudinal analysis 1995–2010): mortgage law's doctrine has been remarkably stable despite socio-economic change; substantive regulation is largely left to "soft law" and contract, with the law focused on procedure.
- **Balance**: the materials's central question – does the law strike the right balance between mortgagor and mortgagee? Too far either way harms the economy or increases homelessness.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was a legal mortgage validly created (deed; registration as a registered charge in registered land)? If not, is there an equitable mortgage (s 2-compliant contract)?
2. Examine each unusual term:
   - Does it postpone or restrict redemption? Is the postponement illusory (*Fairclough*) or negotiated between equals (*Knightsbridge*)?
   - Is there an option to purchase within the mortgage (*Samuel*) or a separate transaction (*Reeve v Lisle*)?
   - Is there a collateral advantage – does it survive redemption, is it unconscionable, a penalty, or repugnant (*Kreglinger*; *Noakes*)?
   - Is a term oppressive or imposed in a morally reprehensible way (*Marden*; *Cityland*; *Paragon*)? Consider bargaining power and legal advice.
3. Consider statutory protection: CCA 1974 s 140A; FSMA 2000 regulated mortgage rules.
4. Consider whether a co-owner's consent was procured by undue influence or misrepresentation (see mortgagee-remedies-undue-influence).
5. Then turn to the lender's remedies on default.
