# Adverse possession – justifications and elements of a claim

## Overview
Adverse possession allows a squatter who has possessed another's land for long enough, without permission, to acquire title, because English law (England and Wales) treats title as founded on, and relative to, possession. The doctrine raises sharp questions of justification ("land theft" or sensible rule of repose?). A claim has two stages: (1) establishing adverse possession – dispossession/discontinuance, factual possession, intention to possess, absence of consent, for the required period; and (2) its effect, which differs between unregistered land (Limitation Act 1980: 12 years, title extinguished) and registered land (LRA 2002 Sch 6: 10 years then an application – see adverse-possession-lra-2002-regime).

## The law

### 1. Definition
1. Lord Browne-Wilkinson in *J A Pye (Oxford) Ltd v Graham* [2002] UKHL 30 [36] urged avoiding the label "adverse possession" where possible: "The question is simply whether the defendant squatter has dispossessed the paper owner by going into ordinary possession of the land for the requisite period without the consent of the owner."
2. Lord Hope [69]: "adverse" does not import "aggression, hostility or subterfuge"; it is a label recognising that the possession is adverse to the owner's interests.
3. Combining *Pye* with Fry J in *Rains v Buxton* (1880), the worksheet identifies four cumulative elements: (1) dispossession or discontinuance; (2) ordinary (actual) possession – factual possession plus intention to possess; (3) without the owner's consent; (4) for the requisite period.

### 2. History (worksheet)
Limitation Act 1623 (20 years); Real Property Limitation Act 1833; Real Property Limitation Act 1874 (12 years); Limitation Act 1939 (defined adverse possession; 30 years for Crown and charitable corporations, 60 for foreshore); Limitation Act 1980 (unregistered land, and registered land where 12 years accrued before 13 October 2003); Human Rights Act 1998; LRA 2002 (registered land – new procedure but relies on the case law test).

### 3. Element 1 – dispossession or discontinuance
Dispossession: the squatter drives the owner out; discontinuance: the owner leaves and the squatter moves in (*Rains v Buxton*, approved in *Buckinghamshire CC v Moran* [1990] Ch 623). No "ouster" is required. Either must be followed by actual possession; what counts is how long the squatter has possessed, not how long the owner has been absent (Millett LJ, *Hounslow LBC v Minchinton* (1997) 74 P & CR 221).

### 4. Element 2(a) – factual possession
1. Slade J, *Powell v McFarlane* (1979) 38 P & CR 452: possession requires both factual possession and intention to possess. Factual possession is "an appropriate degree of physical control", "single and conclusive" (owner and squatter cannot both possess); what suffices depends on the nature of the land and how it is commonly used; open land rarely admits absolute control; the standard is objective (Lord Wilberforce, *West Bank Estates v Arthur* [1967]). The test: "the alleged possessor has been dealing with the land in question as an occupying owner might have been expected to deal with it and that no-one else has done so."
2. Acts on part may evidence possession of the whole (*Clark v Elphinstone* (1880), not for undefined boundaries); squatters need not use all the land (*Higgs v Nassauvian* [1975]; *Bissessar v Lall* (PC)).
3. Motive is irrelevant: *Minchinton* – fencing to stop dogs escaping, plus repairing/painting fences and cutting hedges, was factual possession.
4. No duty to draw attention to the possession absent fraud or deliberate concealment: *Topplan Estates v Townley* [2004] EWCA Civ 1369; Neuberger J in *Purbrick v Hackney LBC* [2003] EWHC 1871 – an owner who makes no use of property for 12 years must keep an eye on it.
5. Typical acts (worksheet table):
   - **Enclosure** – "Enclosure is the strongest possible evidence of adverse possession" (Cockburn CJ, *Seddon v Smith* (1877)); but weak or temporary fencing (a "goose fence" of string between pegs) is not enough (*Boosey v Davis* (1988), as tabulated), and merely patching an existing fence may be insufficient; an unlocked gate did not prevent control in one case; why the fence was erected matters (*Chambers v Havering LBC* [2011] EWCA Civ 1576).
   - **Controlling access/locks** – padlock and chain (*Powell*); new lock and chain on a gate (*Moran*); changing the lock of a council flat door (*Lambeth LBC v Blackburn* [2001] EWCA Civ 912).
   - **Cultivation, crops, animals** – may be weak if temporary or for pleasure (*Powell*); mere straying cattle is not enough (*Kynoch v Rowlands* (1912)); planting trees akin to fencing; cutting trees on unfenceable mountain land sufficed (*West Bank Estates*). In *Pye* the Grahams farmed the land as their own behind hedges with the only gate key.
6. **Type of land and slight acts**: on abandoned or marginal land, slight acts may suffice – shooting over rough land (*Red House Farms v Catchpole* [1977]); gradual improvement (*Roberts v Swangrove* [2008]); *Purbrick*: "it is not what the squatter could have done but what he did".
7. **Continuity**: possession must be unbroken, though 24-hour occupation is not required (*Generay v Containerised Storage* (2005)); intervals are allowed (*Bligh v Martin* [1968]); a boat need not sit on the river bed at all times (*Port of London Authority v Ashmore* [2010]). Successive squatters may add periods if there is no break (*Moran*; textbook). Owner's sporadic visits finding no squatter present affirm the owner's possession (*Solling v Broughton* [1893]). Letting a friend stay is consistent with owner-like behaviour.
8. **Age**: young claimants find it harder to show owner-like dealing (*Powell* – a 14-year-old boy).

### 5. Element 2(b) – intention to possess (animus possidendi)
1. Slade J in *Powell*: an intention "in one's own name and on one's own behalf, to exclude the world at large, including the owner with the paper title… so far as is reasonably practicable and so far as the processes of the law will allow"; courts require "clear and affirmative evidence" that the trespasser made the intention clear to the world.
2. It is an intention to *possess*, not an intention to *own* or to acquire title: *Minchinton*; settled by *Pye*. Lord Browne-Wilkinson: intention is a separate element, often inferred from the acts – e.g. someone in a locked house may be a squatter, an overnight trespasser or a friend house-sitting; "It is not the nature of the acts which A does but the intention with which he does them". In *Pye* it did not matter that the Grahams knew of Pye's development plans or would have paid for a licence.
3. Neuberger J's factors (*Pye* at first instance): activities on the land; nature and history of the land; enclosure and access; the squatter's general attitude; how possession began; the owner's intentions so far as known to the squatter; the squatter's expressed and unexpressed intentions.
4. Evidence: locks, replaced doors, roof repairs, storing equipment and sealing doorways (*Purbrick*); gate with lock and chain (*Moran*); changed locks (*Blackburn*). Contrast *Fruin v Fruin* (1983) – a fence to stop a senile relative wandering showed no intention to possess.
5. Present, not future, intention: future plans to leave (*Moran*) or the owner's future intentions are irrelevant. *Lambeth v Blackburn*: intending to stay until evicted was enough; no need to intend to possess indefinitely.

### 6. Element 3 – without consent ("adverse")
1. Possession under a lawful title or licence is not adverse: *Moran* (Slade LJ); *Ramnarace v Lutchman* [2001] UKPC 25; earlier *Thomas v Thomas* (1855); *Corea v Appuhamy* [1912].
2. Implied licences: negotiations treated as an implied licence in *Colin Dawson Windows v King's Lynn BC* (2005); going beyond a permission may be adverse (*Allen v Matthews* [2007]). Once a lease/licence ends (and rent stops), time can run.
3. The **implied licence heresy**: *Leigh v Jack* (1879) and *Wallis's Cayton Bay Holiday Camp v Shell-Mex* [1975] suggested acts not inconsistent with the owner's intended future use did not dispossess. Reversed by **Sch 1 para 8(4) Limitation Act 1980** (no implied permission merely because occupation is consistent with the owner's present or future enjoyment); rejected in *Moran* and called "heresy" in *Pye*.
4. Annoyance or inconvenience to the owner is not required (*Treloar v Nute* [1977]).
5. Squatters with existing rights of way: *Littledale v Liverpool College* [1900]; *White v Amirtharaja* [2022] EWCA Civ 11 (see Bevan [2023] Conv 92).

### 7. Element 4 – period and interruptions
1. **Unregistered land**: **s 15(1) Limitation Act 1980** – "No action shall be brought by any person to recover any land after the expiration of twelve years from the date on which the right of action accrued to him…"; on expiry the owner's title is extinguished (**s 17**). Longer periods for Crown land (30 years) and foreshore (60 years). Trustees' title is not extinguished until all beneficiaries are barred (s 18).
2. **Registered land**: 10 years then an application (Sch 6 LRA 2002) – see the next note.
3. **Time stops** when:
   - the owner effectively retakes possession – mere letters are insufficient (*Mount Carmel Investments v Peter Thurlow* [1988] 1 WLR 1078); minor disturbances did not stop time in *Doe d Baker v Coombes* (1850), whereas clear ejection did in *Randall v Stevens* (1853) and fence removal with a notice did in *Worssam v Vandenbande* (1868); symbolic, temporary acts (a post and surveyor's tape for 20 minutes) were not enough in *Zarb v Parry* [2011]; planting a screen hedge was not effective control (*Minchinton*); minor use by the owner did not retake possession (*Marshall v Taylor* [1895]);
   - proceedings are issued (and a possession order executed – textbook);
   - the squatter gives a **written, signed acknowledgement** of the owner's title (ss 29–30 LA 1980) – e.g. offering to buy (*Edgington v Clark* [1964]; Upjohn LJ: it suffices that the squatter acknowledges the paper owner's better title, judged on the document's construction); *Colchester BC v Smith* [1992]; *Lambeth LBC v Bigden* (2001); oral statements do not count (*Browne v Perry* [1991]); "without prejudice" letters are inadmissible as acknowledgement (*Ofulue v Bossert* [2009] UKHL 16);
   - the squatter abandons possession – the owner is restored without any act (*Trustees, Executors and Agency Co v Short* (1888)).
4. **Leases**: time does not run against a landlord during a lease; a tenant cannot claim against the landlord until the lease ends (*Hayward v Chaloner* [1968]); a tenant encroaching on the landlord's or a neighbour's land is presumed to do so for the benefit of the tenancy/landlord (*Lord Hastings v Saddler*; *Whitmore v Humphries* (1871); *Smirk v Lyndale Developments* [1975]); a squatter against a tenant bars only the tenant until the lease ends (textbook).

### 8. Effect in unregistered land
The squatter has a fee simple from the start, good against all but the better title; once 12 years run, the owner is barred and title extinguished. The squatter does not take *through* the owner (*St Marylebone Property v Fairweather* (1963), Lord Radcliffe) and takes subject to existing third-party rights (not being a purchaser). If the owner sells and the land is first registered, the buyer is bound only if the squatter is in actual occupation (s 11 LRA 2002 – textbook).

## Debate and critique
- **Justifications** (worksheet, Law Com 271 paras 2.71–2.73): (1) *Repose/stale claims*: Streatfield J in *RB Policies at Lloyd's v Butler* [1950] – those who sleep on their rights are not assisted, and there should be an end to litigation; (2) *Productive use*: Dockray ("Why Do We Need Adverse Possession?" [1985] Conv 272) – fixed time limits encourage use of neglected land; (3) *Certainty of title/conveyancing*: Roger Smith – lost or defective deeds and boundary uncertainty are cured; purchasers need only investigate 15 years (s 44 LPA 1925), a justification weaker under registration; (4) *Reality over paper*: Hopkins – the rules "confirm" rights actually exercised rather than "take" from the owner; Holmes – what you have long enjoyed "takes root in your being". Against: Howard and Hill – long gratuitous enjoyment does not itself justify a right (the newspaper example); the "thief" argument; Alexander and Peñalver's utilitarian analysis of redistribution; housing-shortage arguments both ways (Cowan et al, *Great Debates*).
- **Goodman, "Adverse Possession of Land: Morality and Motive" (1970) 33 MLR 281**: concludes acquisition by adverse possession is not immoral and can serve the policy of security for long possessors; motive, intention or belief is immaterial – deliberate evicter and mistaken encroacher alike should acquire title unless possession was derivative from a valid transaction with the owner.
- **Tee, "Adverse Possession and the Intention to Possess" [2000] Conv 113**: argues *Moran* should be reassessed and a squatter should need an intention to assert ownership in denial of the paper owner; it is "counter-intuitive – indeed, bizarre" that someone who never intended to acquire a fee simple should get one (citing Harman LJ in *Hughes v Griffin*); otherwise judges may manipulate reasoning to avoid inappropriate results. *Pye* rejected this approach (intention to possess suffices). Radley-Gardner and Harpum replied ([2001] Conv 155).
- **Clarity**: The question is how clear and certain the factual possession and intention requirements are – the case law is highly fact-specific (fences, gates, locks), and the line between owner-like and casual use is uncertain.
- **"Possession is nine-tenths of the law"?** The worksheet invites reflection: historically title rested on possession (*St Leonard's v Ashburner*; *Gatward v Alley*), but registration under the LRA 2002 shifts the basis to the register.
- **Criminality**: *Best* (see next note) – criminal residential squatting does not bar a claim.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Registered or unregistered? Dates matter: when did possession start, and had 12 years accrued before 13 October 2003?
2. Dispossession/discontinuance followed by squatter's possession.
3. Factual possession: what did the squatter do (fencing, locks, cultivation, building)? Nature of land? Was anyone else using it? Weak acts (patching fences, unlocked gates, occasional grazing)?
4. Intention to possess: inferred from acts; state of mind (*Pye*; *Blackburn*); irrelevance of willingness to pay or knowledge of owner's plans; contrary purpose (*Fruin*).
5. Consent: any licence, lease or negotiation? When did permission end?
6. Period: count years; check interruptions – owner's re-entry (effective or symbolic?), letters (insufficient), written acknowledgements (offers to buy/rent), abandonment.
7. Unregistered: 12 years → owner's title extinguished (ss 15, 17), squatter subject to existing third-party rights. Registered → move to Sch 6 (next note).
8. Worksheet example: patching a fence and an unlocked gate (weak), then a sign, grazing cows, ejecting a neighbour's cow and locking the gate (stronger); owner's visit removing the gate and handing an unopened letter – does that retake possession (*Zarb*; *Worssam*; *Mount Carmel*)?
