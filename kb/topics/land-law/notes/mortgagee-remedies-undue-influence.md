# Mortgagees' rights and remedies, and undue influence

## Overview
When a borrower defaults, a mortgagee (England and Wales) can take possession, sell, appoint a receiver or – rarely – foreclose. The law restrains these powers in the interests of residential borrowers: duties on mortgagees in possession and on sale, the court's inherent and statutory (s 36 Administration of Justice Act 1970) jurisdiction to postpone possession, the Pre-Action Protocol, and the borrower's own power to seek sale (s 91 LPA 1925). Separately, a mortgage may be set aside against a surety (typically a spouse) whose consent was procured by undue influence or misrepresentation, if the lender had notice and failed to take the *Etridge* reasonable steps. Both areas test whether the law balances lenders and homeowners fairly.

## The law

### 1. Right to possession
1. Why: as a precursor to sale, or to manage the land and use income to meet arrears.
2. Nature: a right independent of default. Harman J, *Four-Maids Ltd v Dudley Marshall (Properties) Ltd* [1957]: the mortgagee "may go into possession before the ink is dry on the mortgage" unless it has contracted out. In practice, mortgagees seek possession orders in the county court.
3. **Limit 1 – duties on mortgagees seeking or taking possession**:
   - possession must be sought bona fide and reasonably to enforce the security: *Quennell v Maltby* (1979) – a wife who bought out the mortgage so as to evict her husband's statutorily protected student tenants was not acting to protect her security; no order (Lord Denning MR);
   - duty to account for income received or that should have been received: *White v City of London Brewery* (1889) – letting a pub as a tied house at a lower rent required accounting for the shortfall;
   - duty to take reasonable care of the property's physical state, not to drain its value (*Palk v Mortgage Services Funding* (1993), as cited in the materials).
4. **Limit 2 – inherent jurisdiction**: short adjournment (about 28 days) to allow payment: *Birmingham Citizens Permanent BS v Caunt* (1962). Not available in negative equity: *Cheltenham & Gloucester v Krausz* (1997).
5. **Limit 3 – s 36 AJA 1970** (dwelling-houses only): where the mortgagee claims possession, the court may adjourn, stay or suspend execution, or postpone delivery of possession "if it appears to the court that… the mortgagor is likely to be able within a reasonable period to pay any sums due under the mortgage or to remedy a default", on conditions it thinks fit (s 36(1)–(4)).
   - "Reasonable period" – starting point is the remaining mortgage term absent unusual circumstances: *Cheltenham & Gloucester BS v Norgan* (1996).
   - "Any sums due" – *Halifax BS v Clark* (1973) read this as the whole loan; reversed by **s 8 AJA 1973**, so it means arrears.
   - Applies only once the mortgagee brings proceedings – no protection against peaceable self-help re-entry: *Ropaigealach v Barclays Bank* (1999).
   - Breach of conditions leads to possession.
6. **Pre-Action Protocol** for possession claims based on mortgage or home purchase plan arrears in residential property (2008, updated 2011): lenders should give information, consider rescheduling, respond to reasonable payment proposals and treat proceedings as a last resort. Criticised by Dixon ("Editor's Notebook" (2008) Conv 474).
7. The textbook also notes protection for spouses under the Family Law Act 1996.

### 2. Power of sale
1. **Arises** (s 101 LPA 1925): mortgage made by deed; power not excluded; mortgage money has become due.
2. **Exercisable** (s 103): (i) three months' default after a notice requiring repayment; or (ii) interest two months in arrears; or (iii) breach of another mortgage term.
3. **Duties on sale** – *Cuckmere Brick Co v Mutual Finance* (1971, CA): auction particulars mentioned permission for 35 houses but not the more valuable permission for 100 flats; the mortgagee refused to postpone; sold for £44,000 against a possible £75,000. Duties: act in good faith; take reasonable care in conducting the sale; obtain a fair/proper (true market) price. Breached.
4. Further points: the mortgagee chooses when to sell and need not wait for a market rise; no hasty, private knock-down sales – *Predeth v Castle Phillips Finance* (1986) (private sale within three months a breach); no duty to pursue new planning permission (*Silven Properties v RBS* (2004)) but must advertise existing permission (*Cuckmere*); considerable latitude – *Serene Construction v Salata* (2021) (receivers relying on a familiar valuer and targeted marketing, accepting £175,000 against a possible £200,000, not in breach).
5. **Sale to associates**: heavy onus on the mortgagee to show fairness and best price reasonably obtainable: Lord Templeman, *Tse Kwong Lam v Wong Chit Sen* (1983).
6. **Remedies for breach**: mortgagor must prove breach; compensation for the shortfall against true value, or setting aside (e.g. serious fraud).
7. **Sale by the mortgagor – s 91 LPA 1925**: with the lender's consent or by court order. *Palk v Mortgage Services Funding* (1993): debt £358,000; borrower wished to sell for £283,000; lender wanted to let and wait for rising prices while interest outran rent by about £30,000 a year; the court had an "unfettered discretion" and ordered sale. *Polonski v Lloyds Bank Mortgages* (1997): non-defaulting borrower allowed to sell despite a £12,000 shortfall for social reasons (better area, schools, jobs) – non-financial factors relevant.

### 3. Foreclosure and receivers
- **Foreclosure**: by court order only; extinguishes the equity of redemption, ends liability, and vests the estate in the mortgagee. Draconian and rare; sale preferred. Law Com No 204 recommended abolition.
- **Receiver**: manages the land to generate income (commercial land); acts as the mortgagor's agent, so the mortgagee is not liable for the receiver's negligence; available by express term or under s 101 LPA 1925 when the power of sale arises.

### 4. Undue influence – setting aside the mortgage (textbook ch 13)
Typical scenario: a spouse or partner consents to a charge over the jointly owned home to secure the other's business debts under pressure; the question is whether the lender is affected (*Barclays Bank v O'Brien* (1994)). Lord Hobhouse in *Royal Bank of Scotland v Etridge (No 2)* (2001) framed three questions:

**Step 1 – proof of undue influence**
1. **Actual** undue influence: positive proof that consent was procured by pressure so that it was not an exercise of free will (Lord Nicholls: impossible to be more precise). No longer any need to show manifest disadvantage (contrast *BCCI v Aboody* (1991)). Regret alone is not enough (*Bank of Scotland v Bennett* (1998)); consent requires knowing what and why (*Stevens v Newey* (2005)).
2. **Presumed** undue influence – an evidential presumption, not a separate category (*Etridge*, rejecting the rigid *Aboody*/*O'Brien* classes 2A and 2B). Arises where (a) there is a relationship of trust and confidence and (b) the transaction is not readily explicable by the relationship. Certain relationships (parent–child, solicitor–client, doctor–patient, spiritual adviser–devotee) are irrebuttably relationships of trust and confidence; spouses are not, though Lord Scott would assume trust and confidence between cohabiting spouses absent contrary evidence.
3. "Manifest disadvantage" (*National Westminster Bank v Morgan* (1985)) was discarded in *Etridge* in favour of Lindley LJ's test in *Allcard v Skinner* (1887): a transaction "not reasonably accounted for on the ground of friendship, relationship, charity, or other ordinary motives". A wife guaranteeing her husband's business borrowing is often explicable. Breach of the "obligation of candour and fairness": *Hewett v First Plus Financial Group* (2010) (husband concealed an affair). Examples: *Abbey National v Stringer* (2006) (elderly mother unable to read English charged home for son's business – presumption arose and was not rebutted); *Turkey v Awadh* (2005) (transaction explicable by family motives – no presumption).
4. **Rebuttal**: evidence the decision was free and informed – especially independent legal advice that was "relevant and effective" (*Pesticcio v Huet* (2004)): competent, impartial, free of the wrongdoer's pressure, fully explaining risks. In *Etridge* itself there was no abuse and Mrs Etridge's appeal failed.

**Step 2 – was the lender put on inquiry?**
1. Absent agency, the lender is affected only if it had notice (*O'Brien*, Lord Browne-Wilkinson).
2. *Etridge*: the lender is put on inquiry in every case where one person stands surety for another's debts and the relationship is non-commercial. It must actually know of the surety arrangement (*Mortgage Business v Green* (2013)). Not where a joint loan is for joint purposes: *CIBC Mortgages v Pitt* (1993) (loan said to be for a holiday home; lender not on inquiry) – unless the lender knows it is for one party's purposes. Commercial guarantors can look after themselves (Lord Nicholls).

**Step 3 – did the lender take reasonable steps?**
Lord Nicholls's protocol: the surety must receive independent legal advice; the lender must give the solicitor the relevant financial information and any suspicions; and obtain the solicitor's written confirmation that the nature and consequences were explained. The solicitor may also act for the husband or lender if no conflict. Advice should be face to face, without the husband, in plain language, stressing the risk of losing the home and the surety's free choice. With written confirmation, the lender is protected even if the advice was negligent (the claim then lies against the solicitor) – Lord Scott.

**Effect**
- Mortgage set aside entirely (*TSB Bank v Camfield* (1995) – misrepresentation that liability was capped at £15,000; no partial enforcement), or tainted part severed where possible (*Barclays Bank v Caplan* (1998)).
- Restitution of benefits may be required: *Dunbar Bank v Nadeem* (1998) (CA: if relief had been due, the wife would have had to restore her beneficial interest, not repay 50% of the advance).
- *First National Bank v Achampong* (2003): where a joint legal charge is set aside against the victim, s 63(1) LPA 1925 leaves an equitable charge over the wrongdoer's beneficial share, severing the joint tenancy; the bank may seek sale under ss 14–15 TOLATA 1996, with the victim receiving her share of proceeds.
- Misrepresentation can also found rescission (*Camfield*).

## Debate and critique
- **Balance between borrower and lender** (revision lecture): does the court go far enough? the materials frames it as David versus Goliath / economic versus social: lenders must be able to enforce to keep credit flowing; homeowners need security against homelessness.
- **Possession "before the ink is dry"**: an anachronism from the mortgage-by-conveyance era, now tempered by s 36, the Protocol and lenders' practice; *Ropaigealach* shows the gap where no proceedings are brought.
- **Haley, "Mortgage Default: Possession, Relief and Judicial Discretion"** (Legal Studies – the handout cites (1997) 17 LS 483): equity's paternalism toward mortgagors is justified by inequality of bargaining power, and discretion to postpone possession is a necessary safeguard but must be exercised for good reason. *Norgan* is welcomed as a sensible reading reflecting the parties' shared intention that the whole term is the repayment period, provided the borrower has a realistic plan; but generous postponement indirectly fetters the power of sale, and it is ironic that borrowers seeking to discharge the debt by selling are treated less generously (*Krausz*).
- **Mortgagee latitude on sale**: *Cuckmere* imposes duties, but *Silven* and *Serene* show wide discretion over timing and marketing; borrowers bear market risk.
- **s 91 sale**: *Palk* and *Polonski* show willingness to protect borrowers from spiralling debt and to weigh social factors.
- **Policy and lived experience**: Wallace ("Feels like I'm doing it on my own" (2012) 11 Social Policy and Society 117) found post-2008 possession-prevention measures only modestly effective and insufficient to bridge a fragmented policy framework and borrowers' circumstances. Whitehouse's longitudinal analysis highlights the stability of doctrine and the role of soft law.
- **Law Com 204 (1991)**: proposed abolishing foreclosure and reforming mortgagee remedies; not implemented.
- **Undue influence**: *Etridge* replaced unworkable categories with a procedural protocol that protects lenders who obtain a solicitor's certificate – arguably shifting protection from substance to box-ticking, with the victim left to sue a negligent solicitor; *Achampong* lets banks still reach the wrongdoer's share, potentially forcing sale of the victim's home.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Default? Identify which remedy the lender seeks.
2. **Possession**: lender's right exists regardless of default (*Four-Maids*). Is the lender acting bona fide to enforce the security (*Quennell*)? Residential property → s 36: can the borrower pay arrears within a reasonable period (remaining term – *Norgan*)? Has a realistic plan been shown? Negative equity / sale-based proposals (*Krausz*)? Has the Protocol been followed? Self-help entry without proceedings (*Ropaigealach*)?
3. **Sale**: has the power arisen (s 101) and become exercisable (s 103)? Was the sale conducted in good faith, with reasonable care, at a true market price – advertised, existing planning permissions disclosed, not rushed (*Cuckmere*; *Predeth*)? Sale to an associate (*Tse Kwong Lam*)? Remedy: compensation for shortfall.
4. **Borrower wants to sell**: s 91 (*Palk*; *Polonski*).
5. **Lender in possession**: liability to account (*White*); duty of care to property.
6. **Undue influence**: (1) actual or presumed (trust and confidence + transaction calling for explanation)? (2) Was the lender put on inquiry (surety for another's debts, non-commercial; joint loan for joint purposes – *Pitt*)? (3) Were *Etridge* steps taken (independent advice, solicitor's confirmation)? (4) Effect – set aside wholly or partly, restitution, *Achampong* equitable charge over the wrongdoer's share and possible sale under TOLATA.
7. Also consider overreaching/actual occupation if a non-owning occupier claims an interest binding the lender (*Boland*; *Flegg*; *Cann*).
