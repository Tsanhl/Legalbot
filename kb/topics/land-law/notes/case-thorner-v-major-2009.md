# Thorner v Major [2009] UKHL 18 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, judgment given in March 2009 (25 March per Wikipedia; [2009] 1 WLR 776). The panel's speeches that are
described in the sources are those of Lord Hoffmann, Lord Walker, Lord Neuberger and Lord Scott. The case is the
leading modern statement that an assurance for proprietary estoppel need not be express, so long as it is clear enough
in its context.

## Facts
David Thorner worked without pay for about 30 years on Steart Farm near Cheddar, Somerset, owned by his father's cousin,
Peter. There was no express promise of inheritance. Over many years Peter's conduct led David to believe he would
inherit the farm; the sources mention a 1990 incident in which Peter gave David an insurance-related document and
referred to death duties. Peter destroyed an earlier will after a family falling-out, made no new one and died intestate. David claimed the farm by proprietary estoppel.

## Decisions
- The trial judge found for David.
- The Court of Appeal reversed, saying there was no sufficiently clear and unequivocal assurance.
- The House of Lords **allowed David's appeal** and restored the trial judge's order, so David was entitled to the farm
  and its farming assets.

## Principles
- The assurance does not have to be express; it can be indirect or inferred from conduct if, in context, it was
  sufficiently clear. Lord Hoffmann put the question as whether the conduct would reasonably have been understood as
  intended to be taken seriously as an assurance that could be relied upon (a paraphrase of the speech as reported).
- Lord Walker set out the central elements as an assurance about a right in land, reasonable reliance and detriment.
- The requirement that the assurance be "clear" is to be applied with the realities of family and farming dealings in
  mind, not with unrealistic rigour (Lord Neuberger, as reported).
- That the extent of the farm would change over time did not make the subject matter too uncertain, because it was
  identified as it would be at death. The case was distinguished from *Cobbe v Yeoman's Row* [2008] UKHL 55, a
  commercial negotiation.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Ask what the benefactor said and did over time, and whether in context a reasonable person would take it as a
   serious assurance of inheritance. Vague or indirect conduct can be enough in a family or farming setting.
2. Show reliance (working unpaid, forgoing other opportunities) and detriment.
3. Distinguish commercial negotiations such as *Cobbe*, where both sides know the deal is not binding.
4. Move on to the remedy, applying *Jennings v Rice* on proportionality.
