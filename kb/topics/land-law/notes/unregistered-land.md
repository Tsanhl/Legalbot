# Unregistered land

## Overview
Unregistered land is land whose freehold or leasehold title is not recorded at HM Land Registry, so ownership is proved by paper title deeds ("title deeds conveyancing"). Fewer than 12% of titles in England and Wales remain unregistered, and almost any dealing now triggers first registration, but the rules still govern those titles and explain why registration was introduced. The core question is enforceability: when unregistered land is sold, which pre-existing third-party rights bind the buyer? The answer depends on whether the right is legal or equitable, and, if equitable, whether it is a registrable "commercial" land charge, an overreachable "family" interest, or a residual interest governed by the doctrine of notice.

## The law

### 1. Conveyancing outline
1. The seller must show a good root of title of at least **15 years** (s 44 LPA 1925, reduced from 30 years by s 23 LPA 1969): an unbroken chain of dealings ending with the seller. Without it the buyer may withdraw.
2. Physical inspection of the land, then completion by deed and payment.
3. A transfer of the freehold, or grant/assignment of a lease of more than seven years, triggers compulsory first registration (s 4 LRA 2002), so the unregistered stock dwindles.
4. Do not confuse unregistered and registered land; they share overreaching but otherwise use different rules.

### 2. Legal rights: universal enforceability
1. Legal estates and interests (s 1 LPA 1925 – legal leases, legal easements, legal mortgages) "bind the world": a purchaser is bound whether or not they knew.
2. Justification: legal rights are usually visible on inspection (tenants in occupation, a worn right of way) and created by deed, which appears in the title bundle.
3. **Exception – puisne mortgage**: a legal mortgage not protected by deposit of title deeds (typically a second legal mortgage) must be registered as a **Class C(i)** land charge to bind a purchaser.

### 3. Equitable rights: three categories
Equitable interests are all those not within s 1(1)–(2) LPA 1925 (s 1(3)). Classify them:

**(a) Commercial interests registrable as land charges – Land Charges Act 1972**
1. Registrable interests are listed in **s 2 LCA 1972** in six classes. Key ones:
   - **C(i)** puisne mortgage (the only legal interest registrable);
   - **C(iv)** estate contract – a contract to convey or create a legal estate or interest: contracts for sale, to grant a lease, options to purchase, contracts to create a mortgage or easement;
   - **D(ii)** restrictive covenants created after 1 January 1926 affecting freehold land (not landlord-tenant covenants);
   - **D(iii)** equitable easements created after 1925;
   - **F** statutory home rights under Part IV Family Law Act 1996 (not to be confused with beneficial interests under a trust).
2. **Not registrable**: pre-1926 restrictive covenants and equitable easements; rights by estoppel; and "family" interests under a trust (which are overreachable).
3. **Names-based system** (s 3): a charge is registered against the *name of the estate owner* at the time, not against the land.
4. **Searches**: personal search (rare, risky) or official search. Under **s 10(4)** an official search certificate is conclusive in favour of the searcher – but only for the names searched – even if the Registry erred; the charge holder may then have a negligence claim against the Registry.
5. **Name problems**:
   - Correct registration + search against wrong name → searcher bound.
   - *Diligent Finance v Alleyne* (1972): wife registered a Class F charge against "Erskine Alleyne" omitting "Owen"; the lender searched against the full correct name, got a clear result, and took free.
   - *Oak Co-operative Building Society v Blackburn* (1968): estate contract registered against "Frank David Blackburn" (the owner was Francis David, known as Frank); the society searched against "Francis Davis Blackburn" (wrong). Russell LJ: registration in a fair version of the name is not a nullity against someone who does not search or searches in the wrong name, though it would not bind someone who searched in the correct full name. Search broadly against variants; register against the full correct name.
   - *Standard Property Investment v British Plastics Federation* (1985): the "fixed point of reference" is the name in the conveyance to the estate owner – a stricter approach.
6. **Effect of registration** – **s 198(1) LPA 1925**: registration is deemed actual notice to all persons for all purposes, so the charge binds purchasers.
7. **Effect of non-registration** – **s 4 LCA 1972**:
   - Classes C(iv) and D: **s 4(6)** – void against a purchaser *for money or money's worth* of a *legal estate*. Not void against a donee, or a purchaser of an equitable interest.
   - Other classes: **s 4(2), (5), (8)** – void against a purchaser of the land or any interest in it (legal or equitable) for *valuable consideration* (wider: includes marriage consideration, excludes nominal).
8. **Attempts to avoid non-registration**:
   1. *Actual notice*: barred by **s 199(1)(i) LPA 1925** (a purchaser is not prejudicially affected by notice of a registrable but unregistered matter). *Midland Bank Trust Co v Green* (1981, HL): father granted son Geoffrey an unregistered option over farmland, then sold the farm (worth about £40,000) to his wife Evelyne for £500, she knowing of the option, to defeat it. The CA held she was not a purchaser (bad faith, gross undervalue). Lord Wilberforce reversed: the Act contains no good-faith requirement; it would be a "bold" departure to imply one; "money or money's worth" was satisfied even at an undervalue; the option was void. Geoffrey's remedy lay in the tort of conspiracy.
   2. *Fraud*: unsettled; Green found no fraud, but a party relying on its own fraud might be bound.
   3. *Possession/actual occupation* via **s 14 LPA 1925** ("This Part of this Act shall not prejudicially affect the interest of any person in possession or in actual occupation…"): weak – it is in the LPA not the LCA, refers to "this Part", and the LCA is clear.
   4. *Estoppel*: perhaps the strongest – *ER Ives Investment v High* (1967); Oliver J in *Taylors Fashions v Liverpool Victoria Trustees* (1982).
   5. *Independent interest* (constructive trust/estoppel): rejected in *Lloyds Bank v Carrick* (1996) as an attempt to correct non-registration.
9. Contract law may still bind the original parties even if the land charge is void against a purchaser.

**(b) Family interests – overreaching**
Beneficial interests under a trust (e.g. a contribution-based interest of someone not on the title) cannot be registered as land charges. They are overreached if there is a conveyance of a legal estate and capital money is paid to at least two trustees or a trust corporation (ss 2, 27 LPA 1925): the interest detaches from the land and attaches to the money. Example: A (sole legal owner) and B each pay half; buyer C pays only A → no overreaching; if A appoints a second trustee and C pays both, B's interest is overreached and B gets half the proceeds. See overreaching.

**(c) Residual category – doctrine of notice**
Interests that are not registrable, not overreachable, or overreachable but *not actually overreached* (e.g. money paid to one trustee), plus pre-1926 covenants/easements and estoppel rights. A purchaser takes free only if "Equity's Darling" (*Pilcher v Rawlins* (1872)):
1. **Bona fide** – good faith; equity's clean hands.
2. **Purchaser of a legal estate** – including a mortgagee; not an equitable estate.
3. **For value** – nominal value is insufficient; undervalue and marriage consideration suffice (s 205(1)(xxi) LPA 1925).
4. **Without notice**: actual (conscious knowledge), imputed (via an agent), or constructive – **s 199(1)(ii)(a)**: matters that would have come to the purchaser's knowledge "if such inquiries and inspections had been made as ought reasonably to have been made". A prudent purchaser inspects the title deeds, inspects the land, and makes inquiries arising.
   - *Hunt v Luck* (1902): duty to inquire into the rights of occupiers other than the seller.
   - *Caunce v Caunce* (1969): wife's occupation was "wholly consistent" with the husband's sole title; banks need not be snoopers – not overruled but outdated.
   - *Kingsnorth Finance v Tizard* (1986): husband (sole legal owner) described himself as "single" on the mortgage form, told the lender's agent he was separated, and arranged a Sunday inspection when wife and children were out; evidence of children was visible. The lender had constructive notice (imputed from the agent): the inconsistency demanded further inquiry and a pre-arranged inspection was not genuine. Opening cupboards and drawers is not expected. The lender could enforce only against the husband's share.
5. Once an interest loses priority to Equity's Darling it cannot be revived against later purchasers, even those with notice: *Wilkes v Spooner* (1911).

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 ss 1, 14, 44, 198(1), 199(1), 205(1)(xxi); LPA 1969 s 23.
- LCA 1972 ss 2, 3, 4, 10(4).
- *Midland Bank Trust Co v Green* (1981, HL): no good-faith requirement; unregistered option void against purchaser for £500.
- *Oak Co-operative BS v Blackburn* (1968); *Diligent Finance v Alleyne* (1972); *Standard Property v British Plastics* (1985): names-based pitfalls.
- *Lloyds Bank v Carrick* (1996): no independent constructive trust to rescue an unregistered estate contract.
- *ER Ives v High* (1967); *Taylors Fashions* (1982): estoppel.
- *Pilcher v Rawlins* (1872); *Hunt v Luck* (1902); *Caunce v Caunce* (1969); *Kingsnorth v Tizard* (1986); *Wilkes v Spooner* (1911): doctrine of notice.

## Debate and critique
- **Names-based registration** is inherently fragile: everything depends on registering and searching against the right name, and the case law (*Oak* versus *Diligent*/*Standard*) is not entirely consistent. Contrast title-based registration under the LRA 2002.
- ***Green*: certainty versus justice.** The House of Lords upheld the clear statutory words, protecting the integrity of registration (Thompson, "Midland Bank v Green (1980): Maintaining the Integrity of Registration Systems", in Gravells (ed), *Landmark Cases in Land Law*). Megarry and Wade note that clarity of policy is bought at the price of injustice in individual cases.
- **Doctrine of notice** is criticised as unpredictable and dependent on a problematic idea of notice. *Tizard* expands what a reasonable inspection requires beyond *Hunt v Luck* and *Caunce*; Thompson ("The Purchaser as Private Detective" [1986] Conv 283) criticises the over-focus on the pre-arranged inspection and the pressure on purchasers to act as detectives. Further reading: Howell [1997] Conv 431; Nield, "Imputed Notice" [2000] Conv 196.
- **Why study it?** It still governs remaining titles, and its pitfalls (bulky deeds, notice, names) are the rationale for the LRA 2002 – useful comparison in essays on registration.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm the land is unregistered (and that no event has triggered first registration).
2. Identify each third-party right and classify it as legal or equitable (s 1 LPA 1925; was a deed used?).
3. Legal → binds the purchaser, unless it is a puisne mortgage (Class C(i)).
4. Equitable → is it registrable under s 2 LCA 1972? If yes: was it registered against the correct name? Did the purchaser search, and against which name? If unregistered, apply s 4 – who is the purchaser (legal estate? money or money's worth? valuable consideration?). Actual notice is irrelevant (s 199; *Green*). Consider estoppel or fraud arguments.
5. Is it a family interest under a trust? Was capital money paid to two trustees? If so, overreached.
6. Otherwise apply the doctrine of notice: good faith, purchaser of legal estate, value, notice (actual, imputed, constructive). Look for occupiers, inconsistent statements, staged inspections (*Tizard*).
7. Remember personal/contractual claims between the original parties.
8. Trap: equitable easements are Class D(iii) only if created after 1925; pre-1926 ones go to the doctrine of notice.
