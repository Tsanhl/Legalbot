# The definition of land, airspace and subsoil

## Overview
"Land" in English law (England and Wales) is far wider than the soil surface. It covers physical things (buildings, minerals, subsoil, fixtures) and intangible rights (easements, rents, mortgages). The hardest practical question is vertical: how far up and down does a landowner's ownership go? The old maxim *cuius est solum* suggests "to the heavens and the depths", but the courts and statute have limited rights in the airspace while the Supreme Court has kept the maxim alive below ground.

## The law

### 1. Statutory definition
**s 205(1)(ix) LPA 1925**: land "includes land of any tenure, and mines and minerals, whether or not held apart from the surface, buildings or parts of buildings (whether the division is horizontal, vertical or made in any other way) and other corporeal hereditaments; also a manor, an advowson, and a rent and other incorporeal hereditaments, and an easement, right, privilege, or benefit in, over, or derived from land."
- *Corporeal hereditaments*: tangible things – houses, shops, soil.
- *Incorporeal hereditaments*: intangible rights – e.g. a right to cross a neighbour's path (easement) or a mortgage.
- Buildings may be divided horizontally (flats) as well as vertically.

### 2. The expansive meaning: *cuius est solum, eius est usque ad coelum et ad inferos*
"Whoever owns the soil owns everything up to the sky and down to the centre of the earth" (a 16th-century maxim). Components of land discussed in the sources:
1. **Subsoil**: the owner usually owns man-made and natural space beneath the land that is capable of ownership, even if created by someone else and even if the owner cannot access it.
2. **Mines and minerals**: included by s 205, but with exceptions. Gold and silver in mines vest in the Crown (originally the *Case of Mines* (1568)); petroleum in its natural state is vested in the Crown (Petroleum Act 1998); coal in the Coal Authority (Coal Industry Act 1994); the materials also mentions natural gas as Crown property.
3. **Water**: water flowing over or through land cannot be owned; only small volumes can. Where water flows in a defined channel the landowner may receive it unpolluted but must not diminish the flow downstream (*John Young & Co v Bankier Distillery* [1893]); percolating water in undefined channels may be appropriated even to the detriment of neighbours (*Ballard v Tomlinson* (1885); *Bradford Corp v Pickles* [1895]; *Cambridge Water v Eastern Counties Leather* [1994]). Larger abstraction requires a licence.
4. **Flora and fauna**: trees, plants and hedges are part of the land. Living wild animals are subject only to a "qualified" right (to catch and kill); once killed they become the landowner's absolute property. (Wild animals are not owned: *Case of Swans* (1592).)
5. **Buildings** with foundations in the ground are part of the land; buildings without foundations are tested under the fixtures rules (see fixtures-and-chattels).
6. **Finds** and **fixtures** – separate notes.

### 3. Airspace: the lower and upper strata
1. **Lower stratum** – landowner has rights, protected by trespass and nuisance, up to "such height as is necessary for the ordinary use and enjoyment of his land and the structures upon it": Griffiths J, *Bernstein of Leigh (Baron) v Skyviews & General Ltd* [1978] QB 479. Above that, the owner has no more rights than any member of the public.
2. Applied literally, the maxim would be absurd: every satellite over a suburban garden would trespass (Griffiths J).
3. How high the lower stratum extends is fact-sensitive. Intrusions held to be trespass:
   - *Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco* (1957): advertising sign projecting 8 inches over claimant's shop – injunction to remove;
   - *Gifford v Dent* (1926): sign projecting 4ft 8in over forecourt;
   - *Laiqat v Majid* (2005): extractor fan projecting 750mm over neighbour's yard;
   - *Woolerton & Wilson v Richard Costain* (1970): crane oversailing neighbour's land – injunction (see also *Graham v KD Morris & Sons* (1974), Australia);
   - *Anchor Brewhouse Developments v Berkley House*: crane oversailing – trespass actionable without damage (also *Kelsen*).
   - *Lemmon v Webb* (1894): overhanging branches are a nuisance; the neighbour may lop them provided they do not enter the other's land.
4. **Aircraft**: **s 76 Civil Aviation Act 1982** – no action in trespass or nuisance "by reason only of the flight of an aircraft over any property at a height above the ground which, having regard to wind, weather and all the circumstances of the case is reasonable". **Rules of the Air Regulations 2007, reg 3(5)** (s 3(5) in the sources): without CAA permission aircraft must not fly closer than 500 feet to any person, vessel, vehicle or structure, and in a congested area not below 1,000 feet above the highest fixed obstacle.
5. **Human rights**: *Hatton v UK* (2003, ECtHR Grand Chamber) – increased night flights at Heathrow did not breach Art 8 ECHR, though the Court criticised the narrow judicial review and lack of private law remedies. Bevan suggests this is unlikely to be the last word given Heathrow expansion.
6. **Upper stratum** – no private rights. *Re the Queen in Right of Manitoba and Air Canada* (1978, Canada): the upper stratum is *res omnium communis*; Manitoba's attempt to tax in-flight sales failed.
7. **Leases and strata**: the presumption that an owner/lessee owns airspace above depends on the grant. *Rosebery Ltd v Rocklee Ltd* [2011] EWHC B1 (Ch): no presumption where the demise was of a horizontally divided part of a building and there were good reasons to confine it; *H Waites Ltd v Hambledon Court Ltd* [2014] EWHC 615 (Ch): presumption applied to airspace above demised garages (vertical division). Flat owners do not normally have rights over airspace above their flat.

### 4. Subsoil and deep strata
*Star Energy Weald Basin Ltd v Bocardo SA* (2010, UKSC): Star Energy, holding a statutory petroleum licence, drilled diagonally under Bocardo's land at depths from 800 to 2,900 feet without consent. Lord Hope held that, below the surface, the maxim is still good law; at enormous depths (pressure, molten rock) it becomes impractical, but not here. The Crown owned the petroleum but not the surrounding strata, so Bocardo owned them; the drilling was an actionable trespass and damages were awarded. No injunction was sought, and one seems unlikely where the trespass is technical and causes no loss.

## Key authorities
- LPA 1925 s 205(1)(ix): statutory definition of land.
- *Bernstein v Skyviews* [1978] QB 479: aerial photography flight not a trespass; lower stratum limited to ordinary use and enjoyment.
- *Kelsen v Imperial Tobacco* (1957); *Gifford v Dent* (1926); *Laiqat v Majid* (2005): small encroachments into low-level airspace are trespass.
- *Woolerton & Wilson v Costain* (1970); *Anchor Brewhouse v Berkley House*: crane oversailing is trespass even without damage.
- *Lemmon v Webb* (1894): overhanging branches as nuisance; self-help lopping.
- Civil Aviation Act 1982 s 76; Rules of the Air Regulations 2007: statutory immunity and minimum heights for aircraft.
- *Hatton v UK* (2003): no Art 8 breach from Heathrow night flights.
- *Re Queen in Right of Manitoba and Air Canada* (1978): upper stratum not owned.
- *Star Energy v Bocardo* (2010): maxim applies below ground; subterranean drilling trespass.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the maxim helpful?** The revision lecture asks what *cuius est solum* means in practice and how far up and down rights extend. Upwards, it has never fully reflected the law (Bernstein); downwards, *Bocardo* applies it robustly. The asymmetry is worth exploring: airspace above a certain height is treated as a common resource, while deep strata remain private even where the owner could never use them.
- **Certainty versus flexibility.** The Bernstein test turns on "ordinary use and enjoyment", which varies with the facts. Low-level fixed intrusions (signs, fans, cranes) are clear trespasses, but drones and other technology raise open questions. (Gray and Gray, "Property in Thin Air" (1991) CLJ, is on the reading list.)
- **Remedies.** Trespass to airspace is actionable per se; injunctions have been granted for cranes and signs, but *Bocardo* shows damages may be limited and injunctions unlikely where loss is nominal.
- **Human rights and public interest.** *Hatton* shows Art 8 offers limited protection against aviation; growing air traffic keeps this live.
- **The width of "land"** reflects Birks' theme of *space*: one cross-section of land supports many uses at different levels (see what-is-land-law).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the intrusion: airspace (lower or upper stratum?), subsoil, or a physical structure on the surface.
2. For airspace: is the intrusion at a height necessary for the claimant's ordinary use and enjoyment (*Bernstein*)? Fixed structures overhanging at low level (tree houses, signs, fans, cranes) point to trespass: *Kelsen*, *Laiqat*, *Woolerton*. Overhanging branches – nuisance and self-help (*Lemmon v Webb*).
3. No damage needed for trespass (*Kelsen*; *Anchor Brewhouse*). Consider remedy: injunction or damages.
4. Aircraft: flying at a reasonable height in compliance with regulations is protected by s 76 CAA 1982; noise complaints face *Hatton v UK*. A planned new runway does not by itself give a private law claim.
5. Underground: *Bocardo* – the surface owner owns the strata, though minerals such as petroleum, coal, gold and silver may belong to the Crown or other bodies.
6. Leasehold/flat cases: check the terms of the demise for airspace (*Rosebery*; *Waites*).
7. Tutorial example: a neighbour's tree house overhanging by half a metre is analogous to *Laiqat v Majid* (750mm fan) – likely trespass; aircraft noise near an airport – s 76 and *Hatton* make a claim unlikely.
