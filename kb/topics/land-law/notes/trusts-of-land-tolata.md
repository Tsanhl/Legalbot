# Trusts of land under TOLATA 1996 - powers, occupation and sale disputes

## Overview
Co-owned land is held on a trust of land under the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996 (TOLATA). The legal owners are trustees and the equitable owners beneficiaries (often the same people). The Act replaced the old trust for sale, gives trustees wide powers, gives beneficiaries rights to be consulted and to occupy, and provides one court procedure (s 14) for disputes, usually about whether the land should be sold. Creditor and bankruptcy disputes are the most contested part.

## The law
**1. How co-ownership ends.** (a) All title ends up in one person (the last surviving joint tenant); (b) a purchaser of the legal estate overreaches the trust and takes free of it; (c) partition, physically dividing the land (s 7 TOLATA). Partition by trustees needs the consent of beneficiaries who are of full age, absolutely entitled and tenants in common; if they refuse, a court order can be sought.

**2. From trust for sale to trust of land.** The old trust for sale made sale the primary aim, with beneficiaries treated as having an interest in the proceeds (the equitable doctrine of conversion). TOLATA:
- s 1: any trust of land takes effect as a trust of land, and existing trusts for sale were converted;
- s 3: conversion abolished, so beneficiaries have interests in the land itself;
- s 4(1): an express duty to sell carries an implied, non-excludable power to postpone sale.

**3. Trustees' powers and limits.**
- s 6(1): all the powers of an absolute owner, including power to acquire land (s 6(3));
- s 7: power to partition;
- s 9: trustees acting together may delegate functions to beneficiaries of full age;
- limits: regard to beneficiaries' rights (s 6(5)); no breach of any rule of equity (s 6(6)); duty of care (s 6(9));
- s 8: in an expressly created trust, the s 6 and s 7 powers may be excluded or restricted, or made subject to beneficiary consent;
- s 11: duty to consult beneficiaries (of full age and entitled to possession) "so far as is practicable", consistent with the general interest of the trust, and give effect to the wishes of the majority by value of interest; can be excluded by the settlor (s 11(2)).

**4. Beneficiaries' right to occupy (s 12).** Two requirements: the trust's purpose was to make the land available for occupation (or it is so held), s 12(1); and the land is suitable for occupation by that beneficiary, s 12(2). In *Chan Pui Chun v Leung Kam Ho* (2003) Parker LJ said suitability looks at the property's general and physical nature and at the particular beneficiary's personal circumstances and requirements.

**5. Limits on occupation (s 13).** Trustees cannot exclude all entitled beneficiaries (s 13(1)) or exclude or restrict unreasonably (s 13(2)); they may impose reasonable conditions such as paying outgoings (s 13(3)); they must have regard to the creator's intentions, the trust's purpose and each beneficiary's circumstances and wishes (s 13(4)); compensation conditions may be imposed on those who stay (s 13(6)); a beneficiary already in occupation cannot be excluded (s 13(7)). *Rodway v Landy* (2001): two doctors in partnership bought as joint tenants and fell out; the Court of Appeal upheld an order dividing the property into two units, each doctor occupying one.

**6. Court applications (s 14).** Any person with an interest may apply: co-owners, mortgagees, trustees in bankruptcy and others. *Bagum v Hafiz* (2015): the court may order sale on terms that the applying co-owner gets first chance to buy at a court-determined price; unusual but within jurisdiction.

**7. Deciding the application (s 15).** Non-hierarchical factors: intentions of those who created the trust; purposes for which the land is held; welfare of any minor who occupies or might reasonably occupy it as a home; interests of any secured creditor.

**8. Creditor applications.** Before TOLATA, under s 30 LPA 1925, the bias was towards sale and creditors usually won, even against children's welfare (*Lloyds Bank v Byrne* (1991)). In *Mortgage Corporation v Shaire* (2001) Neuberger J said s 15 "has changed the law": a neutral starting point, flexible approach. There the house was a family home and an asset; son now adult; lender wanted its 25% share; wife held 75%; the mortgage was effectively converted into a loan on 25% of the value at 3% over base rate, letting her stay. Later cases were less radical and more creditor-friendly:
- *Bank of Ireland Home Mortgages v Bell* (2001): sale ordered; ill health at best postponed sale; debt growing with no payments for 8 years; unfair to keep the bank waiting;
- *C Putnam & Sons v Taylor* (2009): sale ordered despite family-home purpose; wife could rehouse from her equity;
- *Edwards v Edwards and Bank of Scotland* (2010): sale ordered; marriage over; husband's equity could rehouse him;
- *Fred Perry Ltd v Genis* (2014): sale ordered but postponed 12 months; family occupation rights under the Family Law Act 1996 did not create "homestead rights" ring-fencing the home from creditors;
- contrast *Edwards v Lloyds TSB* (2004): forged mortgage; sale postponed until youngest child reached 18; immediate sale "unacceptably severe"; enough equity for the bank to wait.

**9. Bankruptcy of a co-owner.** The trustee in bankruptcy applies under s 14 but the court applies s 335A Insolvency Act 1986, not s 15. Intentions and purpose of the trust are not relevant factors. Timing matters:
- within 12 months of the estate vesting in the trustee: the court balances the s 335A(2) factors, considering all circumstances other than the bankrupt's needs (s 335A(2)(c));
- after 12 months: s 335A(3) directs the court to assume creditors' interests outweigh all else unless circumstances are "exceptional".

"Exceptional" case law: *Hosking v Michaelides* (2004) (out of the ordinary, unusual, special or uncommon); *Re Holliday* (1981, pre-TOLATA: postponed until children were 18); *Re Citro* (1991) (narrowed it: more than the usual hardship of rehousing or schooling disruption); *Re Bremner* (1999) (problems lying wholly outside the normal range); *Grant v Baker* (2016) (daughter with a mental age of 8-9, mobility problems and OCD: exceptional, 12-month postponement); *Pickard v Constable* (2017) (husband on a ventilator: exceptional, 12 months). Human rights: obiter in *Barca v Mears* (2004) questioned whether s 335A(3) is compatible with Art 8 and Art 1 Protocol 1 and suggested that "exceptionally severe" ordinary consequences could count; not adopted afterwards, and *Nicholls v Tan* (2006) held s 335A(3) compatible.

## Key authorities
- TOLATA ss 1, 3, 4, 6-9, 11-15; Insolvency Act 1986 s 335A.
- *Mortgage Corporation v Shaire* (2001), *Bank of Ireland v Bell* (2001), *Edwards v Lloyds TSB* (2004), *Fred Perry v Genis* (2014): creditor-versus-family cases.
- *Rodway v Landy* (2001), *Chan Pui Chun v Leung Kam Ho* (2003), *Bagum v Hafiz* (2015): occupation and court powers.
- *Hosking v Michaelides* (2004), *Grant v Baker* (2016), *Pickard v Constable* (2017), *Barca v Mears* (2004), *Nicholls v Tan* (2006): bankruptcy.

## Debate and critique
- Has s 15 changed the law? Neuberger J in *Shaire* said yes (new neutral starting point; no hierarchy). the materials it largely codifies earlier factors from the "primary purpose" era, and later cases have been creditor-friendly. Dixon (CLJ 2011) describes a flexible, circumstance-dependent approach under which sale is more easily refused or postponed.
- ss 12 and 13 send mixed signals: a new right to occupy that trustees can heavily restrict; the materials asks whether this improves on the trust for sale.
- Reading list (titles only in the sources): Dixon on the "irony" of TOLATA; Pascoe on s 15 and on the right to occupy; Probert on creditors and s 15; Baker on exceptional circumstances and the Human Rights Act.
- Pattern: sale is likelier where debt is spiralling (*Bell*, *Putnam*), postponed where equity is sufficient to wait (*Edwards v Lloyds TSB*); commercial interests tend to prevail over residential security (*Fred Perry*).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify trustees and beneficiaries; is it an express or implied trust of land (can s 6, 7, 11 be excluded under s 8 or s 11(2))?
2. Is the claim about occupation? Check s 12(1) purpose, s 12(2) suitability, then trustees' exclusion under s 13 and reasonable conditions.
3. Is someone seeking sale? Who applies: co-owner, secured creditor or trustee in bankruptcy?
4. Non-bankruptcy: list the s 15 factors; weigh children's welfare, the debt's growth, equity available to rehouse, and the purpose (family home).
5. Bankruptcy: has 12 months passed since vesting? If so, are circumstances exceptional (illness, disability of a dependant) and likely only to postpone sale (usually about 12 months)? Mention Art 8 doubts but note *Nicholls*.
6. Check whether a purchaser might overreach the trust.
7. Traps: applying s 15 in a bankruptcy case; treating "needs of the bankrupt" as relevant; assuming Shaire is the settled approach.
