# What is Land Law? Foundational themes and concepts

## Overview
Land law (England and Wales) is the body of rules on the nature, creation and protection of rights in land, and on how competing rights over the same parcel rank against each other (the "priority" or "enforceability" question). The opening framework sets out the conceptual toolkit used in every later topic: real versus personal property, proprietary versus personal rights, tenure, estates and interests, the legal/equitable divide, and the two systems of title (registered and unregistered land). Understanding these building blocks is essential for both problem questions (who is bound by what?) and essays (why is land treated specially?).

## The law

### 1. What "land law" covers
1. Birks ("Before We Begin: Five Keys to Land Law", in Bright and Dewar (eds), *Land Law: Themes and Perspectives*, 1998) argues land law is not just a subject matter (like "cat law") nor merely a legal category such as "property rights". It is the subset formed when the category of property right is confined to the context of land. Its content is therefore the nature, creation and protection of interests in land.
2. Gray and Gray (*Elements of Land Law*, 5th edn) describe land law as the part of the general law allocating rights and responsibilities in relation to "real" or "immoveable" property. This tells us land law is (a) about legal relationships between people and (b) specifically those relating to real property.
3. **Real versus personal property.** "Real property" (realty) is linked to rights *in rem* (rights in the thing). Historically an action *in rem* allowed recovery of the land itself (e.g. a dispossessed owner getting it back), whereas an action *in personam* lay against a wrongdoer personally and produced only damages. Personal property (personalty) covers moveables: cars, crockery, money.
4. Several interests can exist over one parcel at the same time (for instance a lease and a mortgage). Land law must explain how they co-exist and resolve which takes priority when they conflict.

### 2. Why land is special (Theme 1)
commentary lists the features that justify a distinct body of rules: land is permanent; it is in limited supply; it is connected (what happens on one parcel affects neighbours); each parcel is unique; it is socially and economically important; and it can support multiple simultaneous interests.

### 3. The personal/proprietary divide (Theme 2)
1. Proprietary rights are *capable* of binding third parties (e.g. a purchaser of the land); personal rights bind only the parties who created them. This is what gives interests in land their potency.
2. Standard illustration: a contractual licence is personal, so if the licensor sells to a purchaser the licensee generally cannot assert the licence against the purchaser. A lease is proprietary and is capable of binding the purchaser.
3. Because proprietary status is so powerful, the categories of proprietary right must be controlled; otherwise land would be over-burdened and its value and usability would fall. A permission (e.g. a postwoman walking up your path) is a right in relation to land but not a right *in* land.
4. **Test for proprietary status** – *National Provincial Bank v Ainsworth* (1965), Lord Wilberforce: to be admitted to the category of property a right must be "definable, identifiable by third parties, capable in its nature of assumption by third parties, and have some degree of permanence or stability". On the facts, a deserted wife's right to stay in the matrimonial home was personal only and did not bind the bank.

### 4. Tenure, estates and interests (Theme 3)
1. **Tenure.** After 1066 all land was held from the King; subjects held land as tenants in return for services (the forms ranged from grand sergeanty to socage). The Tenures Abolition Act 1660 effectively ended feudal tenures, but tenure remains the theoretical basis of landholding: all land is still held from the Crown, and land of a person dying intestate with no traceable relatives reverts to the Crown. Strictly, "I own my home" means "I hold an estate in it".
2. **Estates.** An estate is the duration for which a person may enjoy rights of use and possession – Megarry and Wade's "interest in land for some particular duration", a slice of time in land. Under **s 1 Law of Property Act 1925** only two estates can exist at law:
   - the **freehold**: "fee simple absolute in possession". *Fee simple* – it can be passed on in life and by will, giving the character of permanent ownership; *absolute* – not conditional or ended by some event; *in possession* – carrying an immediate right to occupy and enjoy;
   - the **leasehold**: "term of years absolute". It confers exclusive use for a defined term, which may be long or short (99 years, one year, one month) but must be of definable duration; it can be assigned while the term runs; it is "carved out" of a larger estate (a freehold or a longer lease).
   Before 1926 other estates (fee tail, life estate) could exist at law.
3. **Interests in land** are proprietary rights falling short of an estate and conferring no ownership: e.g. easements (rights of way), profits, mortgages, options and restrictive covenants. Being proprietary, they can bind successors in title.
4. The result is a hierarchy (Bevan, Figure 1.7): freehold at the top, then leasehold, then non-possessory proprietary interests (easements, profits, covenants), and at the bottom the purely personal licence.

### 5. The legal/equitable distinction (Theme 4)
1. **History.** Common law courts awarded remedies according to rigid writs and formalities. Petitioners went to the King, who passed cases to the Lord Chancellor; the Court of Chancery developed, acting on conscience and granting remedies such as injunctions and specific performance and recognising new rights such as the trust. The Judicature Acts 1873–75 fused the administration of the two systems, but the distinction remains significant (the famous image of two streams in one channel that do not mingle their waters).
2. **Capacity question.** Is the right capable of being legal? **s 1(2) LPA 1925** lists the interests that can exist at law: easements and profits (equivalent to a legal estate), rentcharges, charges by way of legal mortgage, and rights of re-entry annexed to a legal lease or rentcharge. Anything else (e.g. restrictive covenants, options, life interests, interests under a trust) can only be equitable.
3. **Creation question.** Was the right created with the required formality?
   - Legal rights generally require a **deed**. Under **s 1(2) LP(MP)A 1989** an instrument is a deed only if it is in writing, makes clear on its face that it is intended as a deed, and is validly executed (signed and witnessed) as a deed.
   - Some rights must also be **registered** to take effect at law (see registered-land-lra-2002); failure to register leaves the right equitable only.
   - A right is equitable if (a) it is outside s 1 LPA 1925; (b) it is within s 1 but formalities failed (no deed); or (c) formalities were complied with but required registration was not completed.
   - Even equity generally insists on writing: a contract complying with **s 2 LP(MP)A 1989** or writing under **s 53 LPA 1925**.
4. **The trust** splits ownership between legal title (trustee) and equitable/beneficial title (beneficiary). Maitland called it equity's greatest achievement. Its origin is commonly linked to the medieval "use" (crusading knights conveying land to friends); the textbook notes parallels with the Islamic *waqf*. Trusts may be express, implied or imposed by statute (e.g. whenever land is co-owned – see trusts-of-land-tolata).
5. **Why it matters.** Legal rights pre-1926 bound the whole world. Equitable rights bound everyone except the bona fide purchaser of a legal estate for value without notice ("Equity's Darling": *Pilcher v Rawlins* (1872)). Notice may be actual, imputed (through an agent) or constructive (s 199(1)(ii)(a) LPA 1925: what reasonable inspections and enquiries would have revealed – investigating title and inspecting the land: *Hunt v Luck* (1902); *Kingsnorth Finance v Tizard* (1986)). Today equitable interests remain more precarious and generally need more steps to protect (see unregistered-land, registered-land-lra-2002).

### 6. The 1925 legislation
Coming into force on 1 January 1926, the LPA 1925, Land Charges Act 1925 and Land Registration Act 1925 (among six statutes) aimed to make land more alienable by (a) making title easier to investigate, (b) making third-party rights discoverable, and (c) protecting holders of third-party rights. Key techniques: reducing legal estates to two and legal interests to a short list; **overreaching** (detaching family-type equitable interests under trusts so they attach to purchase money – see overreaching); land charges registration for unregistered land; and title registration (LRA 1925, now replaced by the **LRA 2002**, in force 13 October 2003).

### 7. Two systems of title (Theme 5)
- **Registered land**: each title has a unique title number at HM Land Registry recording owner and interests; a buyer becomes owner on registration. Governed by the LRA 2002. Commentary states over 88% of titles are registered.
- **Unregistered land**: title proved by paper title deeds showing a good root of title; governed by the LPA 1925 and Land Charges Act 1972. Around 12% of titles; increasingly marginal.

## Key authorities
- *National Provincial Bank v Ainsworth* (1965): wife's right to remain in matrimonial home after desertion was personal; Lord Wilberforce's four-part test for proprietary status. Criticised as circular (a right binds third parties if it is "capable of assumption by third parties").
- *Pilcher v Rawlins* (1872): classic statement of the bona fide purchaser rule.
- *Hunt v Luck* (1902); *Kingsnorth Finance Co v Tizard* (1986): what a prudent purchaser must inspect for constructive notice.
- LPA 1925 s 1: two legal estates; list of legal interests; everything else equitable.
- LP(MP)A 1989 s 1(2): requirements of a deed; s 2: contracts for sale or other disposition of an interest in land must be in writing.
- LPA 1925 s 199(1)(ii)(a): constructive notice.
- Tenures Abolition Act 1660: effectively ended feudal tenures.

## Debate and critique
- **Birks' five keys.** Birks identifies *duality* (law and equity: equitable rights as "weak" proprietary rights, the price of equity's relaxed approach), *reality* (property rights as rights in rem distinguished from rights in personam), *formality* (now mainly public registration of rights), *space* (the surface as a cross-section of space with potential for multiple uses) and *time* (dealing in slices of time, especially via the lease). These are driven by two motivations – the *commercial* (land as asset and investment, now dominant) and the *family* (originally dynastic retention of land, now more about passing on financial value) – and unified by one pervasive theme, *facilitation* of what people want to achieve with land. Useful as an analytical frame for any essay: does a given rule facilitate dealings?
- **Ainsworth's circularity.** Bevan's textbook calls the test "horribly circular and largely unhelpful", though it usefully reminds us proprietary rights are rooted in land so they survive changes of ownership. The boundary is particularly contested in the lease/licence distinction (see leases-essential-characteristics, licences).
- **Land as more than subject matter.** Cooke: land law concerns connections between people and land and relationships between people "jostling for space"; it changes as society changes. Essays can draw on social and historical context (feudal origins, the 1925 reforms' commercial orientation).
- **Fusion debate.** How far law and equity are truly fused remains contested; the distinction persists in its effects on enforceability.
- **Unregistered land's decline.** With roughly 12% of titles unregistered, unregistered principles are said to be becoming redundant, yet they explain the rationale of registration.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the right claimed (estate? interest? licence?).
2. Is it proprietary or personal? Use the recognised categories; *Ainsworth* as the general test. If personal (e.g. contractual licence), it generally will not bind a purchaser.
3. Capacity: is it listed in s 1 LPA 1925? If not, it is equitable only.
4. Creation: was a deed used (s 1 LP(MP)A 1989)? If registration is required, was it registered? If not, is there at least writing under s 2 LP(MP)A 1989 / s 53 LPA 1925, giving an equitable interest?
5. Is the land registered or unregistered? Apply the right regime – do not mix them up (the checklist for registered land is in registered-land-lra-2002).
6. Trap: assuming an informal grant is void. It may still be equitable (or support proprietary estoppel: see proprietary-estoppel-establishing).
