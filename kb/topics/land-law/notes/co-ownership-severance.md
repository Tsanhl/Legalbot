# Co-ownership - joint tenancy, tenancy in common and severance

## Overview
Co-ownership means two or more people owning the same land at the same time (concurrent, not successive, ownership). It always operates behind a trust of land governed by TOLATA 1996, so the analysis must be split between the legal title and the equitable (beneficial) title. The key exam questions are: how is the land held in equity (joint tenancy or tenancy in common), and, if it is a joint tenancy, has it been severed before a co-owner's death so that survivorship does not apply?

## The law
**1. The irreducible core.** Unity of possession: without it there is no co-ownership at all. Co-owners can be classed as joint tenants (JT) or tenants in common (TiC).

**2. Joint tenancy.** Each joint tenant holds the whole and not a share (the materials quotes Coke: he "holds the whole jointly and nothing separately"). A JT needs all four unities: possession, interest, title and time. Its defining feature is survivorship: on a joint tenant's death her interest passes automatically to the survivors until one remains absolutely entitled. The "winner takes it all".

**3. Tenancy in common.** Each co-owner has a notional undivided share which can be sold, mortgaged or left by will. Only unity of possession is needed. No survivorship.

**4. Law and equity are analysed separately.**
- At law: only a JT is possible (s 1(6) LPA 1925); there can be no severance at law (s 36(2) LPA 1925); no more than four legal co-owners, being the first four named (of full age) in the conveyance (s 34(2) LPA 1925). There is no cap or age limit in equity.
- In equity: JT or TiC. Three analytical tools:
  (a) an express declaration of trust is conclusive, subject to mistake, fraud or undue influence (*Goodman v Gallant* (1986); *Pankhania v Chandegra* (2012));
  (b) the four unities (a JT requires all four);
  (c) rebuttable equitable presumptions: equity follows the law (JT), so property bought in joint names in the domestic context is presumed a JT (*Stack v Dowden* (2007); *Jones v Kernott* (2011)); equity prefers a TiC on grounds of fairness; unequal contributions to the price point to a TiC; co-lenders on a mortgage are presumed TiC; business partners buying land jointly are presumed TiC (*Bathurst v Scarborow* (2004)); two or more business tenants of premises are presumed TiC (*Malayan Credit v Jack Chia-MPH* (1986)).

**5. Meaning and effect of severance.** Dillon LJ in *Harris v Goddard* (1983): severance separates off a joint tenant's share so that concurrent ownership continues but survivorship stops; the parties hold as tenants in common. Consequences:
- it operates only in equity and does not alter the legal title;
- the severing tenant takes a share proportionate to the number of joint tenants (*Goodman v Gallant*);
- remaining co-owners' status is unchanged unless they too sever;
- it must occur before death, because survivorship operates automatically on death;
- the burden of proving severance is on the party asserting it (*Re Denny* (1947));
- it cannot be reversed.

**6. Methods of severance (five).**

*(a) Written notice, s 36(2) LPA 1925.* Two requirements.
- An effective notice. No set form, but it must show a clear, immediate and unequivocal intention to sever (*Goodman v Gallant* gave a model wording). A divorce summons seeking sale and equal division was effective in *Re Draper's Conveyance* (1969). A divorce petition seeking an order that was "just" lacked immediacy in *Harris v Goddard*, so there was no severance and the survivor took everything.
- Service on all the other joint tenants. Severance takes effect on service. Under s 196 LPA 1925 a notice is served when delivered to the last known address or place of business (registered post: when it would in the ordinary course be delivered); it need not be read or received. *Re 88 Berkeley Road* (1972): recorded-delivery notice signed for by the sender (a co-resident) before her death; severance took effect on delivery. *Kinch v Bullard* (1998): the notice was delivered to the house but the sender, who had changed her mind, intercepted and destroyed it before the husband read it; held served, so severance occurred and both died as tenants in common with 50% each.

*(b) The Williams v Hensman (1861) catalogue* (Page-Wood V-C), three further methods:
- *Operating on one's own share.* The joint tenant acts as if she had a share. Examples: transfer to a third party (the transferee becomes TiC in equity, the transferor stays a legal joint tenant); transfer to another joint tenant; mortgage of her interest; a specifically enforceable contract to transfer or mortgage (equity treats as done what ought to be done). A fraudulent transfer or mortgage (e.g. forged signatures) still severs: *First National Security v Hegerty* (1965).
- *Mutual agreement.* All equitable joint tenants must be party; unilateral acts do not suffice. Incomplete, continuing negotiations lack finality (*Nielson-Jones v Fedden* (1975)), and the agreement must go to ownership of the land, not merely how sale proceeds will be spent (the same case). The more formal and complete, the more likely to sever (*Hunter v Babbage* (1994): solicitors were instructed and a draft agreement to sell and split proceeds was in hand). No specifically enforceable contract is needed: in *Burgess v Rawnsley* (1975) an oral agreement to sell one's share for £750, later repudiated, still evidenced a common intention to sever (Sir John Pennycuick: the significance of an agreement is that it shows a common intention to sever, not that it binds).
- *Mutual course of dealing.* The "last chance saloon": all joint tenants have conducted themselves as though entitled to separate shares. Examples: mutual wills (*Re Heys* (1914)); physical division of the land, only where it shows a mutual intention to end the joint tenancy (*Greenfield v Greenfield* (1979)). Ongoing negotiations lack the necessary immediacy, which is why Lord Denning's alternative reasoning in *Burgess* is doubtful. Courts often blur this head with mutual agreement.

*(c) Forfeiture.* Where one joint tenant unlawfully kills another, public policy and s 1 Forfeiture Act 1982 stop the killer benefiting. The killing is treated as severing the JT so survivorship does not operate. The civil standard of proof suffices; a conviction is not required (though persuasive). Under s 2(1) the court may modify or disapply the rule where "the justice of the case" requires, but not where the conviction is for murder (s 5).

## Key authorities
- *Goodman v Gallant* (1986): express declaration conclusive; notice wording; share proportional to number of joint tenants.
- *Harris v Goddard* (1983): definition of severance; divorce petition not an immediate notice.
- *Re Draper's Conveyance* (1969): divorce summons seeking sale amounted to a notice.
- *Re 88 Berkeley Road* (1972) and *Kinch v Bullard* (1998): service is delivery; notice need not be read.
- *Burgess v Rawnsley* (1975), *Nielson-Jones v Fedden* (1975), *Hunter v Babbage* (1994): mutual agreement.
- *First National Security v Hegerty* (1965): fraudulent mortgage/transfer severs.
- *Re Heys* (1914), *Greenfield v Greenfield* (1979): mutual course of dealing.
- Forfeiture and s 2 relief: *Re K* (1985) (relief granted; wife killed husband after prolonged domestic violence); *Dunbar v Plant* (1997) (relief; failed suicide pact); *Mack v Lockwood* (2009) (refused; 54 stab wounds); *Henderson v Hendrix* (2015) (refused despite son's mental disorder; he had capacity); *Re Amos* (2020) (relief; death by careless driving, early guilty plea); *Challen v Challen* (2020) (relief after coercive control and violence; murder conviction quashed, manslaughter plea).

## Debate and critique
- the materials's recommended reading frames the live arguments: Nield on whether a mortgage by one joint tenant severs; Tee on the scope of severance; Gardner on *Goodman v Gallant*; and Bevan on the status of an executed express declaration after *Stack* and *Jones*. The source files give only the titles, so no further positions are stated here.
- Inconsistency between heads: overlap between mutual agreement and course of dealing, and judicial confusion over terminology (Lord Denning in *Burgess*), make outcomes hard to predict.
- Severance by fraudulent act or by destroyed notice (*Hegerty*, *Kinch*) shows how formal rules can sever against a co-owner's actual wishes.
- Forfeiture relief is fact-sensitive and the case law shows an inconsistent line between sympathetic and unsympathetic killers, with murder an absolute bar.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Law first: who are the legal owners (max four; JT only)?
2. Equity: any express declaration? If none, check four unities, then the presumptions (domestic joint names = JT; business or unequal contributions = TiC).
3. If a JT, ask whether any co-owner died. If so, did severance happen before death?
4. Run each method in turn: written notice (immediate and unequivocal? served per s 196?), own share (sale, mortgage, contract, fraud), mutual agreement (all parties, goes to ownership, finality), course of dealing (mutual wills, division), forfeiture (unlawful killing; s 2 relief unless murder).
5. Trap: divorce proceedings alone (*Harris v Goddard*) and unilateral wishes do not sever; negotiations about proceeds only (*Nielson-Jones*) do not sever.
6. Result: severed means TiC with a proportionate share passing under the will or intestacy; not severed means survivor takes all.
