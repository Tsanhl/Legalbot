# Finds and treasure

## Overview
When an object is found in or on land, several people may claim it: the true owner, the finder, the landowner or occupier, and the finder's employer. Common law rules (England and Wales) settle these competing claims by relative title, depending heavily on whether the item was *in* (embedded in or attached to) the land or *on* its surface. If the item is "treasure" under the Treasure Act 1996, a separate statutory scheme applies and the item vests in the Crown. The 1996 Act was significantly expanded in July 2023 by a new "significance-based" class.

## The law

### Step 1: Is it treasure? (Treasure Act 1996)
1. **Definition – s 1(1)**. Treasure is:
   - (a) any object at least 300 years old when found which (i) is not a coin but has metallic content of which at least 10% by weight is precious metal; (ii) is one of at least two coins in the same find, at least 300 years old, with that percentage of precious metal; or (iii) is one of at least ten coins in the same find at least 300 years old (no precious-metal requirement);
   - (b) any object at least 200 years old belonging to a class designated under s 2(1);
   - (c) any object that would have been treasure trove if found before s 4 commenced;
   - (d) any object which, when found, is part of the same find as an object within (a)–(c) (found at the same time or earlier), or an object found earlier that would be within (a) or (b) if found at the same time.
2. **Exclusions** (textbook): unworked natural objects (fossils, minerals) even if found with treasure; unworked human or animal remains even if found with treasure; and objects that are "wreck" under Part IX Merchant Shipping Act 1995. From 2023, also objects subject to the Church of England faculty jurisdiction and objects found in or under a cathedral or its precinct (covered by ecclesiastical law).
3. **s 2** lets the Secretary of State amend the definition by statutory instrument. Expansions:
   - **January 2003**: any object any part of which is base metal which is one of at least two base-metal objects of prehistoric date in the same find; and any prehistoric object any part of which is gold or silver.
   - **July 2023 – significance class**: any object, any part of which is metal, at least 200 years old, which meets the "significance criteria": (a) it gives an exceptional insight into an aspect of national or regional history, archaeology or culture because of its rarity as an example of its type, the location/region where found, or its connection with a particular person or event; or (b) it does not itself give such insight but does so together with other objects in the same find.
4. **s 3** – interpretation: a "coin" includes any metal token reasonably assumed to have been used as or instead of money; "precious metal" means gold or silver; rules on what is "the same find"; an object is presumed to be the age it reasonably appears to be unless shown otherwise.
5. **s 4 – ownership**: when found, treasure vests, subject to prior interests and rights, in the franchisee (if any) or otherwise **in the Crown**, whatever the nature of the place where found and whatever the circumstances in which it was left (including being lost or left with no intention of recovery).
6. **s 8 – duty to notify the coroner**: a person who finds an object they believe, or have reasonable grounds for believing, might be treasure must notify the coroner within 14 days (from the day after the find, or the day the finder first believes or has reason to believe it is treasure). Failure is a criminal offence, subject to a reasonable excuse defence (s 8(3)).
7. **s 10 – rewards**: rewards may be paid to the finder and landowner.
8. **Process** (textbook): the coroner holds an inquest with expert evidence; if treasure, specialist valuers assess it and the Treasure Valuation Committee recommends a value (appealable) and the division of rewards; a museum wishing to acquire it pays the recommended sum (within four months) and funds rewards. Usually the finder and landowner split 50:50, but the finder's share may be reduced or refused if they lacked permission to be on the land, did not comply with the process, or acted illegally. The Treasure Act Code of Practice (2nd revision) gives detailed guidance.
9. **Reform history**: the 2019 DCMS consultation proposed a fixed date (pre-1714), a value-based class (over £10,000), a class for single gold coins AD 43–1344, and a duty on acquirers to report. The December 2020 Government response rejected a value-based definition as impractical and committed to a significance-based definition (realised in 2023), updating Codes, working with the British Museum, and commencing s 30 Coroners and Justice Act 2009, which will insert s 8A requiring anyone *acquiring* a suspected unreported treasure item to notify the coroner within 14 days (to stop unreported sales).

### Step 2: If not treasure – the common law of finders
1. **True owner first.** A finder must take reasonable steps to locate the true owner (e.g. publicising the find). The true owner, unless they have abandoned the item, beats everyone: *Moffatt v Kazana* (1969) – a biscuit tin of banknotes hidden in the attic by the former owner (Mr Russell) belonged to his estate, not to the later purchaser of the house; the tin and notes were chattels and did not pass with the house.
2. **Finder versus dispossessor.** The finder has title good against everyone except the true owner: *Armory v Delamirie* (1722) – a chimney sweep's boy who found a jewel could recover it from a goldsmith who kept the stones; the finder has "such a property as will enable him to keep it against all but the rightful owner".
3. **Items found *in* or attached to land.** The landowner (or lawful possessor) has the better title than the finder, even if unaware of the item: *Elwes v Brigg Gas Co* (1886) – a prehistoric boat found six feet down by a tenant belonged to the landowner; *Waverley BC v Fletcher* (1995, CA) – a metal detectorist digging nine inches into a council park; the brooch belonged to the council. Auld LJ: where an article is found in or attached to land, as between the owner or lawful possessor of the land and the finder, the owner or possessor has the better title.
4. **Items found *on* the land surface.** *Parker v British Airways Board* [1982] QB 1004 (CA), Donaldson LJ:
   - the finder acquires rights only if (1) the item was lost or abandoned and (2) the finder took it into their care and control;
   - the finder then has a better title than the occupier unless the occupier can show that, *before* the find, it manifested an intention to exercise control over the premises *and the things on them*;
   - the materials checklist also flags whether the finder acted with dishonest intent as a relevant question;
   - the true owner beats both.
   On the facts, Mr Parker found a gold bracelet on the floor of BA's executive lounge, handed it to staff with his details; BA sold it for £850. Parker won. BA controlled entry to the lounge but had not shown an intention to control lost articles: staff instructions were not publicised to users and BA did not search the lounge.
5. *Hannah v Peel* (1945): a soldier found a brooch in a window-frame crevice of a requisitioned house the owner (Major Peel) had never occupied and did not know about. The finder won; only the true owner, or Peel if he had found it first, would have had a better claim.
6. **Employees and agents** – Donaldson LJ in *Parker*: unless otherwise agreed, an employee or agent who finds a chattel in the course of employment (and not merely incidentally) and takes it into care does so for the employer, who acquires the finder's rights. *M'Dowell v Ulster Bank* (1899, Ireland): bank porter sweeping the floor found banknotes – the bank had the better claim. Donaldson LJ suggested the same applies to independent contractors.

## Key authorities
- Treasure Act 1996 ss 1–4, 8, 10; 2003 and 2023 designation orders; Code of Practice (2nd revision).
- *Armory v Delamirie* (1722): finder's relative title against all but true owner.
- *Moffatt v Kazana* (1969): true owner prevails; chattels do not pass with a house sale.
- *Elwes v Brigg Gas* (1886); *Waverley BC v Fletcher* (1995): items in/attached to land go to the landowner.
- *Parker v BA Board* (1982): items on the surface – finder wins unless occupier manifested prior intention to control the land and things on it.
- *Hannah v Peel* (1945): finder beat a non-occupying owner unaware of the item.
- *M'Dowell v Ulster Bank* (1899): employee finds for employer.

## Debate and critique
- **"In" versus "on".** the materials asks whether the distinction is convincing and suggests it arguably is not: whether an object sits on the grass or an inch under the soil can be a matter of chance, yet it determines who takes. The rule favours landowners for embedded items even where they had no knowledge (*Elwes*).
- **Manifesting control.** *Parker* makes outcomes turn on how clearly an occupier signalled control (publicised lost-property policies, searches). Banks and similar restricted premises are more likely to succeed than open public spaces; critics might say this rewards formal policies rather than real connection to the item.
- **Policy of the Treasure Act.** It replaced the narrow, convoluted common law of treasure trove to keep more antiquities for national heritage and museum display, with rewards encouraging reporting. The revision lecture asks whether the finds and treasure rules are defensible, helpful and effective.
- **The 2023 significance test.** A welcome expansion to capture culturally important items (e.g. single objects with low precious-metal content), but the materials asks how easy it will be to apply: "exceptional insight", rarity, and connection to a person or event are evaluative and may cause uncertainty for finders deciding whether to report. Bevan has written on this (C. Bevan, "A New Definition of 'Treasure' under the Treasure Act 1996: Watershed Reform or Missed Opportunity?" (2023) MLR – on the reading list; the text itself is not in the raw folder). Other reading: Bray [2013] Conv 265; Marston and Ross [1997] Conv 273, [1998] Conv 252.
- **Value-based test rejected** (2020): consultees saw value as neither practical nor a good proxy for cultural importance.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Could it be treasure? Check age, metal content, number of coins, same-find rules, prehistoric classes, and the 2023 significance class. If facts are unclear, say so and advise reporting to the coroner within 14 days (criminal liability under s 8). If treasure: vests in the Crown (s 4); discuss rewards and whether the finder had permission to be on the land.
2. If not treasure: can the true owner be found? If so, they win (*Moffatt*).
3. Where was it found? In/attached to land → landowner (*Elwes*; *Waverley*). On the surface → *Parker*.
4. Under *Parker*: was it lost or abandoned? Did the finder take it into care and control? Was the finder a trespasser, dishonest, or an employee/agent acting in the course of employment (*M'Dowell*)? Did the occupier manifest, before the find, an intention to control the land and things on it?
5. If someone takes the item from the finder (e.g. the landowner who promised to trace the owner), the finder can recover against that person unless they have a better title (*Armory*).
6. Tutorial example: a postwoman (lawfully on the path, not in the course of employment as finder in any obvious sense – consider the argument) finds a gold necklace lying on the grass of a private front garden; the owner takes it. Ask whether a private householder manifested control over his garden and things on it (a private home may be easier to show than an open lounge), whether the necklace might be treasure (age? metal content? significance?), and whether the true owner can be traced.
