# Proprietary estoppel – satisfying the equity and Guest v Guest

## Overview
Once a claimant has established an estoppel equity (assurance, reliance, detriment, unconscionability – see proprietary-estoppel-establishing), the court must decide how to "satisfy the equity". Remedies range from a transfer of the freehold to a licence, money, or nothing. For decades the debate was whether the remedy should fulfil the claimant's expectation or compensate their reliance loss, with proportionality emerging as a middle way. The Supreme Court in *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27 (3:2) adopted an expectation-based starting point checked by proportionality; the minority would have adopted a reliance-based approach. The decision is examinable in its own right and heavily criticised.

## The law

### 1. The aim: minimum equity to do justice
1. The equity arises at the time of detrimental reliance; it is not itself a remedy.
2. Scarman LJ, *Crabb v Arun DC* (1976): the court seeks "the minimum equity to do justice" to the claimant. Lord Briggs in *Guest*: this means the minimum needed to prevent or undo the unconscionability, not the least generous remedy available.
3. Deciding whether an equity arises and how to satisfy it is retrospective – looking back from when the promise fell due (Lewison LJ's summary in *Davies v Davies*, as set out by Pawlowski).

### 2. Pre-Guest approaches
1. **Expectation**: give effect to the (objectively understood) promise or its monetary equivalent.
2. **Reliance**: put the claimant in the position they would have been in had they not relied.
3. **Proportionality** – Robert Walker LJ, *Jennings v Rice* (2002): "the most essential requirement is that there must be proportionality between the expectation and the detriment". He distinguished:
   - **bargain cases** (Lord Briggs later: "almost contract" / quasi-bargain) – a clear bargain falling just short of a contract, where the expectation is the natural starting point;
   - **non-bargain cases** – vaguer expectations, where the court weighs factors: conduct, the desirability of a clean break, changes of circumstance. In either, a remedy "out of all proportion" to the detriment should be scaled down.
4. *Davies v Davies* (2016, CA): daughter worked on a Welsh farm (worth about £3m) under shifting, sometimes incompatible assurances; she left and returned several times. The trial award of £1.3m was cut to £500,000. Lewison LJ criticised the broad-brush approach, failure to identify which expectation was vindicated, failure to analyse the parents' £350,000 offer and the detriment (she had not positioned her whole life on the assurances). Principles: the essence is avoiding an unconscionable result; weigh detriment against countervailing benefits; "Proportionality lies at the heart of the doctrine… and permeates its every application"; the court should not abandon expectations, but if expectation is disproportionate to detriment the equity is satisfied more modestly; the discretion is broad but not unfettered. (Followed in *Moore v Moore* (2018).)
5. *Habberfield v Habberfield* (2019, CA): £1.2m lump sum to a daughter who worked 30 years on the dairy farm upheld; her earlier refusal of a partnership did not defeat her claim (it did not meet her expectation of control); expectation is not determinative – the question is whether the award is out of all proportion to the detriment.

### 3. *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27
**Facts.** David and Josephine Guest ran Tump Farm (Monmouthshire) as a partnership. Their son Andrew left school at 16 (1982) and worked on the farm for over 30 years on low wages, living in a cottage on the land, relying on assurances that he would inherit a substantial (unspecified) share sufficient to run a viable farming business; he had siblings who would also inherit. After a falling out the parents made wills in 2014 excluding him; he left in 2015 and sued in 2017. The trial judge awarded a lump sum of 50% of the market value of the business and 40% of the market value of the farm (requiring sale of the farm); the CA dismissed the parents' appeal. In the UKSC the equity was conceded; only the basis and form of remedy were in issue.

**Majority (Lord Briggs; Lady Arden and Lady Rose agreeing) – appeal allowed in part.**
1. The purpose of the remedy is to deal with the unconscionability of the promisor repudiating the promise; it is a mistake to see estoppel purely as compensating detriment (Waghorn, citing [10], [13]).
2. Starting point: an *assumption* (not a presumption) that the simplest way to remove the unconscionability is to hold the promisor to the promise; the promisor (or executors) may show why something less would suffice.
3. Robert Walker LJ's spectrum (*Jennings*) has "real merit".
4. Proportionality is a "cross-check" for injustice, not the core: reduce if the remedy would be "out of all proportion" to detriment. Examples: a carer promised a jewel that turns out to be a Fabergé worth millions; a promise to leave a generous inheritance for lifetime care where the promisor dies two months later.
5. Where the remedy accelerates a future promised benefit, the court should generally discount it.
6. Finally, check "in the round" that the remedy does justice and removes the unconscionability.
7. Result: Andrew was entitled to 50% of the business. For the farm, the parents could choose between (a) putting the farm on trust for their children subject to a life interest for the parents, Andrew taking a 40% remainder interest (they could stay and farm for life), or (b) an immediate payment along the lines of the trial award but discounted for accelerated receipt (a clean break), with valuation remitted to the High Court if not agreed. Lord Briggs reasoned that where there are two ways of removing unconscionability, the persons against whom the equity is asserted should in principle choose ([104], as quoted by Pawlowski).

**Minority (Lord Leggatt; Lord Stephens agreeing).**
1. Rejected expectation as the basis: detrimental reliance does not make an unenforceable promise enforceable; even treating reliance as a substitute for consideration, the other ingredients of a contract (intention to create legal relations, certainty) are usually missing.
2. But reasonable reliance on domestic promises – where demanding a written contract would be superfluous or even offensive – does give rise to an equity the court should protect.
3. The remedy should correct the reliance loss: sometimes performance of the promise is the way to do this (e.g. where loss is hard to quantify, as in *Thorner*), otherwise a payment putting the claimant where he would have been but for reliance; neither excessively generous nor excessively burdensome.
4. Andrew should receive compensation for reliance loss (Sloan and de Contreras report a figure of about £610,000 for lost earnings); no alternative of a trust/life interest.

**What Guest establishes**: (1) satisfying the equity is about removing unconscionability; (2) the starting point is the expectation, not reliance; (3) the *Jennings* spectrum remains useful; (4) proportionality is a cross-check, not a mathematical calculation; (5) discount for acceleration; (6) a final "in the round" justice check.

### 4. The menu of remedies
- **Freehold transfer**: *Pascoe v Turner* (1979); *Teasdale v Teasdale* (2023); *Spencer v Spencer* (2023).
- **Lesser proprietary right**: a lease (*Yaxley v Gotts* (2000)); an easement (*Crabb v Arun* (1976)).
- **Trust with life interest/remainder**: *Guest* option (a); *Thorner* (farm held on trust for David).
- **Personal licence**: *Howe v Gossop* (2021).
- **Money** – increasingly favoured for a clean break: *Wayling v Jones* (1993); *Jennings v Rice* (2002); *Davies* (2016); *Habberfield* (2019); *Guest* option (b). *Gillett v Holt* combined land and £100,000.
- **Alternative remedies at the defendant's election**: *Guest*.
- **No remedy**: *Sledmore v Dalby* (1996), where benefits outweighed detriment.

### 5. Third parties
Registered land: s 116 LRA 2002 – the equity is proprietary from when it arises and its priority follows ss 28–29 (notice or actual occupation). Unregistered land: doctrine of notice (*ER Ives v High* (1967)).

## Key authorities
- *Crabb v Arun DC* [1976] Ch 179: minimum equity to do justice; easement awarded.
- *Jennings v Rice* (2002, CA): proportionality; bargain/non-bargain spectrum.
- *Davies v Davies* (2016, CA): award reduced to £500,000; Lewison LJ's principles.
- *Habberfield v Habberfield* (2019, CA): £1.2m upheld.
- *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27: expectation starting point, proportionality cross-check, acceleration discount; defendants' choice of remedy; Lord Leggatt's reliance-based dissent.
- *Sledmore v Dalby* (1996): no remedy.
- *Pascoe v Turner* (1979); *Yaxley v Gotts* (2000); *Howe v Gossop* (2021): range of remedies.
- LRA 2002 s 116; *ER Ives v High* (1967).

## Debate and critique
- **Has Guest clarified the law?** Supporters see a coherent statement: equity is about unconscionable repudiation, so expectation is the natural benchmark, tempered by proportionality and practical adjustments (Sloan, "Proprietary estoppel in the Supreme Court: Banquo's ghost?" (2023) 82 CLJ 13, as summarised: the majority preserves flexibility while recognising the importance of promises; but the qualifications on the "starting point" may not reduce litigation, and proportionality as a cross-check may still produce inconsistency).
- **Dixon** ("Reading Guest and the Law of Proprietary Estoppel" [2022] Conv 337): critics' view of Guest "might kindly be described as pragmatic, and less kindly as unprincipled". Lord Briggs rejected reliance but gave little concrete guidance on reaching a remedy; "unconscionability" is nebulous, inviting unpredictable decisions and letting judges create property rights outside the strict formality rules. Dixon elsewhere asks whether estoppel is a Titian, a Howard Hodgkin or a Jackson Pollock ([2022] Conv 30).
- **Smyth and Dunbar, "Clear as mud" (2024) 30 Trusts & Trustees 229**: the majority left unconscionability undeveloped; the alternative-remedy order is convoluted, with no method for measuring proportionality or the acceleration discount, risking further litigation; later cases (e.g. *Winter v Winter* (2023); *Thandi v Saggu* (2023)) have not applied Guest strictly, and Guest has "exacerbated the unresolved issues". They also identify practical problems with the minority approach – difficulty quantifying reliance loss, intrusive scrutiny of a claimant's life, and a risk of encouraging promisors to renege.
- **Reliance theory**: Lord Leggatt's dissent aligns with McFarlane's view (2015, with Sales) that estoppel creates a liability to remedy detriment rather than a right to enforce promises; Robertson ([2008] Conv 295) supported expectation-based relief in long-term investment cases (de Contreras, "There are no winners here, only losers" (2023) 45 JSWFL 92, as summarised).
- **Lord Sales' lecture**: highlights the tension between discretion and certainty, grounding the doctrine in detrimental reliance and reciprocity; calls for clearer guidelines and limits on intervention.
- **Waghorn, "Promises in Equity and at Law" (2023) 86 MLR 1504**: analyses the majority's view that the unconscionability lies in repudiating the promise, and the significance of treating equity as enforcing (some) promises the common law would not.
- **Pawlowski** (on Guest): for practitioners the key points are fashioning a remedy appropriate in all the circumstances; limiting remedies out of all proportion to detriment; discounting for acceleration; and an in-the-round justice check.
- **Flexibility – virtue or vice?** Mee's warning against an "indulgent and confused" jurisdiction (see proprietary-estoppel-establishing) applies equally to remedies; others value flexibility in family disputes. The defendants' election in Guest is novel and can be defended as respecting the promisor's autonomy and avoiding forced sale.
- **Family consequences**: farm cases often split families; clean-break money awards may force sales; trust/life interest options avoid that but tie parties together.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm the equity is established; then identify the expectation (what exactly was promised, objectively?). Was it a quasi-bargain or a vague non-bargain case (*Jennings*)?
2. Start with expectation (*Guest*); ask whether the promisor has good reasons why less would suffice (e.g. changed circumstances, benefits already received, the promisee's own conduct).
3. Proportionality cross-check: would full enforcement be out of all proportion to the detriment (*Guest*'s Fabergé and two-month examples; *Davies*)? Balance benefits received (*Sledmore*).
4. Timing: if the promise related to inheritance and the promisor is alive, discount for acceleration, or consider a trust with a life interest/remainder (*Guest*).
5. Choose a form: freehold, lease, easement, licence, trust, or money (clean break where relationships have broken down: *Jennings*; *Davies*; *Habberfield*). Consider offering the defendant a choice (*Guest*).
6. Mention the minority approach (reliance loss) as an alternative analysis and that later cases apply Guest loosely – acknowledge uncertainty.
7. Third parties: if the land has been sold or mortgaged, apply s 116 LRA 2002 and priority rules, or notice in unregistered land.
