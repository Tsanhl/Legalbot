# Leases – essential characteristics and the lease/licence distinction

## Overview
A lease ("term of years absolute", s 1(1)(b) LPA 1925) is one of only two legal estates. It gives the tenant a proprietary right that can bind purchasers and attracts statutory protection (historically the Rent Acts; repairing duties under s 11 Landlord and Tenant Act 1985; fitness for habitation), whereas a licence is a personal permission. Landlords therefore have incentives to dress arrangements up as licences. *Street v Mountford* [1985] AC 809 (HL) settled that a lease exists where there is exclusive possession for a term certain (usually at a rent), whatever the label. This note covers the three elements, shams and pretences, shared occupation, property guardians, certainty of term and the non-proprietary *Bruton* tenancy (England and Wales).

## The law

### 1. The test – *Street v Mountford*
1. Lord Templeman: "To constitute a tenancy the occupier must be granted exclusive possession for a fixed or periodic term certain in consideration of a premium or periodical payments." Elements: (1) exclusive possession; (2) a term certain (fixed or periodic); (3) rent (premium or periodic payments) – but see below on rent.
2. Facts: Mrs Mountford occupied two rooms under a written "licence" at £37 a week "licence fee", terminable on 14 days' notice, with a clause allowing the owner to enter at any time and a declaration that it was not intended to create a Rent Act tenancy. County court: tenant; CA: licensee with exclusive possession; HL: tenant. The labels, the unexercised right of entry and the purported exclusion of the Rent Acts were not decisive.
3. Labels do not matter: "The manufacture of a five-pronged implement for manual digging results in a fork even if the manufacturer… insists that he intended to make and has made a spade." If the agreement satisfies the requirements of a tenancy, the parties cannot alter its effect by calling it a licence.
4. Exclusive possession means the occupier can exercise the rights of an owner, "in the real sense his land albeit temporarily"; a licensee "can in no sense call the land his own".
5. Exceptions where exclusive possession does not create a tenancy (no intention to create legal relations, or another legal basis for occupation): family arrangements, friendship or generosity (*Facchini v Bryson* (1952); *Cobb v Lane* (1952); *Marcroft Wagons v Smith* (1951); *Garland v Johnson* (1982)); service occupiers (*Barnes v Barratt* (1970); *Norris v Checksfield* (1991)).

### 2. Exclusive possession – indicators (the nine factors)
1. **Labels**: words such as "licence" or "licence fee" are not determinative (*Street*; *Aslan v Murphy* (1990); *Antoniades v Villiers*).
2. **Sham/pretence**: Lord Templeman in *Antoniades* preferred "pretence" – a provision is a pretence if never seriously intended, if it does not reflect the substance of the transaction, or if the parties do not regard it as one to which effect would be given. *Gisborne v Burton* (1989): a farmer let to his wife who sub-let, to avoid agricultural tenancy protection – the interposition was disregarded. Contrast *Hilton v Plustitle* (1989): a company let (occupier formed a company to take the lease) was a genuine vehicle, not a pretence.
3. **Retention of keys**: why were they kept and were they used? Emergency or meter-reading access is consistent with exclusive possession; keys for frequent services (bed-making) may negate it – *Aslan v Murphy (Nos 1 and 2)*.
4. **Services**: whether the provider can enter at will and whether services are substantial (*Street*; *Appah v Parncliffe Investments* (1964); *Markou v De Silvaesa* (1986)).
5. **Right to enter at will**: limited, notice-based rights of entry point to exclusive possession.
6. **Supervisory control**: lodgers in hostels lack exclusive possession – *Westminster CC v Clarke* [1992] 2 AC 288 (homeless hostel resident could be moved and had to obey the warden); *Oxford Overseas Student Housing Association v Mukherjee* (1989). See also *Watts v Stewart* (2016) (almshouse).
7. **Social/familial relationships** (above).
8. **Property guardians**: occupiers of empty buildings at below-market rent, often sharing, to deter squatters. Compare *Camelot Property Management v Roynon* (2017, county court – tenancy) with *Camelot Guardian Management v Khoo* [2018] EWHC 2296 (QB) (licence); see also *Southwark LBC v Ludgate House* (2020); *Global 100 v Laleva* (2021) (guardian as contractual licensee – per textbook).
9. **Service occupiers** (above).
Exclusive occupation is not the same as exclusive possession (Windeyer J, *Radaich v Smith* (1959), Australia). Gray and Gray describe exclusive possession as giving exclusory power and immunity from supervisory control.

### 3. Shared occupation and joint tenancies
1. Multiple occupiers may be separate licensees, tenants of individual rooms sharing facilities, tenants with sub-licensees, or joint tenants holding a single right to exclusive possession of the whole.
2. A joint tenancy requires first a tenancy, then the four unities – possession, interest, title, time.
3. *Antoniades v Villiers* (HL): a couple in a one-bedroom attic flat signed separate but contemporaneous "licences" splitting rent 50:50; the landlord reserved a right to share or introduce another occupier – a pretence (absurd in a one-bed flat). Joint tenancy.
4. *AG Securities v Vaughan* (decided together): four occupiers of a four-bedroom flat under separate agreements made at different times, for different periods and rents, with replacements found as people left – no unity of time, interest or title and no collective exclusive possession; licences.
5. Masking devices: separate but interdependent or identical documents can be read together; statements that there is no collective exclusive possession are labels; clauses limiting each occupier to their own share of rent may be pretence; one occupier signing late does not matter if possession is taken at the same time.
6. Consequences of joint tenancy: joint and several liability for all the rent; survivorship; parties cannot act independently (e.g. surrender requires all).

### 4. Term certain
1. The lease must start at a certain time and its maximum duration must be ascertainable at the outset.
2. *Lace v Chantler* [1944] KB 368: lease "for the duration of the war" void for uncertainty (cf. *Bishop v Taylor* (1968, Australia)).
3. *Ashburn Anstalt v Arnold* [1989] Ch 1 (CA): a departure – occupation until three months' notice on a certain event was held sufficient.
4. *Prudential Assurance v London Residuary Body* [1992] 2 AC 386 (HL): a lease of a strip of land at £30 a year "until the land is required" for road widening was uncertain; orthodoxy restored and *Ashburn* disapproved on this point; but because rent was paid yearly, a yearly periodic tenancy was implied, terminable on notice.
5. *Berrisford v Mexfield Housing Co-operative* [2011] UKSC 52: the Supreme Court criticised but affirmed the rule. A monthly occupancy agreement terminable by the co-operative only on specified events was uncertain; at common law it would have been a tenancy for life, which s 149(6) LPA 1925 converts into a 90-year lease determinable on the tenant's death (and on the contractual events). Harrison notes this transformed the understanding of fully mutual housing co-operative tenancies, previously assumed to be periodic.
6. Periodic tenancies satisfy the rule because each period is certain and either party can give notice.

### 5. Rent
1. Lord Templeman's formula mentions payment, but s 205(1)(xxvii) LPA 1925 defines term of years "whether or not at a rent", and Fox LJ in *Ashburn* confirmed a lease can exist without rent. Rent-free exclusive occupation may, however, indicate no intention to create a tenancy (Mummery LJ, *Vesely v Levy* (2007)).
2. Rent may be money or money's worth (bottles of wine – *Pitcher v Tovey* (1692)), even a peppercorn; payments for services/utilities are not rent (*Bostock v Bryant* (1990)); it must be certain or ascertainable by machinery (*Parker v Harris*; cf. *Sudbrook Trading*). Lord Goddard in *Sidney Trading v Finsbury BC* (1952): money paid as consideration for the tenancy is rent.

### 6. The *Bruton* tenancy
*Bruton v London & Quadrant Housing Trust* [1999] UKHL 26: Lambeth LBC licensed a building to a housing trust, which granted Mr Bruton a "licence" of a flat. He sued for repairs under s 11 LTA 1985. Despite *nemo dat* (the trust had no estate to give), the HL held the agreement was a tenancy because it had the *Street* hallmarks. Lord Hoffmann: "it is the fact that the agreement is a lease which creates the proprietary interest. It is putting the cart before the horse to say that whether the agreement is a lease depends upon whether it creates a proprietary interest." The result is a contractual, non-proprietary lease binding only the parties, not the council: applied in *Kay v Lambeth LBC* [2006] UKHL 10 and *Islington LBC v Green* [2005] EWCA Civ 56.

### 7. Why the distinction matters today
Whayman notes that with assured shorthold tenancies the practical gap narrowed: landlords usually need two months' notice for tenants (Housing Act 1988 s 21) and four weeks for licensees (Protection from Eviction Act 1977), but tenants gain standing in tort and statutory repair and fitness obligations (LTA 1985 ss 11–14; Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018). Proprietary status also matters for binding purchasers and registration.

## Key authorities
- *Street v Mountford* [1985] AC 809 (HL): the test; labels irrelevant.
- *AG Securities v Vaughan*; *Antoniades v Villiers* [1990] 1 AC 417 (HL): shared occupation; pretence.
- *Aslan v Murphy* [1990] 1 WLR 766: keys and services.
- *Westminster CC v Clarke* [1992] 2 AC 288: hostel; supervisory control.
- *Gisborne v Burton* [1989] QB 390; *Hilton v Plustitle* [1989] 1 WLR 149: pretence versus legitimate structuring.
- *Lace v Chantler* [1944] KB 368; *Prudential v London Residuary Body* [1992] 2 AC 386; *Berrisford v Mexfield* [2011] UKSC 52: certainty of term.
- *Ashburn Anstalt v Arnold* [1989] Ch 1: rent not essential (certainty point disapproved).
- *Bruton v London & Quadrant HT* [1999] UKHL 26; *Kay v Lambeth* [2006] UKHL 10: non-proprietary lease.
- *Camelot v Roynon* (2017); *Camelot v Khoo* [2018] EWHC 2296: property guardians.
- LPA 1925 ss 1(1)(b), 149(6), 205(1)(xxvii).

## Debate and critique
- **Substance over form / status versus contract**: Bright ("Beyond Sham and into Pretence" (1991) 11 OJLS 138) explains how *Street* and *Antoniades* moved from "sham" to the wider "pretence" doctrine, allowing courts to disregard terms inserted to avoid statutory protection, and examines devices such as company lets; in "Avoiding Tenancy Legislation: Sham and Contracting Out Revisited" (2002) 61 CLJ 146 she revisits sham and contracting-out and the inconsistent case law. Whayman ((2019) Conv 44) traces a shift from status (Street, *Bruton* as its zenith) back toward freedom of contract in *Khoo* and *Watts v Stewart*, with inconsistency (*Roynon* versus *Khoo*) traceable to conflicting statements by Lord Templeman; he proposes adjusting the test to reduce strain and incentivise better landlord practice.
- **Certainty of term**: *Prudential* and *Mexfield* were reached reluctantly; the rule can defeat the parties' bargain. Bridge ([2010] Conv 492, on the CA in *Mexfield*) discusses periodic tenancies with fetters on termination and notes Wilson LJ's dissent, which would have treated the agreement as a monthly (purely contractual, *Bruton*-type) tenancy enforceable between the parties. Williams ("Nothing Lasts Forever" (2015) 74 CLJ 592) defends the rule by placing leases in the system of estates: there are no perpetual estates; uncertain terms risk creating perpetual estates contrary to *nemo dat* and cause difficulties when a superior estate ends (escheat). Bright, "Uncertainty in Leases – Is it a Vice?" (1993) 13 LS 38.
- ***Bruton***: welcomed as protecting occupiers but criticised as confusing the lease's proprietary nature. Lower ([2010] Conv 38) frames the question of whether a lease is primarily an estate or a contract. Roberts ([2012] Conv 87) explains *Bruton* through relativity of title – a person in possession can grant estates good against all but the true owner (*Asher v Whitlock*; *Perry v Clissold*). Pawlowski ([2005] Conv 262) asks whether the decision brings clarity or confusion.
- **Property guardians**: occupiers often vulnerable; the lease/licence boundary decides repair rights and notice. Inconsistent first-instance decisions show the instability of applying *Street* to new business models.
- **Rent**: the tension between Lord Templeman's formula and s 205(1)(xxvii) remains a classic point of tension.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Exclusive possession? Strip away labels. Check keys (why held, used?), services (substantial, entry at will?), rights of entry, supervisory control, any pretence (*Antoniades* three limbs), family/friendship or service occupancy exceptions.
2. Term: fixed or periodic? Is maximum duration certain at the outset? If not (e.g. "until required"), consider a periodic tenancy implied from rent (*Prudential*) or a 90-year lease under s 149(6) for an individual (*Mexfield*).
3. Rent: present? Not essential (s 205(1)(xxvii); *Ashburn*) but its absence may show no intention to create legal relations.
4. Multiple occupiers: one tenancy or several? Four unities (*AG Securities* versus *Antoniades*).
5. Does the grantor have an estate? If not, a *Bruton* tenancy binding only the parties.
6. Then consider formalities and type (leases-creation-formalities-types) and covenants/forfeiture (leasehold-covenants-forfeiture).
7. Trap: stating exclusive possession "makes" a lease without checking term and the exceptions; or treating "licence" wording as decisive.
