# Licences

## Overview
A licence is a permission to be on, or do something on, another's land. In Vaughan CJ's classic words in *Thomas v Sorrell* (1673), a licence "properly passeth no interest, nor alters or transfers property in anything, but only makes an action lawful, without which it had been unlawful". Licences are generally personal rights, so they do not bind purchasers of the licensor's land – which is why the licence/lease boundary matters so much (see leases-essential-characteristics). English law (England and Wales) recognises four types: bare licences, licences coupled with an interest, contractual licences and estoppel licences. Key issues are revocation and whether (exceptionally) a licence can affect third parties.

## The law

### 1. Bare licences
1. A simple, gratuitous permission (e.g. a friend invited for coffee; a postwoman on the path). No contract. Created expressly, orally or by implication.
2. **Revocation**: at any time on reasonable notice: *Robson v Hallett* (1967) (police officer invited in, then told to leave; the officers had a reasonable time to leave). Then the licensee must leave within a reasonable period; no damages or specific performance. What is reasonable depends on the licence – minutes for a police inquiry, perhaps months for residential occupation. *Re Hampstead Garden Suburb Institute* (1995): nine months' notice to a school that had used land for decades was inadequate. Revocation must be clear: *Gilham v Breidenbach* (1982) – mere abuse shouted at police did not revoke their licence.
3. Scope is determined by the circumstances (Scrutton LJ: an invitation to use the staircase is not an invitation to slide down the banister).
4. Binds only the original parties; not a purchaser. Lecture scenario: a free one-day use of a garden for a party is a bare licence; it can be revoked on reasonable notice (an hour before the party might be reasonable) and does not bind a buyer of the house.

### 2. Licences coupled with an interest
1. A licence to enter land ancillary to a proprietary right, typically a profit (to take fish, timber, game): *James Jones & Sons v Earl of Tankerville* (1909) – sale of growing timber implied a licence to enter, cut and remove it.
2. It arises with the grant of the interest, is irrevocable while the interest lasts, and binds whoever is bound by the interest.
3. *Hurst v Picture Theatres* (1915): a cinema-goer ejected by mistake had a licence coupled with an interest, so ejection was an assault – controversial, as there was no proprietary interest; Megarry J in *Hounslow LBC v Twickenham Garden Developments* (1971) would "hesitate very long" before accepting a licence coupled with a non-proprietary right.
4. Bevan questions whether the category is still needed, as such cases could be analysed as implied contractual or bare licences.

### 3. Contractual licences
1. Granted for consideration under a valid contract (intention to create legal relations, offer, acceptance, capacity). Examples: car parks (*Ashby v Tolhurst* (1937)), sporting events (*Wood v Leadbitter* (1845)), hotels, halls of residence, property guardians (*Global 100 Ltd v Laleva* (2021)).
2. Not an interest in land, so s 2 LP(MP)A 1989 writing is not required.
3. Implied contracts in families: *Tanner v Tanner* (1975) – a mother who gave up a rent-controlled tenancy to move into a house bought by the children's father had an implied contractual licence to stay until the children left school; she had been rehoused, so she got £2,000 damages. *Chandler v Kerley* (1978): implied licence terminable on 12 months' notice. But *Horrocks v Forray* (1976): no contract where there was no intention to create legal relations or consideration. Bevan doubts *Tanner* would be followed today; *Southwell v Blackburn* (2014) used proprietary estoppel on similar facts.
4. *Hardwicke v Johnson* (1978): occupation of a house by a daughter-in-law, with payments made and accepted, was held a contractual licence – a useful transition to the lease/licence distinction.
5. **Remedies against the licensor**: injunction, specific performance or damages.

### 4. Revocation of contractual licences
1. **Common law**: revocable at any time, but the licensor pays damages if this breaches contract: *Wood v Leadbitter* (1845) (racegoer ejected).
2. **Equity** construes the contract and may imply terms controlling revocation: *Winter Garden Theatre (London) v Millennium Productions* (1947, HL) – a licence of a theatre with no fixed term and no revocation clause was, on construction, revocable on reasonable notice; one month was sufficient.
3. Categories from the case law:
   - no fixed duration and not intended to be perpetual → revocable on reasonable notice (*Winter Garden*); licences are rarely meant to last "until the crack of doom" (cf. *Prudential Assurance v London Residuary Body* (1992));
   - for a defined purpose or event → irrevocable until it ends (Viscount Simon in *Winter Garden*: a ticket entitles the holder, if he behaves, to stay until the end of the event); forcible ejection may be assault (*Hurst*);
   - until a particular event (*Tanner*);
   - once acted upon, irrevocable for the contract term: *Hounslow v Twickenham Garden Developments* (1971) – building contractor could not be removed mid-contract; "Equity will not assist a man to break his contract";
   - not yet acted upon – specific performance may be ordered: *Verrall v Great Yarmouth BC* (1981) (hire of a hall for a conference);
   - but not where enforcement would be impractical: *Thompson v Park* (1944) – the court "cannot specifically enforce an agreement between two people to live peaceably under the same roof";
   - where the licensee acted to their detriment, estoppel may make the licence irrevocable (*Plimmer v Wellington Corporation* (1884); *Inwards v Baker* (1965)).
4. Equitable remedies are discretionary.

### 5. Estoppel licences
A licence awarded to satisfy a proprietary estoppel equity: *Inwards v Baker* (1965) – a son encouraged to build a bungalow on his father's land was given an irrevocable licence to occupy it as long as he wished. See *Howe v Gossop* (2021) for a personal licence remedy. Under s 116 LRA 2002 the estoppel equity binds successors from when it arises (subject to registration rules), so estoppel licences may be treated differently from contractual ones.

### 6. Licensees and third parties
1. **Trespassers**: a licensee in occupation can sue trespassers (Lord Upjohn in *National Provincial Bank v Ainsworth*). *Manchester Airport plc v Dutton* (2000, CA, 2:1): a contractual licensee *not yet in occupation* could obtain possession against protesters on National Trust land; Laws LJ – a licensee may claim possession where necessary to vindicate its contractual rights of occupation; Chadwick LJ dissented (a claimant out of possession must rely on the strength of its title). Swadling criticises the majority: possession-based rights, not contractual rights, bind third parties. Lord Neuberger MR in *Mayor of London v Hall* (2010) gave obiter support to the majority's approach.
2. **Successors of the licensor**:
   - Orthodoxy: contractual licences are personal and do not bind successors – *King v David Allen & Sons Billposting* (1916, HL); *Clore v Theatrical Properties* (1936). Supported by s 1 and s 4(1) LPA 1925 (no new legal interests).
   - The Denning era: *Errington v Errington and Woods* (1952) (daughter-in-law paying mortgage instalments had a licence binding the widow); *Binions v Evans* (1972) (majority found an equitable life interest; Lord Denning a licence or constructive trust where land was bought "subject to" her right at a reduced price); *DHN Food Distributors v Tower Hamlets* (1976) (constructive trust from an irrevocable licence); applied reluctantly in *Re Sharpe* (1980).
   - Return to orthodoxy: *Ashburn Anstalt v Arnold* (1989, CA), Fox LJ (obiter, as the arrangement was a lease) – a contractual licence is not a property interest; *Errington* was per incuriam on this point; no automatic constructive trust. *Camden LBC v Shortlife Community Housing* (1993), Millett J: *Ashburn* "finally repudiated the heretical view".
   - Lord Wilberforce in *Ainsworth*: the availability of specific performance or injunction does not make a right proprietary – what matters is the nature of the right, not the remedy.
3. **Constructive trust** (personal, binding only that successor): only if the purchaser's conscience is affected by undertaking a "new obligation" (Sir Christopher Slade, *Lloyd v Dugdale* (2001)); a reduced price may evidence it (*Binions*); a mere "subject to" clause does not (*Ashburn*; *Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013)); *Lyus v Prowsa* (1982) is very unusual; *Groveholt v Hughes* (2012) (no new obligation).
4. **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999**: if the purchaser contractually promises the licensor to honour the licence in terms that purport to benefit the licensee (and the s 2 LP(MP)A 1989 requirements are met, per the textbook), the licensee may enforce it directly.
5. **Restrictions**: a licence can be protected indirectly by a restriction requiring the licensee's consent to sale: *Hinton v Wotherspoon* (2022) – the licence remains personal.
6. **Relief from forfeiture**: *Manchester Ship Canal Co v Vauxhall Motors* (2019, UKSC) – relief may be granted for a possessory (non-proprietary) contractual licence; a perpetual licence of pipe outfalls terminated for non-payment of £50 a year was reinstated on payment.

## Key authorities
- *Thomas v Sorrell* (1673): definition.
- *Robson v Hallett* (1967); *Re Hampstead Garden Suburb Institute* (1995); *Gilham v Breidenbach* (1982): bare licences.
- *James Jones v Earl of Tankerville* (1909); *Hurst v Picture Theatres* (1915): licences coupled with an interest.
- *Wood v Leadbitter* (1845); *Winter Garden Theatre v Millennium* (1947); *Hounslow v Twickenham* (1971); *Verrall v Great Yarmouth* (1981); *Thompson v Park* (1944): revocation.
- *Tanner v Tanner* (1975); *Chandler v Kerley* (1978); *Horrocks v Forray* (1976): implied contractual licences.
- *King v David Allen* (1916); *Errington* (1952); *Binions v Evans* (1972); *DHN* (1976); *Ashburn Anstalt v Arnold* (1989): third parties.
- *Lloyd v Dugdale* (2001); *Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013): constructive trust.
- *Manchester Airport v Dutton* (2000); *Manchester Ship Canal v Vauxhall* (2019).
- *Inwards v Baker* (1965); LRA 2002 s 116: estoppel licences.

## Debate and critique
- **Personal or proprietary?** The orthodox position (*King*, *Ashburn*) protects purchasers and the closed list of property rights (s 4 LPA 1925); Briggs argues licences could become proprietary only by "conjuring out of existence" s 4. McFarlane criticises Lord Denning's reasoning: availability of specific performance is at most a necessary, not sufficient, condition of proprietary status. Watt and Maudsley offered support for wider protection (Maudsley's argument for exclusive-possession licences has lost force since *Street v Mountford* widened the lease).
- **Outstanding arguments** (textbook): s 116 LRA 2002 (not Parliament's intention to cover contractual licences); Art 8 ECHR (rarely engaged against private parties and likely to yield to property rights – Lord Neuberger in *Manchester CC v Pinnock* (2010)); Maudsley's argument – none convincing.
- **Inconsistency with estoppel licences**: McFarlane notes that s 116 lets an estoppel equity bind third parties before a licence is awarded, while contractual licences cannot – why? Hill warns against a system where third parties' position depends on a line courts cannot draw.
- ***Dutton***: seen by critics (Swadling; Chadwick LJ) as quietly giving licences proprietary effect; others welcome stronger licensee protection.
- **Implied contracts in families** (*Tanner*): arguably invented contracts to reach a fair result; estoppel is now the more natural tool.
- **Protection gap**: residential licensees lack the statutory security of tenants, which drives landlords to label arrangements as licences (see leases-essential-characteristics).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is it really a licence? Check *Street v Mountford* – exclusive possession for a term at a rent makes it a lease regardless of labels.
2. Classify the licence: gratuitous (bare), for consideration (contractual – look for implied contracts and intention to create legal relations), coupled with a profit, or arising from estoppel.
3. Revocation: bare – reasonable notice. Contractual – construe the contract: fixed event/purpose? acted upon? remedy (damages, injunction, specific performance – *Verrall*; *Thompson v Park*)? Was revocation clear?
4. Third parties: a purchaser is generally not bound (*King*; *Ashburn*). Look for a "new obligation" (reduced price, express undertaking) for a constructive trust (*Lloyd v Dugdale*); a 1999 Act promise; a restriction; or an estoppel equity (s 116, notice/actual occupation).
5. Trespassers: can the licensee sue (*Dutton*)?
6. Consider relief from forfeiture for possessory licences (*Vauxhall*).
