# Proprietary estoppel – establishing the equity

## Overview
Proprietary estoppel (England and Wales) allows a person to acquire rights in another's land informally – without a deed or written contract – where the landowner's assurance has been reasonably relied on to the claimant's detriment and it would be unconscionable for the landowner to go back on it. Moriarty describes it as "the informal creation of interests in land whenever a person has acted detrimentally in reliance upon an oral assurance that he has such an interest". It can be used as a shield (defence to possession) or a sword (positive claim). A claim has two stages: (1) establishing an "equity" (this note) and (2) satisfying the equity by choosing a remedy (see proprietary-estoppel-remedy-guest). Family farm inheritance cases dominate the modern case law.

## The law

### 1. Relationship with formalities
1. Normally, contracts for the sale or disposition of an interest in land must be in writing, signed by all parties and contain all terms (**s 2 LP(MP)A 1989**), and conveyances must be by deed (**s 52 LPA 1925**, subject to s 54(2)). Implied, resulting and constructive trusts are excepted from s 2 (**s 2(5)**). Estoppel operates outside these requirements.
2. Concern that estoppel might circumvent s 2: *Yaxley v Gotts* (2000) – estoppel should not validate an otherwise unenforceable agreement. Lord Scott, obiter, in *Yeoman's Row Management v Cobbe* (2008): estoppel "cannot be prayed in aid in order to render enforceable an agreement that statute has declared to be void… Equity can surely not contradict the statute."
3. Lord Neuberger in *Thorner v Major* (2009): s 2 has no impact on "a straightforward estoppel claim without any contractual connection" (domestic context); *Cobbe* was explicable because the parties deliberately left matters non-binding.
4. *Herbert v Doyle* (2010, CA – a constructive trust case): Arden LJ suggested *Cobbe* bars estoppel where parties intended to make a later formal agreement.
5. *Howe v Gossop* (2021): Snowden J – s 2 targets contract formation; estoppel remedies unconscionability; doubtful s 2 affects estoppel at all, and at most it bars relief that gives effect to the void contract. Estoppel raised as a defence to possession after an oral handshake deal and works on land succeeded; no extra "exceptional circumstances" requirement. See also *Ghazaani v Rowshan* (2015). The position is not finally settled; *Cobbe* still bars claims in ongoing commercial negotiations.
6. *Horsford v Horsford* (2020): a later valid written agreement settling the issues displaces an estoppel based on earlier assurances.

### 2. The elements (Stage 1)
Laid down by Oliver J in *Taylors Fashions v Liverpool Victoria Trustees* (1982), confirmed in *Gillett v Holt* (2001) and *Thorner v Major* (2009). The older "five probanda" (*Ramsden v Dyson*; *Willmott v Barber*) are no longer the test. The elements are applied holistically, not mechanically.

**Element 1 – Assurance**
1. A representation or assurance by the landowner that the claimant has or will have a proprietary right in identified property the landowner owns or will own. The property need not be precisely defined ("this farm will be yours one day").
2. It may be express ("this house is yours") or passive – standing by while the claimant acts on a mistaken belief.
3. **Clarity**: it need only be "clear enough" in context: *Thorner v Major* (2009, HL) – David worked unpaid for about 30 years on his father's cousin Peter's farm; there were no express promises, only hints and oblique remarks over years. The CA required clear and unequivocal assurances; the HL reversed: objectively, the remarks were a commitment rather than a statement of revocable present intention. Peter's estate held the farm on trust for David.
4. Context-sensitive examples: *Habberfield v Habberfield* (2019) ("they are your cows"; work would "pay off"); *Thompson v Thompson* (2018) (no single occasion but long-standing understood family promises); *Spencer v Spencer* (2023) (repeated general remarks during disputes – "it will all be yours one day" – sufficed despite unreliable memory of timing).
5. Not enough: generalised promises of future financial security (*Mate v Mate* (2023)); promises of a future job or role (*Gladstone v White* (2023) – being "in control" of a mansion).
6. **Commercial negotiations**: courts are slow to find assurances. *Cobbe* (2008, HL): oral "gentleman's agreement" to sell flats for £12m once Mr Cobbe obtained planning permission at his own cost; he spent over £100,000; the vendor resiled. No estoppel: both knew the deal was not binding and that further steps were needed; the only expectation was of further negotiation. Mr Cobbe could succeed only in a personal claim for his losses.
7. **Revocability**: an assurance need not be irrevocable, but must be more than a statement of present revocable intention – "tantamount to a promise": *Gillett v Holt* (2001, CA) – farmhand of 40 years told "all this will be yours"; wills naming him were revocable; claim succeeded (freehold of farmhouse, about 42 hectares and £100,000). Affirmed in *Spencer*.

**Element 2 – Reliance**
1. The claimant must reasonably rely on the assurance, judged objectively: *James v James* (2018) – reliance on a remark that the son "would be farming the land one day" was unreasonable.
2. **Presumption of reliance** – *Greasley v Cooke* (1980): presumed where a clear assurance was made, intended to influence the claimant, and would influence a reasonable person; the burden shifts to the landowner.
3. The assurance need not be the sole inducement: *Wayling v Jones* (1993) – a same-sex partner who worked in his partner's hotels for "pocket money" relied on promises of a hotel even if love also motivated him; the estate could not disprove reliance; he received the value of the hotel (over £72,000).
4. Harder in close relationships: *Dobson v Griffey* (2018) – work on the farm was to set up a family home, not in expectation of property rights.

**Element 3 – Detriment**
1. Lord Briggs in *Guest v Guest* (2022): "Without reliant detriment there is simply no equity at all." It must be substantial, more than minimal, and may be detriment that would arise if the claim were refused.
2. Forms: improving the land (*Dillwyn v Llewellyn* (1862); *Inwards v Baker* (1965)); financial disadvantage – giving up other careers or education, low wages or pocket money (*Gillett*; *Davies v Davies* (2016); *Habberfield*; *Wayling*); personal, non-financial sacrifices – caring, housework, gardening (*Jennings v Rice* (2002); *Campbell v Griffin* (2001); *Greasley v Cooke*); decades of farm work on low wages (*Guest*).
3. Benefits received do not necessarily defeat detriment: *Gillett* (housing on the farm); *Spencer* (rent-free housing, expenses, partnership assets and a pension of over £2.1 million, but he had positioned his whole life around the farm).

**Element 4 – Unconscionability**
1. Lord Briggs in *Guest*: would an objective bystander find it unconscionable, in all the circumstances, for the landowner to resile from the assurance relied on to the claimant's detriment?
2. Its place: Oliver J (*Taylors Fashions*) placed it at the heart of the doctrine; Robert Walker LJ in *Jennings v Rice* said it "permeates all the elements" and calls for a broad inquiry. In practice it is an overarching component rather than a separate hurdle.
3. Factors:
   - Benefit/detriment balancing: *Sledmore v Dalby* (1996) – son-in-law who repaired the house but had 18 years rent-free got nothing; see also *Southwell v Blackburn* (2014); *Spencer*.
   - Landowner's conduct, assessed objectively (*Gee v Gee* (2018), "difficult behaviour").
   - Claimant's own misconduct: *Murphy v Rayner* (2011) – a carer's dishonesty (stealing documents, obtaining money) defeated her claim.

### 3. When does the equity arise and whom does it bind?
1. The equity arises when the claimant relies to their detriment, not when a court rules.
2. **Registered land – s 116 LRA 2002**: "for the avoidance of doubt" an equity by estoppel "has effect from the time the equity arises as an interest capable of binding successors in title (subject to the rules about the effect of dispositions on priority)". Priority follows ss 28–29: protect by notice, or rely on actual occupation under Sch 3 para 2 (see overriding-interests-actual-occupation).
3. **Unregistered land**: the doctrine of notice applies (*ER Ives Investment v High* (1967)); estoppel rights are not registrable land charges.

## Key authorities
- *Taylors Fashions v Liverpool Victoria Trustees* (1982): modern elements.
- *Thorner v Major* [2009] (HL): "clear enough" assurance; s 2 irrelevant without contractual connection.
- *Yeoman's Row v Cobbe* [2008] (HL): no estoppel in knowingly non-binding commercial negotiations; Lord Scott on s 2.
- *Gillett v Holt* [2001] (CA): revocable wills; "tantamount to a promise"; detriment despite benefits.
- *Greasley v Cooke* (1980): presumption of reliance.
- *Wayling v Jones* (1993): reliance need not be sole inducement.
- *Sledmore v Dalby* (1996): benefits outweighing detriment; no remedy.
- *Murphy v Rayner* (2011): claimant's dishonesty.
- *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27: detriment essential; objective bystander unconscionability test.
- *Spencer v Spencer* (2023); *Habberfield* (2019); *Thompson* (2018); *Mate v Mate* (2023); *Gladstone v White* (2023); *James v James* (2018); *Dobson v Griffey* (2018).
- *Howe v Gossop* (2021); *Herbert v Doyle* (2010); *Yaxley v Gotts* (2000).
- LP(MP)A 1989 s 2, s 2(5); LPA 1925 s 52; LRA 2002 s 116.

## Debate and critique
- **Flexibility versus certainty**: is estoppel too flexible or uncertain, or is uncertainty a virtue? Lord Sales (Oxford Property Conference lecture) stresses the tension: the doctrine's discretion makes appellate guidance difficult and allows different outcomes without legal error, yet estoppel is needed to soften formality rules in family settings where formality is absent; he grounds it in detrimental reliance and reciprocity and calls for clearer limits.
- **Dixon, "Confining and Defining Proprietary Estoppel: The Role of Unconscionability" (2010) 30 LS 408**: estoppel can be justified and confined by a precise concept of unconscionability. It is unconscionable to deny a "rights assurance" only when it was accompanied by an express or implied "formality assurance" (going back on an assurance about formality), plus detrimental reliance. That explains why "subject to contract", ordinary sale and many inheritance discussions fail, removes the apparent conflict with s 2 LP(MP)A 1989, and makes estoppel a principled way to create property rights "through the front door" rather than an unfettered discretion. Unconscionability used later at the remedy stage is merely descriptive.
- **Mee, "Proprietary Estoppel and Inheritance: Enough is Enough?" [2013] Conv 280**: "It is not in the public interest for the legal system to tolerate an indulgent and confused proprietary estoppel jurisdiction"; he hopes for a more restrained, analytical approach (prompted by the liberal inheritance cases after *Thorner*).
- **Commercial versus domestic**: *Cobbe* and *Thorner* suggest different treatment; Dixon explains the difference by the difficulty of proving a formality assurance in commercial settings rather than by a special rule.
- **Third parties**: McFarlane ((2003) 62 CLJ 661) analyses s 116 – the traditional two-stage view treated the equity as "inchoate" until a court order (e.g. *United Bank of Kuwait v Sahib*), whereas he argues for a model in which rights arise once the elements are met; Bevan's textbook says his "new model" does not alter s 116's obvious intention that the equity is proprietary from the outset. Palser ((2023) 29 Trusts & Trustees 208) examines estoppel and "strangers" – whether a promise to B that C will receive property can be enforced for C.
- **Equality and family**: farm cases raise questions about intergenerational fairness and siblings' expectations (see *Guest*).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify who made what statement, about which property, and when. Is it "clear enough" in context (*Thorner*)? Promise of ownership, or of a job/financial security (*Gladstone*; *Mate*)? Commercial "subject to contract" setting (*Cobbe*)? Revocable will – still a promise (*Gillett*)?
2. Reliance: was reliance reasonable? Use the *Greasley* presumption. Mixed motives (love, family) – *Wayling* versus *Dobson*.
3. Detriment: list concrete sacrifices (work at low pay, lost careers, improvements, care). Weigh against benefits (housing, wages) – *Sledmore*; *Spencer*.
4. Unconscionability: objective bystander (*Guest*); conduct of both parties (*Murphy v Rayner*).
5. Formalities: if there was an oral contract for land, address s 2 and the *Cobbe*/*Thorner*/*Howe* debate; note s 2(5) for constructive trusts.
6. Third parties: registered land – s 116; was a notice entered? Is the claimant in actual occupation (Sch 3 para 2)? Unregistered – doctrine of notice.
7. Then move to remedy (proprietary-estoppel-remedy-guest).
8. Trap: do not treat unconscionability as a box to tick in isolation; discuss it across the elements.
