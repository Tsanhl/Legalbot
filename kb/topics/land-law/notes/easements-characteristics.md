# Easements – nature and essential characteristics

## Overview
An easement is a proprietary right by which one landowner enjoys a limited use of another's (usually neighbouring) land – a right of way, light, drainage, support, parking. Because easements bind successors of the burdened land, the law limits which rights qualify: too wide a category overburdens servient land; too narrow undermines the value of dominant land. The test comes from *Re Ellenborough Park* [1956] Ch 131 (CA), refined by later cases and the Supreme Court in *Regency Villas v Diamond Resorts* [2018] UKSC 57, which recognised purely recreational easements. The "ouster" principle (parking and storage) is a classic battleground. Law of England and Wales.

## The law

### 1. Nature
1. Two plots: the **dominant tenement** (benefited) and the **servient tenement** (burdened).
2. Proprietary – can bind purchasers of the servient land and be enforced by purchasers of the dominant land (unlike licences).
3. Created expressly, by implication, by prescription, by statute or by estoppel; legal or equitable (see easements-creation).
4. Positive (doing something on the servient land, e.g. parking) or negative (e.g. receiving light or air).
5. Distinguish profits à prendre (right to take something from the land – not covered on this course).
6. Analytical framework (lecture): (1) is the right capable of being an easement? (2) has an easement actually been created, and how? (3) is it legal or equitable, and does it bind third parties?

### 2. The *Re Ellenborough Park* characteristics (Evershed MR)
Facts: houses adjoining a park in Weston-super-Mare had rights to use the park; when the War Office requisitioned it, the question was whether these were easements (sharing compensation) or personal licences. Held: easements. Four characteristics:
1. There must be a dominant and a servient tenement.
2. Dominant and servient owners must be different persons.
3. The easement must "accommodate" the dominant tenement.
4. The right must be capable of forming the subject matter of a grant.
These show only that a right is *capable* of being an easement; creation is a separate question.

**Requirement 1 – dominant and servient tenement**: two parcels, both identifiable when the easement is created (*London & Blenheim Estates v Ladbroke Retail Parks* (1994)). No easements "in gross".

**Requirement 2 – separate ownership or occupation**: one cannot have an easement over one's own land (*Roe v Siddons* (1889)), but a tenant may have one over the landlord's land (*Wright v Macadam* (1949)).

**Requirement 3 – accommodation**: the right must benefit the land, be "reasonably necessary for the better enjoyment of that tenement", not merely a personal advantage (Evershed MR). Glosses:
1. *Normal enjoyment*: increased value alone is insufficient; it must relate to the normal use of the land. Lord Briggs in *Regency Villas*: free access to the Oval on test match days for a Kensington house might add value but has nothing to do with its use as a home.
2. *Proximity*: a sufficient geographical nexus (*Bailey v Stephens* (1862)), though not necessarily adjoining.
3. *More than personal advantage*: *Hill v Tupper* (1863) – an exclusive right granted to a lessee to put pleasure boats on a canal was a personal licence (Pollock CB: he would benefit even without any estate). Contrast *Moody v Steggles* (1879) (pub sign on neighbouring wall – benefits the land as used) and *P&S Platt v Crouch* (2003) (mooring rights benefiting a hotel).
4. *Recreation*: Theobald – an easement "must be a right of utility and benefit and not one of mere recreation and amusement"; *Mounsey v Ismay* (1865) (annual horse races – no). But *Re Ellenborough Park* accepted a pleasure garden connected with domestic enjoyment.

**Regency Villas Title Ltd v Diamond Resorts (Europe) Ltd [2018] UKSC 57**: a 1981 transfer gave timeshare owners at Broome Park (Kent) rights to use leisure facilities (outdoor and indoor pools, squash, an 18-hole golf course, croquet, gym, sauna etc.); the facilities fell into disrepair. High Court: easements; CA: facility-by-facility analysis, no rights in a new indoor pool; UKSC (4:1, Lord Carnwath dissenting): appeal dismissed and trial order restored. Lord Briggs: (1) the parties plainly intended a property right, not a personal one; (2) the grant was "a single comprehensive right to use a complex of facilities", including replacements; (3) no implied obligation on timeshare owners to contribute to costs. Recreational and sporting rights were "of service, utility and benefit" to the timeshare land as the pleasure ground had been in *Ellenborough Park*; this was "breaking new ground" with rights of recreation "pure and simple". Lord Briggs: recreational and sporting activity is "so clearly a beneficial part of modern life that the common law should support structures which promote and encourage it". Lord Carnwath: the rights depended on the servient owner's active management; nothing in some 70 authorities supported them; it was "a wholly new form of property right", akin to permanent country club membership.

**Requirement 4 – capable of forming the subject matter of a grant**:
1. *Capable grantor* (holds an estate that becomes the servient tenement) and *capable grantee*.
2. *Sufficient certainty*: rights must be well defined – *William Aldred's Case* (1610); *Browne v Flower* (1911) – the better defined the right, the more likely it can be an easement.
3. *No positive burden on the servient owner* (e.g. no duty to repair). Apparent departures: *Regency Villas* (no positive obligation in the grant itself, so the rule was not breached – is this convincing?); *Liverpool CC v Irwin* (1977) (landlord's implied obligation to repair common parts of a tower block did not prevent easements).
4. *Within recognised categories*: more likely if analogous to accepted easements.
5. *No ouster*: the right must not give possession or exclusive control or prevent the servient owner's reasonable use. Evershed MR: an easement must not "amount to rights of occupation or … substantially deprive the owners of proprietorship or legal possession".
   - Storage: *Wright v Macadam* (coal in a shed – easement); *Grigsby v Melville* (1972) (unlimited storage in a cellar reachable only via the dominant land – exclusive possession, no easement); *Copeland v Greenhalf* (1952) (wheelwright parking unlimited vehicles on a strip – effectively joint possession, no easement).
   - Parking: *Hair v Gillman* (2000) (parking anywhere on a forecourt for two or three cars – easement).
   - **Test**: the "reasonable use" test from *London & Blenheim Estates v Ladbroke* (1992), confirmed in *Batchelor v Marlow* (2003) – does the right leave the servient owner without any reasonable use of the land? In *Batchelor*, a prescriptive right to park six cars 8.30am–6pm Monday to Friday left no reasonable use and made ownership "illusory" (Tuckey LJ). Lord Scott in *Moncrieff v Jamieson* (2007, HL, Scottish appeal) rejected that test in favour of whether the servient owner retains possession and, subject to reasonable exercise of the right, control – an approach more favourable to claimants; Lord Neuberger saw "considerable force" in it, but it was obiter. *Virdi v Chana* (2008) and *Kettel v Bloomfold* (2012) treated *Batchelor* as binding but found that a car filling a space still left reasonable use (choice of surface and planting; use of subsoil and airspace).

### 3. Effect on third parties (summary; detail in easements-creation)
Benefit passes with the dominant land (s 62 LPA 1925). Burden: unregistered – legal easements bind all; equitable post-1925 easements must be registered as Class D(iii) land charges. Registered – express legal easements must be registered (s 27 LRA 2002); implied legal easements may override under Sch 3 para 3; equitable easements need a notice.

## Key authorities
- *Re Ellenborough Park* [1956] Ch 131 (CA): four characteristics.
- *Hill v Tupper* (1863); *Moody v Steggles* (1879); *P&S Platt v Crouch* (2003): accommodation versus personal advantage.
- *Regency Villas v Diamond Resorts* [2018] UKSC 57: recreational easements; single composite right; no positive obligation in grant.
- *Mounsey v Ismay* (1865): recreation (old approach).
- *Wright v Macadam* (1949); *Grigsby v Melville* (1972); *Copeland v Greenhalf* (1952): storage and ouster.
- *London & Blenheim Estates v Ladbroke* (1992); *Batchelor v Marlow* (2003); *Moncrieff v Jamieson* (2007); *Virdi v Chana* (2008); *Kettel v Bloomfold* (2012): parking.
- *Liverpool CC v Irwin* (1977): positive obligations.
- *William Aldred's Case* (1610); *Browne v Flower* (1911): certainty.

## Debate and critique
- **Opening Pandora's box?** Bevan ([2019] Conv 55) sees *Regency Villas* as significant and controversial: it dilutes traditional constraints (notably the rule against positive obligations) and accepts recreation "pure and simple". He identifies three "brakes": (1) accommodation still requires a close factual analysis of the grant; (2) recreational easements will almost always be express (prescription would usually fail for permission; necessity, s 62 and *Wheeldon* are improbable routes, though s 62 remains a risk with poor drafting); (3) the Law Commission's proposed jurisdiction to discharge easements (extending s 84 LPA 1925) would police overly burdensome easements. Open questions: will the approach extend beyond timeshares? Bevan also queries how far changing social attitudes (Gray and Gray's "more hedonistic (or even a more health conscious) age") should shape property rights.
- **Pratt** ([2017] Conv 312) commented on the Court of Appeal decision, which took a more cautious, facility-by-facility approach.
- **Ouster**: Hill-Smith ([2007] Conv 223) argues the principle is applied inconsistently and that *Batchelor* applied the *London & Blenheim* test too strictly, restricting parking easements. Xu ([2012] Conv 291) suggests the ouster principle may be "out" but the problems may worsen. Luther ("Easements and Exclusive Possession" (1996) 16 LS 51) on the reading list. The courts' inventiveness in *Virdi* and *Kettel* shows strain in the test.
- **Law Commission Report No 327, *Making Land Law Work: Easements, Covenants and Profits à Prendre* (2011)**: recommends statutory abolition of the ouster principle (among other reforms – see easements-creation); not implemented.
- **Positive obligations**: *Regency Villas* and *Irwin* show that the "no positive burden" rule bends; critics ask whether this blurs easements and covenants.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the dominant and servient land (both identifiable; separate ownership/occupation – tenants count).
2. Accommodation: does the right relate to the normal use of the dominant land (home, business as carried on there)? Proximity? Personal advantage (*Hill v Tupper*) or land-linked (*Moody v Steggles*)? Recreational rights – after *Regency Villas*, capable, but analyse the grant.
3. Capable of grant: certain? Requires the servient owner to spend money or act? Within a recognised category?
4. Ouster: does the right exclude the servient owner (storage of unlimited goods; parking that fills the whole area at all useful times)? Apply *Batchelor* (binding) and note *Moncrieff* and the lenient applications in *Virdi*/*Kettel*.
5. If capable, move to creation (express, necessity, common intention, *Wheeldon v Burrows*, s 62) and third-party effect.
6. Trap: stopping after *Ellenborough Park* – capability is only step one.
