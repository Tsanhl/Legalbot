# Adverse possession of registered land – LRA 2002 Schedule 6, human rights and criminalisation

## Overview
The Land Registration Act 2002 (in force 13 October 2003) transformed adverse possession of registered land in England and Wales. Time no longer extinguishes a registered owner's title. Instead, after 10 years' adverse possession the squatter may apply to be registered; the owner is notified and can block the application unless one of three narrow exceptions applies, and must then evict within two years. The scheme reflects the Act's principle that title derives from registration, not possession. This note covers the Sch 6 procedure, transitional rules, human rights challenges (*Pye v UK*), and criminalisation of residential squatting (LASPO 2012 s 144; *Best*).

## The law

### 1. Which regime applies?
1. **Unregistered land**: Limitation Act 1980 – 12 years extinguishes title (see adverse-possession-elements).
2. **Registered land, 12 years completed before 13 October 2003**: transitional provisions – Sch 12 para 18 LRA 2002. The squatter is entitled to be registered if the land was registered at that date and 12 years' adverse possession had accrued before it. Under s 75(1) LRA 1925 the registered proprietor had held on trust for the squatter – a device the Law Commission (1998) criticised as unnecessary complexity and a windfall; Sch 12 para 18(1) gives the beneficiary-squatter the right to be registered.
3. **Registered land otherwise**: Sch 6 LRA 2002. No period of adverse possession of itself extinguishes the registered title.

### 2. Schedule 6 procedure
**Stage 1 – application (para 1)**: after 10 years' adverse possession ending on the date of application, the squatter applies (form ST1). Restrictions (paras 1(3), 8, 12): e.g. the proprietor is an enemy or detained in enemy territory, suffers a disability, the squatter is a defendant in possession proceedings, or certain trust situations. Errors on the form: *Hopkins v Beacon* [2011] EWHC 2899 – an express statement invoking para 5 sufficed even if the box was not ticked. Reinstatement of a rejected application: *Franks v Chief Land Registrar* [2011] EWCA Civ 772.

**Stage 2 – notice (para 2)**: the registrar notifies the proprietor, registered chargees, any superior registered estate owner (for leases) and other persons as rules provide (others may be notified under r 17 Land Registration Rules 2003, with 15 business days and fewer options). There is no duty to check receipt. Recipients have **65 business days** to respond. Options: consent; **counter-notice** on form NAP requiring the application to be dealt with under para 5 ("he can't have my land"); **objection** under s 73 LRA 2002 ("he hasn't met the conditions" – no 10 years, no possession, permission); or both. Silence counts as consent. Late counter-notice is ineffective (*Fontaine v Bortolussi* (2010) – arrived at 2:30pm against a midday deadline). Objecting only is risky: if the objection fails the squatter is registered. If an owner fails to respond but the squatter did not in fact qualify, rectification is possible (*Baxter v Mannion* [2011] EWCA Civ 120).

**Stage 3 – the para 5 conditions**: where counter-notice is served, the application fails unless the squatter shows one of:
1. **Estoppel (para 5(2))**: it would be unconscionable because of an equity by estoppel for the proprietor to dispossess the applicant, and the applicant ought in the circumstances to be registered (e.g. squatter built on the land believing it theirs with the owner's knowing acquiescence – Land Registry Practice Guide);
2. **Some other reason (para 5(3))**: the applicant is for some other reason entitled to be registered (e.g. entitled under the deceased owner's will or intestacy, or bought and paid for the land but the legal estate was never transferred – Law Com 271 para 14.43);
3. **Reasonable mistake as to boundary (para 5(4))**: (a) the land adjoins land of the applicant; (b) the exact boundary has not been determined; (c) for at least 10 years ending on the application date the applicant (or predecessor) reasonably believed the land belonged to them; and (d) the land was registered for over a year before the application (so an owner whose land has just been registered has time to evict). Example: walls or fences on an estate in the wrong place (Law Com 271 para 14.46). *Zarb v Parry* [2011] EWCA Civ 1306 – reasonable belief where the claimants were unaware of predecessors' disputes and a survey showed the strip as theirs; *IAM Group plc v Chowdrey* [2012] EWCA Civ 505 – it is the applicant's own belief, not that of an agent or solicitor, that counts.

**Stage 4 – two years to evict**: if the application is rejected, the owner has two years to evict (a mere letter is likely insufficient – cf. *Mount Carmel*). During that period further applications are barred if the squatter is a defendant in possession proceedings, subject to a judgment for possession, or evicted.

**Stage 5 – second application (para 6)**: if the squatter remains in adverse possession for two more years from rejection, they may reapply and are entitled to be registered; notified parties (15 business days under r 17) may object only on the ground that the further two years' possession is not established.

So a squatter can be registered: (1) where there is no response to notice; (2) where a para 5 condition is met; or (3) after the two-year period if the owner does nothing.

### 3. Defences and interplay
1. **s 98 LRA 2002 – defences**: a squatter has a defence to a possession action if, the day before the action was brought, they were entitled to apply under Sch 6 para 1 *and* the para 5(4) boundary condition would have been satisfied (s 98(1)), or were entitled to make a further application under para 6 (s 98(3)). A possession judgment against someone entitled to apply under para 1 ceases to be enforceable after two years (s 98(2)); likewise if, two years after judgment, the person is entitled to apply under para 6 (s 98(4)). Where a court finds a s 98 defence made out, it must order the registrar to register the squatter (s 98(5)). The s 98 defences are additional to any other defences.
2. A squatter in actual occupation with a qualifying proprietary interest may assert an overriding interest under Sch 3 para 2 against a purchaser, subject to the para 2(b)/(c) exceptions (worksheet self-study; see overriding-interests-actual-occupation). Registration under Sch 6 is not automatic, so a squatter who has not applied faces objections from the former owner or a purchaser's lack of obvious occupation.
3. Rectification if a squatter was wrongly registered (*Baxter v Mannion*; see registered-land-lra-2002).

### 4. Human rights
1. Relevant rights: Art 1 Protocol 1 ECHR (peaceful enjoyment of possessions), Art 6 (fair hearing – now addressed by Sch 6's notice requirements), and Art 8 (home – sometimes invoked by squatters).
2. *J A Pye (Oxford) Ltd v Graham* [2002] UKHL 30: the Grahams farmed Pye's development land after a grazing agreement expired, enclosed by hedges with the only gate key; title passed under the LRA 1925/LA 1980 regime. Pye sought about £10 million compensation from the UK.
3. *Beaulane Properties v Palmer* [2005] EWHC 817 (Ch): Nicholas Strauss QC held the pre-2002 registered-land regime involved a deprivation of possessions under A1P1 and was incompatible, reading the legislation down (Dixon [2005] Conv 345 called it potentially one of the most consequential modern property decisions).
4. *J A Pye (Oxford) Ltd v UK* [2005] 3 EGLR 1 (Chamber, 4:3): violation of A1P1 under the LRA 1925 regime.
5. *J A Pye (Oxford) Ltd v UK* [2007] ECHR 700 (Grand Chamber, 10:7): no violation. A1P1 engaged, but limitation rules pursued a legitimate aim and struck a fair balance within the margin of appreciation: "Even where title to real property is registered, it must be open to the legislature to attach more weight to lengthy, unchallenged possession than to the formal fact of registration." The scale of loss and windfall were irrelevant – limitation periods must apply regardless. Five dissentients said that in registered land title depends on registration and the 1925 Act gave insufficient protection; two said the rules served no legitimate aim and were disproportionate.
6. Jones argues it is implicit that the LRA 2002, with greater safeguards, is compliant. *Ofulue v Bossert* [2008] 3 WLR 1253 (CA) followed the Grand Chamber: no A1P1 violation. Bevan: adverse possession now appears human rights-compliant; landowners' best protection is registration under Sch 6.
7. The worksheet notes that in unregistered land there can be no human rights breach claim (the question is posed for reflection).

### 5. Criminalisation of residential squatting
1. Offences (textbook): Criminal Law Act 1977 s 6 (violence to secure entry, with defences for displaced residential occupiers/protected intending occupiers) and s 7 (trespasser failing to leave when required by such occupiers); **LASPO 2012 s 144** (in force September 2012) – squatting in a residential building: living or intending to live in a residential building entered as a trespasser, knowing or where one ought to know of the trespass; **Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022 s 83** – residing on land without consent in or with a vehicle.
2. ***R (Best) v Chief Land Registrar* [2015] EWCA Civ 17**: Mr Best took possession of an abandoned house in 1997, renovated it and moved in permanently in January 2012; the registrar rejected his Sch 6 application because his possession was criminal under s 144. The CA held that criminality does not prevent reliance on adverse possession: Sales LJ – Sch 6 already balances owners' protection against the public interest in use and marketability; s 144 aimed at deterrence and practical help for homeowners and was not meant to have a "collateral effect" on adverse possession; had Parliament intended that, it would have said so. The same reasoning likely applies to s 83 (textbook). See Hickey [2017] Conv 53.

## Debate and critique
- **Is adverse possession of registered land now dead?** Cooke: registered land is "virtually squatter proof"; Dixon speaks of the "emasculation of adverse possession", leaving a minimal role for boundary disputes, unregistered land and clearly abandoned land. Counter-notice plus two years to evict is heavily weighted toward proprietors (textbook).
- **Affirmation of registration**: Bogusz – land as a freely traded commercial commodity makes adverse possession appear outdated; the LRA 2002 "brought land registration into the twenty-first century". Cobb and Fox (2007) 27 LS 236 view the reform as a deliberate policy choice to secure registered titles and incentivise first registration, with critical attention to the morality and politics of urban squatting.
- **Owners who ignore notices**: the protection depends on engagement; if notices are missed or ignored, the squatter is registered – but such an absent owner arguably has little cause to complain.
- **Pre- versus post-2002 justifications**: the worksheet asks how justifications (repose, use, certainty) change once title is constitutive and the register conclusive; boundary-mistake cases remain the strongest justification.
- **Human rights**: *Pye v UK* shows a narrow majority and the strength of the dissenters' point that registration, not possession, is the basis of registered title – a point the 2002 Act embraces.
- **Criminal law versus property law**: *Best* separates the two; critics may find it odd that criminal conduct can found title, while supporters say Sch 6 already contains the relevant balance.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Confirm the land is registered and the dates: if 12 years accrued before 13 October 2003, use the transitional provisions; otherwise Sch 6.
2. Establish adverse possession for 10 years ending on the application (elements as in adverse-possession-elements).
3. Advise the squatter on the ST1 application and whether to invoke para 5 (estoppel, other right, boundary mistake – check all four boundary requirements).
4. Advise the owner: respond within 65 business days; serve counter-notice (NAP) and, if appropriate, object; then evict within two years using proceedings (not just letters).
5. If the owner fails to act within two years, the squatter can reapply and will be registered.
6. Consider whether a squatter in actual occupation has an overriding interest against a purchaser (Sch 3 para 2 exceptions).
7. Note any criminal-law aspects (residential squatting – *Best*), and human rights arguments (unlikely to succeed after *Pye v UK* and *Ofulue*).
8. Worksheet scenario: if in 2003 the owner promised to transfer the land to the squatter, consider para 5(2) estoppel or para 5(3) other entitlement.
