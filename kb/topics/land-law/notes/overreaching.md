# Overreaching

## Overview
Overreaching is the statutory mechanism (LPA 1925 ss 2 and 27) by which a purchaser or mortgagee of land held on trust takes free of the beneficiaries' equitable interests, which are detached from the land and transferred ("commuted") into the purchase money. It operates in both unregistered and registered land in England and Wales. It delivers the "curtain principle" – buyers need not investigate trust interests – but can leave beneficiaries with money they did not want, or no money at all. Its relationship with overriding interests through actual occupation (*Boland*, *Flegg*) is a staple of problem questions.

## The law

### 1. Effect
If the requirements are met, overreachable equitable interests are detached from the land and attach to the money raised; the purchaser takes the land unencumbered. This happens whether or not the beneficiaries consent (*Flegg*). The 1925 legislation used overreaching to let purchasers take free of family-type equitable interests (especially trust interests) while giving the holders monetary value.

### 2. Requirement 1 – an overreachable interest (s 2 LPA 1925)
1. The key overreachable interest is a beneficial interest under a trust of land (e.g. a contribution-based interest of someone not on the legal title).
2. Excluded by **s 2(3)**: notably equitable leases, equitable easements and estate contracts (commercial interests). For these apply the land charges rules (unregistered) or ss 28–29 LRA 2002 (registered).

### 3. Requirement 2 – statutory conditions (ss 2(1), 27 LPA 1925)
1. A **conveyance of a legal estate** to a purchaser. "Conveyance" includes "a mortgage, charge, lease" (s 205(1)(ii)), so a mortgage advance can overreach. The grant of an easement is not a conveyance of a legal estate in land, so cannot overreach: *Baker v Craggs* (2018, CA).
2. **Capital money paid to at least two trustees or a trust corporation** (s 27).
3. Whenever land is co-owned there is a trust, so ask whether money was paid to two or more of the trustees.

### 4. Leading cases
- *City of London BS v Flegg* (1988, HL): Mr and Mrs Maxwell-Brown were registered owners of Bleak House; Mr and Mrs Flegg (her parents) paid about half the price and lived there, so had beneficial interests. The Maxwell-Browns later mortgaged the house without the Fleggs' knowledge and defaulted. Mortgage money was paid to two trustees, so the Fleggs' interests were overreached and could not bind the society, despite their actual occupation; an overreached interest cannot be an overriding interest. In the end the Fleggs received nothing because no surplus remained.
- *Williams & Glyn's Bank v Boland* (1981, HL): mortgage money paid to one trustee (the husband) – no overreaching; the wife's interest plus actual occupation bound the bank.
- *State Bank of India v Sood* (1997, CA): overreaching can operate even where no capital money is raised (a charge securing existing debts); the payment-to-two-trustees requirement does not bite when no capital money arises. Peter Gibson LJ adopted Harpum's analysis that overreaching is the process by which existing interests are subordinated to a later interest created under a trust or power.
- *HSBC v Dyche* (2009): no overreaching where there is fraud, as the conveyance is not in good faith.
- *Shami v Shami* (2012): obiter observations on overreaching with fewer than two trustees.

### 5. If overreaching fails
- **Unregistered land**: the trust interest falls into the residual category governed by the doctrine of notice (see unregistered-land; *Kingsnorth v Tizard*).
- **Registered land**: if a purchaser for value registers (s 29), the non-overreached interest is postponed unless protected – e.g. as an overriding interest through actual occupation (Sch 3 para 2: *Boland*). If the acquirer gave no value (gift), s 28 makes the earlier interest binding.
- **Restrictions**: a beneficiary can enter a restriction (s 40 LRA 2002) requiring money to be paid to two trustees, guaranteeing overreaching (and therefore payment) – trust interests cannot be protected by notice (s 33).

## Debate and critique
- **Balance of interests**: overreaching protects purchasers (dynamic security) while giving beneficiaries money (static security in value only). the materials asks whether beneficiaries actually want money rather than their home and whether the doctrine favours purchasers too much; Owen argues it does ([2013] Conv 377; [2015] Conv 226; Owen and Cahill [2017] Conv 26).
- ***Flegg***: overreaching took place without the Fleggs' consent or knowledge and they recovered nothing – a stark illustration of how two trustees can defeat occupying beneficiaries, which actual occupation cannot cure.
- ***Sood*** extends the reach further: where no money is raised, the protective function of payment to two trustees disappears altogether.
- **Theory – Ferris and Battersby** ((2002) 118 LQR 270; (2003) 119 LQR 94): building on Harpum's "seminal" [1990] CLJ 277 analysis, they argue overreaching occurs when a limited owner exercises an *authority* to dispose; trustees' authority now comes from s 6 TOLATA 1996 (previously s 28(1) LPA 1925), not from the registered proprietor's *ability* to dispose; s 2(1) LPA protects purchasers from improper exercise of the authority but only if the disposition is intra vires and the capital-money rules are complied with; for a mortgagee's power of sale the narrower protection of s 104(2) LPA applies. Overreaching is the corollary of giving limited owners powers of disposition – otherwise those powers would be illusory – and the essential difficulty is balancing purchasers against interest owners when powers are misused. They also discuss s 16 TOLATA (protection of purchasers) and consider the treatment of *Flegg* in later commentary (they reject suggestions that *Flegg* was decided per incuriam).
- **Registered land link**: the curtain principle depends on overreaching; where it fails, actual occupation re-opens the curtain.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there a trust? (Co-ownership at law or a contributor not on the title.)
2. Is the claimant's interest overreachable (a trust interest, not an estate contract/equitable lease/equitable easement)?
3. Was there a conveyance of a legal estate – sale, mortgage or lease (not an easement: *Baker v Craggs*)?
4. Was capital money paid to two trustees or a trust corporation? If two → overreached (*Flegg*) regardless of occupation or consent. If one → not overreached (*Boland*). If no capital money raised → *Sood*.
5. Any fraud (*Dyche*)?
6. If not overreached: registered land – check for a restriction, then actual occupation under Sch 3 para 2 and its exceptions; unregistered – doctrine of notice.
7. Advise on practical protection: enter a restriction; occupiers may be asked to sign waivers.
