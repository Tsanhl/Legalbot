# Registered land and the Land Registration Act 2002

## Overview
Registered land is land whose title (freehold or leasehold) is recorded at HM Land Registry under a unique title number. In England and Wales it is governed by the Land Registration Act 2002 (in force 13 October 2003, replacing the LRA 1925) and the Land Registration Rules 2003. The register, not notice or title deeds, decides whether third-party rights bind a purchaser. Over 88% of titles are registered. This note covers the aims of registration, the LRA 2002's structure, registrable dispositions, the priority rules in ss 28–29, notices and restrictions, and alteration and indemnity. Overriding interests and overreaching have their own notes.

## The law

### 1. What registration is
1. **Title-based**, not names-based (contrast land charges in unregistered land). One parcel can carry several titles (e.g. freehold and a long lease), cross-referenced.
2. No land charges, no doctrine of notice – enforceability turns on the register.
3. **History**: derived from the Torrens system (South Australia, 1858); Land Registry Act 1862 (voluntary); Land Transfer Act 1875; Land Transfer Act 1897 (compulsory on transfer, London only); LRA 1925 (national scheme; compulsory on sale everywhere only from December 1990); LRA 1997; LRA 2002. The Law Commission called the 1925 Act "badly drafted and lacking in clarity".
4. **Conveyancing outline**: negotiation and searches; exchange of contracts; pre-completion search and TR1 transfer; completion; registration. A transfer of a registered estate takes effect at law only on registration.

### 2. Aims of registration
1. General aims: provide publicity (McFarlane) and protection – a complete and accurate picture of title with minimal further inquiry; remove the cost of investigating deeds; reduce fraud; alert buyers to burdens; standardise conveyancing; support the property market. Complete registration is unrealistic; trade-offs are inevitable.
2. **Static v dynamic security** (O'Connor, drawing on Demogue): protecting existing owners/right-holders versus protecting purchasers.
3. **Ruoff's three principles** (policy aspirations, not fully realised):
   - **Mirror**: the register reflects all rights affecting the title – in practice a "cracked mirror" because of overriding interests;
   - **Curtain**: purchasers need not look behind the register at trust interests – achieved partly via overreaching;
   - **Insurance**: the State guarantees title and pays an indemnity for loss caused by errors.
4. **LRA 2002 objectives** (Law Com No 254 (1998), paras 1.3–1.6; Law Com No 271 (2001), paras 1.1–1.14): the 1925 scheme was too complex (148 sections and many rules), antiquated, undermined by many overriding interests, and suffered a "registration gap" between transaction and registration. The new Act aimed to:
   - expand triggers for first registration (unregistered land has "had its day");
   - make the register a complete and accurate reflection of title so title can be investigated online "with the absolute minimum of additional enquiries and inspections";
   - reduce overriding interests;
   - strengthen the State guarantee/indemnity;
   - change attitudes so registering rights is seen as reasonable;
   - adopt **title by registration** rather than registration of title;
   - move to **e-conveyancing** – "a conveyancing revolution";
   - break from simply translating unregistered principles: "the basis of title to unregistered land is possession, whereas … the basis for registered title is the fact of registration" (Law Com 254).
5. E-conveyancing stalled; **Law Com No 380, *Updating the Land Registration Act 2002* (2018)** recommended retreating from that ambition for now (security, fraud, cost), with technical recommendations not yet enacted (Government response updated March 2021).

### 3. Four groups of rights (Bevan's analytical framework)
1. **Substantively registrable estates** (own title): legal freehold; legal lease of more than seven years (ss 2, 3, 4, 27).
2. **Registrable dispositions** that must be completed by registration to be legal (s 27): e.g. transfers, leases over seven years, express legal easements, legal charges.
3. **Interests protected by entry on the register** (notice or restriction): most other third-party interests (easements, covenants, options, estate contracts).
4. **Unregistered interests which override** (Schs 1 and 3) – see overriding-interests-actual-occupation.

### 4. The register and first registration
1. Three parts: **Property Register** (description, estate, benefiting rights); **Proprietorship Register** (class of title, owner, restrictions); **Charges Register** (burdens: charges, covenants, easements). Not to be confused with the Land Charges Register.
2. **Voluntary first registration** (s 3): freehold; lease with more than seven years to run; some other legal interests.
3. **Compulsory first registration** (s 4): transfer of an unregistered freehold; transfer of a lease with more than seven years to run; grant out of an unregistered title of a lease of more than seven years (and other triggers – very broadly drawn).
4. **Classes of title**: absolute, qualified, possessory, good leasehold; upgradeable.
5. **Conclusiveness – s 58**: if a person is entered as proprietor of a legal estate but the estate was not otherwise vested in them, registration vests it. Registration is constitutive. Upheld in *Walker v Burton* (2013) and *Swift 1st v Chief Land Registrar* (2015): the register is conclusive unless and until altered.

### 5. Registrable dispositions and owner's powers
1. **s 27**: registrable dispositions must be completed by registration (Sch 2) to operate at law; otherwise at best equitable. Most important: transfer of registered freehold; transfer of a registered lease; grant of a lease of more than seven years out of a registered estate; express grant or reservation of a legal easement; grant of a legal charge.
2. **ss 23–24**: the registered proprietor has owner's powers to dispose and charge; limitations must appear on the register.
3. **s 26**: a disponee's title is protected – the proprietor's powers are taken to be unlimited unless limited by an entry on the register or by the Act. (The textbook notes debate, following *Mortgage Express*, on how far s 26 extends; the author argues it should not be over-extended so as to undermine the priority provisions.)

### 6. Priority rules – ss 28 and 29
1. **s 28 basic rule**: priority by date of creation – first in time wins – unless s 29 applies.
2. **s 29(1)**: if a *registrable disposition* of a *registered estate* is made *for valuable consideration*, *completion by registration* postpones to the disponee any interest affecting the estate immediately before whose priority is not protected at the time of registration.
3. **s 29(2)**: priority is protected if the interest is a registered charge or the subject of a **notice**; falls within **Schedule 3** (overriding); or appears from the register to be excepted from the effect of registration.
4. **s 29(4)**: the grant of a lease that is not a registrable disposition (seven years or less) is treated as if it were a registered registrable disposition – so short-lease tenants get s 29 protection.
5. **Valuable consideration** excludes marriage and nominal consideration (s 132(1)); a gift does not qualify – then s 28 applies.
6. Checklist: (1) registrable disposition; (2) of a registered estate; (3) for valuable consideration; (4) completed by registration. If all satisfied, the disponee takes free of unprotected interests but subject to registered charges, notices and Sch 3 interests. If not, fall back on s 28.

### 7. Notices and restrictions
1. **Notice** (s 32): "an entry in the register in respect of the burden of an interest affecting a registered estate or charge"; entered on the Charges Register; records rather than guarantees validity; agreed or unilateral (s 34); cancellation procedure (s 36).
2. **Excluded from notices (s 33)**: interests under a trust (protect by restriction instead); leases of three years or less (usually overriding); landlord-and-tenant restrictive covenants. Notices can therefore protect easements (legal or equitable), leases over three years, estate contracts, and freehold restrictive covenants.
3. **Restriction** (s 40): "an entry in the register regulating the circumstances in which a disposition of a registered estate or charge may be the subject of an entry in the register"; on the Proprietorship Register. It confers no priority but limits dealings, e.g. requiring a beneficiary's consent or requiring purchase money to be paid to two trustees (guaranteeing overreaching).
4. **Failure to protect**: generally the interest loses priority under s 29 unless overriding. Attempts to escape:
   - *Peffer v Rigg* (1977, under LRA 1925): purchaser with knowledge held bound for lack of good faith – heavily criticised; the Law Commission said its reasoning "cannot be supported"; out of step with the irrelevance of notice under the 2002 Act.
   - *Lyus v Prowsa* (1982): constructive trust where the purchaser's conscience is affected. *Lloyd v Dugdale* (2001): only where the new owner has undertaken a new obligation to give effect to the interest (classically shown by a reduced price). *Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013): *Lyus* "was a very unusual case and is not likely to be followed in more than a few others".
   - Personal claims (Law Commission; Cooke and O'Connor (2004) 120 LQR 640): breach of contract, misrepresentation, undue influence, breach of trust against the transferor or a wrongdoing transferee.

### 8. Alteration, rectification and indemnity
1. **Schedule 4** governs alteration: to correct a mistake; to bring the register up to date; to give effect to rights excepted from registration; and (registrar only) to remove superfluous entries.
2. **Rectification** = alteration that corrects a mistake and prejudicially affects the registered proprietor's title (Sch 4 para 1). Court or registrar may rectify. Only rectification (or refusal of rectification) gives rise to an **indemnity** under **Schedule 8** – the insurance principle.
3. "Mistake" is undefined and not about fault (e.g. typographical errors, double registration, forged dispositions, wrongful adverse possession registration): *Baxter v Mannion* (2011) – registration of a squatter who had not in fact had ten years' possession was a mistake.
4. Forgery chains (A → forger B → innocent C): the narrow view (*Barclays Bank v Guy* (2008)) treats C's registration as no mistake; the broader "long-arm" view (Lord Neuberger in *Guy*; *Odogwu v Vastguide* (2009); *Gold Harp Properties v Macleod* (2014); *Bakrania v Lloyds Bank* (2017); *Dhillon v Barclays Bank* (2020)) treats later entries as tainted – now the accepted position. The *Malory*/*Fitzwilliam* approach (only legal title passes, A keeps the beneficial title) was held per incuriam in *Swift 1st*.
5. *NRAM v Evans* (2017): whether an entry is a mistake is judged when made; entries flowing from a *void* disposition are mistakes, those from a *voidable* disposition are not (until rescission, the register is then "updated" under Sch 4 para 2(1)(b)), so no indemnity. Applied in *Antoine v Barclays Bank* (2018); approved in *Brelsford v Providence Estate* (2022, PC).

## Key authorities
- LRA 2002 ss 2–4 (estates, first registration), 23–24 (powers), 26 (protection of disponees), 27 (registrable dispositions), 28–29 (priority), 32–36 (notices), 40 (restrictions), 58 (conclusiveness), 132 (valuable consideration), Schs 1, 3, 4, 8.
- *Swift 1st v Chief Land Registrar* (2015); *Walker v Burton* (2013): s 58 conclusiveness.
- *Peffer v Rigg* (1977); *Lyus v Prowsa* (1982); *Lloyd v Dugdale* (2001); *Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013): escaping non-registration.
- *Baxter v Mannion* (2011); *Barclays v Guy* (2008); *NRAM v Evans* (2017): alteration and mistake.
- Law Com Nos 254, 271, 380.

## Debate and critique
- **Has the LRA 2002 succeeded?** Arguments for: clearer structure, wider triggers, narrower overriding interests, constitutive registration, simple priority rule.
- **Gardner, "The Land Registration Act 2002 – the Show on the Road" (2014) 77 MLR 763**: registration of title promotes both static and dynamic security (and, he suggests, neo-republican values and lower transaction costs), but the Act's ideology of "the more registration the better" – a register both *conclusive* and *constitutive* – is over-intense. English land law needs other "repositories of title" for informal rights; the overriding interests provisions, shaped by that ideology, were not thought through and are incoherent (e.g. the treatment of informal legal versus equitable easements). The alteration provisions, meant to respond to land "theft", are "vacuous", and s 58 engenders rather than pre-empts difficulties. Constitutive registration supports party autonomy only if parties themselves register – the promise of e-conveyancing – so its lapse is regrettable. He warns against commercial drivers if the Registry were privatised.
- **Cracked mirror**: overriding interests (especially actual occupation) undermine the mirror and curtain principles (Bevan [2024] Conv 123 on repairing the "cracked mirror").
- **Static v dynamic security** runs through rectification (who bears loss from fraud?) and through overreaching and overriding interests.
- **E-conveyancing** retreat (Law Com 380) leaves the registration gap and constitutive-registration problems unresolved.
- **Personal remedies and constructive trusts** may be seen as undermining the registration focus of the Act.
- Further reading named: Cooke and O'Connor (2004) 120 LQR 640; Jackson (2003) 119 LQR 660.

## Applying it to a problem question
Use the sequence:
1. Is the land registered?
2. Identify the interest (proprietary? legal or equitable?).
3. How is it governed under the LRA 2002 – is it a registrable disposition (s 27) that needed registration to be legal? Do ss 28 or 29 apply (registrable disposition, registered estate, valuable consideration, registered)?
4. Has it been registered / could a notice be entered / could a restriction be entered?
5. Has a notice or restriction actually been entered?
6. If not, could it be overriding under Sch 3 (short legal lease; actual occupation; legal easement)? What is the effect?
7. Has it been overreached (for trust interests)?
8. If the acquirer is a donee or paid nothing, apply s 28 – first in time.
9. Mention escape routes only briefly (*Lyus* is exceptional; *Peffer* not good law) and any personal remedies.
10. If the facts involve fraud or a wrong entry, consider alteration/rectification and indemnity (Schs 4, 8; *NRAM*'s void/voidable distinction).
