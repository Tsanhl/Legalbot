# Overriding interests and actual occupation (LRA 2002 Schedules 1 and 3)

## Overview
Overriding interests ("unregistered interests which override") bind a purchaser of registered land even though they do not appear on the register. They are the main crack in the "mirror" and a direct affront to the LRA 2002's goal of a complete register, but they are justified where it is unreasonable to expect registration – informally created rights and short leases. The most important and litigated category is the interest of a person in **actual occupation** (Sch 3 para 2), which protects, for example, a cohabitant with a contribution-based beneficial interest against a mortgage lender. The LRA 2002 narrowed the category relative to s 70(1) LRA 1925.

## The law

### 1. Rationale and narrowing
1. Law Com No 254: most overriding interests share the feature that it would be unreasonable or unrealistic to expect registration – they arise informally (e.g. trust interests the holder may not even know they have) or are short.
2. Changes under the LRA 2002: some former overriding interests lost status; others (actual occupation) were tightened; some were phased out over ten years (ceasing at midnight on 12 October 2013); a new duty of disclosure requires applicants for registration to disclose known overriding interests; notices were simplified and widened so more rights can be put on the register.

### 2. Two schedules
- **Schedule 1**: interests overriding **first registration** – para 1 legal leases of seven years or less; para 2 interests of persons in actual occupation; para 3 legal easements and profits. Slightly broader, so first registration cannot be used to escape existing rights.
- **Schedule 3**: interests overriding a **registered disposition** under s 29 (the focus of the course). Paras 1–3 below; paras 4–6 (customary and public rights, local land charges) are not covered.

### 3. Sch 3 para 1 – short legal leases
Legal leases granted for **seven years or less** override (under the LRA 1925 the threshold was 21 years). Equitable leases are not within para 1 – they need a notice, or may override under para 2 if the tenant is in actual occupation. Leases of more than seven years are registrable dispositions and cannot override under para 1.

### 4. Sch 3 para 3 – legal easements and profits
1. Only **legal** easements/profits; equitable ones need a notice.
2. Easements overriding before the Act came into force remain overriding.
3. Expressly granted legal easements after the Act are registrable dispositions, so not overriding.
4. **Implied** legal easements (s 62 LPA 1925, *Wheeldon v Burrows*, necessity, common intention, prescription) override only if: registered under Part 1 Commons Act 2006; **or** within the actual knowledge of the disponee; **or** obvious on a reasonably careful inspection; **or** proved to have been exercised in the 12 months ending on the day of the disposition. The last limb means most implied easements will override.

### 5. Sch 3 para 2 – interests of persons in actual occupation
Elements: (1) a qualifying proprietary interest; (2) actual occupation at the relevant time; (3) neither exception in para 2(b) or 2(c) applies. The interest overrides only "so far as relating to land of which he is in actual occupation".

**(a) Qualifying interest**
1. Must be proprietary, not personal: *National Provincial Bank v Ainsworth* (1965); confirmed in *Scott v Southern Pacific Mortgages* (2014, UKSC).
2. Common examples: contribution-based beneficial interests under a trust; leases; options; rights by proprietary estoppel.
3. Interests capable of protection by notice or restriction can have **dual status** and override if coupled with actual occupation: *Williams & Glyn's Bank v Boland* [1981] AC 487.
4. Overreached interests are not qualifying: *City of London BS v Flegg* (1988).

**(b) Actual occupation – meaning**
1. Plain English: Lord Wilberforce in *Boland* – "ordinary words of plain English"; "actual" emphasises physical presence, not adverse possession. In *Boland* the wife, with a contribution-based interest, lived in the home; mortgage money was paid to one trustee (her husband) so there was no overreaching; her interest bound the bank. The House rejected the idea that a wife's occupation was merely a shadow of her husband's (as Lord Denning MR had said in the CA).
2. **Factors** – Mummery LJ, *Link Lending v Bustard* (2010) (non-exhaustive): "the degree of permanence and continuity of presence of the person concerned, the intentions and wishes of that person, the length of absence from the property and the reason for it and the nature of the property and personal circumstances of the person".
3. **Preparatory acts are not enough**: *Abbey National BS v Cann* (1991) – furniture being moved in and carpets laid 35 minutes before completion by Mrs Cann's son and partner while she was abroad were "merely preparatory"; no actual occupation at completion.
4. **Absences**: an intention to return can preserve occupation.
   - *Chhokar v Chhokar* (1984): wife in hospital giving birth when husband sold – still in occupation.
   - *Link Lending v Bustard* (2010): Mrs Bustard, sectioned and detained in a psychiatric unit for a long period, kept furniture and post at the house, her absence was involuntary and she intended to return – in occupation.
   - *Stockholm Finance v Garden Holdings* (1995): Saudi princess abroad for 14 months, never visiting the London house – not in occupation.
   - *AIB v Turner* (2016): living for long periods in Barbados with occasional returns – not in occupation.
5. **Nature of the land**: residential → residence; commercial/derelict → physical presence and control.
   - *Malory Enterprises v Cheshire Homes* (2002): storing goods and fencing derelict land was occupation.
   - *Pennistone Holdings v Rock Ferry Waterfront Trust* (2021, CA): would a visitor think the land abandoned or occupied? An unpaid caretaker visiting from time to time, with an abandoned digger and two containers storing his own tools, was not in occupation on Pennistone's behalf; unlike *Malory*, an inspector would assume abandonment.
   - *Blacklocks v JB Developments* (1982): erecting a barn on farmland sufficed.
   - *Kling v Keston Properties* (1983); *Saeed v Plustrade* (2001): regular car parking may suffice.
6. **Occupation, not use, and not exclusive**: several people can occupy at once; mere use is insufficient – *Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013): using an external staircase to reach flats was use, not occupation.
7. **Relevant date**: completion of the disposition (*Cann*; para 2 speaks of "the time of the disposition"). Lewison J in *Thompson v Foy* (2009) (obiter) favoured requiring occupation at both completion and registration.
8. **Through agents**: *Lloyds Bank v Rosset* (CA) – occupation through builders working on the property.
9. **Minors** cannot be in actual occupation as they are there only as "shadows" of their parents; questioning children would be inappropriate: *Hypo-Mortgage Services v Robinson* (1997).
10. **Part occupation**: *Ferrishurst v Wallcite* (1999, under LRA 1925) gave an overriding interest over the whole land if the occupier's interest extended to it. The LRA 2002 reverses this: the interest overrides only as far as the land actually occupied.

**(c) Exceptions (Sch 3 only – no equivalent in Sch 1)**
1. **Para 2(c) – occupation not apparent.** Overriding status is lost if *both*: (i) the occupation would not have been obvious on a reasonably careful inspection at the time of the disposition; and (ii) the disponee did not actually know of the interest. One limb alone is not enough. Limb (i) is objective and hypothetical – whether or not an inspection occurred (*Thompson v Foy*); what must be obvious is the visible signs of occupation relied on (Ramsey J, *Thomas v Clydesdale Bank* (2010)); limb (ii) requires actual knowledge of the facts giving rise to the interest (*Thomas*). This is not the doctrine of notice. *Trevallion v Watmore* (2016): a buyer's cursory inspection missed a neighbour's fenced-off triangle behind a fuchsia bush; a reasonably careful inspection would have revealed it, so the exception did not apply and the neighbours' rights bound her.
2. **Para 2(b) – rights not disclosed on reasonable inquiry.** Lost if inquiry was made of the occupier before the disposition and they failed to disclose the interest when they could reasonably have been expected to. Only actual inquiries count; they must be addressed to the interest holder (or their solicitor), but may be made by someone other than the purchaser. The new "reasonably expected" element means someone unaware they have an interest cannot be expected to disclose it.
3. **Waivers**: lenders routinely ask occupiers to sign waivers – either confirming they have no interest (treated as a non-disclosure on inquiry) or agreeing to postpone their interest to the mortgage.

## Key authorities
- LRA 2002 s 29(2)(a)(ii); Sch 1 paras 1–3; Sch 3 paras 1–3.
- *Williams & Glyn's Bank v Boland* [1981] AC 487 (HL): wife's beneficial interest plus occupation bound the bank; plain-English meaning.
- *Abbey National BS v Cann* (1991, HL): preparatory acts insufficient; date of completion.
- *Link Lending v Bustard* (2010, CA): factors; involuntary absence with intention to return.
- *Chhokar v Chhokar* (1984); *Stockholm Finance v Garden Holdings* (1995); *AIB v Turner* (2016): absences.
- *Malory v Cheshire Homes* (2002); *Pennistone v Rock Ferry* (2021): commercial/derelict land.
- *Hypo-Mortgage Services v Robinson* (1997): minors.
- *Thompson v Foy* (2009); *Thomas v Clydesdale Bank* (2010); *Trevallion v Watmore* (2016): para 2(c).
- *City of London BS v Flegg* (1988): overreached interests cannot override.
- *Scott v Southern Pacific Mortgages* (2014): need a proprietary interest.
- *Ferrishurst v Wallcite* (1999): part occupation (reversed by the 2002 Act).

## Debate and critique
- **Mirror and curtain**: actual occupation is "the most notorious – and most litigated" category and strikes at the core of the registration project. The Law Commission nevertheless kept it because it is "not reasonable to expect nor sensible to require" registration of informally arising rights, and many holders would not realise they should register.
- **Intentions and wishes – "occupation creep"**: Bogusz ([2011] Conv 268; [2014] Conv 27) notes Mummery LJ formally made intentions and wishes relevant factors, which may extend actual occupation where facts are vague and impact unexpectedly on lenders. She compares it to the holistic, intention-based approach of *Stack*/*Jones* in common-intention trusts and suggests it may be especially valuable for vulnerable occupiers (as in *Link Lending*, or cases of domestic violence), or alternatively should be confined to derivative/notional occupation cases.
- **Bevan, "Overriding and Over-Extended? Actual Occupation: A Call to Orthodoxy" [2016] Conv 104**: intention-based analysis conflicts with the LRA 2002's aim to minimise inquiries; the principles after *Link Lending*, *Thompson* and *Thomas* are "more vigorous, more expansive and more subjective than ever", contradicting predictions that overriding interests were in their final days; he calls for a return to a narrower, more faithful reading of the statutory language. See also Bevan [2024] Conv 123 on repairing the "cracked mirror".
- **Gardner (2014) MLR**: the overriding-interests design is incoherent – e.g. informal legal easements override in circumstances where equivalent informal equitable easements do not, with no clear justification.
- **Part occupation**: J Hill's article (pre-2002) defended *Ferrishurst*: since occupation of part alerts a purchaser just as much as occupation of the whole, there is little policy reason to confine the overriding interest to the occupied part – contrary to the approach the 2002 Act adopted.
- **Static v dynamic security**: actual occupation protects informal right-holders (static) at the expense of purchasers and lenders (dynamic); para 2(b)/(c) and waivers rebalance toward purchasers.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Registered land? Registrable disposition for value, completed by registration (s 29)? If not (e.g. gift), s 28 applies and the earlier interest binds anyway.
2. Was the interest protected by notice or restriction? If not, consider Sch 3.
3. Short legal lease (≤7 years)? → para 1. Equitable lease → para 2 only.
4. Legal easement? Express post-2003 → must be registered. Implied → para 3 conditions (knowledge, obvious, used in last year).
5. Actual occupation (para 2):
   - Qualifying proprietary interest? (trust interest – check contributions; estoppel; lease; option). Was it overreached (two trustees paid)? If so, stop (*Flegg*).
   - Occupied at completion? Preparatory acts (*Cann*)? Absences and intention to return (*Chhokar*, *Link Lending* versus *Stockholm*, *Turner*)? Nature of land (*Malory*, *Pennistone*)? Agents (*Rosset*)? Minors (*Hypo-Mortgage*)? Part only?
   - Para 2(c): would occupation be obvious on a reasonably careful inspection? Did the buyer actually know? Both needed to defeat the claim.
   - Para 2(b): was the occupier actually asked? Could they reasonably be expected to disclose? Any waiver signed?
6. Conclude on whether the purchaser/lender is bound; flag uncertainty from the intentions-based case law and give alternative outcomes.
