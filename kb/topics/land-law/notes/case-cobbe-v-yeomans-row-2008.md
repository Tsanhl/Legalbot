# Cobbe v Yeoman's Row Management Ltd [2008] UKHL 55 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 30 July 2008 ([2008] 1 WLR 1752; [2008] 4 All ER 713). Panel: Lord Hoffmann, Lord Scott of Foscote,
Lord Walker of Gestingthorpe, Lord Brown of Eaton-under-Heywood and Lord Mance. Lord Scott gave the leading speech and
Lord Walker the only other detailed speech; the decision was unanimous. The case confines proprietary estoppel in a **commercial**
negotiation where both sides know the deal is not legally binding.

## Facts
Mr Cobbe, an experienced property developer, orally agreed in principle with Yeoman's Row Management to redevelop the
company's Knightsbridge property. He would apply for planning permission; the company would then sell to him for £12
million plus half of the proceeds above £24 million. Mr Cobbe obtained the permission. The company then demanded
more and refused to sell at the agreed terms. Both sides knew the oral arrangement was not legally binding, and
key terms remained to be negotiated.

## Decisions
- At first instance Etherton J ([2005] EWHC 266 (Ch)) found a proprietary estoppel and awarded about £2 million, half
  the increase in the value of the property produced by the planning permission; the Court of Appeal ([2006] EWCA Civ
  1139) upheld the decision.
- The House of Lords **allowed the company's appeal**. There was no proprietary estoppel and no constructive trust.
- Mr Cobbe was entitled instead to a money payment on a *quantum meruit* / unjust enrichment basis, reflecting a
  reasonable fee for his services and his expenses in obtaining planning permission (reported as £150,000).

## Reasoning (as reported)
- The expectation was not a sufficiently **certain interest** in land, since terms were still to be settled.
- Mr Cobbe knew the arrangement was "in honour" only, which was treated as similar to a "subject to contract"
  understanding. He took a commercial risk with his eyes open (Lord Walker's observation, paraphrased).
- Lord Scott's reasoning that estoppel cannot be used to enforce an agreement that section 2 of the Law of Property
  (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 makes void is noted by commentators as hard to reconcile with *Yaxley v Gotts*;
  Lord Walker expressed no view on it (one source).
- No constructive trust: that is for joint ventures, not where one party already owns the land and merely fails to
  honour an informal agreement.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. In a business setting, check whether both parties understood the deal was not binding (a "subject to contract"
   feel) and whether the interest promised was certain. If not, expect *Cobbe* to defeat a proprietary claim.
2. Ask the money question: restitution or *quantum meruit* for work done.
3. Contrast with the family cases (*Thorner v Major*, *Jennings v Rice*), where the court is more willing to find an
   estoppel because there is no commercial bargain; in *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18 the House distinguished
   *Cobbe* on that basis.
4. Mention section 2 of the 1989 Act as the formality background, noting the commentators' doubts about how far
   *Cobbe* limits *Yaxley v Gotts*.
