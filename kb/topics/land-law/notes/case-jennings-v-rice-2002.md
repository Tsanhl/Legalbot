# Jennings v Rice [2002] EWCA Civ 159 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the judgment itself was not read. Do not quote paragraph numbers or wording from this
> note beyond the short passage attributed below. Check the report ([2003] 1 P & CR 8) before citing a pinpoint.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Civil Division), judgment 22 February 2002. Panel: Aldous LJ, Mantell LJ and Robert Walker LJ. Robert
Walker LJ gave the leading judgment on how the court should satisfy a proprietary estoppel equity once it has arisen;
Aldous LJ also gave a judgment and Mantell LJ agreed. The case is the main authority that **proportionality** between
the claimant's expectation and the detriment suffered is an essential part of choosing the remedy.

## Facts
Mr Jennings did gardening and odd jobs for an elderly woman, Mrs Royle, from about 1970 and, in her last years, gave
her substantial unpaid care and help. He said he had been given assurances that he would inherit her house and its
contents. She died in August 1997, aged 93, without a will. The house was valued at £420,000 and the house with its
furniture at about £435,000. Accounts differ on the exact words said to Mr Jennings, so they are not quoted here.

## Decisions below and on appeal
- The trial judge (HHJ Weeks QC) found a proprietary estoppel and awarded Mr Jennings **£200,000** rather than the
  house and furniture, treating the full expectation as disproportionate.
- The Court of Appeal **dismissed** Mr Jennings's appeal and left the £200,000 award undisturbed.

## Principle
- Once the elements of proprietary estoppel are made out, an equity arises and the court decides how best to satisfy
  it. Robert Walker LJ described the essence of the doctrine as doing what is necessary to avoid an unconscionable
  result, and said the most essential requirement is proportionality between the expectation and the detriment.
- Where the expectation is clear and in proportion to what the claimant gave up, the court will usually fulfil it.
  Where the expectation is uncertain, extravagant or out of proportion to the detriment, the court may give less, but
  does not ignore the expectation altogether.
- Care and services given over many years are hard to price, so the court has a wide discretion. Sources report that
  the court did not set a closed list of factors and treated hourly-rate calculations as a cross-check at most (one source
  only, so treat as indicative).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Establish the equity first: assurance, reliance, detriment, unconscionability (see proprietary-estoppel-establishing).
2. Identify the claimant's expectation and put a value on it.
3. Identify the detriment and compare the two. If the expectation is far larger, argue that full satisfaction would be
   disproportionate and that a smaller award or a sum of money may suffice, as in *Jennings*.
4. If the expectation is clear and proportionate (a farm for a lifetime's work, for example), argue that the court
   will normally give effect to it. Note that the Supreme Court restated the remedial approach in *Guest v Guest*
   [2022] UKSC 27, so cite *Jennings* as part of the history rather than the last word.
