# Easements – creation, termination, third parties and reform

## Overview
Once a right is capable of being an easement (see easements-characteristics), the next questions are whether it was actually created and whether it is legal or equitable – which determines whether it binds a new owner of the servient land. Easements may be created expressly (grant or reservation), impliedly (necessity, common intention, the rule in *Wheeldon v Burrows*, s 62 LPA 1925), by prescription, by statute or by estoppel; the focus is on express and implied creation. Implied easements – especially the "transformative" effect of s 62 – are a favourite essay topic, alongside the Law Commission's 2011 reform proposals (England and Wales).

## The law

### 1. Express grant and reservation
- **Grant**: the owner of land conveys part (or grants a lease) and gives the buyer/tenant an easement over the land retained.
- **Reservation**: the owner conveys part and keeps for itself an easement over the land conveyed.
(Illustrated in Bevan, Figures 11.8–11.9.)

### 2. Legal or equitable?
**Legal easements** require all of:
1. Carved out of a legal estate (legal freehold or legal lease).
2. Duration equivalent to a fee simple absolute in possession or a term of years absolute (s 1(2)(a) LPA 1925) – an easement "for life" can only be equitable.
3. Express creation by deed (s 52 LPA 1925).
4. Registered land: an express easement is a registrable disposition and must be completed by registration to be legal (s 27 LRA 2002).
5. Implied easements are legal if implied into a legal conveyance (a deed transferring land or creating a lease); equitable if implied into a mere contract.
**Equitable easements**: carved out of an equitable interest; for life; created by a specifically enforceable written contract (not deed); failed formalities (e.g. not registered); implied into a contract.

### 3. Implied easements – four routes
**(a) Necessity**
1. Strict: an easement "without which the property retained cannot be used at all, and not merely necessary to the reasonable enjoyment of the property" (Stirling J, *Union Lighterage Co v London Graving Dock Co* (1902)).
2. Refused: *Manjang v Drammeh* (1991) (access by river possible); *Re MRA Engineering* (1988) (no vehicular access where accessible on foot).
3. *Sweet v Sommer* (2004, upheld by CA): through conveyancing errors, land was effectively landlocked for vehicles; a vehicular right of way was implied as the house could not be meaningfully used without it – a more lenient residential approach? The question is whether *Sweet* is an outlier or a contextual development. It can also be seen as a common intention case.

**(b) Common intention**
1. Lord Parker, *Pwllbach Colliery v Woodman* (1915): implied where necessary to give effect to the parties' common intention as to the manner or purpose of the land's use.
2. *Wong v Beaumont Property Trust* (1965): basement let for a Chinese restaurant with covenants to control smells; an easement to install a ventilation duct on the landlord's premises was implied.
3. *Stafford v Lee* (1993): requires (1) a common intention that the land be used for a definite and particular purpose and (2) that the easement is necessary to achieve it. Plans annexed to the conveyance showed a house was intended on woodland, so access over a private road was implied.
4. See also *Donovan v Rana* (2014) (discussed in the textbook).

**(c) The rule in *Wheeldon v Burrows* (1879)**
1. On division or sale of land, *quasi-easements* (rights the common owner exercised over one part for the benefit of another, which would be easements if the parts were separately owned) mature into easements in favour of the transferee.
2. Thesiger LJ's conditions: (1) the transferor was using the quasi-easement for the benefit of the part transferred at the time of transfer; (2) it was "continuous and apparent"; (3) it was reasonably necessary for the enjoyment of the land.
3. Are (2) and (3) cumulative or alternative? Thesiger LJ left this open; Peter Gibson LJ in *Wheeler v JJ Saunders* (1996) treated them as synonymous; *Wood v Waddington* (2015, CA) left it open but gave more weight to (3). Safest to prove all three.
4. Condition (1): use at the time of transfer prevents overburdening (*Alford v Hannaford* (2011)).
5. Condition (2): "continuous" means a permanent, obvious alteration available when needed; "apparent" means discoverable on reasonably careful inspection (*Pyer v Carter* (1857)) – e.g. a visible track or tarmac (*Millman v Ellis* (1996)). Ungoed-Thomas J, *Ward v Kirkland* (1967): a feature that would be seen on inspection, neither transitory nor intermittent, such as drains or paths; entering a neighbour's yard to maintain a wall left no such feature, so no easement.
6. Condition (3): not strict necessity but significant benefit – *Wheeler v JJ Saunders* (an alternative access meant not reasonably necessary); *Borman v Griffith* (1930) (an alternative route impassable for heavy vehicles at times – satisfied).
7. It can be excluded expressly; it does not need a legal conveyance (contracts suffice).

**(d) Section 62 LPA 1925**
1. A word-saving provision: a conveyance passes with the land all existing "liberties, privileges, easements, rights and advantages" appertaining to or enjoyed with it.
2. **Transformative effect**: it can upgrade a precarious permission into a legal easement on a new conveyance: *Wright v Macadam* (1949) – a tenant permitted to store coal in a shed obtained an easement when granted a new tenancy (rooted in *International Tea Stores v Hobbs*).
3. Conditions: (1) a conveyance of a legal estate by deed (s 205(1)(ii) – sale, lease, legal mortgage); (2) the right must satisfy *Re Ellenborough Park*; (3) the right was exercised at the time of the conveyance; (4) s 62 not excluded expressly or impliedly (*Hair v Gillman* (2000)); (5) if there was no prior diversity of ownership or occupation, the right must have been "continuous and apparent".
4. Prior diversity: *Long v Gowlett* (1923) required it; *Wood v Waddington* [2015] EWCA held it is not strictly required, but without it the right must be continuous and apparent (as in *Wheeldon*) – expanding s 62's scope. *P&S Platt v Crouch* (2003) is a further s 62 example (mooring rights benefiting a hotel – textbook).
5. Unlike *Wheeldon*, s 62 needs a legal conveyance.

### 4. Termination
1. Unity of ownership of dominant and servient land.
2. Release by deed, or abandonment proved by conduct.
3. Obsolescence/frustration where exercise becomes impossible.
4. Estoppel.
5. Excessive user – *McAdams Homes v Robinson* (2005): a "radical change in character" of the dominant land's use leading to a "substantial increase" in the burden on the servient land.

### 5. Effect on third parties
- **Benefit** passes on conveyance of the dominant land automatically under s 62 (or expressly).
- **Burden – unregistered land**: legal easements bind the world; post-1925 equitable easements must be registered as **Class D(iii)** land charges, otherwise void against a purchaser for money or money's worth of a legal estate.
- **Burden – registered land**: express legal easements must be registered (s 27) and then bind. Implied legal easements can be protected by notice or override under **Sch 3 para 3** – unless the easement was not within the disponee's actual knowledge *and* would not have been obvious on a reasonably careful inspection; but this exception does not apply if the easement was exercised in the year before the disposition (para 3(2)) – so most implied legal easements will override. Equitable easements need a notice; Sch 3 para 3 does not cover them, and actual occupation (para 2) is hard to apply to them (*Chaudhary v Yavuz* (2013)).

### 6. Reform – Law Commission Report No 327 (2011)
Key recommendations (not implemented, broadly welcomed as rationalising rather than radical):
1. Statutory abolition of the ouster principle.
2. Preventing s 62 from transforming mere licences into easements.
3. A single method of implication, no distinction between grant and reservation, based on what is "necessary for the reasonable use of the land".
4. Clarifying extinguishment, including a rebuttable presumption of abandonment after 20 years' non-use.
(Bevan also notes a proposed jurisdiction to discharge easements, extending s 84 LPA 1925.)

## Debate and critique
- **Strictness of necessity**: is *Sweet v Sommer* an outlier or a sensible modern, contextual development for residential land? The overlap with common intention blurs categories.
- **Do we still need *Wheeldon v Burrows*?** With s 62 extended by *Wood v Waddington*, the question is whether *Wheeldon* is redundant and should be abolished – though *Wheeldon* still matters where there is no legal conveyance (contracts, equitable leases).
- **s 62's "metamorphosis"**: Tee ("Metamorphoses and Section 62" [1998] Conv 115) argues the cases following *International Tea Stores v Hobbs* rest on a logical error and can cause injustice (a landlord's kindness becomes a permanent burden, as in *Wright v Macadam*). She notes the Law Commission's 1971 proposal to amend s 62 (facilities reasonably contemplated as continuing or necessary/intended) and suggests that courts might either read s 62 at face value (passing rights without changing their nature – a licence would remain revocable) or give weight to the parties' intention. Law Com 327 would stop the transformative effect.
- **Douglas, "Reforming Implied Easements" (2015) 131 LQR 251**: the current law already provides a single intention-based test that is coherent and not as complex as its reputation; the Law Commission's statutory "reasonable use" test would not improve it and might confuse matters by drawing an arbitrary distinction between express and implied easements. (Douglas also wrote "How to Reform Section 62" [2015] Conv 13 – the raw file under that name contains a different article; see report.)
- **Registration**: Gardner (see registered-land-lra-2002) criticises the different treatment of informal legal and equitable easements under Sch 3.
- **Recreational easements** after *Regency Villas* are most likely to be created expressly, giving a "brake" through formality (Bevan [2019] Conv 55).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Capable of being an easement (*Ellenborough Park*)?
2. Express? Was there a deed (legal) and, in registered land, registration? If not, a written s 2-compliant contract (equitable)?
3. If no express grant, test each implied route in turn:
   - Necessity: is the land unusable without it (*Union Lighterage*; but *Sweet v Sommer*)?
   - Common intention: definite purpose shared by both, and easement necessary for it (*Stafford v Lee*; *Wong*)?
   - *Wheeldon v Burrows*: common owner using a quasi-easement at the time; continuous and apparent (visible track, drain); reasonably necessary; not excluded.
   - s 62: legal conveyance? Right exercised at the time (often a licence)? Prior diversity of occupation – if not, continuous and apparent (*Wood v Waddington*)? Excluded?
   - Reservation is harder to imply than grant (Law Com 327 would remove the distinction).
4. Legal or equitable result?
5. Binding on a new servient owner? Unregistered: legal binds; equitable – Class D(iii). Registered: express – registered?; implied legal – Sch 3 para 3 (knowledge, obviousness, use in past year); equitable – notice?
6. Any termination (unity, release, abandonment, excessive user)?
