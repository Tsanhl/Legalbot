# Fixtures and chattels

## Overview
An object brought onto land is either a chattel (personal property that keeps its separate identity), a fixture (a chattel so attached that it becomes part of the land), or, after *Elitestone v Morris*, "part and parcel of the land itself" (e.g. a building). The classification decides who owns the item when land is sold, mortgaged or a lease ends. There is no statutory test; the courts apply the two "tests" from *Holland v Hodgson* – degree and purpose of annexation – case by case, producing outcomes that are widely criticised as inconsistent (England and Wales).

## The law

### 1. Three categories
1. **Chattel**: moveable personal property – clothes, rugs, beds, paintings; not part of the land.
2. **Fixture**: a chattel attached so as to become part of the land – built-in furniture, integrated worktops, fireplaces. Maxim: *quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit* ("whatever is attached to the soil becomes part of it").
3. **Part and parcel of the land**: larger structures (bungalows, conservatories) and integral building components (doors, window frames). Lord Lloyd in *Elitestone* adopted this threefold classification from *Woodfall*; one would not ordinarily call a building itself a "fixture". Categories 2 and 3 are both part of the land (s 205(1)(ix) LPA 1925 includes buildings and parts of buildings).

### 2. Why the distinction matters
- **Sale**: fixtures pass to the purchaser automatically (s 62 LPA 1925) unless the contract provides otherwise; chattels stay with the seller. Modern conveyancing forms (lists of fixtures and fittings) mostly pre-empt disputes; common law principles apply where items are omitted.
- **Mortgage**: lenders want items to be fixtures to enlarge their security (*Holland v Hodgson*; *Botham v TSB*).
- **End of lease**: fixtures belong to the landlord, subject to tenant's fixtures exceptions.
- **Statutory status**: in *Elitestone* whether the bungalow was part of the land decided Rent Act protection.

### 3. The test: degree and purpose of annexation
It is a question of fact, decided objectively on all the circumstances, with common sense (*Wessex Reserve Forces and Cadets Association v White* (2006)). Lord Lloyd in *Elitestone Ltd v Morris* [1997] 1 WLR 687, approving Blackburn J in *Holland v Hodgson* (1872): the answer depends on the circumstances "but mainly on two factors, the degree of annexation to the land, and the object of the annexation".

**(a) Degree of annexation**
1. Traditional presumption (Blackburn J): an article attached only by its own weight is generally a chattel unless circumstances show an intention to make it part of the land; an article affixed even slightly is presumed part of the land unless circumstances show it was intended to remain a chattel. The burden lies on the party contending against the presumption.
2. Examples: *Holland v Hodgson* – looms nailed to a mill floor were fixtures; *Leigh v Taylor* (1902) – tapestries tacked to boards nailed to walls were chattels, attachment being as slight as the things allowed and for ornament; *Berkley v Poulett* (1977) – statue on a plinth by its own weight a chattel; *Deen v Andrews* (1985) – freestanding greenhouse on a concrete base a chattel; *Gilpin v Legg* (2017) – beach huts on concrete blocks chattels; *Tower Hamlets LBC v Bromley LBC* (2015) – Henry Moore sculpture resting by its weight a chattel.
3. Modern focus on removal: how easily can it be removed? Would removal require demolition, cause damage, or need a specialist? The harder, the more likely a fixture or part of the land.
4. *Elitestone*: a wooden bungalow resting on concrete pillars, unattached, but removable only by demolition, was part of the land. Lord Lloyd: a house built so it can be removed as a unit or in sections may stay a chattel even if connected to mains services, but a house that can only be removed by destruction "cannot have been intended to remain as a chattel". The landlord's estoppel by convention argument failed: parties' agreements may regulate removal but cannot decide whether the item has become part of the land (citing *Melluish v BMI (No 3)*), an analogy with labels in the lease/licence distinction (Bridge).
5. Contrast: *HE Dibble Ltd v Moore* (1970) – commercial greenhouses moved every few years were chattels; *Chelsea Yacht & Boat Co v Pope* – houseboat moored by ropes and anchor and connected to services was a chattel; *Mew v Tristmire* (2011) – converted landing craft on wooden platforms in a harbour were chattels (removable without undue dismantling; no intention of permanence); *Caddick v Whitsand Bay Holiday Park* (2015) – holiday bungalow removable without damage a chattel; *Spielplatz Ltd v Pearson* (2015) – chalet used as a home on a naturist site part of the land; *Royal Parks v Bluebird Boats* (2021) – boathouse on a concrete slab by the Serpentine part of the land, as removal would destroy it so any re-use would be salvage rather than reinstatement. Bevan's article also notes *Webb v Frank Bevis* (1940) (corrugated iron shed on concrete held a fixture) versus *Billing v Pill* (1954) (army hut bolted to concrete held a chattel).

**(b) Purpose (object) of annexation** – now the more important factor
1. *Hellawell v Eastwood* (1851): if the purpose was temporary and for better enjoyment of the object itself → chattel; if to permanently improve the value, use or enjoyment of the land → fixture.
2. Scarman LJ in *Berkley v Poulett*: a degree of annexation once conclusive "may now prove nothing"; an object heavily attached may still be a chattel if attached for its own better enjoyment; if it cannot be removed without serious damage to the realty "the case for its having become a fixture is a strong one"; an object resting by its own weight can be a fixture if put in place to improve the realty (e.g. part of "a beautiful room as a whole", *Re Whaley*), but prima facie it is not. "If there is no physical annexation there is no fixture."
3. Purpose is inferred objectively – not an inquiry into motive (*Re De Falbe* (1901)); Lord Clyde in *Elitestone*: it is the purpose the object serves, not the purpose of the person who put it there. Relevant circumstances include taste and fashion of the day (*Leigh*, Lord Macnaghten).
4. Architectural design: *D'Eyncourt v Gregory* (1866) – tapestries, statues of lions, stone seats and vases forming part of the architectural design of hall and grounds were fixtures (Lord Romilly MR: whether they were part of the architectural design as distinguished from mere ornaments, not whether cement was used); *Re Whaley* (1908) – tapestries and portraits fixed to create an Elizabethan room were fixtures. In *Berkley* paintings in oak panelling were chattels (hung to be enjoyed as pictures, could be changed); the statue and sundial were chattels though their plinths were fixtures. *Tower Hamlets*: the Moore sculpture was "an entire object in itself", not part of the estate's integral design. *Bluebird Boats*: boathouse built for permanent improvement, inextricably tied to the lakeside concession. The principles of *Berkley* and *D'Eyncourt* were endorsed by the Supreme Court in *Dill v Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government* (Lord Carnwath, [40]–[44]).
5. Items attached merely so they can function (carpets, blinds, display cases) are not necessarily fixtures: *Viscount Hill v Bullock* (1897) – stuffed birds in cases fixed to gallery walls were chattels.

### 4. *Botham v TSB Bank* (1996, CA) – household items
Lender sold a repossessed flat; the court classified items. Roch LJ's indicators: ornamental items attached only to be enjoyed → chattel; ease of movement; damage on removal; **lifespan** (shorter → chattel); **who installed it** (builder → fixture; specialist contractor or occupier → chattel). Results (textbook table): kitchen appliances/white goods – chattels (own weight, short life, easily removed; integrated appliances may differ); kitchen units and fitted sink – fixtures; curtains, blinds, fitted carpets – chattels; light fittings in ceiling recesses – fixtures, track lighting – chattels; decorative gas fireplaces – chattels; bathroom taps, plugs, showerheads, towel rails, soap dishes, toilet-roll holders – fixtures; bathroom mirrors and marble – conceded fixtures. Domestic fittings were "not there … to be enjoyed for themselves" but as lasting improvements.

### 5. Right to remove fixtures
1. **Landowner** may sever a fixture at will, turning it back into a chattel. *The Creative Foundation v Dreamland Leisure* (2015): a tenant wrongly removed rendering bearing a Banksy mural; it was the landlord's fixture and, once removed, remained the landlord's chattel.
2. **Seller**: after contract, the seller cannot remove fixtures in breach of the agreement; fixtures not dealt with pass under s 62 LPA 1925.
3. **Landlord and tenant**: fixtures belong to the landlord at the end of the lease (*Re Roberts, ex p Brook* (1878); *Re Smith* (1974)), except tenant's fixtures: **trade fixtures** (*Mancetter Developments v Garmanson* (1986) – extractor fan); **ornamental and domestic fixtures** removable without damage (*Spyer v Phillipson* (1931)); **agricultural fixtures** (Agricultural Holdings Act 1986; outside the scope of these notes). A tenant removing fixtures must make good damage (*Mancetter*: tenant liable for holes left by removing fan and pipes).

## Key authorities
- *Holland v Hodgson* (1872): the two tests and presumptions.
- *Elitestone Ltd v Morris* [1997] 1 WLR 687 (HL): bungalow on pillars part of the land; threefold classification; removal-only-by-destruction.
- *Berkley v Poulett* (1977, CA): purpose test dominant; statue, sundial and pictures chattels.
- *D'Eyncourt v Gregory* (1866); *Re Whaley* (1908): architectural design makes items fixtures.
- *Leigh v Taylor* (1902): tapestries chattels.
- *Botham v TSB* (1996, CA): household items; lifespan and installer factors.
- *Tower Hamlets v Bromley* (2015): public sculpture a chattel.
- *Royal Parks v Bluebird Boats* (2021): boathouse part of the land.
- *Chelsea Yacht v Pope*; *Mew v Tristmire* (2011): houseboats chattels.
- *Dill* (UKSC): endorsement of *Berkley*/*D'Eyncourt*.
- *Mancetter v Garmanson* (1986); *Spyer v Phillipson* (1931): tenant's fixtures.

## Debate and critique
- **Incoherence** (Bevan, "The law of fixtures and chattels: recalibration, rationalisation and reform" (2022) 42 Legal Studies 358): the law is criticised as "complex and confusing", "elusive" and lacking uniformity. Bevan identifies (a) confusion over the role of the degree test – some cases demand physical annexation, others find fixtures with none, and recent cases (*Elitestone*, *Chelsea Yacht*, *Mew*) paradoxically revive it; (b) a growth of complex, arbitrary analytical factors (lifespan, identity of installer), which shift focus from the item itself to who put it there; (c) lingering uncertainty about intention – Blackburn J's references to intention in *Holland* introduced confusion; Luther shows little reliance on intention in earlier case law and argues Blackburn J's over-general statement increased uncertainty; courts insist the test is objective, yet parties' agreements and subjective intentions keep resurfacing (e.g. *Hobson v Gorringe*). The "architectural design" idea is especially problematic (compare *D'Eyncourt*/*Re Whaley* with *Tower Hamlets*).
- **Botham's arbitrariness**: how can soap dishes and toilet-roll holders be fixtures but light fittings and fires chattels? Haley: it is "a sad commentary" on the law that the Court of Appeal must decide whether a sink and cooker are fixtures. The question is whether the principles are clear and coherent.
- **Bevan's reform model**: a heavy but rebuttable presumption that all items brought onto land are chattels, rebuttable by (1) an agreement (express, implied or inferred) that the item is a fixture, or (2) the court finding removal unreasonable or inequitable (e.g. affecting the structure or fabric). Subjective intentions/agreements would be admissible (drawing on Peter Butt's Australian work). Claimed advantages: simplicity, predictability, encouraging written agreements, while keeping flexibility for retro-fitted items and public art.
- **Bridge (1997) CLJ** welcomed Lord Lloyd's speech as a clear statement steering between extremes of authority and supported the "part and parcel" category, as it is inelegant to call a dwelling a fixture.
- Further reading: Haley [1998] Conv 137; Conway [1998] Conv 418; Iljadica [2016] Conv 242; Bevan [2021] Conv 401 (on *Bluebird Boats*); Luther (2004) 24 OJLS 597.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the context: sale, mortgage, end of lease, or statute. Check whether a contract or fixtures list deals with the item first.
2. Degree: how is the item attached? Resting by its own weight → presumption of chattel (*Holland*). Can it be removed easily, without damage, without a specialist? Would removal destroy it (*Elitestone*, *Bluebird*)?
3. Purpose: for the better enjoyment of the item, or permanent improvement of the land (*Hellawell*; *Berkley*)? Part of an architectural design (*D'Eyncourt*)? Merely attached so it can function (*Viscount Hill*)?
4. Botham factors: lifespan; who installed it; domestic versus business; ornamental or functional.
5. If a fixture and a lease is ending, is it a trade, ornamental/domestic or agricultural fixture the tenant can remove? Damage must be made good.
6. Example: industrial washing machines bolted to the floor (for vibration, by skilled installers, removable without damage) – arguable either way: bolting is attachment but purpose may be the better use of the machines (cf. *Holland* looms versus *Botham* white goods); freestanding air-conditioners on concrete platforms and portable floodlights – likely chattels.
7. Trap: do not treat the degree test as decisive; purpose usually carries more weight, and acknowledge judicial uncertainty with alternative outcomes.
