# Consent as a Defence to Non-Fatal Offences Against the Person

## Overview
Consent makes the application of force lawful, but how far it does so depends on the level of harm. For assault and battery, valid (express or implied, and effective) consent is a defence. For ABH and above (ss47, 20 and 18 OAPA 1861), *R v Brown* [1994] 1 AC 212 holds that consent is a defence only if the conduct falls within a recognised category of exception, such as surgery, tattooing, sport, horseplay or (in limited form) the risk of sexually transmitted infection. These categories rest on public policy rather than a single principle. This is one of the most debated areas in the module: autonomy against paternalism and legal moralism, the inconsistency between *Brown*, *Wilson* and *Emmett*, and the statutory "rough sex" provision in s71 of the Domestic Abuse Act 2021.

## The law

### 1. The general principle
1. **Assault and battery**: consent is a defence if it is (i) express or implied and (ii) effective.
2. **ABH, wounding, GBH**: consent is a defence only if it is (i) express or implied, (ii) effective, *and* (iii) D's conduct falls within a recognised category of exception (*Brown*).
3. Consent is a specific defence to offences against the person, not a general defence. The secondary notes describe it as an area where public policy plays a key role.

### 2. Express and implied consent
- **Express**: V consents by words or conduct (e.g. offering a hand to shake).
- **Implied**: everyday contact is impliedly consented to by everyone who moves in society, such as jostling in a crowd or on public transport, a New Year's embrace, or a pat on the back. *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 3 All ER 374: the test is whether the contact has gone beyond generally acceptable standards of conduct, which depends on the facts.
- *Wood v DPP* [2008] EWHC 1056 (Admin): the contact must genuinely fall within everyday activity. A police officer restraining someone he was not arresting could not rely on implied consent.

### 3. Effective consent
1. **Capacity.** Historically capacity was based on status (*Burrell v Harmer* [1967] Crim LR 169). The more nuanced modern approach draws on the Mental Capacity Act 2005: a person lacks capacity if, at the material time, they cannot make the decision because of an impairment of, or disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain. The secondary notes add that criminal courts are not bound by the Act's definition.
2. **Informed consent.** V must know what the conduct involves before consenting, including risks D has special knowledge of (*Konzani* [2005] EWCA Crim 706).
3. **Fraud.**
   - *As to identity*: consent to touching by X is not consent to touching by D impersonating X. In *Richardson* [1998] 2 Cr App R 200 (also cited [1999] QB 444), patients of a suspended dentist made no mistake as to identity, because they consented to treatment by a dentist and received it. *Melin* [2019] EWCA Crim 557: D falsely presented himself as medically qualified to give Botox injections. The court distinguished *passive* fraud (failure to inform) from *active* fraud (deliberately supplying misinformation).
   - *As to nature*: the deception must go to the core nature of the act, not a collateral matter. In *Mobilio* [1991] 1 VR 339, V consented to an instrument being inserted for medical reasons, but D acted for sexual gratification. Consent was not vitiated, because the act was the same one V had agreed to.
4. **Duress.** Apparent consent obtained by threats is not effective. The consent must be caused by D's threat rather than other factors, and the gravity of the threat is weighed against the act consented to (*Jones and Others* (1986) 83 Cr App R 375). The secondary notes say it is unclear whether this test is objective or subjective.
5. **Belief in consent.** D escapes liability if D genuinely, even unreasonably, believed V consented (*Jones and Others*). This is rarely relied on in practice.

### 4. *R v Brown* [1994] 1 AC 212 (HL)
- **Facts**: a group of men took part in consensual sadomasochistic acts causing ABH and wounding, which they video-recorded. They were charged under ss47 and 20 and pleaded guilty after the trial judge's ruling that consent was no defence.
- **Certified question**: in a sadomasochistic encounter causing ABH or wounding, must the prosecution prove lack of consent?
- **Majority (3:2)**: no. Consent is a defence to assault or battery, but for ABH or worse it is available only within an accepted category of exception. Lord Jauncey drew the line between common assault and s47.
- **Minority**: the activities were consensual and private, and should not be criminal without some clear public harm.
- See also *Attorney General's Reference (No 6 of 1980)* [1981] QB 715 (public interest), and Domestic Abuse Act 2021, s71(2).

### 5. Recognised categories of exception
1. **Surgery**: lawful surgical operations, including gender reassignment surgery (*Corbett v Corbett* [1971]), cosmetic surgery and organ transplants. Body modification by non-medical practitioners falls outside this category (see BM below).
2. **Piercing, tattooing, haircutting** (whether or not done professionally): haircutting (*DPP v Smith* [2006] EWHC 94 (Admin)), ritual (non-medical) circumcision, ear-piercing and tattooing.
   - *Wilson* [1996] Crim LR 573: a husband branded his initials on his wife's buttocks with a hot knife at her request. The Court of Appeal allowed his appeal against an ABH conviction, treating the conduct as akin to tattooing.
   - *BM* [2018] EWCA Crim 560: a "body modification artist" removed an ear and a nipple and split a tongue, all at the clients' request. He was charged under s18. Consent was no defence, and there was "no good reason" to treat body modification as a special exemption. Routine piercing and tattooing remain lawful, but "extreme" procedures do not, and the middle ground is left unclear.
3. **Religious flagellation**: beating or whipping in religious ritual. Lord Mustill in *Brown* mentioned dangerous pastimes, bravado and religious mortification as situations where a person may agree to harm or the risk of it.
4. **Sports**:
   - the sport must be one the law recognises as lawful (e.g. well-established sports with rule-making bodies); injury in an unrecognised sport is not validly consented to;
   - harm caused within the rules, in consensual play, is impliedly consented to. The secondary notes add that harm *intentionally* inflicted, even within the rules, may fall outside;
   - injuries from conduct outside the rules can still be within consent if they are "legitimate" foul play, e.g. inadvertent or reckless conduct such as a late tackle;
   - an offence arises where D deliberately breaches the rules so badly that the conduct is not validly consented to (*Barnes* [2005] 1 WLR 910). The relevant factors are the type of sport, the level of play, the nature of the act, the degree of force, the extent of the risk of injury and D's state of mind.
5. **Horseplay**: rough, undisciplined play. A genuine, even unreasonable, belief in V's consent suffices, but intentionally inflicted injury loses the character of "play".
   - *Jones* [1987] Crim LR 123: schoolboys tossed in the air and dropped.
   - *Aitken* [1992] 1 WLR 1006: RAF officers set fire to each other's fire-resistant clothing, causing severe burns. Consent, or a belief in consent whether reasonable or not, could negate the offence.
   - *A* [2005]: D dropped a non-swimmer, who had been struggling against him, off a bridge, and V drowned. A belief in consent was not credible.
6. **Sexually transmitted infections**:
   - *Dica* [2004] QB 1257: an adult can validly consent to the *risk* of infection with a potentially lethal STI such as HIV, if the consent is informed. On the facts, D had concealed his HIV status, so there was no informed consent and his appeal failed. The reasons given were that criminalising consensual risk-taking would be impractical to enforce, and that sexual relationships are "pre-eminently private". This sits awkwardly with *Brown*.
   - *Konzani* [2005] EWCA Crim 706: D had unprotected sex with three women without disclosing his HIV status, and all three were infected. Consent to the general risks of unprotected sex was not consent to the specific risk of HIV transmission. For an honest belief in consent to go to the jury, there must be evidence on which D could honestly believe V had been informed of the risk, thought about it and accepted it. V can be "informed" without D disclosing, e.g. through a third party or obvious circumstances (para 44).
   - CPS guidance: consent to the specific risk is a defence to *reckless* transmission (s20), and once raised the prosecution must disprove it. It is not a defence to *intentional* transmission (s18) (citing *Donovan* [1934] 2 KB 498; *AG's Ref (No 6 of 1980)*).

### 6. Domestic Abuse Act 2021, s71
- For ss18, 20 and 47 OAPA, it is not a defence that V consented to the infliction of serious harm (ABH, wounding or GBH) for the purposes of obtaining sexual gratification (s71(2)).
- It does not matter whose sexual gratification was in issue (D's, V's or someone else's).
- There is an exception for ss20 and 47 where the harm results from STI infection during sexual activity to which V consented knowing or believing D had the STI.
- Other rules on consent to serious harm are unaffected. The provision puts the *Brown* position on a statutory footing for sexual contexts.

### 7. Lawful chastisement of children
Children Act 2004, s58: battery of a child cannot be justified as reasonable punishment for ss18, 20 and 47 OAPA or for child cruelty under s1 of the Children and Young Persons Act 1933. In England the defence therefore survives only for parents, only for assault and battery, and only where the force was reasonable, proportionate and free of cruelty. In Wales all physical punishment of children is unlawful under the Children (Abolition of Defence of Reasonable Punishment) (Wales) Act 2020.

### 8. Necessity
*Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1990] 2 AC 1, per Lord Goff: necessity can justify otherwise unlawful action taken to assist another person without their consent. For example, there is no assault where D drags V out of the path of an oncoming vehicle. See duress-and-necessity.md.

## Key authorities
- *Collins v Wilcock* [1984]: implied consent to everyday contact.
- *Konzani* [2005] EWCA Crim 706: consent must be informed as to the specific risk.
- *Richardson* [1998]; *Melin* [2019]: fraud as to identity or qualifications.
- *Mobilio* [1991]: fraud must go to the nature of the act.
- *Jones and Others* (1986): duress vitiates consent. An unreasonable belief in consent may suffice.
- *R v Brown* [1994] 1 AC 212: no consent to ABH or above outside recognised exceptions. Sadomasochism is not an exception.
- *Wilson* [1996]: consensual branding by a spouse treated as akin to tattooing.
- *Emmett* (1999): consensual burning with lighter fuel; ABH conviction upheld as going beyond *Wilson*.
- *BM* [2018] EWCA Crim 560: body modification is not an exception. New exceptions are for Parliament.
- *Barnes* [2005] 1 WLR 910: the sports test.
- *Jones* [1987]; *Aitken* [1992]; *A* [2005]: horseplay.
- *Dica* [2004]; *Konzani* [2005]: STI transmission.
- *Re F* [1990] 2 AC 1: necessity.
- Domestic Abuse Act 2021, s71; Children Act 2004, s58.

## Debate and critique
- **Fixed categories.** *BM* states that new exceptions should not be recognised case by case, except perhaps by close analogy. Recognising a new one requires a policy-laden value judgment better made in the political arena than in a criminal trial.
- **No unifying principle.** *BM* also accepts that the exceptions do not yield an easily stated principle. Two features underpin most of them: discernible social benefit, and that it would be unreasonable for the common law to criminalise the activity when the injured party consented.
- **Inconsistency in sexual cases.** Lord Templeman in *Brown*: society may protect itself against "a cult of violence", and pleasure from inflicting pain is "an evil thing". Contrast Lord Russell in *Wilson*: consensual activity between spouses in the matrimonial home is not normally a proper matter for criminal investigation. *Emmett* then upheld an ABH conviction because it went beyond *Wilson*. Critics say the line between *Brown* and *Wilson* reflects attitudes to sexuality and marriage rather than harm.
- **Legal moralism and paternalism in *Brown*.** Moralism: the speeches described the conduct as an "indulgence of cruelty" and "perverted and depraved sexual desire" (Lord Lowry). Paternalism: concern for the younger participants and for spill-over harms (Lord Templeman's fear that it might "breed and glorify violence").
- **Feminist critique.** "Consensual" sadomasochism may disguise domestic violence or the abuse of vulnerable people. In both *Wilson* and *Emmett* the defendant claimed consent but the victim did not give evidence in support. Susan Edwards ("Consent and the 'Rough Sex' Defence") documents how, in male-on-female intimate partner killings, especially strangulation, older excuses such as loss of control or infidelity are being replaced by "she consented to rough sex". Because the dead cannot answer, this narrative is hard to rebut and can reduce sentences. She describes the reform campaign and the Domestic Abuse Bill clause providing "no defence for consent to death". s71 DAA 2021 is the enacted response for serious harm.
- **Social utility v social disutility (Farrugia).** Paul Farrugia ("The Consent Defence: Sports Violence, Sadomasochism, and the Criminal Law") contrasts the "social utility" test (does the activity's benefit outweigh its harm?) with Lord Mustill's "social disutility" approach in *Brown*, under which the law should interfere only where the conduct causes harm justifying criminalisation. He notes Kell's support for the latter as better balancing autonomy and social needs. His conclusion: society tolerates sports violence because of the benefits of contact sport, but sport is "not a licence to abandon the restraints of civilisation". The State's paternalistic role will refuse to recognise consent where harm outweighs benefit. Some activities, such as bare-knuckle fighting and duelling, are treated as too dangerous to consent to.
- **Law Commission Consultation Paper No 139 (Consent in the Criminal Law).** The Commission's starting principle is that a person with capacity should be able to give legally effective consent to any injury up to the level of "seriously disabling injury". It is reluctant to decriminalise consensual seriously disabling injury outside regulated contexts (surgery, risky sports), because without regulation it cannot be sure consent is fully voluntary. It provisionally proposed keeping the criminalisation of injury in casual fighting, to prevent harm to others. It uses "Class I/II/III" labels for exceptions that raise the permitted level of injury, keep it as now, or set age limits. It also considered the secondary liability of the willing "victims" in *Brown*, noting that 26 participants were cautioned for aiding and abetting offences against themselves.
- **Autonomy v welfare.** The *Dica* reasoning (privacy, impracticality) stresses autonomy. *Brown* and *BM* stress welfare and moral or policy limits. the materials also points to Tolmie (2012) on moving away from category-based decision-making.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the level of harm** first, because it decides the structure. Battery or assault: did V consent expressly or impliedly (everyday contact, *Collins v Wilcock*)? ABH or above: is there a recognised category?
2. **Was consent effective?** Check capacity (age, intoxication, mental impairment), informed consent (specific risks: *Konzani*), fraud (identity: *Melin*; nature: *Mobilio*) and duress.
3. **Pick the category.** Sport: was it a lawful sport, and was the conduct within the rules or legitimate foul play, or a deliberate act going beyond what was consented to (*Barnes* factors)? Horseplay: was there a genuine belief in consent, and was the injury inflicted intentionally? Tattoo or body modification: compare *Wilson* and *BM*. Sexual context: s71 DAA 2021 excludes consent for ABH or above. STI: was there informed consent to the specific risk (s20 only)?
4. **Fights.** A consensual fist-fight outside a recognised sport falls into none of the listed categories (and Law Com CP 139 proposed keeping injury in casual fighting criminal), so a fractured skull in a changing-room fight is not excused by consent.
5. **Lecture exercise (football).** The friendly pre-match slap on the back is implied consent to everyday or sporting contact, so no battery. The "teach a lesson" hard tackle causing bruised shins is arguably ABH and arguably deliberate, which points outside consent under *Barnes*. The agreed fist-fight causing a fractured skull (GBH) cannot be consented to. Also check mens rea under s20 or s47 and causation (hitting his head on the bench).
6. **Belief in consent** (*Jones*; *Aitken*) is a route out even where V did not consent, but only within categories where consent could be given.
7. **Lawful chastisement**: in England only for parents, only for battery, only reasonable force. In Wales it is abolished.
