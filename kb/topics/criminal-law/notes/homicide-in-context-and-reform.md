# Homicide in Context, Sentencing and Reform

## Overview
This note puts the doctrinal offences (murder, voluntary manslaughter and involuntary manslaughter) in context. It covers the other statutory homicide offences, homicide statistics, sentencing (especially the mandatory life sentence and minimum terms) and release, and the long-running reform debate. That debate centres on the Law Commission's 2006 report *Murder, Manslaughter and Infanticide* (Law Com No 304), which proposed a three-tier "ladder" of first degree murder, second degree murder and manslaughter. It was largely not adopted. Parliament instead reformed only the partial defences in the Coroners and Justice Act 2009. The discussion asks students to "critically assess the extent to which the law of homicide in England and Wales is in need of reform", so this note is the essay hub.

## The law

### 1. The structure of homicide
1. **Murder**: unlawful killing with intent to kill or cause GBH (murder.md).
2. **Voluntary manslaughter**: murder reduced by loss of control, diminished responsibility or suicide pact (loss-of-control.md, diminished-responsibility.md).
3. **Involuntary manslaughter**: the actus reus of murder without its mens rea. This covers unlawful act manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter (unlawful-act-manslaughter.md, gross-negligence-manslaughter.md).

### 2. Other homicide offences
- Corporate manslaughter (Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007).
- Driving offences: causing death by dangerous driving (Road Traffic Act 1988, s1), by careless driving (s2B), or while under the influence (s3A).
- Infanticide (Infanticide Act 1938).
- Child destruction (Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929).

### 3. Statistics (April 2022 to March 2023)
- 590 recorded homicides, down from 684.
- 9.9 per million population overall: 14.2 for males and 6.0 for females. For comparison, 63 per million in the US and 3.9 in Malta.
- 51 victims aged 13-19.
- 82% involved a knife or sharp instrument.
- 100 domestic homicides (17%): 70 female victims and 30 male victims.
- The discussion also covers long-term trends, who kills whom, how and where (charts not reproduced in the source).

### 4. Sentencing for homicide
**Murder: mandatory sentence.**
- Under 18: detention at His Majesty's pleasure.
- 18-21: custody for life.
- 21 and over: mandatory life sentence.

**Minimum term.** Before 2003 this was set by the Home Secretary. Since 2003 it has been set by the trial judge, and the High Court "reset" pre-2003 terms. It is a *minimum*, not a release date. The starting points are in the Sentencing Act 2020, Sch 21:
| Starting point | Who/what |
|---|---|
| Whole life order | aged 21+, seriousness "exceptionally high" (para 2) |
| 30 years | aged 18+, seriousness "particularly high" (para 3) |
| 25 years | aged 18+, knife or other weapon taken to the scene (para 4) |
| 15 years | aged 18+, other cases (para 5) |
| 12 years | under 18 (para 6) |

Aggravating and mitigating factors (para 8) can move the term to any length. Premeditation is an aggravating feature .

**Manslaughter**: the maximum is life imprisonment, and sentencing is discretionary. The Sentencing Council guidelines cover unlawful act, gross negligence, loss of control and DR manslaughter separately. The "Reflections" article reports ranges of 1-24 years for unlawful act and 1-18 years for gross negligence. It notes rising manslaughter sentence levels influenced by *Appleby* (2009). It identifies gaps: no guideline for suicide-pact or corporate manslaughter, and no explicit proportionality with Sch 21 murder terms or with causing death by dangerous driving. For DR the starting point for a single offence is 7 years (range 3-12), and hospital orders are available (*Calocane* [2024] EWCA Crim 490).

### 5. Release of life sentence prisoners
- After the minimum term, release is decided by the Parole Board, which must be satisfied that confinement is no longer necessary for the protection of the public.
- Release is on life licence, with recall to prison possible.

### 6. Law Commission No 304 (2006): the proposed ladder
1. **First degree murder** (mandatory life):
   - (a) killing intentionally;
   - (b) killing with intent to do serious injury, coupled with awareness of a serious risk of causing death.
2. **Second degree murder** (discretionary life maximum):
   - (a) killing with intent to do serious injury;
   - (b) killing with intent to cause some injury, or a fear or risk of injury, while aware of a serious risk of causing death;
   - (c) killing where a partial defence applies to what would otherwise be first degree murder.
3. **Manslaughter** (discretionary life maximum):
   - (a) killing through gross negligence as to a risk of causing death;
   - (b) killing through a criminal act intended to cause injury, or where D was aware the act involved a serious risk of causing injury;
   - (c) participating in a joint criminal venture in which another participant commits first or second degree murder, where it should have been obvious that this might happen.
4. **Partial defences** (provocation, meaning gross provocation or fear of serious violence; diminished responsibility; suicide pact) would reduce first degree murder to second degree murder only.

The Consultation Paper (CP No 177, 2005, *A New Homicide Act for England and Wales?*) had preceded the report. The Government responded in 2008 (Ministry of Justice CP 19/08) and enacted only the partial-defence reforms in the CJA 2009.

## Key authorities
- Sentencing Act 2020, Sch 21: murder minimum-term starting points.
- Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007; Road Traffic Act 1988 ss1, 2B, 3A; Infanticide Act 1938; Infant Life (Preservation) Act 1929: other homicide offences.
- Law Com No 304 (2006): the three-tier ladder. Law Com CP 177 (2005).
- Coroners and Justice Act 2009, ss52-56: the reforms actually enacted.
- *Appleby* (2009): rising manslaughter sentences (per the sentencing guidelines article).
- *Calocane* [2024] EWCA Crim 490: DR manslaughter and hospital orders.

## Debate and critique
- **The mandatory life sentence**. It is a political compromise given the alternative (the death penalty had been abolished). It is "draconian" given that release is possible anyway. It covers a broad spectrum of murderers, from sadistic killers to mercy killers. There is no judicial discretion except over the minimum term.
- **Mitigation and extenuating circumstances.** One option is to allow a finding of extenuating circumstances that prevents the mandatory life sentence. Lord Lloyd's amendment to the 2009 Bill for an "extenuating circumstances" rider had wide support in the Lords but was rejected (Miles). The Law Commission evidence suggested public opinion is more nuanced than Government assumed.
- **Intention to cause GBH: a fair label?** The GBH rule labels as murderers people who never foresaw death (Lord Goff; Mitchell; see murder.md). LC 304 would move intent-to-cause-serious-injury killings without awareness of a serious risk of death into second degree murder.
- **Laddering and fair labelling (Tadros, "The Homicide Ladder" (2006) 69 MLR 601).** Victor Tadros describes the existing law as "the worst of both worlds". Some morally significant mens rea concepts are picked out but poorly defined, a narrow range of partial defences does too little, and the rigid tariff structure from the 2003 Act makes the results likely to be systematically unfair. Reform must choose between precisely defined categories (consistent but blind to some moral factors) and broad moral standards (flexible but inconsistent between juries). He welcomes the Commission's use of intent to kill to define the top tier and its wider partial defences. But he argues the definitions exclude too much morally relevant information, the partial defences are still too narrow, and, because they apply only to first degree murder (where intent to kill will rarely be proved), the line between second degree murder and manslaughter would track moral differences only crudely. He rejects the Commission's argument that allowing partial defences against second degree murder would require them for all offences. He questions why duress alone is a complete defence to homicide offences and raises the possibility that duress is sometimes a justification.
- **Rogers, "The Law Commission's Proposed Restructuring of Homicide" (2006) 70 J Crim L 223.** Jonathan Rogers supports the restructuring. He recommends concrete guidance for when jurors agree on second degree murder for different reasons. He argues that the Commission's reasons for not considering whether consent to be killed should reduce first degree murder to second degree murder do not stand up, and he sketches such a partial defence. He calls for the definition of "reckless indifference" to be clarified, noting common issues across oblique intention, reckless indifference and gross negligence.
- **Murder 1, Murder 2 and manslaughter: principle v pragmatism.** This commentary on LC 304 argues that putting intent to cause serious injury plus awareness of risk in first degree murder rests on a "moral equivalence" with intent to kill, but blurs the line between first and second degree murder and risks inconsistent verdicts. "Serious injury" is undefined, and "serious risk" asks jurors to balance probability and moral judgment. It reports Ashworth's view that the definition of intent should be tightened for first degree murder.
- **The "dog's breakfast" (Miles) and imperfect justification (Norrie).** Parliament took only the partial-defence reforms, and critics describe the result as incoherent (see loss-of-control.md). Norrie notes that LC 304 justified provocation pragmatically, as mitigation in the face of the mandatory sentence, whereas LC 290 (2004) relied on a more principled justificatory theory.
- **Gender and partial defences.** The reform debate lists gender bias and qualifying triggers, and asks whether justice is seen to be done (see loss-of-control.md).
- **Children.** The Law Commission's proposal to recognise developmental immaturity within DR was rejected, and doli incapax was abolished (Crime and Disorder Act 1998 s34; *T* [2009]), as noted by Miles.

## Applying it to a problem question
Problem questions rarely test this note directly, but it shapes conclusions:
1. **Classify the killing correctly.** Murder (intent to kill or GBH)? Voluntary manslaughter (partial defence)? Involuntary manslaughter (no murder mens rea: unlawful act or gross negligence)? Or a statutory homicide (driving, corporate)?
2. **State the consequence.** Murder means a mandatory life sentence with a Sch 21 minimum term. Note the 25-year starting point if D took a knife to the scene. Manslaughter means discretionary sentencing up to life.
3. **Advise strategically.** The model note suggests that where a partial defence is doubtful, an early guilty plea to manslaughter may be considered. For DR, mention hospital disposals.
4. **For essays**, use the ladder (LC 304), Tadros' "worst of both worlds" critique, the GBH rule debate (Goff), the 2009 partial-defence reforms (Norrie, Miles), the mandatory life sentence, and the Government's failure to implement LC 304.
