# R v Jones and Smith [1976] 1 WLR 672 (CA) (case note)

> Caution: written from secondary sources; the original judgment was not read. Check any wording or pinpoint against the report before citing.

## Overview

A Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) decision of 9 March 1976, also reported at [1976] 3 All ER 54 and (1976) 63 Cr App R 47 (and sometimes listed as R v Jones (John)). It is a leading authority on what "enters ... as a trespasser" means in burglary under s 9 of the Theft Act 1968. The court held that a general permission to enter does not protect someone who enters beyond the scope of that permission, provided he knows this or is reckless about it.

## Facts

- In the early hours of 10 May 1975 police noticed a car with a television set protruding from it. Two television sets taken from the house of Smith's father were found in the car.
- Smith told police that they had gone in and taken the sets to look after them.
- Smith and Jones were charged with burglary contrary to s 9(1)(b) of the Theft Act 1968 (having entered as a trespasser, stealing).
- The defence relied on the father's general permission for his son to come and go. The father was called by the Crown and treated as a hostile witness. He gave evidence that his son "would not be a trespasser in the house at any time".
- Both were convicted. On appeal, the main argument was that a person with general permission to enter cannot enter "as a trespasser".

## Held

Appeals dismissed; the judgment of the court was given by James LJ.

- A person is a trespasser for the purposes of s 9(1)(b) if he enters the premises of another knowing that he is entering in excess of the permission given to him, or being reckless as to whether he is doing so.
- What matters is the facts known to the accused. If they enable him to realise he is going beyond the permission, or he is reckless about that, a jury may find that he entered as a trespasser. Whether he did is a question of fact for the jury.
- So the general permission to enter the father's house did not prevent a burglary conviction. The jury was entitled to find that the defendants knew the permission did not extend to entering to take the sets.
- The reasoning builds on R v Collins [1973] QB 100 (trespass in s 9 requires a mental element). It also draws on the civil-law idea that an invitation for one purpose does not cover another: "when you invite a person into your house to use the staircase you do not invite him to slide down the banisters" (the analogy associated with Hillen and Pettigrew v ICI (Alkali) Ltd [1936] AC 65).

## Later treatment

- It remains the standard authority for the "exceeding permission" route to trespass and is routinely paired with R v Collins in accounts of burglary.
- It has been cited outside England, including by the High Court of Australia.

## Applying it to a problem question

1. Identify the permission: who gave it, whether it was express or implied, and what purposes it covered. Permission to enter a home, shop or workplace is usually limited by purpose.
2. Ask whether D's purpose on entry fell outside it. Purpose is usually inferred from evidence such as the time of entry, any tools carried, and what D did inside.
3. Apply the mental element: D must know, or be reckless as to, the facts that put the entry beyond the permission. A defendant who honestly believes the entry is within the permission, and is not reckless about it, does not enter as a trespasser.
4. Treat each defendant separately: a companion's liability turns on his own knowledge or recklessness about the scope of the permission.
5. The "entry as a trespasser" element is common to s 9(1)(a) and (b), so the reasoning can be used under either limb, even though Jones and Smith was a s 9(1)(b) case.
6. Present the conclusion as what a jury could properly find, since trespass is a question of fact.
