# R v Cheshire [1991] 1 WLR 844 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1991] 3 All ER 670; 93 Cr App R 251) before citing them.
> The two accounts differ on whether the trial direction counted as a misdirection; see Held.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), 1991. The case is the leading statement on when **negligent medical treatment**
breaks the chain of causation between the defendant's injury and the victim's death.

## Facts
Cheshire shot a man during an argument. The victim had surgery and was fitted with a tracheotomy tube. He later
developed breathing difficulties, first put down to anxiety, and died some weeks after the shooting. A post-mortem
found his windpipe had narrowed near the tracheotomy site, and medical evidence attributed the death to the failure to
diagnose the cause of his breathlessness. The trial judge told the jury that medical negligence could break the chain
only if it amounted to recklessness. Cheshire was convicted of murder.

## Held
- Appeal **dismissed**. The defendant's act need not be the sole or main cause of death; it must make a **significant**
  contribution.
- Negligent treatment, even if the immediate cause of death, relieves the defendant only if it was **so independent of
  his acts, and in itself so potent in causing death**, that the contribution of his acts was insignificant.
- The focus is the causal effect of the treatment, not the degree of fault of the medical staff. (LawTeacher treats the
  recklessness direction as wrong but harmless; Wikipedia records the misdirection argument as rejected.)

## Why it matters
- Confirms that only extraordinary or independent medical treatment will be a *novus actus interveniens*.
- Compare *R v Smith* [1959] (earlier treatment case) and *Blaue* (the victim's own choices).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Did D's act significantly contribute to death (not de minimis)?
2. Was the treatment negligent? If so, was it so independent and potent that D's contribution became insignificant?
3. Only treatment that is independent of the original injury and itself potent in causing death will break the chain.
