# R v Quick [1973] QB 910 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1973] 3 WLR 26; [1973] 3 All ER 347; 57 Cr App R 722) before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), reported as *R v Quick and Paddison*. The case distinguishes **insane** from
**non-insane (sane) automatism**: a malfunction caused by an external factor, here prescribed insulin, is not a
"disease of the mind", so automatism should go to the jury.

## Facts
Quick, a diabetic nurse at a mental hospital, was charged with assaulting a patient (OAPA 1861 s 47) causing black
eyes, bruising and a broken nose. He said he could not remember the events and had been in a hypoglycaemic state
after taking insulin, eating little and drinking some alcohol. The trial judge ruled that the evidence could support
only a defence of insanity, so Quick changed his plea to guilty and appealed. Paddison, charged as a secondary party,
was also convicted.

## Held
- The trial judge was wrong. The alleged mental condition, if it existed, arose from the **external** factor of
  insulin prescribed by a doctor, not from the diabetes itself; automatism should have been left to the jury.
- Convictions of Quick and Paddison **quashed** as unsatisfactory. The court did not decide that automatism was made
  out.
- Obiter: a self-induced incapacity, or one that could reasonably have been foreseen (for example, failing to eat
  properly after insulin), does not excuse. The jury would have to consider whether he recognised the onset of the
  episode and failed to take advised precautions.

## Why it matters
- Fixes the internal/external distinction for diabetic episodes: **hypoglycaemia** (insulin) is generally treated as
  automatism, whereas conditions arising from the internal disease may fall within insanity.
- The self-induced limit was developed in *R v Bailey* [1983].

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the cause of the loss of control: external (insulin, a blow, a drug) or internal (the disease)?
2. External cause: sane automatism, if control was lost entirely; consider whether the defendant was at fault in
   bringing it about.
3. Internal cause: consider insanity (see the insanity note).
