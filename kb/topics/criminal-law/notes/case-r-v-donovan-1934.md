# R v Donovan [1934] 2 KB 498 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (KB pages 498–510) before citing them in coursework.

## Overview
Court of Criminal Appeal (Lord Hewart CJ, Swift and du Parcq JJ; judgment of Swift J). The case is cited for two
points: the **meaning of "bodily harm"** (the basis of actual bodily harm under OAPA 1861 s 47), and the rule that
**consent is no answer** where blows are likely or intended to cause bodily harm, subject to recognised exceptions.

## Facts
Donovan caned a 17-year-old girl in a garage for his sexual gratification. He was charged with indecent assault and
common assault. The issue at trial was whether she consented; a doctor who saw her two days later found marks
consistent with a fairly severe beating. He was convicted on both counts.

## Held
- **Bodily harm** includes any hurt or injury calculated to interfere with the health or comfort of the victim. It need
  not be permanent but must be more than merely transient and trifling.
- As a general rule, subject to exceptions (e.g. lawful sports and rough play), it is unlawful to beat another with
  violence likely or intended to cause bodily harm, and the other's **consent does not make it lawful**.
- The jury should therefore have been asked, first, whether the blows were likely or intended to cause bodily harm
  (if so, consent is irrelevant); only if not, whether the prosecution had disproved consent.
- The first question was never left to the jury and the directions on consent (burden of proof; the girl's conduct
  as a whole) were inadequate, so the **conviction was quashed**.

## Why it matters
- Standard authority for the ABH threshold: harm "more than transient or trifling" (used in the course seminar to
  treat a gash needing stitches as ABH).
- A starting point for the **consent / public-policy line** later developed in *Attorney-General's Reference (No 6 of
  1980)* and *R v Brown* [1994] 1 AC 212.
- Procedural lesson: an appellate court cannot fill in a factual question the jury was never asked.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the injury more than transient or trifling? (bruises, cuts needing treatment → ABH.)
2. If the harm was likely or intended, consent is only a defence within a recognised exception (sport, surgery,
   tattooing, horseplay…), see the consent-defence note.
