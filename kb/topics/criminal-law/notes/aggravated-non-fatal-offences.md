# Aggravated Non-Fatal Offences (ABH, s20 and s18 GBH)

## Overview
Above common assault and battery, the Offences against the Person Act 1861 (OAPA) provides three main aggravated offences: assault occasioning actual bodily harm (s47), unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm (s20), and wounding or causing GBH with intent (s18). Together they form a "ladder" of seriousness, but their wording is Victorian, their mens rea requirements do not match their harms, and the ladder has been criticised as "broken". Problem questions usually involve choosing the right rung and applying each element precisely. Essays focus on the case for reform.

## The law

### 1. Section 47: assault occasioning actual bodily harm
**Statute.** OAPA 1861, s47: whoever is convicted on indictment of any assault occasioning actual bodily harm is liable to penal servitude. It is triable either way. The maximum is five years on indictment, and six months and/or an unlimited fine summarily.

**Actus reus.**
1. A **base offence**: D either caused V to apprehend immediate unlawful force (assault) or applied unlawful force to V (battery). See assault-and-battery.md.
2. **Occasioning** = causing. This is a matter of ordinary causation (*Savage, Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699). In *Roberts* (1972) 56 Cr App R 95, V jumped from a moving car to escape D's sexual advances. D was liable unless V's reaction was so unexpected that no reasonable person could have foreseen it (see causation.md).
3. **Actual bodily harm.**
   - *Donovan* [1934] 2 KB 498 at 509: ABH has its ordinary meaning and includes any hurt or injury calculated to interfere with V's health or comfort. It need not be permanent but must be more than "transient and trifling".
   - It includes loss of consciousness (*T v DPP* [2003] EWHC 266).
   - It covers all parts of the body, inside and out, including hair (*DPP v Smith* [2006] EWHC 94).
4. **Psychiatric injury** can be ABH, because the mind is part of the body:
   - it must be more than emotions such as fear, distress or anxiety (*Chan-Fook* [1994] 2 All ER 552; *Ireland, Burstow* [1998] AC 147);
   - clinical depression and anxiety qualified in *Constanza* [1997] 2 Cr App R 492;
   - expert evidence is required to prove it (*Chan-Fook*; *Ireland, Burstow*; *Morris* [1988] 1 Cr App R 386);
   - it must be a recognised psychiatric illness (*D* [2006] EWCA Crim 1139).
5. **CPS charging standards**:
   - typically battery level: grazes, scratches, abrasions, minor bruising, swelling, reddening of the skin, superficial cuts;
   - typically ABH: damaged teeth or bones, extensive or severe bruising, cuts requiring stitches, loss of consciousness;
   - the assessment takes account of V's age, health and other relevant factors (*Bollom* [2003] EWCA Crim 2846).

**Mens rea.** Only the mens rea of the base assault or battery is needed: intention or recklessness as to causing apprehension or applying force (*Savage, Parmenter*). No mens rea is required as to the ABH. s47 is therefore a **constructive** offence: D commits the base offence, the additional actus reus element (ABH) is "constructed" on top of it, and the actus reus and mens rea do not correspond.

### 2. Section 20: unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting GBH
**Statute.** OAPA 1861, s20: whoever unlawfully and maliciously wounds or inflicts any grievous bodily harm upon any other person, with or without any weapon or instrument, commits an offence. It is triable either way. The maximum is five years on indictment (the same as s47), and six months and/or a fine summarily.

**Actus reus.** An act by which D unlawfully (a) wounded V and/or (b) inflicted GBH on V.
1. **Wound**: both layers of skin (dermis and epidermis) must be broken (*Moriarty v Brooks* (1834) 6 C&P 684).
   - This includes breaking the inner skin of the cheek, lip or urethra (*Smith* (1837) 8 C&P 173).
   - A wound cannot be purely internal (*JJC (A Minor) v Eisenhower* [1984] QB 331).
   - A scratch is not enough (*Morris* [2005] EWCA Crim 609).
2. **GBH = really serious harm** (*DPP v Smith* [1961] AC 290 at 334: "grievous" means no more and no less than "really serious").
   - A direction of "serious" harm is sufficient (*Saunders* [1985] Crim LR 230).
   - It need not be permanent or life-threatening (*Ashman* (1858) 1 F&F 88).
   - The totality of injuries in one attack can be assessed together (*Birmingham* [2002] EWCA Crim 2608).
   - The effect on the particular victim is taken into account (*Golding* [2014] EWCA Crim 889; *Bollom*).
   - The discussion adds that whether harm is serious is assessed objectively, not by V's own subjective view (citing *Brown* [1988] Crim LR 484).
3. **Wound v GBH**: one does not imply the other. A broken collarbone without broken skin is not a wound but may be GBH (*Wood* (1830) 1 Mood CC 278). A needle injection is technically a wound but probably not GBH.
4. **Psychiatric harm** can be GBH if it is serious. As with ABH, it must be a recognised injury (not mere distress or fear) and proved by expert evidence.
5. **"Inflict" v "cause"**: there is no "radical divergence in meaning" (*Ireland, Burstow* [1998] AC 147 at 160). No violent application of force to the body is needed. Examples: phone calls (*Ireland*), HIV transmission (*Dica* [2004] 3 All ER 593), and D sitting on a railing and falling onto V (*Brady* [2006] EWCA Crim 2413).

**Mens rea: "maliciously".**
1. "Malice" means intention or recklessness (*Savage and Parmenter*; *Cunningham* [1957] 2 QB 396). Diplock LJ in *Mowatt* [1967] 1 QB 421 at 425-6 said "unlawfully and maliciously" was a fashionable drafting phrase in 1861 and does not carry the old vague sense of wickedness.
2. **Only some harm need be foreseen.** D must intend or foresee (Cunningham recklessness) that *some physical harm*, "albeit of a minor character", might result to some person. D need not foresee harm of GBH gravity (*Mowatt* at 426, affirmed in *Savage and Parmenter*).
3. s20 is therefore also partly constructive: wounding or GBH is required in the actus reus, but foresight of only minor harm is required in the mens rea.

### 3. Section 18: wounding or causing GBH with intent
**Statute.** OAPA 1861, s18: whoever unlawfully and maliciously, by any means whatsoever, wounds or causes any GBH to any person, with intent to do some GBH to any person, or with intent to resist or prevent the lawful apprehension or detainer of any person, commits an offence. It is triable only on indictment, with a maximum of life imprisonment.

**Actus reus.** D unlawfully wounds V or causes GBH to V (the same meanings as above).

**Mens rea.** There are four routes:
1. causing GBH with intent to cause GBH;
2. wounding with intent to cause GBH;
3. causing GBH with intent to resist or prevent lawful apprehension or detainer;
4. wounding with intent to resist or prevent lawful apprehension or detainer.

Key points:
- Intent to cause GBH may be direct or oblique (*Moloney*; *Woollin*).
- **Intent to wound is not enough** for s18 (*Taylor* [2009] EWCA Crim 544). For the GBH-intent routes "maliciously" adds nothing, because anyone who intends GBH foresees that GBH may result.
- For the resisting-arrest routes, D must intend to resist or prevent lawful apprehension or detainer *and* be malicious (foresee the possibility) as to the wounding or GBH. Example: D pushes an officer to resist arrest, foreseeing GBH may occur, and she suffers GBH (or a head wound) from hitting the pavement.

### 4. The ladder summarised
| Offence | Harm (AR) | Mens rea | Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assault/battery (CJA 1988 s39) | apprehension / force | intent or recklessness as to that | 6 months |
| s47 | ABH | intent or recklessness as to assault/battery only | 5 years |
| s20 | wound or GBH | intent or recklessness as to *some* harm | 5 years |
| s18 | wound or GBH | intent to cause GBH (or to resist arrest + malice) | life |

## Key authorities
- *Donovan* [1934] 2 KB 498: the definition of ABH.
- *T v DPP* [2003]; *DPP v Smith* [2006] EWHC 94: loss of consciousness and cutting hair can be ABH.
- *Chan-Fook* [1994]; *Ireland, Burstow* [1998]; *D* [2006] EWCA Crim 1139: psychiatric injury must be a recognised illness proved by expert evidence.
- *Savage, Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699: s47 needs no mens rea as to the ABH. "Maliciously" means intentionally or recklessly.
- *Moriarty v Brooks* (1834); *JJC v Eisenhower* [1984]: a wound requires a break in the whole skin.
- *DPP v Smith* [1961] AC 290; *Saunders* [1985]: GBH = (really) serious harm.
- *Bollom* [2003]; *Golding* [2014]: V's characteristics matter in assessing severity.
- *Mowatt* [1967] 1 QB 421: s20 needs foresight of some harm only.
- *Ireland, Burstow* [1998] AC 147; *Dica* [2004]: "inflict" is equivalent to "cause". GBH can be inflicted without direct force, including by disease transmission.
- *Taylor* [2009] EWCA Crim 544: intent to wound does not satisfy s18.
- OAPA 1861 ss18, 20, 47 (official statute text).

## Debate and critique
- **The "rag-bag".** J.C. Smith's description (commentary on *Parmenter*, cited in *Savage, Parmenter* at 752) of the 1861 Act as a rag-bag of offences gathered without any attempt at consistency is the starting point of the essay question. *Burstow* [1997] 1 Cr App R 144 at 149 insists this "should be a very practical area of the law".
- **The broken ladder (Jefferson, J Crim L 2012).** Michael Jefferson criticises the archaic language and the absence of a proper hierarchy. A ladder can be seen across ss18, 20 and 47, but it is "broken": s20 and s47 carry the same maximum despite the different harms, and the differences in actus reus, mens rea and penalty are not clearly spelt out. He proposes three harm offences (very serious harm; serious but not very serious harm; harm that is not serious), each with intention or recklessness *as to the harm caused*. This respects fair labelling and correspondence and follows the Law Commission's 1993 draft Bill, under which, unlike now, a person accused of the s47 replacement would not be liable without foreseeing injury. He closes by quoting Henry LJ in *Lynsey* that "Bad laws cost money and clog up the courts".
- **Law Commission No 218 (1993).** The draft Bill would replace ss18, 20 and 47 with: cl 2, intentionally causing serious injury; cl 3, recklessly causing serious injury; cl 4, intentionally or recklessly causing injury. This follows the CLRC's conclusions that there should be a distinction between serious injury and other injury, and, for serious injury, between intentional and (subjectively) reckless causing. Consultation responses "overwhelmingly" supported this structure. The discussion records that no Government has acted on the Commission's 2015 proposals either.
- **Gardner's defence of the 1861 Act ((1994) 53 CLJ 502).** John Gardner identifies two Law Commission arguments: the *rule of law argument* (the provisions are too unclear to guide conduct) and the *rationality argument* (the differences between the offences cannot be rationally explained). He argues that neither makes as strong a case as claimed, and that the Commission's proposals might do more harm than good. On s47 he argues that constructive liability is principled. By committing an assault, D changes their own normative position and takes the "secondary risk" of the more serious liability if harm results. Ashworth's "correspondence principle" (that every actus reus element needs a matching mens rea element) is not, and never has been, a principle of English law. The real principle is *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*, which constructive offences satisfy because the base offence carries mens rea. He calls the House of Lords' confirmation in *Savage* that s47 has no further mens rea "overdue".
- **Correspondence and fair labelling.** The mens rea discussion treats s47 as the paradigm constructive offence (lack of correspondence). The tension between Gardner's moral-luck-friendly view and the subjectivist correspondence view (Ashworth, Jefferson, Law Commission) is the core essay debate.
- **Stretching "inflict" and "bodily harm".** *Ireland, Burstow* and *Dica* extended the 1861 language to phone calls, psychiatric illness and disease transmission. This is pragmatic, but critics say it strains fair warning. It also links to the consent debate about HIV (consent-defence-nfoap.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Classify the injury first** using the CPS standards and case law. Trivial marks mean battery. Bruising, loss of consciousness, cut hair or recognised psychiatric illness mean ABH. A break in both skin layers is a wound. Really serious harm, looked at in total and in light of V's characteristics, is GBH.
2. **Start with the most serious possibility.** If GBH or a wound and D *intended GBH* (purpose, or virtual certainty plus appreciation), charge s18. If D intended only to wound, s18 fails (*Taylor*), so drop to s20.
3. **s20.** Did D foresee some harm, however minor (*Mowatt*)? If D only foresaw contact, not harm, s20 fails, so consider s47.
4. **s47.** Prove the assault or battery, its mens rea, and causation of ABH. No foresight of harm is needed.
5. **Psychiatric harm.** Look for evidence of a recognised condition proved by an expert. Fear or panic alone is not enough.
6. **Transferred malice.** The discussion: D's intent toward the intended target transfers to the actual victim because the offence is the same (*Latimer*; see mens-rea.md). A large, deep cut needing multiple stitches is a wound, and probably GBH depending on its seriousness. The question then is whether D intended GBH (s18) or only foresaw harm (s20). D's later statement that he did not mean to hurt that person does not prevent transfer.
7. **Exercise (hairdresser).** Cutting hair can be ABH (*DPP v Smith* [2006]), with battery as the base. Stabbing in the abdomen causing a punctured liver needing emergency surgery is GBH and a wound. "In a rage" suggests direct intent to cause GBH, so s18. A full recovery does not prevent GBH (*Ashman*).
8. **Defences.** Then consider consent (not available above battery level except in recognised categories), self-defence, intoxication (s18 is specific intent; ss20 and 47 are basic intent) and others.
