# Automatism (non-insane automatism)

## Overview
Automatism is a plea that the defendant's conduct was involuntary: the body moved without the mind controlling it. It is treated as a "denial of crime" rather than a true defence, because it attacks the voluntariness that is part of the actus reus (the discussion groups it with insanity and intoxication as capacity-related pleas). If it succeeds the result is a complete acquittal. If the cause is internal (a "disease of the mind") the plea is instead insanity, with the special verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity. Most of the difficulty lies in drawing that line and in deciding when the defendant's own fault bars the plea.

## The law
**1. Definition.** Involuntary movement of the body or limbs following a "complete destruction of voluntary control" (*Watmore v Jenkins* [1962] 2 QB 572). Lord Denning in *Bratty v AG for Northern Ireland* [1963] AC 363 described an involuntary act as one done by the muscles without any control by the mind (spasm, reflex, convulsion) or by a person not conscious of what he is doing (concussion, sleepwalking).

**2. Evidential burden and proof.** The defendant must raise evidence that the act was involuntary. Whether the evidence is enough to go to the jury is a question of law for the judge. Medical evidence is in practice essential: the defendant's own word, such as claiming to have "blacked out", will not suffice (*Bratty*; *Dervish* [1968] Crim LR 37). Once the issue is left to the jury, the prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt.

**3. Three elements.** The evidence must show that:
- (a) there was a **complete loss of voluntary control**;
- (b) it was caused by an **external factor**; and
- (c) the defendant was **not at fault** in losing control.

**4. Complete loss of control.** The standard is high: only "total destruction of voluntary control" counts (*Broome v Perkins* [1987] Crim LR 271; *AG's Reference (No 2 of 1992)* [1994] Crim LR 692; *Coley* [2013] EWCA Crim 223). Partial or impaired control is not enough. There is no defence of irresistible impulse (*AG for South Australia v Brown* [1960] AC 432). In *Watmore v Jenkins* the driver kept steering and holding a steady speed, so there was no automatism. In *AG's Reference (No 2 of 1992)* a driver "without awareness" could still steer and respond to significant stimuli, so again no automatism. In *Coley* and *McGhee*, Hughes LJ separated "involuntary" from merely "irrational" action; disinhibition (there from temazepam and alcohol) "is exactly not automatism" (as quoted in Claydon's article).

**5. External cause.** Internal cause (disease of the mind) means insanity (*Bratty*; *Sullivan* [1984] AC 156). The cases show how fine the line is:
- *Quick* [1973]: hypoglycaemia caused by insulin taken without eating. The cause was the insulin, an external factor, so automatism (the trial judge's insanity ruling was overturned).
- *Hennessy* [1989] 2 All ER 9: hyperglycaemia from not taking insulin, aggravated by stress. The cause was the diabetes itself, so insanity. Stress, anxiety and depression were rejected as external factors because they are a state of mind "prone to recur".
- *Rabey* (Canada, [1980] SCR 513): dissociation after rejection by a woman. The rejection was one of the ordinary stresses of life, so the true cause was internal. Not automatism.
- *R v T* [1990] Crim LR 256: dissociative state from post-traumatic stress after rape; rape is an external factor, not an ordinary stress of life. Automatism.
- Sleepwalking: cause seems internal, yet Lord Denning (citing *Tolson*) accepted that a sane sleepwalker should be acquitted. *Burgess* [1991] 2 QB 92 treated sleepwalking as insanity; the discussion also cites *Lillienfield* (The Times, 17 October 1985) and *Finegan v Heywood* (The Times, 10 May 2000). The discussion comments that sleepwalking lacks the clear pathology of epilepsy (*Sullivan*: epileptic seizure, insane automatism).

**6. Self-induced (prior fault) automatism.** A defendant cannot rely on automatism where responsible for the condition (*Kay v Butterworth* (1945) 173 LT 191: a driver who feels drowsiness must stop; *Quick*: self-induced incapacity, or one reasonably foreseeable from doing or omitting something, does not excuse). The detailed rules track the specific/basic intent distinction borrowed from intoxication:
- **Specific intent offences** (intention, knowledge or dishonesty, e.g. theft, murder, wounding with intent): automatism can always be raised (*Bailey*).
- **Basic intent offences** (typically recklessness suffices): automatism may be raised except where it results from voluntary intoxication by dangerous substances (*Quick*), or from non-dangerous drugs or any other conduct where the defendant knew the risk and unreasonably took it, i.e. was reckless about becoming automatic (*Bailey* [1982] 1 WLR 760; the discussion cites it as [1983] 2 All ER 503).
- *Bailey* test: did the defendant appreciate the risk of becoming aggressive, unpredictable or uncontrolled so that he might injure others, and persist or fail to take remedial action when he knew it was needed? If so the jury may find recklessness.

## Key authorities
- *Bratty v AG for NI* [1963] AC 363: definition; evidential burden; internal/external distinction.
- *Watmore v Jenkins* [1962] 2 QB 572: "complete destruction of voluntary control"; continued driving is not automatism.
- *Broome v Perkins* [1987]; *AG's Reference (No 2 of 1992)*; *Coley* [2013]: only total loss counts.
- *Quick* [1973] QB 910 and *Hennessy* [1989] 2 All ER 9: external insulin versus internal diabetes.
- *Sullivan* [1984] AC 156; *Burgess* [1991] 2 QB 92: epilepsy and sleepwalking as insanity.
- *Rabey*; *R v T* [1990]: psychological blows as internal or external.
- *Kay v Butterworth* (1945); *Bailey*: prior fault and recklessness.

## Debate and critique
- **Internal/external distinction.** The discussion says the boundary is ambiguous and can turn on subtle factual differences (*Quick* against *Hennessy*). Courts are said to favour insanity for public protection (*Hennessy*, *Burgess*), so outcomes may reflect policy rather than concept. Why should a condition be labelled "insane" only because it is internal, particularly for diabetics, epileptics and sleepwalkers who would not normally be detained?
- **Prior fault and specific/basic intent.** The discussion asks why prior fault should affect whether automatism succeeds, and why a distinction imported from intoxication should matter here.
- **Judicial scepticism.** Claydon notes that English law has moved to a narrower view of involuntariness, with purposive actions treated sceptically. She also notes that sleepwalking cases such as that of Brian Thomas ended in a not guilty verdict only because the prosecution stopped seeking the insanity verdict.
- **Reform.** The Law Commission's discussion paper (as described by Claydon) proposed an acquittal where the defendant wholly lacked capacity to control conduct and the loss of capacity was not due to a recognised medical condition (Proposal 13). Cases involving a recognised medical condition would go to a new defence of "not criminally responsible by reason of recognised medical condition" (Proposals 2 and 3). Claydon stresses that total lack of capacity is central, so a partly retained capacity (e.g. a patient with a brain tumour who tried to hide his behaviour) would struggle. She also discusses what neuroscience on agency might add. She cites *Hayes* [2013] EWCA Crim 897, where a frontal-lobe tumour affected sentencing.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the plea: is the defendant saying the act was involuntary (automatism) or that lack of understanding stems from a disease (insanity)?
2. Evidential burden: is there medical evidence? A bare claim of blackout fails.
3. Was control totally lost? Look for purposive steering, planning, hiding, or reaction to stimuli, which point against.
4. Cause: external (blow, insulin, drugs taken as prescribed, rape trauma) or internal (diabetes untreated, epilepsy, sleep disorder)? If internal, switch to insanity and note the special verdict.
5. Fault: did D cause the state? Classify the offence as specific or basic intent. Specific: plea open. Basic: ask whether intoxication by dangerous drugs applies, or otherwise whether D knew of the risk of aggression or loss of control and ignored it (*Bailey*).
6. Conclude the outcome: full acquittal, insanity verdict, or conviction. Typical model scenario: a pensioner mixing her pills wrongly and then committing theft (specific intent, plea open) and battery (basic intent, depends on whether she knew the combination could make her aggressive).
