# Beckford v R [1988] AC 130 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
Privy Council, on appeal from Jamaica (so persuasive, not binding, in England, but treated as authoritative). The
Board held that a person may use such force as is reasonable in the circumstances **as he honestly believes them to be**;
his belief need not be reasonable. Lord Griffiths approved Lord Lane CJ's reasoning in *R v Williams (Gladstone)*.

## Facts
Beckford was a Jamaican police officer, armed with a shotgun, sent with other officers to a house after a report that
a man was threatening a woman with a gun. He said the man ran from the house holding what looked like a firearm and
that he shot him dead in fear for his life. No gun was found, and the prosecution case was that the man was unarmed.
The trial judge directed the jury that the officer's belief in the danger had to be reasonable as well as honest.
Beckford was convicted of murder.

## Held
- The appeal was **allowed** and the conviction quashed: the direction requiring a *reasonable* belief was wrong.
- The question is whether the defendant honestly believed he was in danger; the force must then be reasonable on the
  facts as he believed them to be.
- The reasonableness of the belief remains relevant evidence of whether it was genuinely held.

## Why it matters
- Confirms for homicide (and for defence of another) the subjective approach to mistake in *Williams (Gladstone)*.
- Source of the two-part formulation: **subjective** belief about the facts, **objective** reasonableness of the force
  used.
- Rejects the idea that the defendant must wait to be attacked: a pre-emptive strike can be justified (Lord Griffiths,
  as summarised in secondary sources).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify what the defendant genuinely believed (did he think he was about to be shot?).
2. Judge the force against that belief, not against the real facts.
3. Use the reasonableness of the belief only as a pointer to honesty.
