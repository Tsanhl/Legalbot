# R v Clegg [1995] 1 AC 482 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them.

## Overview
House of Lords, on appeal from the Court of Appeal in Northern Ireland. The Lords held that **excessive force** used
in self-defence, prevention of crime or lawful arrest does not reduce murder to manslaughter, even for a soldier or
police officer acting in the course of duty. Any change was for Parliament.

## Facts
A soldier manning a vehicle checkpoint in Northern Ireland (30 September 1990) fired four shots at a stolen car that
had driven through at speed. The first three, fired as the car approached, were accepted at trial as justified.
The fourth was fired after the car had passed and was driving away; it killed a rear-seat passenger. The occupants
were joyriders. At a non-jury trial the judge found the fourth shot was not in self-defence and convicted him of
murder. The Court of Appeal in Northern Ireland dismissed his appeal and certified a point of law: whether a soldier
or police officer who kills with intent while using excessive and unreasonable force in such circumstances is guilty
of manslaughter, not murder.

## Held
- Appeal **dismissed**; the certified question was answered no. Self-defence is all or nothing (following *Palmer*).
- There is no principled basis for treating excessive force in preventing crime or making an arrest differently from
  excessive force in self-defence, as the defences overlap.
- Being a soldier or police officer on duty does not change the rule. The Lords saw merit in a manslaughter
  alternative but held that creating one was a matter for Parliament, not the courts.

## Why it matters
- Confirms the all-or-nothing rule for all force-based justifications.
- Illustrates the rule that force used after the threat has passed cannot be defensive.
- The Lords left reform (including the mandatory life sentence for murder) to Parliament.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Divide the incident into acts: was each act of force still needed when it was used?
2. A later act that goes beyond what was necessary is not protected by the earlier justified acts.
3. If the defence fails on a killing with murder intent, look for other partial defences rather than "excessive
   self-defence".
