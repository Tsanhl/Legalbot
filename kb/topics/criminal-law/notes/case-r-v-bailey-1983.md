# R v Bailey [1983] 1 WLR 760 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1983] 2 All ER 503; [1983] EWCA Crim 2) before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), decided 11 March 1983. The case deals with **self-induced automatism** that is not
caused by alcohol or illegal drugs, here hypoglycaemia after insulin. It rejects a blanket bar on the defence and
ties liability for basic-intent crimes to **recklessness**.

## Facts
Bailey, an insulin-dependent diabetic, went to see the new boyfriend of his former partner. He said he felt unwell and
was given sugar and water. He then struck the man on the head with an iron bar, causing a wound needing stitches.
He was charged with wounding with intent (OAPA 1861 s 18), with s 20 as an alternative, and said he had been in a
state of automatism from low blood sugar after not eating following his insulin. The trial judge, following *Quick*,
told the jury that self-induced incapacity was no defence. He was convicted.

## Held
- The direction was wrong. The Court of Appeal found the jury was misdirected but **dismissed the appeal**: the
  defence had little evidential support (he had armed himself, gave detailed police accounts inconsistent with a
  sudden loss of awareness, and the medical evidence made his account unlikely), so there was no miscarriage of justice.
- **Specific intent (s 18):** self-induced incapacity can prevent the prosecution proving the specific intent, as with
  intoxication (*Majewski*).
- **Basic intent (s 20 and assaults):** self-induced automatism of this kind may be evidence of recklessness; the
  question is whether he knew his condition was likely to make him aggressive or uncontrolled and risk injury, and
  persisted or failed to take remedial steps. There is no automatic bar like that for voluntary intoxication.

## Why it matters
- Sets the test for automatism that is partly the defendant's own fault: **foresight of the risk** of dangerous
  behaviour, not mere causation of the state.
- Separates insulin-related incapacity from alcohol and drugs, which remain governed by *Majewski*.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was control lost entirely (not merely impaired)?
2. How did it come about? Intoxication: use the *Majewski* analysis. Other self-induced state: ask what the defendant
   knew about the risk and whether he took precautions.
3. Specific-intent charge: the state may negate intent. Basic-intent charge: recklessness may still be proved.
