# Assault and Battery

## Overview
Assault and battery ("common assault") are the lowest rungs on the ladder of non-fatal offences against the person (NFOAP). They protect bodily autonomy against even trivial interference. In *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 1 WLR 1172 at 1177 the court described every person's body as inviolate, protected against any form of physical molestation, not only injury. They are also the *base offences* for more serious crimes such as assault occasioning actual bodily harm (s47 OAPA 1861), assault with intent to resist arrest, and assaulting a police officer. Both are defined by case law, not by statute, and the key controversies concern words and silence, immediacy, indirect force and omissions.

## The law

### 1. Statutory basis
- **Criminal Justice Act 1988, s39(1)**: common assault and battery are summary offences. The maximum penalty is six months' imprisonment, a fine (the section refers to level 5 on the standard scale), or both.
- The statute gives **no definition**. The words were treated as ones "which everyone would understand without further explanation" (*Ireland, Burstow* [1998] 1 AC 147 at 164). The definitions come from *Fagan v MPC* [1969] 1 QB 439 and *Ireland, Burstow*.
- They are **separate offences**. They can be charged separately and are often charged together.
- **Language trap**: "assault" or "common assault" is often used loosely for both offences, or for both together. There is no verb for "battery", so D is said to have "assaulted" V even when the offence is battery. Battery is sometimes called "common assault by beating".

### 2. Definitions
- **Assault**: any act by which D, intentionally or recklessly, causes V to apprehend immediate and unlawful personal violence. No force needs actually to be applied.
- **Battery**: any act (the discussion asks: or omission?) by which D, intentionally or recklessly, inflicts unlawful personal violence on V. V does not need to apprehend anything.
- **Common features**: no actual "violence" is needed despite the wording. No injury is needed (*Afolabi* [2017] EWHC 2960), and D need not foresee injury (*Logdon v DPP* [1976] Crim LR 121). No particular degree of force is required. No hostility, rudeness or aggression is required (*Faulkner v Talbot* [1981] 3 All ER 468 at 471).

### 3. Assault: actus reus
**(a) Any act**
1. An act is required, not an omission (*Fagan v MPC*).
2. **Words** in any medium (letter, call, WhatsApp, social media post) can constitute an assault. *Constanza* [1997] 2 Cr App R 492: what matters is that fear was in V's mind, and how it got there is irrelevant (at 495).
3. **Silence** can be an assault. *Ireland, Burstow* (at 161): the silent caller intends by his silence to cause fear and is so understood. V, unsure of his intentions, may fear the possibility of immediate personal violence.
4. **Words can negate an assault.** *Tuberville v Savage* (1669) 1 Mod 3: a man who laid his hand on his sword while saying that, if it were not assize-time, he would not take such language, committed no assault. The discussion refers to Horder's critique of this.

**(b) Apprehension**
1. V must actually apprehend imminent unlawful force. There is no assault if V has their back to D, or is asleep or unconscious.
2. It does not matter that V was not in fact in danger. *Logdon v DPP*: V believed the gun was real or loaded and so apprehended being shot.

**(c) Immediate unlawful personal violence**
1. D's conduct must make V think that force is about to be applied there and then (*Lamb* [1967] 2 QB 981).
2. There is no assault if it is obvious to V that D cannot carry out the threat (e.g. V is safely locked in a car away from D). But immediacy can still be satisfied where V is struck with terror by D, as in *Smith v Chief Superintendent of Woking Police Station* (1983) 76 Cr App R 234.
3. The impact cannot be remote: a threat to hit V next week is not enough. It must be a *present fear of immediate violence*, not an immediate fear of future violence. Fear of violence "at any time, not excluding the immediate future" can suffice (*Constanza*).

### 4. Assault: mens rea
1. **Intention**: D's aim or purpose to cause V to apprehend immediate unlawful force (*Moloney*). Oblique intent is rarely needed because recklessness is enough.
2. **Recklessness**: *Venna* [1976] QB 421 established that assault and battery can be committed recklessly. D must foresee a risk that V may apprehend immediate unlawful violence, and unreasonably go on to take it. It is not enough that D ought to have foreseen it, or that others would have (*Savage, Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699). D may claim not to have foreseen the risk, and the jury may disbelieve D on the evidence.

### 5. Battery: actus reus
**(a) An act**
1. Again, an act is required, not an omission (*Fagan*). But see *Santa-Bermudez* below.
2. Force can be applied through a weapon or instrument that D controls. In *Fagan*, D drove a car onto V's foot. Laying a stick on a person is no different from laying a hand (at 444). In *Murgatroyd v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire* [2000] All ER (D) 1742, D set a dog on V.
3. **Creating a dangerous situation**: *DPP v Santa-Bermudez* [2003] EWHC 2908 (Admin). D assured a police officer before a search that he had no needles or sharps. The officer's finger was pierced by a hypodermic needle in his pocket. Liability followed from D's creation of the danger and his failure to warn (compare *Miller*, see actus-reus-and-omissions.md).
4. **Indirect battery**: *Haystead v Chief Constable of Derbyshire* [2000] 2 Cr App R 339. D punched a woman, causing her to drop the baby she was holding. Her loss of grip was entirely and immediately the result of D's punch, and there was no logical difference from using a weapon to fell the child. *DPP v K (A Minor)* [1990] 1 WLR 1067 is the acid-in-the-hand-dryer case discussed in the "George and Tom" hypothetical.

**(b) Inflicts unlawful personal violence**
1. This is a matter of causation.
2. Any unlawful physical contact with V's body counts. V need not be aware of it (V could be asleep).
3. There must be an actual application of force (confirmed in *Ireland*). An assault is not needed for a battery to occur.
4. Force is defined very broadly to include contact with V's clothes. In *Thomas* (1985) 81 Cr App R 331, touching a person's clothes while they are wearing them was treated as equivalent to touching them (at 334).

### 6. Battery: mens rea
1. **Intention** to apply unlawful force (*Moloney*).
2. **Recklessness** (*Venna*): D foresaw a risk of unlawful physical contact with V and unreasonably took it. *Savage, Parmenter* again excludes an objective "ought to have foreseen" test.
3. **Unlawfulness and mistaken belief**: D's belief need not be reasonable. *Williams* (1984) 78 Cr App R 276: D honestly but wrongly believed he was protecting a young man from attack by V. An honest belief in circumstances that would make the force lawful negates the mens rea (see self-defence.md).

### 7. Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea
In *Fagan*, D did not have mens rea when the car first came to rest on V's foot. He then formed it and refused to move the car. Battery was treated as a continuing act, so the actus reus and mens rea coincided.

### 8. Unlawfulness and consent
Force is unlawful unless justified or excused, for example by self-defence or valid consent. For assault and battery the general rule is that V's consent makes the touching lawful. See consent-defence-nfoap.md.

## Key authorities
- Criminal Justice Act 1988, s39(1): makes assault and battery summary offences and sets the penalties.
- *Collins v Wilcock* [1984] 1 WLR 1172: the inviolability of the body.
- *Fagan v MPC* [1969] 1 QB 439: definitions, an act required, force via an instrument (the car), and a continuing act.
- *Ireland, Burstow* [1998] 1 AC 147: silent phone calls can be an assault. Battery requires actual force. The courts should treat the ladder of offences as a coherent body of law.
- *Constanza* [1997] 2 Cr App R 492: words, including letters, can found an assault. Fear of violence "at any time" may suffice.
- *Tuberville v Savage* (1669): words can negate an assault.
- *Logdon v DPP* [1976] Crim LR 121: apprehension is enough even with an imitation gun.
- *Lamb* [1967] 2 QB 981; *Smith v Chief Supt of Woking* (1983): immediacy.
- *Venna* [1976] QB 421; *Savage, Parmenter* [1992] 1 AC 699: subjective recklessness suffices.
- *Haystead* [2000]; *DPP v K* [1990]; *Santa-Bermudez* [2003]: indirect force and creating a danger.
- *Thomas* (1985): touching clothes counts.
- *Williams* (1984) 78 Cr App R 276: an honest mistaken belief need not be reasonable.

## Debate and critique
- **Is the 1861 Act/common law coherent?** Professor J.C. Smith (commentary on *Parmenter*, cited in *Savage, Parmenter* at 752) called the Act "a rag-bag of offences" with no attempt at consistency of substance or form. The discussion asks students to assess this statement and the case for reform. *Ireland, Burstow* urged that the ladder be treated as coherent "so far as possible".
- **Why criminalise mere touching?** The discussion cites Blackstone: because the law cannot draw lines between degrees of violence, it forbids even the first and lowest stage, every person being "sacred". The values protected are the right to non-interference and privacy.
- **Psychic assault (Horder, [1998] Crim LR 392).** Jeremy Horder argues that psychic assault is an anomaly, largely overlapping with statutory threat offences. He supports the proposals to put it on a statutory footing (cl 6 of the Law Commission's draft, carried into the Offences Against the Person Bill), but only in modified form. The Law Commission/Bill definition focuses on V's *belief* that force or impact is imminent. Horder says this wrongly stresses the cognitive state D induces. On his account, the distinctive wrong is the fear created by a *threatening confrontation*: the sensory, contemporaneous experience of being threatened with physical interference, whether or not that interference is to happen immediately. He notes that the courts already read "imminence" very flexibly (*Smith v Woking*; the silent-caller dictum in *Ireland*). He also argues that assault works as a "gateway" offence (e.g. s38 OAPA, assault with intent to resist arrest), which has fair-warning benefits only if the gateway offence is itself clearly defined.
- **Indirect violence (Hirst, [1999] Crim LR 557).** Michael Hirst argues against the majority textbook view that battery can be committed by indirect means such as booby traps. *Clarence* and *Martin* are not real authority on the point, *DPP v K* was wrongly decided, and the House of Lords has impliedly defined battery as a direct physical attack. As a result, traps causing less serious injury may go unpunished. Hirst says the Draft Offences Against the Person Bill would largely solve this (an offence of intentionally or recklessly causing injury, and an assault offence covering causing "an impact on the body of another").
- **Gardner on DPP v K.** John Gardner ((1994) 53 CLJ 502) says *DPP v K* misconstrued an endangerment mischief (dangerous chemical in a hand dryer) as an assault. This shows the need for new endangerment offences rather than stretching assault.
- **Reform.** The Law Commission's *Legislating the Criminal Code* (Law Com No 218, 1993) draft Bill, cl 6, defines assault as intentionally or recklessly applying force to or causing an impact on another's body without consent (or, where injury is intended or likely, with or without consent), or causing the other to believe such force or impact is imminent. It excludes force that is generally acceptable in the ordinary conduct of daily life. The discussion notes that the Law Commission's 2015 reform proposals have not been acted on by any Government. Michael Jefferson (J Crim L 2012) criticises the archaic language and the "broken" ladder (see aggravated-non-fatal-offences.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Choose the right offence.** If force was applied, consider battery. If V feared force, consider assault. If there is injury beyond the trivial, move to s47 (aggravated-non-fatal-offences.md), using assault or battery as the base.
2. **Assault checklist.** Was there an act, words or silence? Did V actually apprehend force (V must be aware: watch for a V who was asleep or had their back turned)? Was the feared force immediate, i.e. here and now, or "at any time" in a stalking or phone-call context? Did words negate the threat (*Tuberville*)? Was D able to carry out the threat, or was V safe?
3. **Battery checklist.** Was there contact, including with clothes, via an instrument, an animal, or indirectly through another person (*Haystead*)? Did D create a danger and then fail to warn (*Santa-Bermudez*)? Did the contact happen during a continuing act (*Fagan*)?
4. **Mens rea.** Did D intend, or foresee a risk of, V's apprehension or of contact (*Venna*; *Savage*)? Do not use an objective test.
5. **Unlawfulness.** Consider consent (everyday contact, sport, horseplay) and self-defence, including honest mistaken belief (*Williams*).
6. **Exercise (hairdresser).** The hairdresser's gesture with scissors may make the client apprehend force on her hair, which raises the question whether the threatened contact is "unlawful" given her consent to a trim. The client's grabbing of the scissors, twisting his wrist and causing minor bruising, may be battery, and possibly s47. Then consider whether she was acting in defence of her property or person.
7. **Exercise (boxer friend).** Light contact during joking with no injury is a battery at most, not s47. Then consider consent and horseplay (consent-defence-nfoap.md). Apprehension is judged subjectively, by what V actually apprehended.
