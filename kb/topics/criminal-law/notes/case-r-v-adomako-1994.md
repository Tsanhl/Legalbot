# R v Adomako [1995] 1 AC 171 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. Reported at [1995] 1 AC 171; [1994] 3 WLR 288; [1994] UKHL 6. Check any
> pinpoint against the report before citing it.

## Overview
House of Lords, 1994. Lord Mackay of Clashfern LC gave the speech setting out the test for involuntary manslaughter by
gross negligence. Sources differ on the exact day (30 June or 1 July 1994), so the day is not given. The full
bench is not confirmed from two sources.

## Facts
Adomako was an anaesthetist responsible for a patient during an eye operation. The tube supplying oxygen became
disconnected from the ventilator. He did not notice or react properly to the signs, and the patient suffered a
cardiac arrest and later died. A jury convicted him of manslaughter by gross negligence, and the Court of Appeal and
then the House of Lords upheld the conviction.

## Held
- The appeal was **dismissed**.
- The ordinary principles of the law of negligence decide whether the defendant owed the victim a duty of care and
  broke it. The breach must have caused the death.
- The jury must then decide whether the breach was so bad, having regard to the risk of death, that it should be judged
  criminal. That is a matter of degree and a jury question.
- It is not necessary to direct the jury using the technical recklessness test from *Lawrence*, though a judge may use
  the word "reckless" in its ordinary sense where appropriate. The test is acknowledged to be somewhat circular.

## Four elements (as commonly stated from the speech)
Duty of care; breach; causation of death; and gross negligence amounting to a crime.

## Unconfirmed or omitted
- The names of the other Law Lords and the date of the trial and sentence are not confirmed.
- Later Court of Appeal glosses on the test are not covered here.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Establish a duty of care by ordinary negligence principles (for example doctor and patient).
2. Show breach: conduct below the standard of a reasonable person in that role.
3. Show that the breach caused the death (or significantly contributed to it).
4. Argue the jury question: given the risk of death, was the conduct so bad as to be criminal? Weigh the seriousness of
   the departure and the level of risk.
