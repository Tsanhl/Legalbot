# R v Latimer (1886) 17 QBD 359 (case note: transferred malice)

> Written from the House of Lords' account of the case in *Attorney-General's Reference (No 3 of 1994)* [1998] AC 245
> and the course slides. The 1886 judgment itself is not held.

## Overview
Court for Crown Cases Reserved (Lord Coleridge CJ). The classic authority for **transferred malice**: where D, with
the mens rea for an offence against X, causes the actus reus of the **same** offence to Y, the mens rea "transfers".

## Facts
Two men quarrelled in a public house. Latimer struck at the other man with his belt; the blow glanced off and struck
a woman standing nearby, wounding her severely. He was convicted of unlawfully and maliciously wounding her, contrary
to **OAPA 1861 s 20**.

## Held
The conviction stood. Latimer had acted with malice (an intent to injure a person); it did not matter that the person
actually injured was not the one he aimed at. *R v Pembliton* (1874) was **distinguished**: there D threw a stone at
people, missed and broke a window, and was acquitted of malicious damage, because his mens rea related to a different
kind of offence (against the person, not property).

## The limits (from the course slides and A-G's Ref No 3 of 1994)
- Transferred malice works only **within the same offence / same kind of harm** (Latimer v Pembliton).
- In **A-G's Reference (No 3 of 1994)**, Lord Mustill described the doctrine as an anomaly resting on an outdated idea of
  "general malice" and refused a **double transfer** (from mother to foetus, then to the child born alive) to found
  murder; manslaughter could be available instead.

## Applying it to a problem question
D aims at X, hits Y: charge the offence against Y and transfer D's intent or recklessness as to X, provided it is the
same offence (e.g. s 20 to s 20, battery to battery). Do not transfer mens rea from a person-offence to a property-offence
(Pembliton). On the football facts (Johannes swings at Carlos, hits Oti), transferred malice supports s 18 / s 20 against Oti.
