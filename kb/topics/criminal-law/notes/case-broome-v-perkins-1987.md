# Broome v Perkins (1987) 85 Cr App R 321 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1987] RTR 321; [1987] Crim LR 271) before citing them.
> One account says the conviction was "upheld"; the others say the case was sent back with a direction to convict.

## Overview
Divisional Court (prosecution appeal from the magistrates). A diabetic driver in a hypoglycaemic state who still
reacted to the road did not have the **total loss of voluntary control** needed for automatism.

## Facts
Perkins, a diabetic, was charged with driving without due care and attention. His driving was erratic over about five
miles and ended in an accident. He said he had been in a hypoglycaemic episode and driven in an automatic state. He
had steered away from other vehicles, braked behind a stationary queue and, after being disturbed, restarted the car
and drove home. The lay justices accepted automatism; the prosecutor appealed.

## Held
- The appeal succeeded: the case was **remitted with a direction to convict** of driving without due care and
  attention.
- Automatism requires involuntary movement with a **complete** destruction of voluntary control. Impaired, reduced or
  partial control is not enough.
- A car cannot be driven for several miles in this way without some control; for parts of the journey his mind was
  directing his limbs, so he was driving.

## Why it matters
- Strict approach to the **total loss of control** requirement in driving cases (compare *Watmore v Jenkins*).
- The same reasoning means that a defendant who responds to the environment is unlikely to show automatism.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was control lost entirely, or only reduced (confusion, drowsiness, poor judgment)?
2. Look for evidence of purposeful, responsive actions (steering, braking, restarting, parking).
3. Where those exist, treat the defendant as acting voluntarily at least for part of the conduct.
