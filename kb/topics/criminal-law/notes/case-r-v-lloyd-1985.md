# R v Lloyd [1985] QB 829 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (reported as R v Lloyd, Bhuee and Ali; also [1985] 2 All ER 661) before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division); Lord Lane CJ gave the judgment. Temporary taking of property is **not** theft
unless the borrowing is in effect an outright taking: Theft Act 1968 s 6(1) applies only where the thing is returned
after all its "goodness" or practical value has gone.

## Facts
Lloyd, a cinema chief projectionist, took feature films from work over several months. Bhuee and Ali copied them for
unlawful distribution and the films were quickly returned to the cinema. All three were convicted of conspiracy to
steal (Criminal Law Act 1977 s 1) and the judge left intention to permanently deprive to the jury.

## Held
- Appeal **allowed**, convictions quashed.
- The films were returned in essentially the same condition and still had their value to the owners (they could
  still be shown to paying audiences), so the defendants did not intend to deprive the owners permanently.
- Section 6(1) deems an intention to permanently deprive where property is treated as the taker's own to dispose of,
  including borrowing for a period and in circumstances making it equivalent to an outright taking or disposal. That
  was not shown here.

## Why it matters
- Authority on the **limits of s 6(1)**: borrowing is caught only in the "equivalent to an outright taking" situation
  (a thing returned only after its goodness or virtue has gone).
- Shows that a temporary taking will usually fail on the intention to permanently deprive element.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Did the defendant intend to return the item, and in what state?
2. If returned with its value intact, s 6(1) is unlikely to apply.
3. If theft fails on this element, consider whether other offences are available on the facts.
