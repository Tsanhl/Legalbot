# R v Golds [2016] UKSC 61 (case note)

> The judgment itself was not read; this note rests on the Supreme Court's case page and official press summary,
> with news reports. Paragraph references below are those given in the press summary; check them against the
> judgment before citing.

## Overview
UK Supreme Court, seven justices (Lord Neuberger, Lady Hale, Lord Kerr, Lord Reed, Lord Hughes, Lord Toulson, Lord
Thomas), judgment 30 November 2016, on appeal from [2014] EWCA Crim 748. Lord Hughes gave the unanimous judgment. The
issue was whether, and how, a trial judge must explain "substantially" in s 2(1)(b) Homicide Act 1957 (as amended by
the Coroners and Justice Act 2009), where the defence says the defendant's ability to understand his conduct, form a
rational judgment or exercise self-control was substantially impaired.

## Facts
Golds admitted in court that he had killed his partner. The medical evidence was that he had an abnormality of mental
functioning arising from a medical condition, and the only issue was diminished responsibility. The trial judge told
the jury he would not define the everyday word "substantially" unless they asked for help; they did not ask, and they
convicted him of murder. He argued on appeal that "substantial" should have been defined as "more than merely
trivial". The Court of Appeal dismissed his appeal, and he appealed to the Supreme Court.

## Held
- The appeal was **dismissed**.
- In ordinary usage "substantial" can mean either (1) present rather than illusory or fanciful, or (2) important or
  weighty. In diminished responsibility it has always been used in the **second sense**, which also fits principle: a
  reduction from murder to manslaughter needs a weighty reason, not one that merely passes the trivial ([27], [36]).
- The authorities on the pre-2009 wording continue to apply to the amended section ([30]-[35]).
- The judge is not ordinarily required to direct the jury beyond the statutory words and should not try to define
  "substantially". If there is a risk the jury will misunderstand it (for example where they have heard argument that
  any impairment beyond the trivial is enough, or about a "spectrum" between trivial and total), the judge must direct
  that, while an impairment must be more than merely trivial to be substantial, it is not the case that any impairment
  more than trivial will suffice ([43]).
- Where help is given, the judge should avoid substituting a single synonym, and it is usually better to avoid the
  "spectrum" direction derived from *R v Lloyd* [1967] 1 QB 175 ([37]-[42]).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Set out the s 2(1) elements: a recognised medical condition, substantial impairment of one of the abilities in
   s 2(1A), and that the condition provides an explanation for the killing (a significant contributory factor).
2. On "substantial", say it is an ordinary word for the jury, meaning important or weighty, and that *Golds* rejects a
   definition of "more than trivial".
3. Apply the medical evidence to the degree of impairment and avoid asserting a precise threshold.
