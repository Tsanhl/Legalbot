# R v Blaue [1975] 1 WLR 1411 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1975] 3 All ER 446; 61 Cr App R 271) before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), 1975. The **thin skull rule** extends to the victim's religious beliefs: a
victim's refusal of life-saving treatment on religious grounds does not break the chain of causation.

## Facts
After a young woman rejected his advances, Blaue stabbed her four times, puncturing a lung. In hospital she needed
surgery and a blood transfusion. As a Jehovah's Witness she refused the transfusion, even when told she would die
without it, and died. It was accepted that she would have survived with treatment. Blaue was charged with murder but
convicted of manslaughter on grounds of diminished responsibility, and appealed.

## Held
- Appeal **dismissed**. The stab wound remained an operating and substantial cause of death; her refusal did not
  break the chain.
- Lawton LJ: those who use violence on others "must take their victims as they find them". This covers the whole
  person, including religious beliefs, and the defendant cannot argue that the belief was unreasonable.
- Causation is a jury question, but where the facts are not in dispute the judge may explain the legal result.

## Why it matters
- Leading authority applying the thin skull rule to **beliefs** as well as physical vulnerability.
- Shows the limit on arguments based on the victim's failure to mitigate harm.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the victim's vulnerability or choice (physical, religious, cultural).
2. Is the original injury still an operating and substantial cause?
3. The defendant takes the victim as found, so an unreasonable refusal of treatment will not usually break the chain.
