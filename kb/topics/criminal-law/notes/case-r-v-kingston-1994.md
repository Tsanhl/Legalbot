# R v Kingston [1995] 2 AC 355 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. This note is built from secondary sources that agree on the points below. Also
> reported as [1994] UKHL 9, [1994] 3 WLR 519 and [1994] 3 All ER 353; Court of Appeal [1994] QB 81. Check any
> pinpoint or quotation against the report before citing it.

## Overview
House of Lords, 21 July 1994 (reported in the 1995 volume of the Appeal Cases), on the Crown's appeal from the Court
of Appeal (Criminal Division). Lord Mustill gave the only reasoned speech and the other members of the committee
agreed. The case decides that involuntary intoxication is not a free-standing defence where the defendant in fact
formed the required intent.

## Facts
Kingston was secretly drugged by his co-accused, Penn, who was acting for former business associates seeking
compromising material to blackmail him. While under the influence he indecently assaulted a 15-year-old boy who had
been drugged and was asleep in Penn's flat; Penn photographed and recorded the assault. The trial judge (Potts J)
directed the jury that a drugged intent is still an intent, and Kingston was convicted by a majority. The Court of
Appeal (Lord Taylor CJ presiding) quashed the conviction on the footing that a defendant who was not to blame for the
intoxication that led him to form the intent should not be liable. The Crown appealed.

## Held
- The House of Lords **allowed the Crown's appeal** and restored the conviction; the case went back to the Court of
  Appeal to deal with the remaining grounds of appeal.
- There is no rule of English law that involuntary intoxication is a defence where the defendant had the necessary
  intent. A drugged intent is still an intent: disinhibition caused by a drug does not remove mens rea that was
  actually formed.
- Involuntary intoxication matters to liability only where it prevents the defendant forming the required intent.
  Where intent was in fact present, the involuntary character of the intoxication goes to mitigation of sentence.
- Lord Mustill declined to create a new defence, treating any reform as a matter for the Law Commission and Parliament.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify whether the intoxication was voluntary or involuntary (for example, drink or drugs spiked without D's
   knowledge). If voluntary, apply the *Majewski* rules instead.
2. Ask whether D, despite the intoxicant, actually formed the mens rea. If yes, *Kingston* means no defence: the
   involuntary intoxication is relevant only to sentence.
3. If the intoxicant genuinely prevented D forming the intent (the evidence is key), the prosecution cannot prove
   mens rea. Secondary sources say the *Majewski* restriction on basic intent offences does not apply where the
   intoxication was involuntary; check this against the report before relying on it.
4. Do not argue "it was not his fault" as a stand-alone defence; the question is always the state of mind.
