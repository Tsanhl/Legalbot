# Causation

## Overview
For result crimes such as murder, manslaughter and the non-fatal offences that require harm, it is not enough that the result happened. The prosecution must prove that D's conduct *caused* it. The law uses a two-stage test: causation in fact (the "but for" test) and causation in law (was D's conduct a substantial, blameworthy and operating cause?). Much of the case law concerns intervening acts and events (*novus actus interveniens*), which may break the chain of causation. The discussion itself says this case law is "not particularly coherent".

## The law

### 1. When causation matters
Causation applies only where the offence has a result element (murder, manslaughter, s47/s20/s18 OAPA harms). Conduct and circumstance crimes, such as theft, need no causation analysis.

### 2. Role of judge, jury and experts
The judge explains the legal principles. The jury applies them to the facts. Expert evidence may be given but experts do not decide causation (discussed in *Kimel* [2016] EWCA Crim 1456).

### 3. Causation in fact: the "but for" test
1. Would the result have occurred but for D's conduct? If it would have happened in the same way regardless, there is no factual causation.
   - *White* [1910] 2 KB 124: D poisoned V intending to kill her, but she died of an unrelated heart condition. D was not guilty of murder for lack of factual causation, but was guilty of attempted murder, which is not a result crime.
2. **Contribution is enough.** D's conduct need only contribute to the result, not be its sole or main cause.
   - *Dyson* [1908] 2 KB 454: D's blow and V's meningitis. The discussion treats acceleration of a death that was already likely as causing it.
   - The contribution must be more than negligible (the *de minimis* principle): *Hennigan*; *L* [2010] EWCA Crim 1249.
3. **Multiple causes.** Several causes do not prevent D's conduct being a factual cause. In *Warburton and Hubbersty* [2006] EWCA Crim 627, V died of injuries inflicted by several people. The jury did not need to be sure that D's injuries alone killed V. Hooper LJ said the test is whether D's acts "significantly contribute[d] to the victim's death".

### 4. Causation in law
The core question is whether D's conduct was a **substantial, blameworthy and operating** cause of the result. Both factual and legal causation must be established.
1. **Substantial.** The cause must be more than negligible, not so minute as to be ignored under the *de minimis* principle (*Cato* [1976] 1 All ER 260 at 265-6).
2. **Blameworthy.** The prosecution must prove that D's conduct was blameworthy, that it caused the result, and that its blameworthiness was central to the causation. In *Dalloway* (1847) 2 Cox CC 273, D was driving a horse and cart with the reins resting negligently on the horse, and a young child who ran in front of the cart was killed. As Simester describes the case, D could be convicted of manslaughter only if it was proved that the child would not have died had D been using the reins properly.
3. **Operative.** D's conduct must still be a significant cause when the result occurs, with no break in the chain of causation.

### 5. Intervening acts and events
**(a) D's own subsequent conduct.** If D's later act is part of the same transaction (e.g. stabbing then shooting), D remains liable. If it is not part of the same transaction, the analysis changes. On coincidence of actus reus and mens rea, see *Thabo Meli*, *Church* and *Le Brun* in mens-rea.md.

**(b) Naturally occurring events.** The discussion contrasts an earthquake that kills V after D has punched him and V has chosen to stay on the floor (likely a break) with a tide that drowns V after D punched him unconscious on the beach (no break). The chain is *not* broken if the natural event was:
- foreseeable as likely to occur in the normal course of events; or
- personally foreseen by D as likely; or
- of a general form and risk of further harm that was reasonably foreseeable, even if unpredictable.

**(c) Third-party intervention.**
- Acts of an **innocent agent**, or **involuntary** acts, do not break the chain.
- **Justified or excused responses** to D's acts do not break the chain. A foreseeable act done instinctively for self-preservation or in the line of duty will not. In *Pagett* (1983) 76 Cr App R 279, a firearms officer fired back "out of instinct" after D fired at him. That response did not break the chain.
- A **reasonably foreseeable** third-party act does not break the chain. *Girdler* [2009] EWCA Crim 2666: causing death by dangerous driving, on a motorway at night, involving the hard shoulder and a lorry crash. Simester cites *Girdler* for the test of whether the later event could "sensibly have been anticipated".
- A **free, deliberate and informed** act of a third party *will* break the chain.

**(d) Medical treatment (policy-based approach).**
- *Jordan* (1956) 40 Cr App R 152: V's stab wound had mostly healed when doctors gave him large quantities of a drug he was intolerant to. The treatment was "palpably wrong" and D's conviction was quashed. Later cases (*Smith*, *Blaue*) describe *Jordan* as a very particular case depending on its exact facts.
- *Smith* [1959] 2 QB 35: V was stabbed. The treatment was "thoroughly bad": V's pierced lung went unnoticed by a busy medical officer, and (per Simester) he was dropped twice on the way to the medical station. D was still liable because the original wound was still an "operating and substantial cause" of death. Only if the original wound is merely the setting in which another cause operates does death not result from it.
- *Cheshire* [1991] 3 All ER 670: D shot V, and V later died from complications of a negligently managed tracheotomy when the original wounds were no longer life-threatening. Negligent treatment breaks the chain only if it is "so independent of [D's] acts, and in itself so potent in causing death" that D's contribution is insignificant. D was liable.

**(e) Victim intervention.**
1. **Take your victim as you find them (the "thin skull" rule).** This covers V's mind and beliefs as well as V's body.
   - *Blaue* [1975] 3 All ER 446: D stabbed a young woman who was a Jehovah's Witness. She refused a blood transfusion on religious grounds and died. D was liable. The victim is taken holistically, including religious beliefs.
   - *Holland* (1841) 2 Mood & R 351: V refused surgery on the wound D had inflicted, developed lockjaw and died. It did not matter that V had not taken the best course of treatment. The question was whether D's wound was the real cause of death, and the reasonableness of V's conduct was irrelevant.
2. **Self-neglect** does not break the chain. In *Wall* (1802) 28 State Tr 51, the Governor of Goree ordered 800 lashes to be illegally inflicted on V. V made his condition worse by drinking spirits. D was guilty of murder.
3. **V's escape or response.**
   - *Roberts* (1972) 56 Cr App R 95: V jumped from a moving car to escape D's unwanted sexual advances. D remains liable if V's reaction is within the range of responses that might be expected. The chain breaks only if the reaction was so unexpected that no reasonable person could have foreseen it.
   - *Williams and Davies* [1992] 2 All ER 183: V jumped from a moving car. The response must be within the ambit of reasonableness and "not so daft" as to make it V's own voluntary act.
4. **V's free and informed choices.**
   - *Kennedy (No 2)* [2007] UKHL 38: D prepared a syringe of heroin and handed it to V, who injected himself and died. V's free, deliberate and informed decision to self-inject breaks the chain. Lord Bingham said the criminal law generally assumes free will, and that informed adults of sound mind are treated as autonomous beings able to decide how to act.
   - *Wallace* [2018] EWCA Crim 690: D threw acid at V, causing catastrophic injuries. V later chose to be lawfully euthanised in Belgium. The Court of Appeal held it was open to the jury to find that V's request and the euthanasia were not acts independent of D's conduct, and so were not fully voluntary.
   - *Field* [2021] EWCA Crim 380: D, who was in an abusive and manipulative relationship with V and had drugged him and changed his will, left whiskey to tempt V, who drank it and died. The discussion uses this to ask whether the result is morally right and how it fits with *Kennedy (No 2)*. See also *Rebelo* [2021] EWCA Crim 306.

### 6. Omissions and causation
For an omission, D's unperformed act must have prevented the result, not merely possibly prevented it (*Morby*; *Broughton* [2020] EWCA Crim 1093). See actus-reus-and-omissions.md.

## Key authorities
- *White* [1910] 2 KB 124: no but-for causation where V died of an unrelated heart condition. Conviction for attempt only.
- *Dyson* [1908] 2 KB 454: accelerating death is causing death.
- *Warburton and Hubbersty* [2006] EWCA Crim 627: a significant contribution is enough where there are multiple attackers.
- *Cato* [1976] 1 All ER 260: the contribution must be more than *de minimis*.
- *Dalloway* (1847) 2 Cox CC 273: the blameworthy part of the conduct must be causative.
- *Pagett* (1983) 76 Cr App R 279: an instinctive, self-protective act by police does not break the chain.
- *Girdler* [2009] EWCA Crim 2666: a reasonably foreseeable third-party act does not break the chain.
- *Jordan* (1956) 40 Cr App R 152: "palpably wrong" treatment broke the chain. Confined to its facts.
- *Smith* [1959] 2 QB 35 and *Cheshire* [1991] 3 All ER 670: negligent treatment rarely breaks the chain.
- *Blaue* [1975] 3 All ER 446 and *Holland* (1841): the thin skull rule extends to V's beliefs and treatment choices.
- *Roberts* (1972) 56 Cr App R 95 and *Williams and Davies* [1992]: escape cases. Foreseeable reactions do not break the chain, "daft" ones do.
- *Kennedy (No 2)* [2007] UKHL 38: the free, deliberate and informed act of V or a third party breaks the chain.
- *Wallace* [2018] EWCA Crim 690; *Field* [2021] EWCA Crim 380; *Rebelo* [2021] EWCA Crim 306: limits on how far V's choices are treated as voluntary.

## Debate and critique
- **Incoherence.** The discussion describes the intervening-act case law as developed case by case and "not particularly coherent". Medical-treatment cases, for example, rest on policy rather than principle.
- **Norrie's critique** ("A Critique of Criminal Causation"). Alan Norrie argues that Hart and Honoré's account of legal causation is individualistic. It treats each human action as an isolated event originating in the actor's own will, and ignores the social context and relationships that shape decisions. He uses J.B. Priestley's *An Inspector Calls*, where a suicide is socially conditioned by others' conduct, and US cases (*Carbo v State*, *State v Preslar*) to show that whether an act counts as "voluntary" or "natural" depends on how widely the context is drawn. Applied to escape cases such as *Roberts* and *Williams and Davies*, the marker notes suggest that judges may underestimate the fear felt by (often female) victims. Calling a response "daft" treats as voluntary what may in fact be a fear-driven, self-preserving reaction. For Norrie, these vague concepts create contradictions and gaps in the law.
- **Simester, "Causation in (Criminal) Law" (2017) 133 LQR 416.** Andrew Simester warns against fully "moralising" causation. He starts from the Supreme Court in *Hughes* (2013), where, to avoid a harsh strict-liability conviction for causing death while uninsured, the Court required some fault-involving act or omission in D's driving. Simester insists that mechanical, physical causation is real and central. Normatively loaded tests risk making causation lose its shape. On his analysis *Hughes* itself would have come out differently, because H's car's momentum was a direct cause of death, and any unease should be directed at the legislator. He discusses the "FDI rule" (a free, deliberate and informed intervention breaks the chain) endorsed in *Kennedy (No 2)*. He calls it relatively uncontroversial as criminal law but puzzling in its metaphysical basis, since reasons can themselves be causes (drawing on Moore and Davidson). He notes the rule is a feature of criminal law that tort law does not share.
- **Addiction and autonomy.** The discussion asks how "free" a drug addict really is when offered the drug they are addicted to. This challenges *Kennedy (No 2)*'s assumption of autonomy and links to *Field* and *Wallace*.
- **Kennedy and Evans.** Baker argues that the supply-then-omission structure reconciles the two cases (see actus-reus-and-omissions.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is it a result crime?** If not (e.g. theft), skip causation.
2. **But for.** Ask whether the result would have happened anyway. Watch for "V would have died anyway" facts (*White*), and for evidential gaps. Problem-question technique warns that if the facts do not say what actually killed V, you must analyse each possible cause and show that D is responsible under every alternative.
3. **More than minimal contribution?** Apply *Cato*, *Dyson* and *Warburton*. A minor cut alongside a fatal stab wound by someone else may be *de minimis*.
4. **Intervening events.** Classify each one:
   - natural event: was it foreseeable?
   - third party: instinctive, self-protective or dutiful (*Pagett*)? Foreseeable (*Girdler*)? Or free, deliberate and informed (break)?
   - doctors: was the treatment so independent and potent that D's wound became merely the setting (*Cheshire*; *Jordan*)? Normally there is no break.
   - victim: thin skull or beliefs (*Blaue*), neglect (*Wall*), escape (*Roberts*/*Williams*: foreseeable or "daft"?), or an informed adult choice (*Kennedy (No 2)*), contrasted with coerced or vulnerable victims (*Wallace*; *Field*).
5. **Conclude** that D's conduct was (or was not) an operating and substantial cause, then move on to mens rea.
6. **Common traps:** treating V's unreasonable refusal of treatment as a break (it is not: *Blaue*, *Holland*), and forgetting that an attempt charge needs no causation.
