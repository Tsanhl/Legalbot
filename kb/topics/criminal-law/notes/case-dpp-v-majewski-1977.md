# DPP v Majewski [1977] AC 443 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (also [1976] UKHL 2; [1976] 2 All ER 142) before citing them.

## Overview
House of Lords (judgment given in 1976, reported 1977; Lord Elwyn-Jones gave the leading speech). Voluntary
intoxication by drink or drugs is **no defence to a crime of basic intent**; it may be relevant to a crime of specific
intent.

## Facts
Majewski took large quantities of amphetamines and barbiturates and drank alcohol, then fought in a pub, assaulting
the landlord and customers, and later assaulted a police officer and an inspector. He was convicted of four counts of
assault occasioning actual bodily harm and three counts of assaulting a constable. He argued that his intoxicated state
left him unaware of what he was doing, so he lacked mens rea.

## Held
- Appeal **dismissed**. Intoxication, including intoxication so extreme as to resemble automatism, is not a defence to
  basic-intent offences when it is self-induced.
- Lord Elwyn-Jones described a specific-intent crime as one whose mens rea goes beyond the actus reus (an "ulterior
  intent"), and said a crime that can be committed recklessly is a basic-intent crime. Lord Simon proposed a
  "purposive element" test instead.
- Becoming voluntarily intoxicated supplies the necessary blameworthiness (recklessness) for basic-intent crimes.
- For specific-intent crimes, intoxication may prevent the intent being formed, leaving liability for a lesser
  basic-intent offence.

## Why it matters
- The leading authority on voluntary intoxication; applies *Beard*.
- Basic-intent examples: assault, battery, ABH, s 20 GBH. Specific-intent example: murder.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the offence charged as specific or basic intent.
2. Specific intent: can the defendant show the intoxication left him unable to form the intent? If so, move to the
   lesser basic-intent offence.
3. Basic intent: voluntary intoxication will not assist; consider whether the intoxication was truly voluntary.
