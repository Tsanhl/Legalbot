# Actus Reus, Acts and Omissions

## Overview
The actus reus is the external, "guilty act" part of an offence. It can include conduct (an act or an omission), circumstances, and sometimes a result that D's conduct must cause. Positive acts are usually straightforward. Liability for *omissions* is far more controversial, because English law does not impose a general duty to help others. D can be liable for a failure to act only where the offence can be committed by omission and D was under a legally recognised duty to act. Causation of results is covered in causation.md.

## The law

### 1. The elements of the actus reus
- **Conduct**: D's positive movement (act) or failure to act (omission).
- **Circumstances**: facts that must exist alongside the conduct. They may be written into a statute or implied at common law:
  - statutory examples: V aged under 13 (Sexual Offences Act 2003, s5, a physical circumstance); V did not consent (SOA 2003, s1, a mental circumstance); the property belongs to another (Theft Act 1968, s1, a state of affairs);
  - common law example (murder): V must be a person, the killing must be under the King's peace, and the killing must be unlawful.
- **Results**: for result crimes only (e.g. murder), D's conduct must cause the result. Conduct-only and circumstance-only crimes such as theft need no causation analysis.
- D is liable only if *every* element of the offence is satisfied as a whole.

### 2. The general rule: no liability for pure omissions
English criminal law "does not require people to do good; it merely prevents them from behaving badly". The classic example: A sees B drowning and could save him just by holding out a hand, but deliberately abstains so that B drowns. A commits no offence. The dog-walking example (a stranger lets a drowning woman die) makes the same point when there is no special relationship.

Omissions liability arises in two situations:
1. **Offences of "mere omission"**: offences defined in terms of a failure to act. These are mostly statutory.
2. **Offences of omission where D is under a duty to act**: result offences such as homicide committed by failing to act when under a duty.

### 3. Test for non-specific omission-based liability (four steps)
1. **Recognised offence.** The offence charged must be one that the law recognises can be committed by omission. These are mostly statutory, with some common law examples. Courts are generally reluctant to extend common law offences to omissions. (The gross negligence manslaughter material notes that unlawful act manslaughter needs an *act* (*Lowe* [1973]), whereas gross negligence manslaughter can be committed by omission where there is a duty.)
2. **Duty to act.** There must be a legally recognised duty. The categories are:
   - **Family relationship**: *Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) 13 Cr App R 134.
   - **Contractual duties**: *Pittwood* (1902) 19 TLR 37.
   - **Creating a dangerous situation**: *Miller* [1983] 2 AC 161.
   - The Evans materials add **voluntary assumption of responsibility**, *Stone and Dobinson* [1977], and a parent's duty to a child, recognised in *Evans* for the mother.
   - **Statutory duties** listed in the Evans material: the Domestic Violence, Crime and Victims Act 2004 (duties regarding children and vulnerable adults) and the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (criminalising neglect of those lacking capacity).
3. **Breach.** D satisfies the conduct element not simply by failing to act, but by failing to do what the duty required. The duty requires D to do what is *reasonable*, and no more (*Miller*; *Evans* [2009] EWCA Crim 650).
4. **Causation.** D's failure must cause the result. Factual causation is required: but for the omission, the result would not have occurred. Liability is limited to results that D's unperformed act *would* (not merely *might*) have prevented. The discussion cites Williams (1987) 7 LS 92 at 106-7, citing *Morby* (1882) 15 Cox CC 35, recently confirmed in *Broughton* [2020] EWCA Crim 1093.

### 4. The *Miller* principle (creation of danger)
In *Miller*, a squatter fell asleep while smoking and set fire to his mattress. When he woke and saw the fire, he did nothing to put it out or call the fire brigade, but moved to another room. He was convicted of arson. Lord Diplock's reasoning, as described in the Baker article, was that D had created the danger by his own act and so came under a duty to counteract it. Intentionally or recklessly failing to do so made him liable. Glanville Williams (as quoted by Baker) explains the principle: where a person accidentally creates a danger, mens rea formed after the act but before the result, while D could still prevent it, can found liability.

### 5. Drug cases: *Evans*
In *Evans* [2009] EWCA Crim 650, D (aged 24) bought heroin and supplied her 16-year-old half-sister, who injected herself at the family home. D saw the symptoms of overdose (blue lips, unresponsiveness). She and the girl's mother decided not to call for help for fear of getting into trouble, put the girl to bed and checked on her. The girl died of heroin poisoning. Both were convicted of gross negligence manslaughter. At trial, the judge treated the mother's parental duty as well established. A sibling relationship did not by itself create a duty, but D's role in procuring the drug was "capable of giving rise to a duty of care". The Court of Appeal (Lord Judge CJ) upheld the conviction. It held that D's role as intermediary created a "plain and obvious" duty to provide assistance, relying on *Miller* (duty from creating a danger) and *Willoughby* [2004], and drawing on civil analogies. The court accepted that deciding whether a duty exists is a judicial task, but held that leaving it to the jury was not fatal here.

Earlier cases referred to in the Evans material:
- *Khan* [1998]: heroin sold to a 15-year-old novice who overdosed and was left to die. Convictions for gross negligence manslaughter were quashed because the jury was not properly directed on duty. Swinton Thomas LJ (obiter) advised caution about enlarging the categories of duty to cover drug suppliers.
- *Sinclair, Johnson and Smith* [1998]: a methadone overdose. A duty might arise for a close friend (Sinclair), described as a relationship akin to siblings, but not for the flat owner (Johnson), who had no close relationship and had not assumed responsibility.
- *Willoughby* [2004]: additional facts may combine with other circumstances to establish a duty.

### 6. Acts v omissions: the borderline
Whether conduct is an act or an omission can be contested (e.g. switching off life support). Glanville Williams accepts that the distinction can be hard to draw but says this does not undermine it in the ordinary case. The discussion contrasts holding V's head under water (a positive act causing death) with watching V drown without a duty (no liability).

## Key authorities
- *Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) 13 Cr App R 134: a duty to act arising from a family relationship.
- *Pittwood* (1902) 19 TLR 37: a duty arising from contract.
- *Miller* [1983] 2 AC 161: D who creates a dangerous situation comes under a duty to take reasonable steps to counteract it. Here a squatter's fire led to an arson conviction.
- *Stone and Dobinson* [1977]: a duty arising from voluntary assumption of responsibility for a vulnerable person (as cited in the Evans and Baker materials).
- *Evans* [2009] EWCA Crim 650: the *Miller* principle extended to drug supply. A supplier who realises V is in danger must take reasonable steps (summon help). The duty only requires what is reasonable.
- *Broughton* [2020] EWCA Crim 1093: the omission must be shown to have caused the result, i.e. the omitted act *would* have prevented it (confirming *Morby* (1882) 15 Cox CC 35).
- *Kennedy (No 2)* [2007] UKHL 38: V's free, deliberate and informed self-injection breaks the chain of causation from supply (unlawful act manslaughter). The Evans material notes that it left gross negligence manslaughter unaddressed.
- *Khan* [1998] and *Sinclair, Johnson and Smith* [1998]: courts were hesitant to impose duties on drug suppliers and companions.

## Debate and critique
- **The conventional view v social responsibility.** In "Criminal Omissions: The Conventional View", Glanville Williams replies to Andrew Ashworth's "social responsibility" view. His arguments, as summarised in the marker notes, are: (1) society should prioritise punishing active wrongdoing; (2) there is a real moral and psychological difference between acts and omissions; (3) omissions liability cannot apply to everyone and so must target specific groups; (4) the principle of legality requires forbidden conduct to be clearly defined, so convicting non-doers under offences framed as acts is unfair labelling; (5) practically, the justice system could not handle widespread omission liability.
- **Ashworth's three proposals**, criticised by Williams: (a) a duty to assist people in peril; (b) a duty to take reasonable steps towards law enforcement (e.g. reporting crime); (c) a duty to ensure the health and welfare of one's children. Williams says (c) is already the law. Ashworth also argued that courts should be able to read verbs like "obstruct" as covering omissions. Williams responds that statutes should be read in their plain meaning and that extending them to omissions is a matter for Parliament, not judges. Williams also rejects the claim that the conventional view rests on narrow individualism or the autonomy principle.
- **Is the duty concept too vague?** The Evans material records J.C. Smith's criticism that the boundaries of duty in omissions liability are unclear, which risks inconsistent outcomes. Glenys Williams questions extending duties to intermediaries and borrowing civil concepts such as "assumed responsibility". O'Doherty warns of judicial overreach. Ormerod and Fortson criticise *Evans* for relying on *Miller* without properly addressing causation or when duties arise. Virgo warns against conflating civil and criminal principles. Further criticisms in the material: applying *Miller* (a criminal damage case) to drug homicide was not adequately justified; the court did not decide whether *Miller*'s recklessness mens rea suits manslaughter; and the use of civil cases (*Smith v Littlewoods* [1987]; *Mitchell v Glasgow City Council* [2009]) blurred the line between civil and criminal law.
- **Reconciling *Kennedy* and *Evans*.** Dennis J. Baker (J Crim L 2010) argues the two cases are consistent. *Kennedy (No 2)* holds that V's voluntary self-injection breaks the causal chain from the act of supply. *Evans* does not disturb this, but holds that a supplier who then fails to summon help, when she can see it is needed, is causally linked to the death. Baker adds that causation could be disputed if help would not have changed the outcome. He also argues on fair labelling grounds that defendants like Stone, Dobinson, Evans and Hood are "stupid rather than dangerous". Labelling them as killers through gross negligence manslaughter serves no retributive goal, and a lesser negligence offence would be fairer.
- **Autonomy v welfare.** Omissions liability is a test of how far the criminal law should go beyond preventing harm to requiring good conduct (see introduction-and-principles.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Act or omission?** Identify D's conduct precisely. If there is a positive act (e.g. supplying, pushing, holding under water), analyse it as an act, and only fall back on omission if needed.
2. **Can the offence be committed by omission?** Murder and gross negligence manslaughter can be. Unlawful act manslaughter needs an act. Assault and battery are generally act-based, though *Miller*-type reasoning can create liability (see assault-and-battery.md).
3. **Is there a duty?** Work through the categories: family/parent (*Gibbins and Proctor*; the mother in *Evans*), contract (*Pittwood*), assumption of responsibility (*Stone and Dobinson*), creation of danger (*Miller*; *Evans*), statute. Traps: a sibling relationship alone is not enough (*Evans* at trial). A stranger owes nothing. A friend may owe a duty if there is a close relationship or assumed responsibility (*Sinclair*).
4. **Was the duty breached?** D only has to do what is reasonable (e.g. call an ambulance), not take heroic or dangerous steps.
5. **Causation.** Would the omitted act have prevented the result? "Might have" is not enough (*Morby*; *Broughton*). Look for medical evidence in the facts.
6. **Mens rea.** Apply the mens rea of the offence at the time of the omission. Under *Miller*, mens rea formed after the dangerous act but while D could still act is enough.
7. **Drug supply facts.** Separate (a) the supply, where *Kennedy (No 2)* says self-injection breaks the chain for unlawful act manslaughter, from (b) the later failure to summon help, which can be gross negligence manslaughter under *Evans*.
