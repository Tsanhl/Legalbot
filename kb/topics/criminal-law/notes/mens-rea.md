# Mens Rea

## Overview
Mens rea is the fault element of an offence: the "guilty mind" that must exist at the time of the actus reus. The module covers a range of fault terms, from high to low culpability: intention (direct and oblique), knowledge, belief, recklessness, wilfulness/maliciousness and negligence. It also covers doctrines that shape mens rea: strict liability, mistake, transferred malice, constructive liability and coincidence of actus reus and mens rea. Intention and recklessness are the most heavily examined, and the meaning of intention is a classic essay topic.

## The law

### 1. Subjective v objective fault
- **Subjective** mens rea looks into D's own mind: what did D foresee or know?
- **Objective** mens rea judges D against an external standard, the reasonable person in D's situation: what would they have foreseen or known?

### 2. Intention
**General approach.** Lord Bridge in *Moloney* [1985] 1 All ER 1025 set out the "golden rule": when directing a jury on intent, the judge should normally avoid elaborating or paraphrasing what intent means and leave it to the jury's good sense. the materials describes intention as concerned with the law of evidence rather than substantive law as such.

**(a) Direct intention.** A result is intended when it is D's aim or purpose to cause it (*Moloney*).
- Premeditation, planning and motive are not needed, but the intention must exist when the actus reus occurs.
- D may be reluctant or sad about acting and still have direct intent. Motive must be distinguished from intent (post-seminar slides).
- It does not matter that D thinks they are likely to fail.

**(b) Oblique (indirect) intention.** A jury *may* find that a result was intended, even though it was not D's purpose, where:
1. the result was a virtual certainty (barring some unforeseen intervention) as a result of D's actions; and
2. D appreciated that it was a virtual certainty; and
3. the jury find that this appreciation amounts to intention.

The *Woollin* [1999] AC 82 model direction: the jury are not entitled to find the necessary intention unless they feel sure that death or serious bodily harm was a virtual certainty (barring some unforeseen intervention) as a result of D's actions and that D appreciated this. The decision is for the jury on all the evidence.
- In *Woollin*, D lost his temper and threw his three-month-old son onto a hard surface, fracturing his skull. The judge directed in terms of "substantial risk". The House of Lords (Lord Steyn) held this was a material misdirection, because it blurred the line between intention and recklessness and so between murder and manslaughter. Manslaughter was substituted.
- Lord Steyn approved the *Nedrick* "virtual certainty" test as pitched at the right level and noted that academics (J.C. Smith, Glanville Williams, J.R. Spencer, Ashworth) had welcomed it. He substituted the words "to find" for "to infer" in Lord Lane's direction. He held that the direction is not confined to cases where the only evidence is D's actions and their consequences, and may be appropriate whenever D may not have desired the result. He also dropped the two preliminary questions from *Nedrick* (how probable was the consequence? did D foresee it?) as unhelpful.
- Lord Steyn accepted that the test may exclude murder in the "terrorist bomb disposal" example, but said manslaughter, with a possible life sentence, is available.
- *Matthews and Alleyne* [2003] EWCA Crim 19: foresight of virtual certainty is a basis on which the jury *may* find intention. It is not a rule that such foresight *is* intention, so the jury is not obliged to find it.

**Development of the law** (secondary notes and Goff, all from the sources): *Hyam v DPP* [1975] AC 55 treated foresight of a highly probable result as sufficient. *Moloney* treated foresight as evidence from which intent may be inferred, not intent itself, and referred to "natural consequences". *Hancock and Shankland* [1986] (striking miners who dropped a concrete block from a bridge, killing a taxi driver) rejected the "natural consequence" wording as potentially misleading and stressed that probability affects whether foresight indicates intent. *Nedrick* then introduced virtual certainty, which *Woollin* confirmed.

**Lecture summary of intention:**
1. Was the outcome D's main aim or purpose? If yes, it was intended (*Moloney*).
2. If not, was it a virtually certain consequence and did D realise this? If yes, the jury may, but need not, find intention (*Woollin*; *Matthews*). If no, it was not intended.

### 3. Knowledge
Knowledge is generally seen as equal in culpability to intention. D knows an element if:
1. D believes it to be the case. This is subjective, and belief that something "may be" the case is not enough. In *Saik* [2006] UKHL 18, knowledge was said to mean true belief and the word should not be "watered down".
2. D is correct in that belief. This is objective: *Montila* [2004] 1 WLR 3141 (you cannot know that something is A when in fact it is B).

### 4. Belief
Belief falls short of knowledge. It is belief that a circumstance exists or that a result will be caused, meaning D foresees it as highly likely. It has a subjective limb only, so D does not need to be correct. It is generally used for circumstance elements.

### 5. Recklessness
**Current test: *R v G* [2003] UKHL 50**, restoring *Cunningham* [1957] 2 QB 396 subjective recklessness. Both limbs must be satisfied:
1. **Subjective limb.** D foresaw a risk that the relevant circumstance exists or will exist, or that the result will occur.
   - Any level of foreseen risk is enough (*Brady* [2006] EWCA Crim 2413).
   - How much thought D gave to the risk is irrelevant. It does not matter if D suppressed the risk or it was driven out of D's mind by emotion (*Parker* [1977] 1 WLR 600; *Stephenson* [1979] 3 WLR 193).
   - D's attitude to the risk is irrelevant.
2. **Objective limb.** It was unreasonable, in the circumstances known to D, to take the risk. This protects socially useful risk-taking, such as a surgeon operating while foreseeing a risk of harm.

*R v G* overruled the objective *Caldwell* test, under which D could be reckless about an obvious risk D never noticed. the materials Lord Bingham's view that it is neither moral nor just to convict a defendant on the basis of what someone else would have appreciated if D had no such awareness. *AG's Reference (No 3 of 2003)* [2004] EWCA Crim 868 confirmed that the *R v G* approach applies generally.

### 6. Wilfulness and maliciousness
"Maliciously" and "wilfully" in older statutes now mean the same as recklessness when they apply to circumstance or result elements (*AG's Reference (No 3 of 2003)*). For "maliciously" see *Cunningham*. For "wilfully" see *D* [2008] EWCA Crim 2360 and *Sheppard* [1981] AC 394.

### 7. Negligence
Negligence does not look at D's state of mind. It asks whether D's conduct in relation to a risk was reasonable: what was D's duty of care, and did D breach it? Recklessness is the *conscious* taking of an unjustified risk. Negligence is the *inadvertent* taking of one. For gross negligence manslaughter see gross-negligence-manslaughter.md.

### 8. Strict liability
Some statutory offences need no mens rea as to one or more elements. There is a **rebuttable presumption that mens rea is required** (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970] AC 132). Factors that may rebut it:
- the language of the statute;
- the existence of defences such as due diligence;
- whether any actus reus element implies mens rea (e.g. possession: *Warner* [1969] 2 AC 256);
- whether strict liability is necessary to achieve the statute's policy.

Examples are driving offences and regulatory crimes.

### 9. Mistake
- A mistake may be relevant if it concerns a definitional element of the offence or an excuse.
- *DPP v Morgan* [1976] AC 182: an honest, even if unreasonable, mistaken belief can negate mens rea. There, an honest belief in consent was a complete defence to rape at common law. (For sexual offences, the Sexual Offences Act 2003 now requires *reasonable* belief in consent: see sexual-offences-consent.md.)
- Mistakes relating to justifications are dealt with under self-defence, duress and intoxication.
- Mistake about or ignorance of the law is irrelevant.

### 10. Transferred malice
- If D intends to kill B, misses and kills C, D's mens rea towards B transfers to C, and D is guilty of murdering C.
- The actus reus and mens rea must be for the **same offence** (*Latimer* (1886) 17 QBD 359).
- It does not transfer where the offence that occurred is different from the one intended (*Pembliton* (1874) LR 2 CCR 119).
- *Attorney-General's Reference (No 3 of 1994)* [1997] UKHL 31 is cited on the limits of transfer. The born-alive article notes it confirmed that a foetus lacks legal personality and so cannot itself be the victim of murder or manslaughter (see murder.md).

### 11. Constructive liability
Some offences require mens rea only for a lesser offence than the one committed, so the mens rea and actus reus do not correspond. Assault occasioning actual bodily harm (s47 OAPA) is the materials's example (see aggravated-non-fatal-offences.md). Unlawful act manslaughter and the GBH rule in murder are other examples (see unlawful-act-manslaughter.md, murder.md).

### 12. Coincidence in time
In principle, actus reus and mens rea must coincide, and the materials calls this an absolute rule. The courts have been "ingenious" in stretching it:
- *Thabo Meli* [1954] 1 All ER 373: this fits the materials's example (D attacks with intent to kill, believes V dead, disposes of the "body", and the disposal kills V). What is in fact a series of acts cannot be divided up. There was an antecedent plan. D was guilty of murder.
- *Church* [1966] 1 QB 59: similar facts but no antecedent plan. D's conduct was "a series of acts which culminated in [V's] death".
- *Le Brun* [1992] QB 61: D knocked his wife unconscious, then dropped her while dragging her body, killing her. No preconceived plan was needed. It is murder if D had intent to kill or cause GBH when he first struck her. *Church* principles apply to murder as well as manslaughter.
- *Miller*-type continuing acts and omissions also bridge gaps (see actus-reus-and-omissions.md).

## Key authorities
- *Moloney* [1985] AC 905: the golden rule. Purpose is the core meaning of intent, and foresight is only evidence of it.
- *Hancock and Shankland* [1986]: the "natural consequence" wording rejected. Probability matters.
- *Nedrick* (as discussed in *Woollin*): the virtual certainty direction.
- *Woollin* [1999] AC 82: the virtual certainty test confirmed and "infer" changed to "find". A "substantial risk" direction is a misdirection.
- *Matthews and Alleyne* [2003] EWCA Crim 19: virtual certainty is evidence from which intent may be found, not a rule of substantive law.
- *Saik* [2006] UKHL 18; *Montila* [2004] 1 WLR 3141: knowledge means a true belief.
- *Cunningham* [1957] 2 QB 396; *R v G* [2003] UKHL 50: subjective recklessness.
- *Brady* [2006] EWCA Crim 2413: foresight of any risk is enough.
- *Parker* [1977]; *Stephenson* [1979]: a risk driven out of D's mind still counts.
- *Sweet v Parsley* [1970] AC 132: the presumption of mens rea.
- *DPP v Morgan* [1976] AC 182: an honest mistake negates mens rea.
- *Latimer* (1886); *Pembliton* (1874); *AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)* [1997]: transferred malice and its limits.
- *Thabo Meli* [1954]; *Church* [1966]; *Le Brun* [1992]: coincidence through a continuing transaction.

## Debate and critique
- **Is oblique intention really intention?** The law treats foresight of virtual certainty as *evidence* from which intention may be found (*Matthews*), not as intention itself. G. Coffey (J Crim L 2009) argues that the resulting uncertainty makes a precise statutory definition "imperative". He discusses the Law Commission's models from its homicide consultation (CP No 177, 2005), which build on the Draft Criminal Code (cl 18(b)). On that model D acts intentionally if D acts in order to bring a result about, or knowing it will be virtually certain to occur, or knowing it would be virtually certain to occur if D succeeded in a purpose of causing some other result. There is a proviso that D does not intend a result which it was D's specific purpose to avoid. Coffey concludes that English (and Irish) law has not yet equated foresight of virtual certainty with intention, and that certain reckless killings may be as heinous as intentional ones.
- **The "purpose to avoid" proviso.** Itzhak Kugler ("The Definition of Oblique Intention", 2004) argues that the Law Commission was wrong to exclude cases where D's whole purpose is to avoid the result. The reasons for oblique intention (moral culpability tied to near-certainty, and D's *choice* to bring about a known result) apply equally there. Such actors should be treated as obliquely intending the result.
- **"Common sense" and ordinary language.** Nicola Lacey ("A Clear Concept of Intention: Elusive or Illusory?") re-reads *Moloney* and argues that the judges' reliance on "common sense" and "ordinary language" assumes a clarity the concept does not have. It leaves room for jury confusion and inconsistent decisions, and calls into question the ideals of certainty and consistency that drive the debate. She contrasts her view with that of J.C. Smith and the Law Commission on the purpose-to-avoid issue.
- **Too narrow or too wide for murder?** Lord Goff ("The Mental Element in the Crime of Murder") criticises the GBH rule (*Vickers* [1957]) as over-inclusive, because it labels as murderers those who neither intended nor foresaw death. He also warns that equating foresight with intent could widen murder unfairly. He favoured a concept based on "reckless indifference" (see murder.md). Lord Steyn in *Woollin* defended virtual certainty as the right level and rejected special rules for terrorism.
- **Subjectivism and recklessness.** K. Amirthalingam ("Caldwell Recklessness is Dead, Long Live Mens Rea's Fecklessness", (2004) 67 MLR 491) welcomes the subjective basis of *R v G* but argues that mens rea needs a residual objective, normative element to decide whether the defendant is actually blameworthy. He proposes a dualistic model. The "mens" is the subjective mental state that attributes responsibility. The "rea" is a normative evaluation of whether that state was blameworthy, for example whether D *should* have foreseen further consequences of conduct D chose. *Caldwell*'s mistake was using an objective test for the *existence* rather than the *quality* of the mental state. He draws on Ashworth's idea of duties of citizenship.
- **Constructive liability and correspondence.** s47 ABH, unlawful act manslaughter and the GBH rule in murder all break the correspondence principle, under which mens rea should match the actus reus. This is a recurring theme in essays on non-fatal offences and homicide reform.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Identify the mens rea term** in the offence (intention, recklessness, "maliciously", knowledge, belief, negligence) and which actus reus element it attaches to (circumstance or result).
2. **Intention.** First ask whether the result was D's aim or purpose. If not, was it virtually certain and did D foresee it as such? If so, the jury *may* find intent (*Woollin*; *Matthews*). Do not say a jury "must" find intent. A "substantial risk" or "highly probable" result points to recklessness, not intention.
3. **Recklessness.** Did D personally foresee *some* risk (*Brady*)? Was it unreasonable to take it? If D did not foresee the risk at all, there is no recklessness even if it was obvious (*R v G*). Treat "maliciously" as recklessness.
4. **Mistake.** Does D's honest mistake of fact negate the mens rea (*Morgan*)? Check whether the offence requires a *reasonable* belief instead (e.g. SOA 2003). Ignore mistakes of law.
5. **Wrong victim or wrong object.** Same offence type: transfer (*Latimer*). Different offence: no transfer (*Pembliton*).
6. **Timing.** If the mens rea and the fatal act are separated, use *Thabo Meli*, *Church* and *Le Brun* (one continuing transaction).
7. **Strict liability.** For regulatory or statutory offences, start from the presumption of mens rea and test it against the *Sweet v Parsley* factors.
8. **Trap.** Motive is not intention. A reluctant or regretful D who acts in order to bring about a result still has direct intent.
