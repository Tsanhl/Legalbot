# R v Williams (Gladstone) (1984) 78 Cr App R 276 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report before citing them. Year of report: see the note under Overview.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division); Lord Lane CJ gave the leading judgment. The case is cited for the rule that a
defendant who **genuinely (even if unreasonably) believes** facts that would justify force in self-defence or the
prevention of crime is judged on the facts as he believed them. Reasonableness goes only to whether the belief was
honestly held.

Citation note: the Cr App R volume is usually cited as 1984 (LawTeacher and e-lawresources both give (1984) 78 Cr App R
276); Wikipedia gives both the Cr App R and All ER reports as 1987 ([1987] 3 All ER 411) and dates the decision
to 28 November 1983. The sources therefore differ on the reporting year; the decision itself predates both.

## Facts
Williams saw a man seizing a youth in the street. He believed the youth was being unlawfully attacked and
intervened. In fact the youth had just robbed a woman and the man was trying to stop him escaping. The man told
Williams he was a police officer but could not produce a warrant card, and a struggle followed in which he was
injured. Williams was charged with assault occasioning actual bodily harm (OAPA 1861 s 47). The trial judge told the
jury that any mistaken belief had to be reasonable; Williams was convicted.

## Held
- The conviction was **quashed**. The direction requiring the mistake to be reasonable was wrong.
- If the defendant may genuinely have held the mistaken belief, he is entitled to be judged on the facts as he
  believed them; the prosecution has not proved its case if he may have thought force was needed to repel an attack
  or prevent a crime.
- The reasonableness of the belief is relevant only as evidence of whether it was in fact held.

## Why it matters
- Founding authority for the **subjective approach to mistaken belief in self-defence**, approved by the Privy Council
  in *Beckford v R* [1988] AC 130.
- Contrasts the honest-belief test with the (objective) question whether the **force used** was reasonable in the
  circumstances as the defendant believed them to be (see *Palmer* and *Beckford*).
- Later authority holds that a mistake induced by voluntary intoxication cannot ground the defence (see the
  intoxication note).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. What did the defendant honestly believe about the threat (even if wrong or irrational)?
2. Was the force used reasonable on the facts as he believed them to be?
3. Unreasonableness of the belief is evidence against honesty, not a bar to the defence.
