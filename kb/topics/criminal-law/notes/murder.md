# Murder

## Overview
Murder is the most serious homicide offence in England and Wales and carries a mandatory life sentence. It is a common law offence: the Homicide Act 1957 assumes a definition but does not supply one. The actus reus is the unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace. The mens rea ("malice aforethought") is an intention to kill or an intention to cause grievous bodily harm (GBH). The main debates are whether intent to cause GBH should be enough (the "GBH rule"), how oblique intention should be defined, and where the line with manslaughter should fall. Partial defences (loss-of-control.md, diminished-responsibility.md) reduce murder to voluntary manslaughter.

## The law

### 1. Definition
Sir Edward Coke (3 Inst 47) defined murder as when a person "of sound memory, and of the age of discretion, unlawfully killeth within any county of the realm any reasonable creature in rerum natura under the King's peace, with malice aforethought, either expressed by the party or implied by law". the materials calls this an "increasingly inaccurate definition". For example:
- the **year and a day rule** was abolished by the Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996, so there is no time limit between the unlawful act and death (the materials's example is the Andrew Devine case, where death came 32 years after the injuries, in July 2021);
- "within any county of the realm" is modified by OAPA 1861, s9 (see below).

The problem method handout advises against reciting Coke's full definition in exams (word count). Use: causing the death of another person with malice aforethought under the King's peace.

### 2. Actus reus
1. **Unlawfully.** The killing is unlawful if actus reus and mens rea are present and no lawful defence applies (e.g. self-defence makes the killing lawful: see self-defence.md).
2. **Causes death.**
   - Factual causation: "but for" (*White* [1910] 2 KB 124).
   - Legal causation: an operative and substantial cause of death (*Smith* [1959] 2 QB 35).
   - Take V as you find them (*Blaue* [1975] 1 WLR 1411).
   - The chain can be broken, e.g. by medical intervention (*Jordan* (1956) 40 Cr App R 152; contrast *Smith* and *Cheshire* [1991] 1 WLR 844). See causation.md.
3. **Of a human being.**
   - A foetus is not a person. V must be fully expelled from the womb and wholly outside the mother (*Poulton* (1832) 5 C&P 329), with an existence independent of the mother (*Enoch* (1833) 5 C&P 539; *C v S* [1988] QB 135). It does not matter whether the umbilical cord has been cut, provided the body is fully delivered (*Reeves* (1839) 9 C&P 25).
   - *Attorney-General's Reference (No 3 of 1994)* [1998] AC 245: D stabbed his pregnant girlfriend. She recovered, but the baby was born prematurely and died 120 days later. The House of Lords held D was not guilty of murdering the child, because the intention must be to kill a *person* and the child was not a person when stabbed. D was possibly liable for unlawful and dangerous act manslaughter of the child through the stabbing of the mother.
   - **End of life**: there is no authoritative legal definition, only medical ones. On body or brain death see *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] 1 AC 789.
4. **Under the King's peace.** This concerns time and place: killing an enemy in war is excluded. In problem questions, unless there is a state of war, events occur under the King's peace. Under OAPA 1861, s9, a killing by a British subject outside the UK may be tried in the UK as if committed here.

### 3. Mens rea: malice aforethought
1. **Intention to kill** (express malice), or
2. **Intention to cause GBH** (implied malice). GBH means really serious harm (*DPP v Smith* [1961] AC 290: see aggravated-non-fatal-offences.md).
3. Nothing short of intention will do. Recklessness, however extreme, is not enough. Intention may be **direct** (aim or purpose: *Moloney*) or **indirect/oblique** (death or GBH a virtual certainty barring unforeseen intervention, D appreciated this, and the jury *find* intent: *Woollin* [1999] AC 82; *Matthews and Alleyne* [2003] EWCA Crim 19). See mens-rea.md.
4. **The GBH rule is constructive.** Intent to kill matches the actus reus (death). Intent to cause GBH does not: no mens rea is required for the additional element, death.
5. **Intention is not premeditation.** Planning is legally different from intention, which may be formed a second before the act. Premeditation is instead an aggravating feature for the minimum term under the Sentencing Act 2020, Sch 21. Equally, a merciful or compassionate motive does not negate intention (Goff: mercy killing is still murder).
6. **Transferred malice.** Mens rea aimed at V1 transfers to V2 (*Latimer* (1886) 17 QBD 359), but only where it relates to the same offence. There can be no "double transfer" (*AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)*: from mother to foetus to the child later born).

### 4. Development of oblique intent (as traced in the sources)
- *Hyam v DPP* [1975] AC 55: foresight of death or serious harm as highly probable was treated as sufficient.
- *Moloney* [1985] AC 905: foresight is evidence of intent, not intent itself. The jury should normally be left to its common sense (the "golden rule"). Lord Bridge said the foreseen probability must be "little short of overwhelming". His example was a man who boards a plane he knows is bound for Manchester to escape pursuit: he intends to go to Manchester even though it is the last place he wants to be.
- *Hancock and Shankland* [1986]: striking miners dropped a concrete block from a bridge, killing a taxi driver. The "natural consequence" wording was rejected, and the greater the probability, the more likely it was foreseen and intended.
- *Nedrick* and *Woollin*: the virtual certainty test. In *Woollin*, D threw his three-month-old son onto a hard surface. A "substantial risk" direction blurred murder and manslaughter, so the murder conviction was quashed and manslaughter substituted.
- Lord Goff (1988 LQR) concluded that after *Smith*, *Hyam*, *Moloney* and *Hancock* the law was essentially back where *Vickers* [1957] left it: intent to kill or intent to cause GBH, with foresight as evidence only.

## Key authorities
- Coke, 3 Inst 47: the classic definition.
- Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996: abolished the time limit.
- *Poulton* (1832); *Enoch* (1833); *Reeves* (1839); *C v S* [1988]: when a child becomes a "person".
- *AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)* [1998] AC 245: a foetus is not a person. No murder of the later-born child. No double transfer of malice. Unlawful act manslaughter possible.
- *Airedale NHS Trust v Bland* [1993] 1 AC 789: the end of life.
- OAPA 1861, s9: jurisdiction over killings abroad by British subjects.
- *Vickers* [1957]: intent to cause GBH suffices for murder (as discussed by Goff).
- *Moloney* [1985]; *Hancock and Shankland* [1986]; *Woollin* [1999]; *Matthews and Alleyne* [2003]: the intention cases.
- *Latimer* (1886): transferred malice.
- Sentencing Act 2020, Sch 21: premeditation relevant to the minimum term (see homicide-in-context-and-reform.md).

## Debate and critique
- **The GBH rule is too wide (Lord Goff, "The Mental Element in the Crime of Murder" (1988) 104 LQR 30).** Goff argues that an intent to cause really serious harm should not of itself be the mental element for murder. It labels as murderers people who never contemplated a risk of death. He gives a glassing case he tried, where the jury would not call the assailant a murderer even though he plainly intended serious harm and was horrified when V died. He criticised the Criminal Law Revision Committee's 14th Report (1980), which proposed murder where death is caused by an act intended to cause serious injury and known to involve a risk of death, plus a terrorism category. In one sense it is too narrow: why require intended serious injury if knowledge of a risk of death is what matters? Consider the haemophiliac nicked by a minor cut. In another sense it is too wide: one may see a risk and discount it or hope to avoid it. Goff favoured Scots law's "wicked recklessness" (e.g. *Cawthorne* (1968), firing shots into an occupied room), which captures utter indifference to life, as a replacement for the GBH rule.
- **Murder should require a voluntary choice to kill (Mitchell, "Thinking about Murder", 1992).** Barry Mitchell argues the current definition is too broad. It includes those who intended only serious harm, and those who act in emotionally charged circumstances or mercy killings (e.g. *Cocker* (1989)) where voluntariness is reduced. He argues murder should be confined to deliberate, voluntary choices to kill, so that everyone convicted deserves the full stigma of the label.
- **Legal v social murder (Norrie, "Legal and Social Murder: What's the Difference?").** Alan Norrie uses the Grenfell Tower fire (72 deaths) and Engels' idea of "social murder" to ask whether systemic actors who ignored warnings (e.g. after the 2009 Lakanal House fire) could meet the legal test for murder. If they foresaw death or serious harm as virtually certain, *Woollin* would allow a jury to find intent. He criticises the flexibility of intention as a "rule of evidence" (*Woollin*, *Matthews*) that lets juries' and courts' moral judgments shape outcomes. He also refers to *Jogee* (2016) on foresight in complicity.
- **Is virtual certainty too narrow?** Lord Steyn in *Woollin* accepted the "terrorist bomb disposal" gap but regarded manslaughter, with a discretionary life sentence, as an adequate safety net. Coffey and Kugler debate statutory definitions (see mens-rea.md).
- **The born-alive rule under pressure.** Elizabeth Chloe Romanis (Medical Law Review) argues that fetal surgery (a foetus temporarily removed and then returned, e.g. "Baby Boemer") and artificial womb technology (the "biobag" lamb trials) strain the born-alive rule, producing entities such as "gestatelings" and "fetal operatees". Their legal status affects pregnant women's rights, medical negligence liability, abortion law and stillbirth definitions. *AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)* confirms that legal personality begins only at birth.
- **Mandatory life sentence and labelling.** Because murder carries a fixed life sentence, the breadth of its mens rea matters more. This is a key point in the Law Commission's 2006 proposals for a ladder of first and second degree murder (see homicide-in-context-and-reform.md).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Actus reus.** Is V a person (born alive and independent, or a foetus)? Did D's act cause death, factually and legally, considering thin skull and intervening acts? Unlawfulness: flag any justification such as self-defence.
2. **Mens rea.** First, did D aim to kill or to cause really serious harm? If not, was death or GBH virtually certain and did D appreciate that (*Woollin*)? Words such as "teach a lesson", "wanted to hurt", or using a knife on vital areas point towards intent to cause GBH. Rage or drink does not negate intention, but consider intoxication (intoxication.md).
3. **Transferred malice.** A different victim of the same offence: transfer. A foetus: no murder (*AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)*), so consider manslaughter.
4. **Lecture exercise.** D punches V, who falls, hits his head on a stool and dies of an undiagnosed brain haemorrhage. Factual causation is satisfied, and legal causation through the thin skull rule. There was no intent to kill. Whether "teach him a lesson" shows intent to cause GBH determines murder. If it does not, consider unlawful act manslaughter (unlawful-act-manslaughter.md).
5. **Then consider partial defences** (loss of control, diminished responsibility) and general defences. The problem method handout warns: if asked only about murder, do not discuss lesser offences, and do not add facts.
6. **Conclude** firmly. Remember the mandatory life sentence and the minimum-term factors.
