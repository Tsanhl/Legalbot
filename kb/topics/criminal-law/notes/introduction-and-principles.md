# Introduction to Criminal Law and General Principles

## Overview
Criminal Law covers the substantive criminal law of England and Wales: the rules that decide what conduct counts as a crime, whether a particular defendant (D) satisfies every element of an offence, and whether any defence is available. The syllabus divides the syllabus into a **general part** (actus reus, mens rea, defences such as self-defence, duress and insanity, and "denials of crime": automatism, insanity and intoxication) and a **special part** (selected non-fatal offences against the person, homicide, selected sexual offences and selected property offences). Across both parts students are expected to question the scope and role of the criminal law and whether reforms have met their stated aims. This note sets out the framework concepts that every later topic relies on: what a crime is, who the parties are, the burden and standard of proof, the sources of criminal law, the guiding principles (fair warning, fair labelling, autonomy and welfare) and the internal structure of offences.

## The law

### 1. What is a crime?
The introductory lecture identifies three overlapping ways of understanding a crime:
1. **A public wrong**: conduct with a harmful effect on the public, not just on an individual.
2. **A moral wrong**: conduct that offends collective morals.
3. **Harm**: the wrongfulness comes from the harm caused.

The substantive criminal law is concerned with how offences are *defined*: working out what conduct is criminalised, comparing those requirements with D's behaviour, deciding whether every element is satisfied, and then exploring possible defences.

### 2. Criminal v civil law and the role of the State
- Prosecutions are brought in the name of the Crown (R v D). The "victim" (V) is not the prosecutor but a witness.
- A prosecution redresses harm to *society*. The criminal law is a coercive method of control and regulation. the materials adds that the substantive criminal law also, in part, *limits* how far the State may regulate our conduct.
- Prosecutions are mainly brought by the **Crown Prosecution Service (CPS)**. The CPS applies two tests: (a) there is enough evidence for a "realistic prospect of conviction"; and (b) prosecution is in the public interest.
- The parties are D, V (as witness) and, in the Crown Court, the jury.

### 3. Burden and standard of proof
1. **Burden on the Crown.** The prosecution must prove D's guilt. D does not have to prove innocence.
2. **Standard: beyond reasonable doubt.** The justification is that innocent people should not be wrongly convicted, and that the State's power and resources are far greater than most defendants'.
3. **Presumption of innocence.** *Woolmington v DPP* [1935] AC 462, per Lord Sankey LC: the "golden thread" running through English criminal law is that the prosecution must prove the prisoner's guilt.
4. **Reverse burdens.** For some defences D carries the burden, but only on the **balance of probabilities**. By that stage the prosecution has already proved the offence beyond reasonable doubt. Examples elsewhere in the module are insanity and diminished responsibility (see insanity.md and diminished-responsibility.md).

### 4. Courts
- Cases are tried in the **magistrates' court** or the **Crown Court**. The verdicts are guilty or not guilty.
- Appeals go from the magistrates' court to the Crown Court, and on to the **Court of Appeal**. The **Supreme Court** is the final court of appeal.
- In the Crown Court the judge explains the legal principles and the jury decides the facts and applies the law to them. Causation is a good example (see causation.md).

### 5. Sources of substantive criminal law
1. **Common law**: judicial decisions and the classic common law writers (Hale, Coke, Hawkins). Murder is still a common law offence. The Homicide Act 1957 does not define murder; it assumes the common law definition (case problem method handout).
2. **Statute**: primary legislation (Acts of Parliament) and secondary legislation (statutory instruments made by ministers and other bodies).
3. **Statutory construction**: the courts' interpretation of statutory offences. Two examples are the presumption of mens rea (*Sweet v Parsley* [1970] AC 132, see mens-rea.md) and reading "maliciously" in the Offences against the Person Act 1861 as recklessness.

### 6. Principles of criminal law
1. **Fair warning.** The law must be communicated to the public, because deterrence requires knowledge. This raises the questions of how criminal law should be communicated and whether common law crimes should be codified, and if so how. the materials asks whether the law's difficulty (badly drafted, complicated, too many offences, new offences not "advertised") should ever be a defence.
2. **Ignorance of the law is no defence.** Citizens are under a duty to know the criminal law. the materials points to a critique of this rule by Matthews (2018). A mistake of law is irrelevant to liability, while a mistake of fact may negate mens rea (see mens-rea.md).
3. **Fair labelling.** The name of an offence should accurately match the conduct it criminalises, and the law should correctly distinguish different mischiefs. A conviction carries stigma, so the label must fit what D did. The values behind this are accuracy, fairness, and meeting society's expectation that people are labelled correctly. The principle recurs in the module: in the ladder of non-fatal offences, in the murder/manslaughter divide, in Clarkson's work on theft (see theft.md), and in Baker's argument that negligent omitters such as Evans should not be labelled killers (see gross-negligence-manslaughter.md).
4. **Autonomy (minimum criminalisation).** Individuals should be free to act and pursue their goals without restraint, so offences should be kept to a minimum. The law should not criminalise conduct D cannot avoid, and offences should involve voluntary or intentional acts. This links to the voluntariness requirement and to automatism (see automatism.md).
5. **Welfare.** This is the counterweight to autonomy. The law's role is to protect society from harm and to protect individuals from others who would unfairly interfere with them, physically or otherwise. Welfare can justify greater restrictions through the criminal law. The tension between autonomy and welfare runs through omissions liability, consent (*Brown*) and drug-supply manslaughter (*Kennedy (No 2)*).

### 7. Internal structure of offences
1. **Actus reus**: the guilty act or wrongful conduct.
2. **Mens rea**: the guilty mind, meaning D's state of mind or fault.
3. **Defences**: elements that involve neither the guilty act nor the guilty mind.
4. D is liable only if *all* elements of the offence, taken as a whole, are satisfied, and no defence applies.

Actus reus elements can be further divided:
- **Conduct crimes**, e.g. theft. D's conduct does not have to cause any result, so causation rules do not apply.
- **Circumstance ("state of affairs") crimes**, e.g. being in charge of a mechanically propelled vehicle on a road or other public place while unfit to drive through drink or drugs (Road Traffic Act 1988, s4(2)).
- **Result crimes**, e.g. murder, where D's conduct must *cause* the prohibited result (see causation.md).

### 8. Innocent agency
The post-seminar slides introduce innocent agency. Where D uses someone without criminal responsibility to carry out the actus reus, D is the principal. The illustration is *Michael* (1840) 9 C & P 356. D, intending to kill her baby, gave the baby's nurse a bottle of "medicine" (in fact poison) to administer. The nurse left it on the mantelpiece and her five-year-old son gave it to the baby. Both the nurse and the child were innocent agents, so D was responsible. The slides cite Alldridge (1990) and Glanville Williams (1992) on innocent agency.

## Key authorities
- *Woolmington v DPP* [1935] AC 462: the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt (the "golden thread", Lord Sankey LC). This is the foundation of the presumption of innocence.
- *Michael* (1840) 9 C & P 356: poison passed on through an unknowing nurse and a five-year-old child. D was liable because the intermediaries were innocent agents.
- Road Traffic Act 1988, s4(2): the materials's example of a circumstance ("state of affairs") offence.
- *Sweet v Parsley* [1970] AC 132: the rebuttable presumption that statutory offences require mens rea (see mens-rea.md).

## Debate and critique
- **What makes a wrong "public"?** Ambrose Lee ("Public Wrongs and the Criminal Law") accepts that crimes are public wrongs, but argues that the phrase should not just mean wrongs that properly concern the public. It should mean wrongs that the State, acting as the public, *ought to punish*. He builds this view by contrasting criminal law with tort and contract, and he draws out its implications for criminalisation. The article engages with Blackstone and with Duff and Marshall.
- **Coercion and respect.** In "Legal Coercion, Respect & Reason-Responsive Agency", Lee asks why legal coercion seems morally troubling. His answer is that threat-based coercion, the model suggested by deterrence justifications of punishment, fails to respect individuals as reason-responsive agents. Human beings are owed that respect simply because they are human. Ekow Yankah's "The Force of Law: The Role of Coercion in Legal Norms" is also on the reading list on the place of coercion in law.
- **Overcriminalisation.** Yankah ("A Paradox in Overcriminalization") argues that liberal criminal theory has done little to slow the growth of criminal law. He uses the functional decriminalisation of marijuana to argue that reform succeeds when advocates build consensus across philosophical starting points, liberal and non-liberal, rather than relying only on liberal theory. This is useful for essays on autonomy and minimum criminalisation.
- **Preventive justice and the presumption of innocence.** Kimberly Kessler Ferzan examines the "Catch-22" the State faces when it tries to prevent harm by responsible but dangerous people. Using the criminal law raises concerns about punishing early inchoate conduct. Using civil preventive regimes raises concerns about bypassing criminal procedural protections. That second concern is Andrew Ashworth's charge that such regimes evade the presumption of innocence. Ferzan separates two strands of the presumption: "substantive priority" (criminal law comes first in assessing blame) and "procedural symmetry". She tentatively concludes that proof beyond reasonable doubt is warranted even in the preventive regime she defends.
- **Ignorance of law.** the materials flags Matthews (2018) as a critique of the rule that ignorance of law is no excuse. The rule sits uneasily with fair warning when there are very many offences and the law is complex.
- **Acts v omissions.** The minimal-intervention, autonomy-based view of criminal law underlies the "conventional view" that there is no general duty to rescue (Glanville Williams' reply to Ashworth). See actus-reus-and-omissions.md.

## Applying it to a problem question
The revision lecture and the "Case Problem Method" handout give the expected technique:
1. **Read the instructions carefully.** Identify whose liability you are asked about, and for which offence. If only one offence is asked about, do not discuss lesser included offences. Do not add facts that are not in the scenario.
2. **Identify the possible offences.** Start with the actus reus: look for harms caused by one party to another, note each potential D and V, then the facts relevant to mens rea and to any defence.
3. **Start with the most serious offence** that may apply. Name the exact statute, section and subsection. Examine one offence at a time.
4. **Subsumption.** For each element, state the rule with its authority and then match the facts that satisfy it. Repeating the facts is not argument. Do not jump ahead to the "juicy" issue before working through the earlier elements.
5. **Check actus reus and mens rea before defences.** You cannot know whether a defence matters until you have established the offence.
6. **Style.** Skip introductions and conclusions. Use numbered sub-headings for each offence and defendant. Write in an academic register without "I"/"we" or contractions. Do not "hide behind the jury": reach a view. Avoid filler words such as "likely" and "clearly". Do not announce that you need to prove actus reus and mens rea.
7. **Keep the burden in mind.** The prosecution must prove each element beyond reasonable doubt. Where D carries a reverse burden (insanity, diminished responsibility), say so and apply the balance of probabilities.
8. **Essay technique** (revision lecture): use a clear introduction, a signposted body and a concise conclusion. Engage with cases and academic articles, not just the textbook. Give your own argued view, focus on problematic areas and reform proposals, and ask why proposals have not been enacted or whether recent reform is working. The exam is 3,500 words: three questions, one essay from Part A and both problem questions in Part B.
