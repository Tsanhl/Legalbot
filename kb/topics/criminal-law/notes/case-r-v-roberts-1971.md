# R v Roberts (1971) 56 Cr App R 95 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report ([1971] EWCA Crim 4) before citing them.

## Overview
Court of Appeal (Criminal Division), 1971. A victim's attempt to escape from the defendant's assault breaks the chain of
causation only if her reaction was something no reasonable person could have foreseen; otherwise it is a natural
consequence of the defendant's conduct.

## Facts
Roberts gave a young woman he had just met a lift in his car after a party. He made sexual advances, which she
rejected, and tried to pull off her coat while driving. She jumped from the moving car and was injured (concussion,
cuts and bruises). He was acquitted of indecent assault but convicted of assault occasioning actual bodily harm
(OAPA 1861 s 47) and appealed, arguing that her jump was a break in the chain of causation.

## Held
- Appeal **dismissed**; the jury was correctly directed.
- The question is whether the victim's act was a **natural result** of what the defendant said or did. If it was,
  the defendant is liable even if he did not foresee that particular reaction.
- The chain breaks only if the victim's reaction is so unexpected, "daft" in the court's words as summarised, that no
  reasonable person could have foreseen it, and so is a remote consequence.
- For s 47 the defendant need only intend or be reckless as to the assault or battery, not the actual bodily harm.

## Why it matters
- Standard authority on **victims who flee** from danger and are injured.
- Sets the natural-consequence test later restated in terms of reasonable foreseeability.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Did the victim react to the defendant's threat or violence (flight, jumping, running into traffic)?
2. Was that reaction within the range of responses a reasonable person might expect?
3. If yes, the defendant remains the cause; if wholly disproportionate or unforeseeable, the chain may break.
