# Ivey v Genting Casinos (UK) Ltd t/a Crockfords [2017] UKSC 67 (case note)

> Civil case, noted for its effect on **criminal** dishonesty. The Ivey judgment itself was not read; its holding and
> test are taken from a secondary account and the Court of Appeal's treatment of it in *Barton and Booth*.
> Check paragraph references ([2018] AC 391) against the report before citing them.

## Overview
UK Supreme Court (Lord Neuberger, Lady Hale, Lord Kerr, Lord Hughes, Lord Thomas), judgment 25 October 2017. The
decision itself concerned a casino's refusal to pay winnings. Its significance for criminal law is the Court's
(obiter) rejection of the second, subjective limb of the *R v Ghosh* test of dishonesty, adopted for criminal cases in
*R v Barton and Booth* [2020] EWCA Crim 575.

## Facts
Phil Ivey, a professional poker player, won about £7.7m playing Punto Banco at Crockfords casino using "edge
sorting", a technique he had the croupier help with by how the cards were handled. The casino refused to pay,
saying he had cheated. The trial judge found that Ivey did not believe he was cheating but that he had cheated; the
Court of Appeal agreed (by a majority) that dishonesty was not required.

## Held
- The appeal was **dismissed**: cheating in the contractual and statutory sense does not require dishonesty, and Ivey
  cheated by deceptively influencing the croupier to defeat the randomness of the game.
- **Obiter on dishonesty:** the fact-finder first establishes the defendant's actual state of mind as to knowledge or
  belief about the facts (which need not be reasonable), then decides whether the conduct was dishonest by the
  standards of ordinary decent people. The defendant need not appreciate that his conduct would be seen as dishonest.
- The Court criticised *Ghosh*, which asked whether the defendant himself realised that ordinary people would regard
  his conduct as dishonest, and saw no reason for different civil and criminal tests.

## Effect in criminal law
- In *R v Barton and Booth* [2020] EWCA Crim 575 (judgment of the Lord Chief Justice, handed down 29 April 2020) the
  Court of Appeal held that the *Ivey* test governs dishonesty in all criminal cases and that *Ghosh* is no longer
  law. Although the Supreme Court's comments were obiter, the Court followed them where all the Supreme Court
  justices agreed on the effect of the decision.
- The two-stage test from *Barton and Booth*: (1) the defendant's actual knowledge or belief as to the facts
  (subjective); (2) whether the conduct was dishonest by the standards of ordinary decent people (objective). Directing
  the jury as in *Ivey* is enough.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Establish what the defendant knew or believed about the facts.
2. Apply the objective standard of ordinary decent people to that conduct.
3. Do not ask whether the defendant realised others would call it dishonest.
