# Unlawful Act Manslaughter

## Overview
Involuntary manslaughter covers killings where D causes death (the actus reus of murder) without the mens rea for murder. It sits on a "spectrum of blameworthiness" and takes two forms: unlawful act manslaughter (UAM) and gross negligence manslaughter (GNM). UAM is also called "unlawful and dangerous act" or "constructive" manslaughter. D commits a criminal offence that carries an objective risk of some harm, and death results. No mens rea is needed as to the dangerousness or the death. That makes UAM a classic case of constructive liability and moral luck: the "one-punch killer" is the standard example. It is heavily criticised in the literature.

## The law

### 1. Definition
D commits UAM where:
1. D has committed a **base offence** (an unlawful act);
2. the base offence is **dangerous**, carrying an objective risk of some harm to V; and
3. the unlawful act **causes V's death**.

No mens rea is required for dangerousness or death beyond the mens rea of the base offence. This is constructive liability.

### 2. Element 1: the base offence
1. It must be a **criminal offence**, and D must be the one who commits it (*Kennedy* [2008] 1 AC 269).
2. V need not be the target. In *Watson* [1989] Crim LR 730, a burglary led to the death of a 79-year-old occupant from a heart attack caused by the shock (as described in the sentencing guidelines article).
3. **All elements of the base offence must be proved, with no valid defence** (*Lamb* [1967] 2 QB 981). In *Lamb*, D shot his best friend in jest, not realising that the revolver's mechanism would rotate a bullet into the firing position. Without mens rea for an assault there was no unlawful act (as described in *JF*).
4. **Restrictions**:
   - the base offence must require subjective mens rea. It cannot be negligence-based (*Andrews v DPP* [1937] AC 576), where GNM is more appropriate. Logically it cannot be strict liability either, though there is no authority (SHO p198);
   - it must be an **act, not an omission** (*Lowe* [1973] QB 702).
5. **Common base offences**:
   - offences against the person, e.g. one-punch deaths and pranks or hazing (*A* (2005));
   - criminal damage, e.g. fires (*Farnon*/*JF* [2015] EWCA Crim 351);
   - property offences, e.g. burglary (*Watson*);
   - immigration offences, e.g. human trafficking (*Nica* [2021] EWCA Crim 1790).

### 3. Element 2: dangerousness (objective)
1. *Church* [1966] 1 QB 59: the act must be such that "all sober and reasonable people would inevitably recognise" it must subject the other person to at least the risk of *some harm*, albeit not serious harm. Not every unlawful act causing death is manslaughter.
2. The harm must be **physical**, not merely emotional (*Dhaliwal* [2006] EWCA Crim 1139).
3. The test is **objective**: what the reasonable person would foresee, not what D foresaw (*DPP v Newbury* [1977] AC 500). In *Newbury*, two 15-year-old boys pushed a paving stone off a railway bridge into an oncoming train, killing the guard. Lord Salmon held that D is guilty if he intentionally did an act that was unlawful and dangerous and that inadvertently caused death, and that it is unnecessary to prove D knew the act was unlawful or dangerous.
4. The risk is not always foreseeable. The discussion cites *Dawson* (1985) 81 Cr App R 150 for the point that the relevant risk may depend on special knowledge that is not apparent to a reasonable person. The risk must be one the reasonable person *would* have foreseen, not merely *may* have foreseen.
5. **No adjustment for age or capacity.** In *JF and NE* [2015] EWCA Crim 351, a boy of 14½ (with limited mental capacity) and a girl of 16 set fire to a duvet in the basement of a derelict former Sea Cadet building, killing a man inside. The court refused to adapt the objective test to their age or capacity. The law is "clear and well established", and any change is for Parliament, given the Law Commission's recommendations (CP 135 (1994), Law Com 237 (1997), Law Com 304 (2006)) to require subjective foresight of some injury.

### 4. Element 3: causation of death
1. The ordinary principles of factual and legal causation apply (causation.md).
2. No mens rea as to death is needed. Death is a strict liability element (*AG's Ref (No 3 of 1994)* [1998] AC 245, where UAM was possible for the death of the child born after the stabbing of its mother).
3. **Breaking the chain**:
   - "fright and flight": V fleeing the scene of the crime (*Lewis* [2010] EWCA Crim 151);
   - drug supply: V's voluntary self-administration of drugs supplied by D breaks the chain (*Kennedy (No 2)* [2007] UKHL 38 / [2008] 1 AC 269). Supply is not itself the act that kills, and V's free, deliberate and informed injection intervenes;
   - suicide (*Dhaliwal*).

### 5. Mens rea
Only the mens rea of the base offence is required (*Lamb*). There is no separate mens rea for the constructive offence.

## Key authorities
- *Church* [1966] 1 QB 59: the sober and reasonable person recognising a risk of some harm. A series of acts can culminate in death.
- *DPP v Newbury* [1977] AC 500: objective dangerousness. D need not foresee harm.
- *Lamb* [1967] 2 QB 981: there must be a complete unlawful act, including its mens rea.
- *Andrews v DPP* [1937] AC 576: negligent performance of a lawful act is not UAM.
- *Lowe* [1973] QB 702: an omission (wilful neglect) is not enough for UAM.
- *Dawson* (1985) 81 Cr App R 150; *Watson* [1989] Crim LR 730: the knowledge attributed to the reasonable person.
- *Dhaliwal* [2006] EWCA Crim 1139: physical harm required. Suicide may break the chain.
- *Kennedy (No 2)* [2007] UKHL 38: self-injection breaks the chain from supply.
- *Lewis* [2010] EWCA Crim 151: flight cases.
- *JF and NE* (*Farnon*) [2015] EWCA Crim 351: no age or capacity adjustment to the objective test. Reform is for Parliament.
- *Larkin* [1943]: waving a razor to frighten. V stumbled onto it and died, giving manslaughter (as described in Leigh).

## Debate and critique
- **Moral luck and one-punch killers (Barry Mitchell, "Minding the Gap" (2008) 72 J Crim L 537).** The one-punch killer has crossed a moral threshold by assaulting V, but death may be both unforeseen and unforeseeable. The gap between foreseeable harm and death is too great, and a manslaughter conviction puts too much weight on luck. Mitchell rejects the "hard world, consequences matter" moral-luck argument. Everyday blame is not equivalent to state punishment, and outcomes often depend on others. The criminal law "should censure people for wrongs, not misfortunes". He argues manslaughter liability should require a foreseeable or foreseen risk of death or at least serious (preferably life-threatening) harm, with the activity's inherent riskiness and V's apparent vulnerability relevant. He considers and rejects an Irish Law Reform Commission compromise, and regrets that the homicide review ignored the issue. Example cases he discusses: *Mallett* [1972] Crim LR 260 (a punch, V hit his head on a paving stone) and *Williams* [1996] 2 Cr App R (S) 72 (a slap and push, V hit her head on a heater).
- **Two kinds of UAM (Gavin Leigh, "Deconstructing Unlawful Act Manslaughter" (2017) 81 J Crim L 112).** Leigh argues that current law merges two historically separate forms: (1) manslaughter by an act of intended bodily harm (*Fenton* (1830); *Larkin*), and (2) manslaughter by an unlawful and dangerous act (*Church*). Each needs its own justification. Form (1) can be justified by the intention to harm changing D's "normative position" (Horder), the malice and proportionality principles, and the "pursuit" of harm showing a dependence on luck. Without intended harm, the correspondence principle suggests liability only for a foreseen or foreseeable risk of death. He criticises *JF* for refusing a capacity exception when *S* allowed GNM's reasonable person to share D's age and experience. He proposes UAM where a likely risk of death from an advertent crime could be foreseen and avoided by someone of D's age with normal mental and physical capacity. If UAM is to be limited to violent crimes, intended ABH is an appropriate threshold. He notes that the 1839 Criminal Law Commissioners already doubted punishing wholly unexpected consequences, and that Ashworth argued an intended ABH causing death should not be labelled homicide.
- **The Law Commission's "criminal act manslaughter".** LC 304 proposed manslaughter by a criminal act intended to cause injury, or one D was aware involved a serious risk of causing injury. This adds a subjective element (awareness of risk). It has not been enacted, and the court in *JF* expressly left it to Parliament.
- **Omissions and the "reckless manslaughter" gap (Richard Taylor, building on Findlay Stark).** Stark argues there is no need for a third category of reckless manslaughter. The "school dinner" example (a provider lets a child with a known pine-nut allergy eat a sandwich containing nuts, foreseeing serious injury but not death) fits neither GNM, since the risk of death is not obvious, nor orthodox UAM. Taylor argues *Lowe* has been misread as excluding all omissions from UAM. Where there is a duty to act and the omission satisfies the elements of an offence (e.g. s20 OAPA), UAM by omission is possible.
- **Constructive liability generally.** Supporters (cf. Gardner on s47, aggravated-non-fatal-offences.md) say committing a crime changes D's normative position and D takes the risk of worse consequences. Opponents (Ashworth, Mitchell, Leigh) invoke the correspondence principle and fair labelling.
- **Sentencing.** The manslaughter guideline gives UAM a range of 1-24 years. The "Reflections" article notes that sentence levels have risen (*Appleby* (2009)).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is there murder?** If D lacked intent to kill or cause GBH (e.g. a single punch to "teach a lesson"), move to involuntary manslaughter.
2. **Identify the base offence precisely** (battery, s47, criminal damage, burglary, supply of drugs) and prove *all* its elements, including mens rea and the absence of defences (*Lamb*). It must be an act, not an omission (*Lowe*). Negligence-based or strict liability offences do not qualify.
3. **Dangerousness.** Would a sober and reasonable person, with D's knowledge of the circumstances, inevitably recognise a risk of some physical harm (*Church*; *Newbury*)? Fear alone is not enough, but consider whether V's vulnerability (e.g. the elderly occupant in *Watson*) made physical harm foreseeable, and whether the risk depended on knowledge not apparent to a reasonable person (*Dawson*). D's youth or low capacity is irrelevant (*JF*).
4. **Causation.** Thin skull (a hidden brain haemorrhage: still caused). V's flight (*Lewis*, *Roberts*). Drug self-injection (*Kennedy*: a break, so consider GNM by omission under *Evans* instead).
5. **Exercise (pub punch).** Battery is the unlawful act. A punch carries an obvious risk of some harm, so it is dangerous. Death via the stool and an undiagnosed haemorrhage is caused (thin skull). This is UAM if murder fails for lack of intent to cause GBH.
6. **Distinguish GNM.** If the facts involve a duty and a gross breach (doctors, carers, drug companions), or an omission, analyse GNM (gross-negligence-manslaughter.md).
