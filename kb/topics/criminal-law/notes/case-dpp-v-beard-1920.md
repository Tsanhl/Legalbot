# DPP v Beard [1920] AC 479 (case note)

> Case note written from secondary accounts and a web reproduction of the Lord Chancellor's speech; the report itself is
> not in the knowledge base. Check pinpoints against the report (also 36 TLR 379; 14 Cr App R 159) before citing them.
> One study summary describes the Lords as substituting manslaughter, which conflicts with the speech; see Held.

## Overview
House of Lords (Lord Birkenhead LC giving the principal speech). The classic statement of how **voluntary
drunkenness** relates to criminal intent, and the source of the later specific/basic intent distinction.

## Facts
Beard, a night watchman, raped a young girl (aged 13 on one account, 12 on another). To stop her screaming he put his
hand over her mouth, and she died of suffocation. The evidence of drunkenness was slight. He was convicted of murder at
Chester Assizes. The Court of Criminal Appeal held that a killing in the course of a violent felony such as rape is
murder, but that the trial judge had misdirected the jury on drunkenness; it substituted manslaughter. The
Attorney-General certified a point of law and the case went to the House of Lords.

## Held
- On the account of the speech, the Lords **allowed the appeal and restored the murder conviction**: there was no
  evidence he was too drunk to form the intent to rape, and a killing in the course of rape is murder.
  (A single study summary says manslaughter was substituted; it is inconsistent with the speech.)
- Lord Birkenhead's propositions, in outline: (1) insanity, however caused, is a defence; (2) evidence of drunkenness
  that renders the accused **incapable of forming the specific intent** must be weighed with the other evidence in
  deciding whether he formed it; (3) drunkenness short of that, which merely makes a person more prone to violent
  passion, does not rebut the presumption that he intends the natural consequences of his acts.
- Where the intoxicated defendant could not form the intent for murder, the verdict could be manslaughter.

## Why it matters
- Origin of the rule that intoxication is relevant to **specific intent** crimes only if it prevents the intent being
  formed.
- Developed and applied to basic-intent offences in *DPP v Majewski* [1977] AC 443.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the mens rea required and whether the offence is specific or basic intent.
2. Specific intent: did intoxication leave the defendant incapable of forming it? Mere disinhibition is not enough.
3. If specific intent is negated, consider the lesser offence (e.g. manslaughter for murder).
