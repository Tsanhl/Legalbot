# Intoxication

## Overview
Intoxication is classed with automatism and insanity as a "denial of a crime": it is not an excuse or justification but evidence about whether the defendant (D) had the required mens rea. The law is driven by public policy, because alcohol is involved in a large proportion of violent offences (the materials cites ONS 2021 figures: alcohol perceived to be involved in 53% of violent incidents). The result is a rule that depends on whether the intoxication was voluntary or involuntary and, if voluntary, whether the crime is one of "specific" or "basic" intent.

## The law
1. **No excuse as such.** It is never a defence to admit the offence and say one would not have done it sober (*DPP v Beard* [1920] AC 479). Intoxication matters only if it prevents D forming mens rea: D may be too drunk to think, unaware of facts, or blind to an obvious risk. It can also help explain otherwise improbable claims about mens rea.
2. **A drunken intent is still intent.** If D had the mens rea, D is guilty; a drunken awareness of risk is recklessness. It is irrelevant that D would not have offended sober (*Sheehan and Moore* [1975] 1 WLR 739).
3. **Involuntary intoxication** can defeat liability for any crime, but only if it stops D forming the mens rea. Examples: spiked drinks, medically prescribed drugs, non-dangerous drugs taken without recklessness.
   - *Kingston* [1994] 3 All ER 353: D's drink was spiked so that he would give in to his urges and he sexually assaulted a 15-year-old boy. He still had the mens rea, so the fact he would never have acted sober made no difference (relevant to sentence only).
   - A mistake about the nature or strength of the substance is irrelevant (*Allen*).
   - *Hardie* [1985] 1 WLR 64: D took Valium given to him by another and told it would do no harm; *Majewski* did not apply. The jury should ask whether D's taking of the Valium was itself reckless, which needs subjective awareness of the risk.
4. **Voluntary intoxication** (*DPP v Majewski* [1977] AC 443):
   - specific intent crimes: it can negate mens rea (evidence of lack of the required intent);
   - basic intent crimes: self-induced intoxication, "however gross", cannot excuse, even if it produced a condition like automatism. Becoming intoxicated is itself a reckless course of conduct which supplies the mens rea (*Majewski*, supported obiter by Lord Diplock in *Caldwell* [1982] AC 341).
5. **Classifying offences.** Offences where recklessness suffices (battery, ABH, s 20 wounding/GBH) are basic intent. In *Heard* [2007] EWCA Crim 125 Hughes LJ said specific intent crimes are those requiring proof of purpose or consequence, including (but not confined to) ulterior intent; sexual assault required only basic intent so voluntary intoxication could not negate it. The court said there is "no universally logical test", a large element of policy, and categorisation is offence by offence.
6. **Link to automatism.** Where automatism is caused by voluntary intoxication with dangerous substances, it is no defence to basic intent crimes (*Quick*); for non-intoxicant causes, the prosecution must show D was reckless as to the state (*Bailey* [1982] 1 WLR 760).

## Key authorities
- *DPP v Beard* [1920] AC 479: no defence of "would not have done it sober".
- *DPP v Majewski* [1977] AC 443: voluntary intoxication irrelevant to basic intent offences.
- *Sheehan and Moore* [1975] 1 WLR 739: drunken mens rea still mens rea.
- *Kingston* [1994] 3 All ER 353: involuntary intoxication does not help if mens rea is formed.
- *Hardie* [1985] 1 WLR 64: non-dangerous drug; recklessness question.
- *Heard* [2007] EWCA Crim 125: meaning of specific and basic intent; sexual assault is basic intent.

## Debate and critique
- Law Commission 1995 (para 3.17): several competing theories of the specific/basic distinction create "a great deal of uncertainty" which it did not regard as satisfactory; the lack of accepted criteria means that for any new offence it cannot be known which category it falls in until the courts decide (para 3.27).
- Theories of what *Majewski* does: a rule of law (fault in becoming intoxicated supplies the mens rea, effectively a modified recklessness about the intoxication itself); a rule of evidence (intoxication evidence excluded, asking whether D would have had mens rea sober); a policy decision; or morality (moral equivalence between offending sober and getting voluntarily intoxicated).
- Pragmatic approach: "specific intent" simply means a crime for which evidence of voluntary intoxication negativing mens rea is a defence (Smith, Hogan and Ormerod, as quoted).
- Essay prompts in the materials: should the distinction be abandoned and replaced by listing which fault elements (knowledge, dishonesty, etc.) intoxication can negate; or should the special rules be abolished so intoxication is simply dealt with as mens rea evidence in the usual way?

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Voluntary or involuntary? (Spiked, prescribed, non-dangerous drug taken without recklessness = involuntary.)
2. Involuntary: did D nevertheless form mens rea? If yes, guilty (*Kingston*); if not, no liability for any crime.
3. Voluntary (or reckless taking of a dangerous or risky drug): is the offence specific or basic intent? Apply *Majewski* and *Heard*; check what the courts have held for that offence.
4. Specific intent: argue lack of the intent; if no intent, consider whether a lesser basic intent alternative (e.g. s 20 instead of s 18) applies.
5. Basic intent: intoxication is no defence; the drunken state supplies recklessness.
6. Trap: do not treat the claim "I would never have done it sober" as relevant; do not confuse with automatism.
