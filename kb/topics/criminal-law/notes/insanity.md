# Insanity and the boundary with automatism

## Overview
Insanity concerns the defendant's mental state when the offence was committed (and, separately, at the time of pleading). It is a "denial of crime" governed by the M'Naghten Rules (1843). A successful plea leads to a special verdict, not a straightforward acquittal. The key difficulty is the internal/external distinction which separates insanity (internal disease) from "sane" automatism (external cause, full acquittal).

## The law
1. **Unfitness to plead.** Test: D cannot understand the charge, distinguish guilty from not guilty pleas, challenge jurors, instruct counsel or follow proceedings (*Robertson* [1968] 3 All ER 557); written evidence from two or more registered practitioners; the jury decides whether D did the act. If it did, the judge chooses the disposal (hospital order, supervision order, absolute discharge; hospital order mandatory for murder). Statutory basis: Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act 1964 ss 4 and 4A.
2. **Verdict.** "Not guilty by reason of insanity": Trial of Lunatics Act 1883 s 2(1).
3. **M'Naghten Rules.** (1) Everyone is presumed sane until the contrary is proved. (2) The defence must be clearly proved: at the time D was labouring under a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, so as (a) not to know the nature and quality of the act, or (b) if he did, not to know he was doing wrong. The rules came from judges answering questions put by Parliament after M'Naghten's acquittal; not binding precedent but treated as authoritative.
4. **Who decides and who may raise it.** Whether a defence is insanity is a question of law for the judge on medical evidence (*Dickie* [1984] 3 All ER 173). A judge can rule a plea of automatism is really insanity. The prosecution may raise insanity (Lord Denning in *Bratty* [1963] AC 386: "The old notion that only the defence can raise a defence of insanity is now gone") and may use it to rebut diminished responsibility, bearing the burden.
5. **Three elements:**
   - *Disease of the mind*: a legal, not medical, term. *Kemp* [1957] 1 QB 399: the law concerns the mind (reason, memory, understanding), not the brain; curability and permanence are irrelevant. Lord Denning in *Bratty* endorsed: a mental disorder that has shown itself in violence and is prone to recur is a disease of the mind. *Sullivan* [1984] AC 156: epilepsy; organic or functional and transient or permanent make no difference if the faculties are impaired when the act is done.
   - *Defect of reason*: reasoning powers must be impaired. *Clarke* [1972] 1 All ER 219: absent-mindedness during shoplifting while depressed was not a defect of reason; M'Naghten does not apply to those who retain reasoning power but momentarily fail to use it.
   - *Nature and quality / wrongness*: "nature and quality" refers to the physical character of the act (*Codere* (1916)). "Wrong" is unclear: M'Naghten and *Codere* suggested a moral standard, but *Windle* [1952] 2 QB 826 said "wrong" means contrary to law, confirmed in *Johnson* [2007] EWCA Crim 1978 (on the reading M'Naghten himself would have been convicted).
6. **Internal v external (the automatism boundary).** Automatism requires: complete loss of voluntary control (a high standard: *Broome v Perkins* [1987]; *AG's Reference (No 2 of 1992)*, driving without awareness, was not automatism); an external cause; and no fault in D losing capacity (*Kay v Butterworth*; *Quick*). Evidence: D's own word is insufficient without medical evidence (*Bratty*; *Dervish*).
   - *Quick* [1973] QB 910: hypoglycaemia from insulin plus not eating was external (insulin), so automatism. *Hennessy* [1989] 2 All ER 9: hyperglycaemia from failing to take insulin, stress and anxiety was internal (diabetes), so insanity; stress and depression are a state of mind prone to recur.
   - *Rabey* (Ontario): dissociation after rejection was due to ordinary stresses, so internal, not automatism. *R v T* [1990] Crim LR 256: dissociation after rape was an external factor; automatism.
   - Sleepwalking: seems internal but, per Lord Denning citing *Tolson*, a sane person acting in somnambulism should be acquitted; see *Burgess* [1991] 2 QB 92.

## Key authorities
- *M'Naghten* (1843) 10 Cl & F 200; *Kemp*; *Bratty* [1963] AC 386; *Sullivan* [1984] AC 156; *Hennessy*; *Clarke*; *Windle*; *Johnson*; *Quick*; *Codere*; *Dickie*; *Robertson*.
- *Calocane* [2024] EWCA Crim 490 ("spotlight", not an insanity case): paranoid schizophrenia; diminished responsibility accepted and hospital order with restrictions imposed, illustrating public policy concerns.

## Debate and critique
- Criticisms of M'Naghten: legal language not reflecting psychiatry; absurd internal/external distinctions (*Quick* v *Hennessy*); over-inclusive (diabetics, epileptics, sleepwalkers who would not normally be detained under the Mental Health Act 1983) and under-inclusive (delusions not preventing knowing what one does, as in *Windle*; uncontrollable impulses such as kleptomania).
- Reform proposal: "not criminally responsible by reason of a recognised medical condition" where capacities are impaired (rational judgment, understanding wrongdoing, or physical control), with only an evidential burden on D supported by two experts, plus a reformed automatism defence for total loss of control not caused by a medical condition (e.g. reflex).
- Prompts: labelling problem for those with diabetes, epilepsy or sleep disorders; why a defendant might avoid pleading insanity; why prior fault should affect automatism.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the issue fitness to plead (time of trial) or state at the offence?
2. Was there a disease of the mind (any internal condition, even transient)? Or an external cause (insulin, blow, rape trauma) pointing to automatism?
3. Did it produce a defect of reason (not mere forgetfulness)?
4. Nature and quality, or wrongness (use the *Windle* "contrary to law" test)?
5. Who bears the burden? Everyone is presumed sane and insanity must be "clearly proved" under the Rules.
6. Consequence: special verdict and disposal, so advise on why D may prefer a denial of mens rea or automatism instead.
7. For automatism with prior fault: specific v basic intent and *Bailey* recklessness.
