# R v Kennedy (No 2) [2007] UKHL 38 (case note)

> Case note written from two consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (decided 17 October 2007 per Wikipedia) before citing them.

## Overview
House of Lords. A person who supplies a controlled drug to a fully informed, responsible adult who freely and
voluntarily self-injects it, and dies, **does not cause the death** and is not guilty of unlawful act manslaughter.

## Facts
Kennedy and the victim, Bosque, were in the same hostel. Bosque asked for "a bit to make him sleep". Kennedy prepared a
dose of heroin and handed over the syringe; Bosque injected himself and died hours later, choking on vomit while
affected by opiates (and alcohol on one account). Kennedy was convicted of manslaughter and supplying a controlled
drug, and the case reached the Lords after earlier appeals and a referral by the Criminal Cases Review Commission.
Only the manslaughter conviction was in issue.

## Held
- The manslaughter conviction was **quashed**.
- Bosque's **free, voluntary and informed** act of self-injection was a novus actus interveniens, so supply was not a
  cause of death (following *R v Dias*). The supply made self-injection more likely but did not cause it.
- Kennedy had not "administered" the drug or caused it to be administered under OAPA 1861 s 23, and he was not a
  secondary party, because Bosque committed no offence by self-injecting.
- The prosecution must identify clearly the unlawful act on which an unlawful act manslaughter charge rests.

## Why it matters
- Leading authority on **informed adult autonomy** breaking the causal chain in drug-supply cases.
- Shows the importance of identifying the unlawful act and the cause in unlawful act manslaughter.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Did the victim make a free, deliberate and informed choice (an adult of sound mind, not coerced or deceived)?
2. If so, the choice generally breaks the chain between supply and death.
3. If D himself injects or helps to inject, consider s 23 administration and causation again.
