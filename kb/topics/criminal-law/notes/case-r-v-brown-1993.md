# R v Brown [1994] 1 AC 212 (case note)

> The original judgment was not read. This note is built from secondary sources. Also cited as [1993] UKHL 19;
> [1993] 2 WLR 556; [1993] 2 All ER 75. The House of Lords decision is dated 11 March 1993; the AC volume is 1994.

## Overview
House of Lords, majority 3-2. Lord Templeman, Lord Jauncey and Lord Lowry formed the majority; Lord Mustill and Lord
Slynn dissented. The case is known as the "Spanner" case after the police operation behind it.

## Facts
A group of men took part, over several years and in private, in consensual sadomasochistic activity which caused
injuries but no lasting harm, and none complained. The police found out in an unrelated investigation. They were charged
with assault occasioning actual bodily harm (s 47) and unlawful wounding (s 20) under the Offences Against the Person
Act 1861. The trial judge ruled that consent was no defence, the appellants pleaded guilty, and the Court of Appeal
upheld the convictions.

## Held
- The appeals were **dismissed**. The certified question (whether the prosecution must prove absence of consent to
  s 20 and s 47 offences in a sadomasochistic encounter) was answered in the negative.
- Consent is not a defence to injury of this kind. Lord Templeman said society is "entitled and bound to protect
  itself against a cult of violence".
- Lord Mustill and Lord Slynn would have allowed the appeals.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the level of harm. Where no actual bodily harm is caused, consent can prevent the touching being an
   assault. Where ABH or wounding is caused, say consent is not generally a defence.
2. State the *Brown* rule: consent is no defence to ss 47 and 20 injuries inflicted for sexual gratification.
3. Consider whether the case falls within a recognised exception (properly conducted sport, medical treatment) and
   say that *Brown* leaves those exceptions intact; check the cases on each.
4. Contrast the dissents if arguing about personal autonomy.
