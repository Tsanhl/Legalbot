# Sexual offences and consent (Sexual Offences Act 2003)

## Overview
The Sexual Offences Act 2003 (SOA) makes the absence of consent, combined with the absence of a reasonable belief in consent, the core of rape (s 1), assault by penetration (s 2), sexual assault (s 3) and causing a person to engage in sexual activity without consent (s 4). Consent is part of the actus reus, not a defence: the focus is on the complainant (B)'s state of mind. The Act gives three routes for deciding consent: conclusive presumptions (s 76), rebuttable presumptions (s 75) and the general definition (s 74). This note covers ss 1-4.

## The law
**1. Three routes to consent.**
- *Section 76 (conclusive presumptions).* If D did the relevant act (ss 1-4: s 77) and either (a) D intentionally deceived B as to the nature or purpose of the act, or (b) D intentionally induced consent by impersonating a person known personally to B, it is conclusively presumed that B did not consent and D did not believe B consented. Intentional deception is required, so B's unilateral mistake is not enough. Older cases about not understanding the mechanics of sex: *Flattery* (1877) (believed it was surgery); *Williams* [1923] (believed it was to help her singing). *Jheeta* [2007] EWCA Crim 1699: lies that she must have intercourse to avoid a police fine or his suicide did not go to nature or purpose; "common or garden lies" rarely do, and the conclusiveness of s 76 calls for circumspection (see also *Devonald*, *Bingham*). Impersonation needs a causal link (*Elbekkay* [1995]); lying about attributes such as being a medic is not covered (*Richardson* [1998]). *Assange* [2011] EWHC 2849 (Admin) settled that deception can fall under s 74 as well as s 76; courts have since been reluctant to rely on s 76 for deception.
- *Section 75 (evidential presumptions).* If the prosecution proves the relevant act, a s 75(2) circumstance, and that D knew of it, D must raise sufficient evidence to rebut it; otherwise B is taken not to have consented and D not to have reasonably believed consent. Circumstances: (a) violence used, or immediate violence feared by B; (b) B fears violence to another; (c) B unlawfully detained (D not); (d) B asleep or unconscious; (e) B physically unable to communicate consent because of disability; (f) a substance given without B's consent capable of stupefying or overpowering B. The fear must be of immediate violence, not property damage or emotional distress. The circumstance need not have caused the lack of consent. Evidence to rebut must be more than speculative or fanciful (*Cicarelli*); if the judge finds sufficient evidence to raise an issue, the matter goes to the jury under s 74 and the s 75 route is barred (*White* [2010]). Old law: *Mayers* (asleep), *Larter* [1995] (unconscious). Separate offence: s 61 (administering a substance with intent to stupefy).
- *Section 74 (definition).* "A person consents if he agrees by choice, and has the freedom and capacity to make that choice."

**2. Freedom.** Absence of protest, resistance or injury is not required; consent must exist at the time of the act; more pressure means less freedom, judged case by case (coercive control: *Ivor* [2021] EWCA Crim 923). *C* [2012] EWCA Crim 2034: apparent consent after years of abuse and domination from childhood could be "apparent, not real". Submission is distinct from consent: *Olugboja* [1982] QB 320 (kept at a bungalow until she submitted: no consent); *Doyle* [2010] EWCA Crim 119 (partner violence; reluctant but free consent differs from submission, and the jury may need help); *Ali* [2015] on grooming (it does not necessarily vitiate consent but may limit the capacity to choose freely).

**3. Choice (conditional consent and deception).** *Assange*: consent given only if a condom was used was undermined by sex without one. *R (F) v DPP* [2013] EWHC 945 (Admin): consent on the basis of withdrawal before ejaculation; his deliberate disregard of that basis could found rape. *B* [2006] EWCA Crim 2945: non-disclosure of HIV status did not negate consent, but *Lawrance* [2020] EWCA Crim 971 rejected the distinction between non-disclosure and active deception. *McNally* [2013] EWCA Crim 1051: deception about gender can, depending on circumstances, vitiate consent; deceptions about wealth, age or marital status will not. *R (Monica)* [2018] EWHC 3508 (Admin): an undercover officer's deception about identity was not enough; deception closely connected to the nature or purpose of the act itself can negate choice. *Lawrance*: lying about fertility concerns consequences and does not negate consent, whereas lying about condom use concerns the physical performance of the act and does. Working rule: deception not closely connected to the performance of the act does not vitiate consent.

**4. Capacity.** Capacity is about deciding to have sex with this person at this time (*C (Cooper)* [2009] UKHL 42: the person must understand and weigh the information). Intoxication: a drunk B who can still choose and agrees consents; loss of capacity means no consent (*Bree* [2007] EWCA Crim 804; *Kamki* [2013] EWCA Crim 2335 jury directions: unconscious means no capacity; incapacity may arise before unconsciousness; lowered inhibitions are not incapacity). *H* [2007] EWCA Crim 2056: the prosecution need not prove B communicated lack of consent, and B's inability to remember is not fatal.

**5. The offences.**
- *Rape (s 1)*: intentional penetration of vagina, anus or mouth with a penis; B does not consent; D does not reasonably believe B consents. Penetration is a continuing act from entry to withdrawal (s 79(2); *Kaitamaki* [1985] AC 147; *C (Cooper)*), so consent withdrawn mid-act matters if D knows and does not withdraw. Surgically constructed parts count (s 79(3)); "vagina" includes vulva (s 79(9); *F* [2002]). Triable only on indictment; maximum life imprisonment (s 1(4)). Basic intent offence. Oral penetration was included because forced oral sex is equally demeaning and traumatising (*Ismail* [2005]: no sentencing difference).
- *Assault by penetration (s 2)*: intentional penetration of vagina or anus with a part of the body or anything else; sexual (s 78: a reasonable person would consider it sexual by its nature, or by nature possibly sexual and sexual by circumstances or purpose); no consent, no reasonable belief. Life maximum; no mens rea is needed as to the "sexual" element (*AG's Reference (No 1 of 2020)* [2020] EWCA Crim 1665; *Abdullahi* [2022] EWCA Crim 412). There is no offence of reckless penetration.
- *Sexual assault (s 3)*: intentional sexual touching without consent or reasonable belief; touching includes clothing (*H* [2005] EWCA Crim 732; s 79(8)); no need for an assault or hostility, or for B to be aware; physical contact is required (psychiatric assault does not suffice). Triable either way; ten years maximum on indictment.
- *Causing sexual activity without consent (s 4)*: intentionally causing B to engage in sexual activity; can be by words alone (e.g. a text message); D need not be present or participate; the activity must actually occur; D need only be "a" cause (Ormerod and Laird). Life where penetration is involved (s 4(4)); otherwise ten years on indictment; triable either way unless penetration.

**6. Reasonable belief in consent.** Reasonableness is judged having regard to all the circumstances, including any steps D took to find out whether B consented (ss 1(2), 2(2), 3(2)). The mens rea covers D purposely acting without B.s consent, being aware B might not consent, having no belief at all, and holding an unreasonable belief. The test is nearer objective but D's characteristics (age, learning needs, experience) are considered when assessing the reasonable steps; delusional belief due to paranoid schizophrenia cannot be relied on (*B* [2013] EWCA Crim 3). *Ivor*: knowledge of another person's abusive, coercive control of B can make a belief unreasonable. No particular steps are compulsory.

## Debate and critique
- The Act breaks from force-based definitions but struggles to define consent positively; much discretion is left to juries. Legal consent falls short of ideals of enthusiastic consent (the view). The discussion also criticises the "submission" language as outdated.
- Rubenfeld (2013): rape is better understood as a violation of self-possession than of consent and autonomy, which explains why rape by deception is not rape.
- Temkin and Ashworth (2004): should sleep be a conclusive presumption; the reasonableness test leaves open the influence of stereotypes about men, women and sexual situations. Munro and Finch (2003): s 75(2)(f) is limited to drugs that stupefy, missing drugs that reduce resistance or alter inclination.
- The unsettled line between deception about the act and about circumstances (*Assange*, *McNally*, *Monica*, *Lawrance*) and courts' reluctance to use s 76 make outcomes hard to predict.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify the offence and its actus reus (penetration, sexual, touching) and mens rea (intention as to the act; no reasonable belief in consent).
2. Consent in order: s 76 (intentional deception on nature or purpose; impersonation); s 75 (listed circumstance and D's knowledge; has D raised enough evidence?); then s 74 (freedom, choice, capacity).
3. Deception: is it closely connected to the sexual act (condom, gender, disease risk) or only wider circumstances (wealth, status, fertility)?
4. Intoxication: ask about capacity, not regret or memory.
5. Reasonable belief: steps taken and circumstances known to D.
6. Traps: treating consent as a defence; relying on s 76 for ordinary lies; forgetting penetration is continuing.
