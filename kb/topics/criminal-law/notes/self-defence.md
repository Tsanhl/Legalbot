# Self-Defence, Prevention of Crime and Defence of Property

## Overview
Self-defence, prevention of crime and defence of property are justificatory "public and private" defences that make D's use of force lawful. They are complete defences, including to murder. The test has two stages: (1) was the use of *some* force necessary, on the facts as D honestly believed them to be? and (2) was the *degree* of force reasonable, judged objectively on those believed facts? The Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 (CJIA), s76, put the common law on a statutory footing. The Crime and Courts Act 2013 then added special "householder" rules allowing force that is disproportionate but not "grossly disproportionate". The main critiques concern mistaken and intoxicated beliefs, the householder provisions (*Collins*, *Ray*), Article 2 ECHR, and the failure of self-defence for abused women who kill.

## The law

### 1. Sources and categories
- **Public defence: prevention of crime.** Criminal Law Act 1967, s3(1): a person may use such force as is reasonable in the circumstances in the prevention of crime, or in effecting or assisting in the lawful arrest of offenders, suspected offenders or persons unlawfully at large. Under s3(2) this replaces the common law rules for those purposes.
- **Private defences: self-defence (including defence of others) and defence of property.** These are governed by common law as clarified by CJIA 2008, s76, which placed self-defence on a statutory footing for the first time. The statute is the starting point.
- The two often overlap, but not always. If V is under 10 (and so cannot commit a crime), only self-defence applies. The range of permitted force, and D's relationship to V's crime, may also differ.
- **Burden**: D raises the defence with some evidence. The prosecution must then disprove it beyond reasonable doubt.

### 2. Structure
1. Exclusions.
2. Element 1: necessity of force (the "trigger").
3. Element 2: reasonableness of the force used (the "response").
4. Exceptional categories: intoxicated, insane or delusional mistakes; householders.

### 3. Exclusions
- **"Force"**: s3(1) CLA 1967 and s76(1) CJIA. This covers violence and forceful conduct such as breaking a window or a door (*Swales v Cox* [1981] 1 QB 849). Acts lacking a violent or percussive character are not "force" (*Blake* [1993] Crim LR 586).
- **Prior fault**: this is not as restrictive as in duress. D may still rely on self-defence even if D provoked V or was the initial aggressor (*Rashford* [2005] EWCA Crim 3377). But D cannot rely on it if D contrived the situation so as to have an excuse to use force (*Mason* (1756) 1 East PC 239; *Browne* [1973] NI 96, 107). *Keane* [2010] EWCA Crim 2514 (Hughes LJ): where D provoked V, the question is whether V's response was so disproportionate that D was entitled to defend himself. D keeps his right to life and bodily integrity.

### 4. Element 1: was force necessary? (s76(3))
1. **Subjective part.** The facts are taken as D *honestly believed* them to be, even if D was mistaken and even if the mistake was unreasonable (s76(3)-(4)).
   - *Yaman* [2012] EWCA Crim 1075: D used a hammer on three men he believed were robbing his shop. The judge was wrong to direct the jury to consider whether D's belief was reasonable. The question was whether it was honest or genuine.
   - This is the *Williams (Gladstone)* [1987] / *Beckford v R* [1988] principle discussed by Leverick.
2. **Objective part.** On those believed facts, was the use of force sufficiently necessary to prevent an imminent or ongoing attack (self-defence) or crime (prevention of crime)?
   - D must use force *in order to* defend or prevent crime. Force used in revenge for a past attack is excluded (*Williams* [2020] EWCA Crim 193).
   - If D knows a safe alternative is readily available, D should ordinarily take it.
3. **No duty to retreat** (s76(6A)). Force may still be necessary even though D could have retreated and did not. D has the right to act lawfully where D is entitled to be (*Field* [1972] Crim LR 435). But the jury may take the opportunity to retreat into account in deciding whether force was necessary.
4. **Pre-emptive force.** D need not wait to be struck if an attack is imminent. "Immediacy" may be looser for prevention of crime (*Kelly* [1989] NI 341). the materials flags women who kill abusive partners as the hard case.
5. **Innocent parties.** Force may be used against someone who is not committing a crime (e.g. a person who is insane, or a person mistakenly believed to be attacking). *Hichens* [2011] EWCA Crim 1626: proportionate force may be used against an innocent third party if it is the only way to prevent a crime by another.

### 5. Element 2: was the degree of force reasonable?
1. Taking the facts as D believed them, was the level of force *objectively reasonable* (s76)? Force that is disproportionate in the circumstances as D believed them is not reasonable (s76(6)).
2. **Focus on the force, not its consequences** (*Keane*; *Noye* [2011] EWCA Crim 650). For example, D pushes V away and V falls on a hidden object and dies: the push may still be reasonable.
3. **Allowance for the heat of the moment.** s76(7)(a): D "may not be able to weigh to a nicety the exact measure of any necessary action". s76(7)(b): evidence that D did only what D honestly and instinctively thought necessary is strong evidence that the force was reasonable. Hindsight should be avoided (*Reed v Wastie* [1972] Crim LR 221).
4. **Do not confuse the two stages.** An unreasonable mistake about the *need* for force can still succeed at stage 1. But if D wrongly believed the *degree* of force was reasonable and the jury disagree, the defence fails at stage 2.

### 6. Exceptional categories
**(a) Intoxicated mistakes.** s76(5) confirms the common law: D cannot rely on a mistaken belief attributable to voluntary intoxication (*O'Grady*; *Hatton* [2005] EWCA Crim 2951, as discussed by Dingwall). See intoxication.md.

**(b) Insane mistakes.** In *Martin (Anthony)* [2001] EWCA Crim 2245, D claimed his personality disorder and depression made him perceive a greater threat. The Court of Appeal distinguished (a) the facts on which D's perception of an attack was based from (b) the danger those facts presented, and refused to take account of the psychiatric condition. the materials this is inconsistent with the general principle on mistaken belief, but it can be justified by public protection.

**(c) Non-insane delusional mistakes.**
- *Press* [2013] EWCA Crim 1849: D's PTSD led him to believe force of that gravity was needed. His mental condition could be referred to in support of his belief that the force was necessary, when assessing proportionality on the facts as he believed them.
- *Taj* [2018] EWCA Crim 1743: D's psychotic belief was linked to earlier drug and alcohol use, and the court applied the intoxication rule. the materials calls this "a bit contrived": if *Martin* had applied, D would have had no defence.

### 7. Defence of property
- Coke (1604): a man's house is "his Castle and Fortress". There is a long-standing common law right to protect property with reasonable force (*Green v Goddard* (1704) 2 Salk 641), though it raises human rights questions.
- The same two elements apply. D may use reasonable force to prevent V appropriating D's property, to repel an attack, and to eject a non-forceful trespasser. In the last case D must start with peaceable means, asking V to leave and allowing time, but escalation to reasonable force is not necessarily unlawful (*Green v Goddard*).

### 8. Householder cases (s76(5A), (8A)-(8F), added by Crime and Courts Act 2013, s43)
- These were enacted after the controversy in *Martin* to give householders more latitude.
- They apply only to a "householder defendant" acting in **self-defence**, not in prevention of crime under s3 CLA 1967. D must not be a trespasser, and must be in, or partly in, a building that is D's dwelling (or armed forces accommodation).
- D must *believe* V is a trespasser. V need not actually be one, and D's belief need not be reasonable. V's status can change during a visit: in *Cheeseman* [2019] EWCA Crim 149, a welcome guest became a trespasser by starting to wreck D's possessions.
- **s76(5A)**: in a householder case, force is not reasonable in the circumstances D believed them to be if it was **grossly disproportionate**.
- ***R (Collins) v Secretary of State for Justice* [2016] EWHC 33 (Admin)**: D held an intruder in a headlock for over six minutes, causing brain damage. The CPS declined to prosecute on the view that disproportionate (but not grossly disproportionate) force by a householder was lawful. V sought judicial review, arguing the law breached Article 2 ECHR. Leveson P held that the CPS had misunderstood the law. The householder provision does not *authorise* merely disproportionate force. Such force may or may not be reasonable, and the jury must still decide whether the force was reasonable in the circumstances as D believed them (s76(3)), paying attention to s76(7)(b).
- ***Ray* [2017] EWCA Crim 1391**: D argued *Collins* undermined Parliament's intended latitude. The Court of Appeal held:
  - in non-householder cases, disproportionate force is always unreasonable;
  - in householder cases, grossly disproportionate force is always unreasonable, but for merely disproportionate force the jury must decide whether it was reasonable in all the circumstances as D believed them. It may be or may not be.

## Key authorities
- Criminal Law Act 1967, s3: prevention of crime and arrest.
- CJIA 2008, s76 (as amended by Crime and Courts Act 2013, s43): the statutory framework, the householder rule, the intoxication bar and no duty to retreat.
- *Yaman* [2012] EWCA Crim 1075: an honest belief in the facts need not be reasonable.
- *Williams (Gladstone)* [1987]; *Beckford* [1988]: honest belief (as discussed by Leverick).
- *Rashford* [2005]; *Keane* [2010]; *Mason* (1756): prior fault and initial aggressors.
- *Williams* [2020] EWCA Crim 193: revenge is not self-defence.
- *Field* [1972]: no duty to retreat.
- *Hichens* [2011]: force against innocent third parties.
- *Keane* [2010]; *Noye* [2011]: assess the force, not the consequences.
- *Martin (Anthony)* [2001]; *Press* [2013]; *Taj* [2018]: psychiatric and delusional mistakes.
- *Swales v Cox* [1981]; *Blake* [1993]: the meaning of "force".
- *Green v Goddard* (1704): defence of property and ejecting trespassers.
- *Collins* [2016]; *Ray* [2017]; *Cheeseman* [2019]: householder cases.

## Debate and critique
- **Justification or excuse? (Horder, "Redrawing the Boundaries of Self-Defence").** Jeremy Horder engages with Suzanne Uniacke's argument that self-defence is a justification (the right to repel an unjust immediate threat), with "weak" and "strong" versions. He draws on Waldron's idea that the law may permit acts that are morally questionable. The classification matters for mistaken self-defence (is a mistaken defender justified or merely excused?) and for the rights of the person attacked.
- **Article 2 ECHR (Leverick, [2002] Crim LR).** Fiona Leverick starts from the police shooting of Harry Stanley, who was mistakenly believed to be armed. She examines Ashworth's claim that allowing an honest but *unreasonable* mistaken belief to justify a killing (*Williams (Gladstone)*; *Beckford*) breaches Article 2's requirement that lethal force be "no more than absolutely necessary". She rejects J.C. Smith's counter-argument that self-defence killings are not "intentional" deprivations of life, and concludes that English law is incompatible with Article 2 because it gives inadequate protection to the right to life.
- **Householders and the rule of law (Elliott, (2015) J Crim L).** Catherine Elliott argues that the amended s76 creates a dilemma: only reasonable force is allowed, yet force is unreasonable only if *grossly* disproportionate, whereas courts had previously treated "reasonable" and "proportionate" as synonyms. It should be read consistently with the rule of law (legality and equality before the law), keeping the traditional impartial, objective concept of reasonableness, and compatibly with the ECHR, to avoid repeating the problems of the lawful chastisement defence.
- **"Defenceless Castles" (comment on *Collins*, 2016).** This article examines whether the Divisional Court correctly identified Parliament's intention in the 2013 amendment. It notes that the interpretation "disturbed the understanding of many" and would force textbooks to be rewritten, and assesses how the householder defence works in practice, including with intoxication.
- **A contextual objective standard (Pichhadze).** Amir Pichhadze argues that the Court of Appeal in *Martin* was right to reject a psychologically individualised standard, but should have developed a "contextual objective standard". The purely objective test is too rigid for vulnerable defendants such as battered women. He traces the classical framework to *Palmer v R* (1971).
- **Intoxicated mistakes (Dingwall, (2007) 70 MLR 127).** Gavin Dingwall criticises *Hatton* for following *O'Grady*, under which a voluntarily intoxicated mistaken belief in the need for force cannot ground self-defence even for murder (a specific intent crime). He points to (1) the inconsistency between intoxicated aggression (where intoxication can negate specific intent) and intoxicated mistakes about self-defence, and (2) inconsistencies across types of intoxicated mistake. He proposes alternative approaches. He notes that alcohol features in around 40% of violent incidents.
- **Provocateurs (Lisa Hecht, Criminal Law and Philosophy 2019).** Hecht distinguishes aggressors (who initiate unlawful force) from provocateurs (who incite but pose no immediate wrongful threat). She argues that a strict forfeiture rule wrongly leaves them defenceless against excessive retaliation, and proposes judging both by their moral responsibility for an unjust threat. Compare *Rashford* and *Keane*.
- **Women who kill (Howes, Swaine Williams and Wistrich, [2021] Crim LR).** Drawing on research by the Centre for Women's Justice and Justice for Women, and on the cases of Farieissia Martin and Emma-Jayne Magson, the authors argue that self-defence rarely succeeds for women who kill abusive partners. This is because of evidential barriers, judicial attitudes, and a failure to integrate coercive control (recognised in the Serious Crime Act 2015) into the tests of necessity, imminence and reasonableness. They propose procedural and legal reforms, possibly including a partial defence specific to domestic-abuse killings. This links to the materials's flag on pre-emptive force.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Establish the offence first** (e.g. battery, s18, murder). Then raise self-defence or prevention of crime, citing CLA 1967 s3 and CJIA s76. The prosecution must disprove it.
2. **Exclusions.** Was it "force"? Did D contrive the attack? Being the initial aggressor or provoker does not on its own bar the defence (*Rashford*; *Keane*).
3. **Element 1: belief and necessity.** What did D honestly believe (even unreasonably: *Yaman*)? Was that belief due to voluntary intoxication (s76(5): disregard it) or to insanity (*Martin*) or PTSD (*Press*)? On those facts, was some force needed to meet an imminent or ongoing threat? Was it revenge (*Williams* [2020])? Could D have retreated? (There is no duty to, but it is relevant.) Was the force pre-emptive?
4. **Element 2: degree.** Was the force objectively reasonable for the threat as D perceived it, allowing for the heat of the moment (s76(7))? A knife against fists is suspect. Judge the force, not the outcome (*Keane*).
5. **Householder?** Was D in D's own dwelling, not a trespasser, believing V a trespasser, and acting in self-defence? If so: grossly disproportionate force is never reasonable, and merely disproportionate force may be reasonable for the jury (*Ray*; *Collins*). Otherwise, disproportionate force is unreasonable.
6. **Lecture exercise (hairdresser).** Grabbing the scissors to stop an unwanted haircut, causing bruising: there was a belief in an imminent unlawful touching, and the force used was minor and probably reasonable, so the defence is likely to succeed. Stabbing him in the abdomen "in a rage" after the haircut fails: it looks like revenge, the threat was over, and the force was grossly excessive.
7. **Abused defendants.** Consider self-defence (pre-emptive force, imminence) alongside the loss of control fear trigger and diminished responsibility. Note the critique in "Women Who Kill".
