# Duress and Necessity

## Overview
Duress and necessity are general defences of compulsion. D admits the actus reus and mens rea of the offence but claims that they acted because of a threat of death or serious injury. That threat may come from a person (duress by threats) or from circumstances (duress of circumstances). Necessity, by contrast, involves choosing the lesser of two evils. Duress is classified as an **excuse**: D's conduct is still wrong, but D is not blamed. Necessity is treated as a **justification**, like self-defence. Duress is a complete defence to most crimes, but not to murder, attempted murder or some forms of treason. Whether that exclusion is defensible, and how restrictive the courts have become since *Hasan*, are the central debates.

## The law

### 1. Nature, rationale and burden
- In *Graham* [1982] 1 All ER 801, Lord Lane described duress as the case where one person's words or actions break the will of another.
- **Rationales**:
  - D is less morally blameworthy because D did not act from free choice;
  - acting under duress does not reveal bad character;
  - deterrence has little force, because the reasonable person would not have resisted.
- Horder (1992): duress operates where inflicting harm is the only step that could reasonably be regarded as likely to protect the autonomy of the person attacked.
- Chan and Simester (2005): giving in to the threat may be not just understandable but reasonable, even though it violates the victim's rights.
- *Hasan* [2005] UKHL 22, Lord Bingham: adults of sound mind are ordinarily responsible for their crimes. Since the fourteenth century there has been a limited exception for those compelled by others' threats.
- **Burden**: D bears an evidential burden, meaning D must raise some evidence. The prosecution must then disprove duress beyond reasonable doubt (*Bone* [1968] 1 WLR 983).

### 2. Duress by threats: the *Graham* test (approved in *Howe* and *Hasan*)
1. **Subjective.** Was D, or may D have been, impelled to act as D did because, as a result of what D *reasonably believed* the threatener had said or done, D had good cause to fear that otherwise the threatener would kill D or cause D serious injury?
2. **Objective.** If so, has the prosecution made the jury sure that a sober person of reasonable firmness, sharing D's characteristics, would *not* have responded as D did?

### 3. Element 1: the threat and the demand
**(a) Content of the threat.**
- It must be a threat of **death or serious personal injury** (*Hudson and Taylor* [1971] 2 QB 202; *DPP for NI v Lynch* [1975] AC 653; *Hasan*).
  - A threat to property or of lesser harm is not enough. Duress is not a "balance of evils" defence.
  - Extreme pain without injury is not enough (*Quayle* [2006] 1 All ER 988).
  - Serious psychological harm is not enough (*Baker and Wilkins* [1997] Crim LR 497).
  - A threat to damage property is not enough (*M'Growther* (1746) Fost 13).
  - Threats to expose sexual immorality are not enough (*Singh* [1973] 1 WLR 1600; *Valderrama-Vega* [1985] Crim LR 220).
  - Rape can amount to serious harm (*R v A* [2012] EWCA Crim 434).
- The threat must be a *sine qua non* of D's offending, but need not be the only reason (*Valderrama-Vega*).
- **Mistaken belief**: there need be no objective evidence of a threat, provided D *reasonably* believed it existed (*Safi* [2003] EWCA Crim 1809). Otherwise there could be no duress from an unloaded gun.

**(b) Source and target.**
- The threat must come from another person. It can be relayed through a third party, but cannot originate inside D. In *Rodger and Rose* [1998] 1 Cr App R 143, D's own suicidal state was not duress. Allowing it would be "a licence to commit crime" dependent on personal vulnerability. Duress is a concession to *human* frailty, not *individual* frailty.
- It may be directed at D, D's immediate family, someone close to D, or a person for whose safety D would reasonably regard himself as responsible (*Wright* [2000] Crim LR 510; *Hasan*). Threats to the general public are not enough (*Shayler* [2001] EWCA Crim 1977).

**(c) The demand.** The threatener must indicate the type of crime required, though not its precise details. In *Cole* [1994] Crim LR 582, moneylenders did not specify any crime and there was no imminence, so no duress. In *Ali* [1995] Crim LR 303, the specificity was sufficient. D cannot choose for himself which crime to commit.

### 4. Element 2: D's response
1. **Causal link**: the offence must be compelled by the threat.
2. **Imminence and evasive action.**
   - *Hudson and Taylor*: the threat can be "present" even if its execution is delayed, but the defence fails if D did not take an opportunity reasonably open to render the threat ineffective (e.g. police protection).
   - *Hussain* [1999] Crim LR 570 (referred to in *Re A* as *Abdul-Hussain*; hijacking a plane to escape torture and possible execution): "imminent" is more appropriate than "immediate", and the reaction need not be spontaneous.
   - *Hasan* (restrictive): if the threatened harm is not reasonably expected to follow "immediately or almost immediately" on non-compliance, there may be little doubt that D could have taken evasive action, for example by going to the police.
3. **The reasonable person.** This is a sober person of reasonable firmness sharing D's relevant characteristics (*Graham*).
   - *Bowen* [1996] 4 All ER 837: age, sex, pregnancy, serious physical disability, and recognised mental illness or psychiatric condition (e.g. PTSD; *Emery* (1992)) may be relevant.
   - Not relevant: being more timid than normal, low IQ short of mental impairment, or characteristics resulting from self-induced abuse such as alcohol or drugs.
   - In most cases only age and sex will matter.

### 5. Exclusions
1. **Offences**: there is no defence to murder (*Howe* [1987] AC 417), attempted murder (*Gotts* [1992] 2 AC 412), or some forms of treason. Duress *is* available for s18 GBH with intent, for conspiracy to murder (*Ness*), and presumably for assisting or encouraging murder.
2. **Prior fault (voluntary association).**
   - *Sharp* [1987] 1 QB 853; *Fitzpatrick* [1977] NI 20: D who voluntarily, and knowing its nature, joined a criminal gang that D knew might pressure him to offend cannot rely on duress. See also *Shepherd* (1987).
   - *Hasan*: the defence is excluded if, through voluntary association with people engaged in criminal activity, D foresaw *or ought reasonably to have foreseen* the risk of being subjected to *any* compulsion by threats of violence. D need not foresee coercion to commit the type of crime charged (Lord Bingham said *Baker* mis-stated the law by requiring that).
   - *Ali* [2008] EWCA Crim 716 goes further: the defence is lost whenever D ought to have foreseen the risk of threats.

### 6. Duress of circumstances
- The threat comes from circumstances rather than a person's demand. The requirements are the same as for duress by threats (*Martin* [1989] 1 All ER 652): a risk of death or serious injury, not originating from D.
- It developed in driving cases: *Willer* (1986) 83 Cr App R 225; *Conway* [1988] 88 Cr App R 159.
- It is now a general defence (*Pommell* [1995] 2 Cr App R 607, possession of a firearm). The discussion notes *Pommell* is better seen as necessity ("doing the right thing", with no personal sacrifice), which illustrates the courts' confusion between the defences.
- **Duty to desist**: D must stop the offending as soon as reasonably possible once the threat ceases. In *DPP v Jones* [1990] RTR 33, D may have had duress when he began driving over the limit, but it did not justify driving the whole two miles home (as discussed by Reed).

### 7. Necessity
- Necessity is a "lesser of two evils" justification and remains limited. It has developed case by case, largely in medical contexts.
- **Judicial reluctance**: *Southwark LBC v Williams* [1971] 2 All ER 175. Homeless people occupying empty houses had no defence to trespass. The court warned that necessity "would open a door which no man could shut".
- **Implied recognition**:
  - *Bourne* [1938] 3 All ER 615: an abortion on a suicidal 14-year-old rape victim to save her life;
  - *Gillick* [1985] 1 All ER 553: contraceptive advice to an under-age girl was not aiding and abetting.
- **Direct recognition**:
  - *Re F (Mental Patient: Sterilisation)* [1989/1990]: sterilisation of a woman lacking capacity, in her best interests;
  - Lord Goff also identified action to assist another without consent, such as dragging V from an oncoming vehicle (see consent-defence-nfoap.md);
  - police directing people to break traffic regulations to protect life or property (*Johnson v Phillips* [1975] 3 All ER 682).
- **The test** (Brooke LJ in *Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation)* [2001] Fam 147):
  1. the act is needed to avoid inevitable and irreparable evil;
  2. no more is done than is reasonably necessary for the purpose; and
  3. the evil inflicted is not disproportionate to the evil avoided.
- **Murder**:
  - *Dudley and Stephens* (1884) 14 QBD 273: shipwrecked sailors who killed and ate the cabin boy had no defence. The court asked who is to judge this sort of necessity, and by what measure the comparative value of lives is to be assessed. The weakest was chosen. Confirmed by *Howe*.
  - *Re A*: separating conjoined twins (Jodie and Mary) would inevitably kill Mary, who depended on Jodie's heart, but without it both would die. Necessity was available on these unique facts. Brooke LJ said this was not a weighing of the relative worth of two lives, and not a step down a slippery slope.
  - *R (Nicklinson) v Ministry of Justice* [2013] EWCA Civ 961: the time had not come to recognise necessity as a defence to murder in euthanasia cases.
  - Bohlander (2006) 70 JCL 147 argues the maxim that necessity is no defence to murder "can no longer be regarded as sacrosanct", raising the example of shooting down hijacked aircraft.

## Key authorities
- *Graham* [1982]; *Howe* [1987]; *Hasan* [2005] UKHL 22: the two-stage test. Restrictive on imminence and association.
- *Hudson and Taylor* [1971] 2 QB 202; *Hussain* [1999]: imminence and evasive action.
- *Lynch* [1975]; *Quayle* [2006]; *Baker and Wilkins* [1997]; *Valderrama-Vega* [1985]; *R v A* [2012]: the type of threat.
- *Safi* [2003]: a reasonable belief in a threat is enough.
- *Rodger and Rose* [1998]: the threat must be external.
- *Wright* [2000]; *Shayler* [2001]: who may be threatened.
- *Cole* [1994]; *Ali* [1995]: the specificity of the demand.
- *Bowen* [1996]: relevant characteristics.
- *Howe* [1987]; *Gotts* [1992]: no duress for murder or attempted murder.
- *Sharp* [1987]; *Fitzpatrick* [1977]; *Ali* [2008]: voluntary association.
- *Willer* (1986); *Conway* (1988); *Martin* (1989); *Pommell* (1995): duress of circumstances.
- *Southwark v Williams* [1971]; *Bourne* [1938]; *Re F*; *Re A* [2001]; *Dudley and Stephens* (1884); *Nicklinson* [2013]: necessity.

## Debate and critique
- **Duress and murder: the main essay issue.**
  - *Reed* ((1997) 61 J Crim L): Alan Reed calls for "urgent reform". He argues the jury, as the audience to the "theatre of morality", is well placed to judge what society can expect of a coerced actor. He favours the US Model Penal Code approach, which leaves the excusing decision, even for murder, to juries in context.
  - *Walters* ((1988) 8 LS 61): Lynden Walters supports duress as a defence to murder. The law should recognise human frailty and not demand heroism. Duress excuses rather than justifies, so it does not undermine the sanctity of life. Existing limits (imminence, seriousness, no evasive option) and jury caution guard against abuse.
  - *Arenson* (J Crim L, "The Paradox of Disallowing Duress as a Defence to Murder"): Kenneth Arenson points to the "indefensible" anomaly that provocation, but not duress, partially excuses murder. He argues Lord Hailsham's reasons in *Howe* are "seriously flawed" (illustrated by a kidnapped mother and child), and surveys reform options. He notes that some Australian jurisdictions (Victoria, Western Australia, the ACT, the Commonwealth) allow duress as a complete defence to murder.
  - *Tadros* ((2006) MLR): Victor Tadros questions why duress alone is a complete homicide defence where it is allowed, suggesting duress may sometimes be a justification for intending lesser harm.
  - The Law Commission (as noted by Reed) has engaged with the concerns expressed in *Howe*.
- **The steadfastness requirement (K.J.M. Smith, "Duress and Steadfastness: In Pursuit of the Unintelligible").** Smith argues that the "overborne will" idea is incoherent alongside a requirement of reasonable firmness, since a person whose will is overborne cannot also be required to resist reasonably. Given the fixed threshold of a threat of death or serious injury, the steadfastness requirement is superfluous and confusing (citing *Graham*).
- **How many defences? (Chan and Simester, (2005) 16 KCLJ 121).** Chan and Simester describe duress by threats and duress of circumstances as two versions of one generic duress defence. They examine Clarkson's proposal ([2004] Crim LR 81) to merge duress, self-defence and necessity into a single defence of "necessary action", asking whether D's response was reasonable and proportionate to the danger. They argue that duress overlaps with necessity only where giving in is the lesser evil, which is not always so. The defences rest on different rationales and cannot simply be collapsed.
- **Necessity as moral involuntariness (Canada v England).** The comparative article notes that the Supreme Court of Canada treats necessity as an excuse based on "moral involuntariness", whereas England folded excusatory necessity into duress of circumstances. Because England's objective test assumes a certain level of courage and largely excludes D's characteristics, the author questions whether England has taken "the wrong path" for a defence centred on fear. The opening quotation: a person "should not be liable to life imprisonment for failing to be a hero".
- **The *Hasan* restrictions.** Requiring a threat to follow "immediately or almost immediately", and excluding anyone who "ought reasonably to have foreseen" any compulsion, makes duress very narrow. This is criticised for objectifying prior fault (*Ali* [2008]) and for ignoring victims of coercive relationships who cannot realistically go to the police.
- **Necessity's open door.** *Southwark v Williams* and *Dudley and Stephens* express rule-of-law fears. *Re A* and the medical cases show case-by-case expansion. Bohlander, Ost (euthanasia) and Wilson ("the structure of criminal defences") are listed as further reading.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Is the charge murder or attempted murder?** If so, duress is unavailable (*Howe*; *Gotts*). Note the murder exclusion and consider loss of control or diminished responsibility instead. For s18, duress is available.
2. **Threat.** Was there a threat, or a reasonably believed threat (*Safi*), of death or serious injury (not property, pain, psychological harm or exposure) to D or someone D is responsible for? Did it come from an external source?
3. **Demand.** Did the threatener nominate the type of crime (*Cole*; *Ali*)? If D chose the crime himself (e.g. "get me the money"), the defence is weak.
4. **Imminence and escape.** Could D have gone to the police or otherwise escaped (*Hudson*; *Hasan*)? Time gaps, or opportunities to escape (D left alone, phone available), defeat the defence.
5. **The *Graham* objective limb.** Would a sober person of reasonable firmness, with D's relevant characteristics (age, sex, pregnancy, disability, recognised psychiatric condition, but *not* timidity, low IQ or intoxication), have done the same?
6. **Prior fault.** Did D voluntarily associate with criminals or violent people such that D ought to have foreseen any compulsion (*Hasan*; *Ali* [2008])? Drug debts and gang membership are typical traps.
7. **Duress of circumstances.** For driving or escape scenarios, use the same test. Did D stop offending as soon as the danger passed?
8. **Necessity.** In medical or best-interests scenarios, or "lesser evil" cases with no threat of death or serious injury, apply the *Re A* three-stage test. Remember the reluctance shown in *Southwark* and the murder bar except in *Re A*-type uniqueness.
9. **Burden.** Once D raises evidence, the prosecution must disprove duress beyond reasonable doubt.
