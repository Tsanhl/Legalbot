# Gross Negligence Manslaughter

## Overview
Gross negligence manslaughter (GNM) is the second form of involuntary manslaughter. It applies where D, owing V a duty of care, breaches it in a way that creates a serious and obvious risk of death, causes death, and the breach is so bad that it deserves criminal rather than civil sanction. It can be committed by act or omission, and it has no subjective mens rea. It is the main vehicle for prosecuting medical professionals, employers and those who abandon people they have endangered (e.g. drug suppliers in *Evans*). Its circular "gross" test, its limits (*Rudling*, *Rose*) and the calls for a capacity-based reckless manslaughter are the essay issues.

## The law

### 1. Definition and elements
GNM: D's negligent conduct, by act or omission (e.g. a medical professional doing or failing to do something), causes V's death, without subjective mens rea. the materials lists six elements, drawing on *Adomako* [1995] 1 AC 171 and *Rose* [2018] QB 328:
1. a **duty of care** owed by D to V;
2. a **breach** of that duty;
3. the breach created a **serious and obvious risk of death**;
4. the risk of death was **reasonably foreseeable** at the time of the breach;
5. the breach **caused** death;
6. the negligence was **gross**, i.e. criminal.

### 2. Duty of care
- The duty must exist when D engages in the conduct that causes death. It is interpreted broadly and not limited to the civil standard. Examples:
  - doctor and patient (*Adomako*);
  - transport driver and passengers (*Barker* [2003] 2 Cr App R 110);
  - employer and employees (*Dean* [2002] EWCA Crim 2410);
  - smugglers of illegal immigrants (*Wacker* [2003] QB 1203: a lorry driver failed to ventilate his truck and 58 people died, as described in the sentencing article);
  - creating a foreseeable risk of death (*Evans* [2009] EWCA Crim 650).
- **Drug supply: *Evans*.** D supplied heroin to her half-sister, who injected herself. D saw the overdose symptoms but did not call for help, fearing liability. The duty arose from D's creation of, or contribution to, the dangerous situation (*Miller* principle). Compare *Kennedy*, where self-injection breaks the chain for UAM. In *Evans* the liability rests on the later omission. Earlier, more cautious cases are *Khan* [1998] and *Sinclair* [1998] (see actus-reus-and-omissions.md).
- In *Evans* the court accepted that whether a duty exists is a judicial question, but treated leaving it to the jury as not fatal.

### 3. Breach
- **Act**: would a reasonable person have refrained from D's acts, or acted differently?
- **Omission**: how would a reasonable person in D's position have behaved?
- Ordinary negligence principles apply. A doctor's error of diagnosis is not necessarily negligent: it depends on whether D acted as a reasonable doctor would (Laird, citing Jones).

### 4. Serious and obvious risk of death
- The risk must be of **death**, not just serious injury (*Misra* [2004] EWCA Crim 2375 at [52], following *Adomako*). The court noted the DPP's policy of requiring an obvious risk of death. It recorded the debate between Blackstone's editors, who favoured extending the risk to GBH for symmetry with murder, and Professor Smith, who favoured limiting it to death.
- The risk must be serious and obvious, to the class of persons to whom D owed the duty.

### 5. Reasonably foreseeable risk of death
- The risk must exist in fact and be one a reasonable person would have foreseen. The test is objective: D need not have foreseen it (*Singh* [1999] Crim LR 582; *Misra*).
- It is judged **prospectively, at the moment of breach**, not with hindsight and not "but for the breach" (*Rose*).
  - *Rudling* [2016] EWCA Crim 741: a GP failed to make a home visit after a phone call about a child, who died the next day of Addison's disease, which is very rare in children. A "recognisable risk of something serious" is not a risk of death. An obvious risk is "a present risk which is clear and unambiguous, not one which might become apparent on further investigation". The no-case ruling was upheld.
  - *Rose* [2017] EWCA Crim 1168, [2018] QB 328: an optometrist failed to examine the back of a boy's eyes during a routine sight test, as statute required. Had she done so she would have seen signs of treatable hydrocephalus. He died five months later. The conviction was quashed. What D *would* have known had she not breached her duty is not taken into account.

### 6. Causation
- Factual and legal causation are required, for acts and omissions alike.
- For omissions: what would a reasonable person have done, and would it have prevented V dying as V did (*Evans*)? See also *Broughton* [2020] EWCA Crim 1093 (the omission must be shown to have caused the result).

### 7. "Grossly" negligent
- The negligence must be bad enough to warrant criminal rather than civil liability. *Adomako* (Lord Mackay): the jury considers whether, having regard to the risk of death, D's conduct was so bad in all the circumstances that it amounted to a criminal act or omission.
- "Truly exceptionally bad" (*Sellu* [2016] EWCA Crim 1716, [2017] 4 WLR 64; *Bawa-Garba*, upheld with this direction).
- It is a question of fact for the jury, relying on expert evidence.
- In *Sellu* the conviction was quashed because the jury lacked a sufficiently detailed direction on what "gross" means (Laird).

### 8. Mens rea
There is no mens rea as such. Negligence functions as the fault element. Knowledge may be relevant, but foresight of risk, intention and recklessness are not required.

### 9. Certainty challenge: *Misra*
In *Misra* the defendants were two senior house officers who failed to diagnose and treat a post-operative patient's toxic shock syndrome. They argued that GNM's circular definition (negligence is criminal if the jury thinks it criminal) breached ECHR Article 7 ("no punishment without law") and Article 6. The Court of Appeal rejected this. The ingredients defined in *Adomako* involve no uncertainty. References to "criminal" help the jury distinguish simple negligence from crime. Juries routinely decide similarly evaluative questions (dishonesty, reasonable force, dangerous driving).

## Key authorities
- *Andrews v DPP* [1937] AC 576: simple lack of care is not enough. A very high degree of negligence is required.
- *Adomako* [1995] 1 AC 171: the modern test (duty, breach, causation, gross negligence, risk of death).
- *Misra* [2004] EWCA Crim 2375: the risk must be of death. The offence is compatible with Article 7.
- *Wacker* [2003] QB 1203; *Barker* [2003]; *Dean* [2002]: duty situations.
- *Evans* [2009] EWCA Crim 650: a duty arising from creating danger in drug supply. Liability for failing to summon help.
- *Singh* [1999]: the objective foreseeability of risk.
- *Sellu* [2017] 4 WLR 64; *Bawa-Garba*: "truly exceptionally bad".
- *Rudling* [2016] EWCA Crim 741; *Rose* [2018] QB 328: the risk of death must be serious, obvious and present at the time of breach.
- *Stone and Dobinson* [1977]: a duty arising from assumption of responsibility (omissions).

## Debate and critique
- **Laird, "The Evolution of Gross Negligence Manslaughter" (Arch Rev 2018).** Karl Laird argues that *Rudling* and *Rose* were driven by concern for healthcare professionals, producing a counterintuitive rule that "rewards egregious degrees of negligence". His train conductor example: a conductor who checks the platform carelessly may be liable, but one who does not check at all may escape, because the risk never became "obvious". This creates perverse incentives, such as a landlord who avoids installing a carbon monoxide detector so as to stay ignorant. The court's fears for doctors are overstated, because misdiagnosis is not automatically negligent. GNM applies to everyone, so it should not be shaped by its effect on one profession. He suggests either a specific offence for medical professionals (by analogy with s20 Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015, which applies only to care workers), or raising the threshold to subjective recklessness. In any event, an authoritative Supreme Court ruling is needed. The Court of Appeal declined to certify a point in *Rose*.
- **Crosby, "Gross Negligence Manslaughter Revisited: Time for a Change of Direction?" (2020) 84 J Crim L 228.** Cath Crosby argues that the House of Lords "took a wrong turn" in *Adomako* by failing to revise the *Caldwell*/*Lawrence* guidance. GNM should be replaced by **reckless manslaughter using an objective, capacity-based test**, covering acts and omissions. It would be underpinned by a hybrid theory of culpability, drawing on character theory, the capacity element of choice theory, and a subjectivised "role" theory. Such a test would target conscious departures from good practice rather than unfortunate medical mishaps, and would ask why D failed to see the risk. Where the failure shows a reprehensible character flaw, liability should follow. She traces Lord Taylor's non-exhaustive list in the Court of Appeal in *Adomako*: indifference to an obvious risk; foresight of a risk and deciding to run it; and negligent attempts to avoid a risk showing serious incompetence.
- **Fair labelling for negligent omitters (Baker).** Dennis Baker argues that defendants such as Stone, Dobinson, Evans and Hood are "stupid rather than dangerous". A homicide label serves no retributive goal, and a lesser negligence offence would be fairer (see actus-reus-and-omissions.md).
- **Duty concept and civil analogies.** The Evans commentary (Glenys Williams, O'Doherty, Ormerod and Fortson, Virgo) criticises the vagueness of criminal duties and the borrowing of civil "assumption of responsibility" ideas.
- **Circularity.** *Misra* rejected the Article 7 challenge, but academics continue to criticise the jury's open-ended role in deciding "gross".
- **A gap for reckless manslaughter?** Stark and Taylor argue that UAM and GNM between them cover most cases, using the "school dinner" example (see unlawful-act-manslaughter.md). Crosby, by contrast, would replace GNM.
- **Reform.** LC 304 kept "killing through gross negligence as to a risk of causing death" as manslaughter. The sentencing guideline range for GNM is 1-18 years.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **When to use GNM.** No murder mens rea, and either no unlawful act (lawful activity done badly: surgery, driving a lorry, running an event) or an omission. Or UAM fails because of *Kennedy* (self-injection) and D then failed to get help.
2. **Duty.** Find the relationship: doctor/patient, employer, carer, a contract, or creation of danger (*Evans*/*Miller*). A sibling alone is not enough. A drug "intermediary" may owe a duty.
3. **Breach.** Measure against the reasonable professional or person in D's role. Consider whether the conduct is a mere error of judgment.
4. **Risk of death.** At the moment of breach, was there a serious and obvious risk of *death* (not just of serious illness) that a reasonable person would have foreseen? Do not use hindsight or what an examination would have revealed (*Rudling*, *Rose*). Watch for exam facts with a rare undiagnosed condition.
5. **Causation.** Would proper conduct (e.g. calling an ambulance) have prevented the death? If V would have died anyway, causation fails.
6. **Gross.** Was the negligence "truly exceptionally bad", deserving criminal punishment? Use the facts: duration, warnings ignored, motives (*Evans*: avoiding trouble), and expert evidence.
7. **Conclude**, and mention the alternative of UAM if there was also an unlawful act.
