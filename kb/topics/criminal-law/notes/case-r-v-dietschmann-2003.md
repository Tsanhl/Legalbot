# R v Dietschmann [2003] UKHL 10 (case note)

> The judgment was read only through an online transcript and secondary sources; it is not held in the knowledge
> base. The case concerns the pre-2010 wording of s 2 Homicide Act 1957 ("abnormality of mind"), which the Coroners
> and Justice Act 2009 later reformed. Reported at [2003] 1 AC 1209. Check pinpoints against the report.

## Overview
House of Lords, 27 February 2003. Panel: Lord Nicholls of Birkenhead, Lord Lloyd of Berwick, Lord Hutton, Lord
Hobhouse of Woodborough and Lord Rodger of Earlsferry. Lord Hutton gave the leading speech, with which the others
agreed. The case deals with diminished responsibility where the defendant has a mental abnormality and was also drunk
when he killed.

## Facts
Dietschmann had been in a relationship with his aunt, who was much older than him; she died about six weeks before
the killing. Both psychiatrists agreed that he suffered from an adjustment disorder (a depressed grief reaction to her
death). After heavy drinking he killed the victim in a violent attack, following a dispute about a watch the aunt had
given him. At trial (before Maurice Kay J) he admitted the killing and relied on diminished responsibility. The judge
directed the jury, in effect, to ask whether he would have killed as he did if he had not been drinking, and whether
he would then have been under diminished responsibility. He was convicted of murder, and the Court of Appeal
dismissed his appeal, following *R v Atkinson* and *R v Egan*.

## Held
- The appeal was **allowed**. The direction was wrong: a defendant who had been drinking does not have to show that
  he would have killed, and been under diminished responsibility, without the drink. *Atkinson* and *Egan* were held
  to be wrongly decided.
- Where the defendant has an abnormality of mind and has also been drinking, the jury should disregard the effect of
  the drink on the abnormality itself, and then ask whether, despite the drink, the abnormality substantially impaired
  his mental responsibility for the killing.
- The abnormality need not be the sole cause of the killing. That drink also contributed does not defeat the defence if
  the abnormality still substantially impaired responsibility. The effects of the alcohol itself cannot be treated as
  the abnormality of mind.
- **Disposal:** the House remitted the case to the Court of Appeal to decide whether to quash the murder conviction
  and order a retrial, or to substitute a verdict of manslaughter and pass sentence. What happened on remission was
  not checked.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify a recognised abnormality or condition (for example depression or an adjustment disorder) separate from
   the drink.
2. Note that D was also intoxicated, and treat the drink as irrelevant to the abnormality itself.
3. Ask whether the abnormality, despite the drink, substantially impaired D's mental responsibility for the killing; it
   need not be the only cause of the killing.
4. Flag that the defence is now in the amended s 2 (Coroners and Justice Act 2009), so say how the post-2009 wording
   (recognised medical condition) applies and that *Dietschmann* was decided under the older wording.
