# Palmer v R [1971] AC 814 (case note)

> Case note written from consistent secondary accounts; the judgment itself is not in the knowledge base.
> Check pinpoints against the report (the self-defence passage is usually cited at 832) before citing them.

## Overview
Privy Council, on appeal from the Court of Appeal of Jamaica (judgment 1970, reported 1971). The case is cited for
(i) the rule that self-defence is **all or nothing**: excessive force in self-defence does not reduce murder to
manslaughter, and (ii) the classic jury direction that a person under attack need not judge the exact measure of
defensive force.

## Facts
Palmer and Irving were convicted of murder in the Supreme Court of Jamaica and sentenced to death. Both relied on
self-defence. The trial judge left the jury only two verdicts: murder or not guilty. The Jamaican Court of Appeal
refused leave to appeal. The question on appeal to the Privy Council was whether the jury had to be directed that, if
they thought the accused intended self-defence but used excessive force, they could return manslaughter.

## Held
- The appeals were **dismissed**. There is no rule requiring a manslaughter direction in every case where self-defence
  is raised; self-defence either succeeds, and the defendant is acquitted, or fails.
- The defence is straightforward and juries readily understand it. Where an attacked person honestly and instinctively
  does what he thinks necessary, that is strong evidence that the action was reasonable. A person in "a moment of
  unexpected anguish" cannot be expected to "weigh to a nicety the exact measure of his necessary defensive action".
- The prosecution must disprove self-defence beyond reasonable doubt.

## Why it matters
- Authority behind the all-or-nothing rule applied in *R v Clegg* [1995] 1 AC 482.
- The heat-of-the-moment direction is now reflected in the statutory statement of self-defence (CJIA 2008 s 76).
- Reasonableness of the force is a jury question on all the circumstances.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was force necessary on the facts as the defendant saw them (see *Beckford*)?
2. Was the force proportionate? Allow for the pressure of the moment; do not demand exact calculation.
3. If force was excessive, self-defence fails entirely; a reduction to manslaughter needs another route (e.g. loss
   of control, diminished responsibility).
