# Kay v Butterworth (1945) 61 TLR 452 (case note)

> Short note built from secondary accounts; the report was not read. The citation (1945) 61 TLR 452 and the judge
> (Humphreys J) are confirmed; other report citations sometimes given for the case were not confirmed and are omitted.
> The accounts give few facts beyond those below.

## Overview
King's Bench Division (Divisional Court), 1945, on a prosecutor's appeal from justices; Humphreys J gave the judgment
that is quoted in later cases. Lord Goddard CJ relied on it in *Hill v Baxter* [1958] 1 QB 277. It is the standard
example of the rule that **a driver who falls asleep at the wheel cannot rely on automatism**, because he is at fault
in carrying on driving when drowsiness comes on.

## Facts
The defendant fell asleep while driving after a heavy day's work. (The accounts consulted do not describe the
accident itself.) He was charged with dangerous driving and with driving without due care and attention. The justices acquitted him, and the
prosecutor appealed.

## Held
- Falling asleep was no answer to the charges. A driver who feels drowsiness coming on must stop and rest until he is
  fully awake; he is taken to know that drowsiness is overtaking him, so the fault lies in continuing to drive.
- In the course of his judgment Humphreys J contrasted a driver who, through no fault of his own, becomes unconscious
  or loses control, for example through a sudden illness or an attack by a swarm of bees; such a driver should not be
  criminally liable. *Hill v Baxter* took up the bees example.

## Why it matters
- Origin of the "driver who falls asleep" illustration used in the automatism rules: the defence fails where the
  defendant is at fault in bringing the state about or in failing to take precautions.
- Contrasts with sudden, unforeseeable loss of control (illness, bees, a blow).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was there a total loss of voluntary control, and was it sudden or foreseeable?
2. Did the defendant have warning (drowsiness, an earlier episode) and continue to drive?
3. If so, the defence is likely to fail for prior fault, as in *Kay v Butterworth*.
