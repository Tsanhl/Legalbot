# Theft

## Overview
Theft is the core property offence in the syllabus. Under the Theft Act 1968, s1(1), a person is guilty of theft "if he dishonestly appropriates property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it". There are three actus reus elements (appropriation, property, belonging to another) and two mens rea elements (dishonesty, intention to permanently deprive), each defined or shaped by ss2-6. The controversies centre on the very wide meaning of "appropriation" after *Gomez* and *Hinks* (consent is irrelevant, and even a valid gift can be appropriated), the replacement of *Ghosh* by *Ivey* for dishonesty, and the artificiality of s6. The revision lecture treats theft as a likely problem-question topic. the materials's conclusion is that there are "5 steps to make out a Theft Offence", a "broad spectrum of offences", and "LOTS of subjectivity".

## The law

### 1. The offence: Theft Act 1968, s1
- s1(1): the definition above.
- s1(2): it is immaterial whether the appropriation is made with a view to gain or for the thief's own benefit.
- s1(3): ss2-6 govern interpretation.
- **Five elements**: (1) appropriation (s3); (2) property (s4); (3) belonging to another (s5); (4) dishonesty (s2 and *Ivey*); (5) intention permanently to deprive (s6).

### 2. Appropriation (s3)
**Statute.** s3(1): any assumption by a person of the rights of an owner amounts to an appropriation. This includes, where D came by the property (innocently or not) without stealing it, any later assumption of a right to it by keeping or dealing with it as owner. s3(2) protects a good-faith purchaser for value from liability for later dealings caused by a defect in the seller's title.

**Key rules.**
1. In most cases "appropriating" can be treated as "taking", e.g. surreptitiously removing V's wallet.
2. **Any one of the owner's rights is enough**: *R v Morris, Anderton and Burnside* [1984] AC 320 (Lord Roskill). Switching price labels in a shop interfered with the shop's right to set prices.
3. **Timing**: an appropriation occurs when D assumes any of the owner's rights (*Gomez* [1993] AC 442). *Atakpu* [1994] QB 69: theft can happen "in an instant", but can also involve a course of dealing with several appropriations before the transaction is complete.
4. **Consent is irrelevant**:
   - *Lawrence v MPC* [1972] AC 626/636: a taxi driver took more than the proper fare from a foreign student's proffered wallet. The Act is not to be read as if it said "without the consent of the owner". Viscount Dilhorne: the owner's consent goes to dishonesty, not appropriation;
   - *Morris*: Lord Roskill had suggested appropriation involves "adverse interference with or usurpation" of the owner's rights, not an act authorised by the owner;
   - *Gomez* (HL): the House preferred *Lawrence*. An act authorised by the owner (here, consent obtained by deception) can be an appropriation. Lord Keith said there was no question of departing from *Lawrence*.
5. **Valid gifts**: *R v Hinks* [2000] UKHL 53, [2001] 2 AC 241. Acceptance of a gift that was valid in civil law (from a vulnerable donor) can be an appropriation. Whether it is theft then turns on dishonesty.
6. **Later appropriation**: if D first gets the property lawfully (e.g. borrows a friend's laptop with permission) and later decides to keep it, the keeping is a later assumption of the owner's rights (s3(1)). This is the revision lecture's John and Sarah example.

### 3. Property (s4)
- s4(1): property "includes money and all other property, real or personal, including things in action and other intangible property".
- **Limits**:
  - Land (s4(2)) cannot generally be stolen, except by trustees and similar persons dealing in breach of confidence, by someone not in possession who severs things forming part of the land, or by tenants taking fixtures.
  - Wild plants (s4(3)): picking wild mushrooms, flowers, fruit or foliage is not theft unless done for reward, sale or another commercial purpose.
  - Wild creatures (s4(4)) are property, but cannot be stolen unless tamed, ordinarily kept in captivity, or reduced (or being reduced) into another's possession.
- **Not property**:
  - Electricity (*Low v Blease* (1975) 119 SJ 695). There is a separate offence of abstracting electricity under s13.
  - Confidential information (*Oxford v Moss* (1979) 68 Cr App R 183).
  - Corpses. But a corpse or body parts (hair, blood, semen etc.) can be property if reduced into another's possession or control, or if special skill has been applied to them (*R v Kelly* [1999] QB 621).

### 4. Belonging to another (s5)
- s5(1): property belongs to anyone having **possession or control** of it, or any **proprietary right or interest** in it (other than an equitable interest arising only from an agreement to transfer or grant an interest).
  - **Possession or control**: *R v Turner (No 2)* [1971] 2 All ER 441 / 55 Cr App R 336. As the materials uses it, this means even an owner can steal from someone who has possession or control of the owner's property.
  - **Proprietary right or interest**: *R v Marshall* [1998] 2 Cr App R 282. This is determined by civil law principles of ownership, possession and equitable rights.
  - **Abandoned property**: *R (Ricketts) v Basildon Magistrates' Court* [2010] EWHC 2358.
- s5(2) trusts: beneficiaries with a right to enforce the trust count as owners.
- s5(3): where D receives property from or on account of another and is under an obligation to retain and deal with it or its proceeds in a particular way, it is regarded (as against D) as belonging to the other.
- s5(4): where D gets property by another's mistake and is under an obligation to restore it (or its proceeds or value), it is treated as belonging to the person entitled to restoration, to the extent of that obligation. An intention not to restore counts as an intention to deprive.

### 5. Dishonesty (s2 and *Ivey*)
**s2(1): D is not dishonest if D appropriates in the belief that:**
- (a) D has in law the right to deprive the other of it, on D's own behalf or a third person's;
- (b) D would have the other's consent if the other knew of the appropriation and the circumstances; or
- (c) the owner cannot be discovered by taking reasonable steps (except where the property came to D as trustee or personal representative).

These are honest beliefs and need not be reasonable. Under **s2(2)**, D may be dishonest even though willing to pay for the property.

**Outside s2, the common law test applies.**
- *Ghosh* [1982] QB 1053 (the old two-stage test): (1) was the conduct dishonest by the ordinary standards of reasonable and honest people? (2) If so, did D realise that it was dishonest by those standards?
- *Ivey v Genting Casinos (UK) Ltd* [2017] UKSC 67 (the current test):
  1. What was D's actual state of knowledge or belief as to the facts? This is subjective. The reasonableness of a belief is evidence of whether it was genuinely held, but is not an extra requirement.
  2. Given that state of mind, was D's conduct dishonest by the **objective standards of ordinary decent people**? There is no requirement that D appreciated it was dishonest by those standards.
- *DPP v Patterson* [2018] 1 Cr App R 28: it is "difficult to imagine" the Court of Appeal preferring *Ghosh* to *Ivey* in future.
- *R v Barton and Booth* [2020] EWCA Crim 575: confirms that *Ivey* is the test in criminal law.

### 6. Intention to permanently deprive (s6)
- The basic case is that D means the owner to lose the thing permanently.
- **s6(1)**: even without meaning the other permanently to lose the thing itself, D is regarded as intending permanent deprivation if D intends "to treat the thing as his own to dispose of regardless of the other's rights". A borrowing or lending may amount to this, "but only if" it is for a period and in circumstances making it equivalent to an outright taking or disposal.
- **s6(2)**: where D, having possession or control (lawfully or not), parts with the property under a condition as to its return which D may not be able to perform (e.g. pawning), for D's own purposes and without the owner's authority, this amounts to treating it as D's own to dispose of regardless of the owner's rights.
- **Cases** (revision lecture):
  - *R v Velumyl* (1989): taking money intending to return *different* notes and coins is an intention to permanently deprive.
  - *R v Lloyd* (1985): borrowing is equivalent to outright taking only if the property is returned in such a changed state that all its "goodness, virtue and practical value" has gone.

## Key authorities
- Theft Act 1968, ss1-6 (official text).
- *Lawrence v MPC* [1972]: consent is irrelevant to appropriation.
- *Morris* [1984] AC 320: assuming any one of the owner's rights is enough (label-switching).
- *Gomez* [1993] AC 442: an authorised act obtained by deception is still an appropriation. *Lawrence* preferred to *Morris*'s "adverse interference" dictum.
- *Atakpu* [1994] QB 69: appropriation can be instantaneous or continuing.
- *Hinks* [2001] 2 AC 241: accepting a valid gift can be an appropriation.
- *Oxford v Moss* (1979); *Low v Blease* (1975); *Kelly* [1999]: the limits of "property".
- *Turner (No 2)* [1971]; *Marshall* [1998]; *Ricketts* [2010]: belonging to another.
- *Ghosh* [1982]; *Ivey* [2017] UKSC 67; *Patterson* [2018]; *Barton and Booth* [2020]: dishonesty.
- *Lloyd* (1985); *Velumyl* (1989): s6.

## Debate and critique
- **Theft and fair labelling (Clarkson, (1993) MLR).** Christopher Clarkson criticises *Gomez* for giving little reasoning and for collapsing the distinction between theft and obtaining by deception, contrary to the Criminal Law Revision Committee's scheme. Paradigm theft is a surreptitious or forcible taking without consent, where the owner is powerless and violence is a risk. Deception involves the victim's (manipulated) participation. He quotes Shute and Horder: the thief attacks a social practice "from the outside", while the deceiver is "the traitor within". Merging the two offends fair labelling.
- **Defending *Hinks* (Shute, [2002] Crim LR 445).** Stephen Shute reviews the arguments against *Hinks*: the framers' intentions, over-reliance on dishonesty, the risk of inappropriate prosecutions, breach of the harm principle, conflict between civil and criminal law, and Beatson and Simester's "no benefit" objection. He concludes that none is decisive. Once *Gomez* was the starting point, the House had no real choice. The harm principle can sometimes allow criminalising harmless wrongs. Simon Gardner and Lord Steyn rejected the civil-criminal conflict argument. Despite the "torrent of academic criticism", the majority position can be justified.
- **The concept of appropriation (Melissaris, (2007) MLR).** Emmanuel Melissaris argues that replacing the Larceny Act's "taking and carrying away" with "appropriation" produced confusion. *Gomez* and *Hinks* blur theft and deception, and criminalising civilly valid transactions disconnects criminal from civil law. He proposes understanding appropriation through "proprietary subjectivity", i.e. D's mental assumption of ownership. He discusses cases such as *Pitham and Hehl* (1977), where furniture was "sold" without being touched.
- **Implications for civil and criminal law (Clements).** L.M. Clements traces the move from larceny to appropriation and the ambiguities this created around consent and ownership (*Lawrence*, *Morris*, *Gomez*, *Hinks*).
- **Consent after the House of Lords (Mitchell C. Davies, (1993) 13 Legal Studies 308).** This article critiques the effect of *Gomez* on the law of theft, as its title says ("taking and leading astray").
- **Reform: "taking without consent" (Tamblyn).** Nathan Tamblyn argues that *Gomez* and *Hinks* make appropriation over-broad and distort property law. He proposes redefining theft as intentionally or recklessly taking or keeping another's property, knowingly or recklessly without that other's consent. This would drop "appropriation", "dishonesty" and the intention to permanently deprive, align theft with criminal damage (recklessness), and provide defences for belief in a right or in consent.
- **Section 6 (Alex Steel, (2008) 17 Nottingham LJ 3).** Steel argues that s6's language ("dispose of", "borrowing or lending") is unclear and open to narrow or wide readings. *Lloyd*'s "goodness or virtue" test is artificial because it ignores serious disruption of the owner's rights where the item comes back physically unchanged (e.g. borrowing a student's laptop just before an exam). He proposes assessing borrowing by the degree of harm to the owner's rights rather than the property's physical condition.
- **Dishonesty after *Ivey*.** Removing *Ghosh*'s second (subjective) limb makes the test more objective. Critics note this increases jury-dependent variability ("LOTS of subjectivity") and the risk of convicting people who did not realise ordinary people would regard their conduct as dishonest.

## Applying it to a problem question
Work through the five elements in order:
1. **Appropriation.** Identify the exact act: taking, using, selling, switching labels, keeping after lawful receipt. Consent does not prevent appropriation (*Gomez*). A gift may be appropriated (*Hinks*). Pinpoint *when* it happened (*Atakpu*).
2. **Property.** Check the exclusions: information (*Oxford v Moss*), electricity (s13), wild plants and creatures, land, corpses (*Kelly*). Tickets, phones, laptops, money and things in action are all property.
3. **Belonging to another.** Who has possession, control or a proprietary interest at the moment of appropriation? Consider s5(3) (money given for a specific purpose) and s5(4) (overpayments by mistake).
4. **Dishonesty.** First check s2(1)(a)-(c): an honest belief in a legal right, in consent, or that the owner cannot be found. Then apply *Ivey*: establish D's genuine beliefs about the facts, then judge by the standards of ordinary decent people. Do not apply *Ghosh*'s second limb.
5. **Intention to permanently deprive.** If D intends to return the item, consider s6(1): is the borrowing equivalent to outright taking (*Lloyd*: all goodness gone, e.g. a used-up season ticket)? Or does D intend to return equivalent rather than identical money (*Velumyl*)? Pawning falls under s6(2).
6. **Lecture exercise (season ticket).** A flatmate who previously let D use the ticket: D's honest belief in consent under s2(1)(b) is a key issue. Using the ticket for one match and returning it may not satisfy s6 unless its value for that fixture is "used up". Consider *Lloyd*.
7. **Revision examples.** An employee taking a phone "for a few weeks": appropriation, property and belonging are clear. Dishonesty under *Ivey* is likely. For intention to permanently deprive, use s6(1): is a few weeks' use equivalent to an outright taking? A student keeping a lent laptop: a later appropriation by keeping (s3(1)), with dishonesty and intention to permanently deprive both likely.
8. Note that the official consolidated Theft Act text in the knowledge base shows s15 (obtaining by deception) as repealed. Deception-based conduct is now typically fraud, but this module tests theft.
