# Loss of Control (Partial Defence to Murder)

## Overview
Voluntary manslaughter arises where D has the actus reus and mens rea of murder but a **partial defence** reduces liability to manslaughter. That avoids the mandatory life sentence and reflects D's lower culpability. The three partial defences are loss of control, diminished responsibility and suicide pact. Loss of control (Coroners and Justice Act 2009, ss54-56) replaced the common law defence of provocation, which was criticised for gender bias, trivial triggers and an unworkable reasonable-person test. The reform was meant to help abused women who kill out of fear, and to exclude revenge and sexual-infidelity killings. Whether it succeeds is a core essay topic.

## The law

### 1. Voluntary v involuntary manslaughter
- Manslaughter is a common law offence and can be committed in various ways.
- **Voluntary**: D has the actus reus and mens rea for murder, but has a partial defence.
- **Involuntary**: D has the actus reus for murder but not the mens rea (see unlawful-act-manslaughter.md, gross-negligence-manslaughter.md).
- Partial defences lower culpability rather than excusing D altogether: murder becomes manslaughter.

### 2. The old law of provocation (abolished)
- The common law defence focused on sexual jealousy and possession (*Mawgridge* (1707) Kel 119).
- Homicide Act 1957, s3: (1) things said or done provoked D; (2) D suffered a *sudden and temporary* loss of self-control; (3) the reasonable man would have responded in the same way.
- *Duffy* [1949] 1 All ER 932 required an immediate reaction, without time for reflection or for forming an intention to take revenge.
- **Problems**: the "sudden and temporary" requirement. The uncertain characteristics of the reasonable person (*Camplin* [1978] AC 705; *Smith* [2001] 1 AC 146, which was overruled in effect by the Privy Council in *AG for Jersey v Holley* [2005] UKPC 23). Trivial triggers (*Doughty* (1986) 83 Cr App R 319: a crying baby). Gender bias: the defence was easier for men who killed partners in rage than for women who killed abusive partners out of fear. *Ahluwalia* [1992] 4 All ER 889 is the classic illustration of "slow-burn" reactions after prolonged abuse.
- **Abolition**: CJA 2009, s56 abolished the common law defence (and s3 HA 1957).

### 3. The new defence: CJA 2009, s54(1)
D is not guilty of murder (but guilty of manslaughter) if:
1. D's acts or omissions in killing resulted from D's **loss of self-control** (s54(1)(a));
2. the loss of self-control had a **qualifying trigger** (s54(1)(b); s55); and
3. a person of D's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint and in D's circumstances, **might have reacted in the same or a similar way** (s54(1)(c)).

**Procedure and burden.**
- The judge must be satisfied that there is sufficient evidence on which a properly directed jury could reasonably conclude that the defence might apply, i.e. every element (s54(6)). Otherwise it must not be left to the jury.
- Once it is raised, the **Crown bears the burden of disproving it** beyond reasonable doubt. (Contrast diminished responsibility, where the burden is on D.)

**Exclusion: revenge.** The defence does not apply where D acted in a "considered desire for revenge" (s54(4)), even if there was a qualifying trigger. The defence concerns heat-of-the-moment killings, not premeditated ones.

### 4. Element 1: loss of self-control (s54(1)(a))
- This is subjective: did *this* D lose self-control? Whether a reasonable person would have done so is irrelevant at this stage.
- The threshold is high.
- The loss **need not be sudden** (s54(2); *Dawes* [2013] EWCA Crim 322).
- But the longer the delay, the harder it is to prove a loss of control, and the more likely the killing is seen as planned or vengeful (*Jewell* [2014] EWCA Crim 414).
- Loss of self-control is **not the same as panic or fear** (*Martin* [2017] EWCA Crim 1359).

### 5. Element 2: qualifying trigger (s55)
1. **Fear trigger** (s55(3)): D's fear of serious violence from V against D or another identified person.
   - It is subjective: the fear must be genuine and of *serious* violence. It need not rest on a correct assessment of the facts, and need not be reasonable.
   - It is excluded where D caused the fear by inciting the thing as an excuse to use violence (s55(6)(a)).
2. **Anger trigger** (s55(4)): things done or said (or both) which (a) constituted circumstances of an extremely grave character and (b) caused D to have a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged.
   - *Clinton* [2013] QB 1: it is subjective that D must actually feel seriously wronged. It is objective whether that feeling was justifiable and the circumstances extremely grave.
   - It is excluded where D incited the thing done or said (s55(6)(b)).
   - **Sexual infidelity** is to be disregarded as a thing done or said (s55(6)(c)). This was intended to stop possessive or abusive defendants relying on it. It becomes complex where infidelity is bound up with other abuse or taunting.
3. **Combination** of fear and anger (s55(5)).

### 6. Element 3: the objective test (s54(1)(c))
- Could a person of D's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint, in D's circumstances, have reacted in the same or a similar way?
- It must be objectively understandable that *anybody* might have reacted as D did, not merely that D had good reason to lose control. It shows the offence did not, in part, reflect a bad character.
- **D's circumstances** include all D's circumstances except those whose only relevance is to D's general capacity for tolerance or self-restraint (s54(3)):
  - D's mental condition is not relevant to the objective limb (*Rejmanski* [2017] EWCA Crim 2061);
  - voluntary intoxication is relevant only to D's general capacity for self-control, and so is disregarded (*Asmelash* [2013] EWCA Crim 157);
  - but D's history, e.g. abuse, may be part of the circumstances, including the gravity of a taunt.

### 7. Other partial defences
- Diminished responsibility (HA 1957 s2, as amended): see diminished-responsibility.md.
- **Suicide pact** (HA 1957, s4): D agreed with V that they would both die, and when killing V D intended to die in pursuance of the agreement (s4(3)). The burden is on D.

## Key authorities
- *Mawgridge* (1707): the old provocation focus on jealousy.
- *Duffy* [1949]: "sudden and temporary" loss of control.
- *Ahluwalia* [1992] 4 All ER 889: an abused woman's "slow-burn" reaction. The case exposed the limits of provocation.
- *Doughty* (1986): trivial provocation (a crying baby) could qualify under the old law.
- *AG for Jersey v Holley* [2005] UKPC 23: the objective standard; D's characteristics affecting self-control are irrelevant.
- CJA 2009, ss54-56: the new defence, abolition of provocation, qualifying triggers and exclusions.
- *Dawes* [2013] EWCA Crim 322: the loss need not be sudden.
- *Jewell* [2014] EWCA Crim 414: delay suggests planning or revenge.
- *Clinton* [2013] QB 1: the subjective/objective structure of the anger trigger.
- *Martin* [2017] EWCA Crim 1359: panic or fear is not loss of control.
- *Rejmanski* [2017] EWCA Crim 2061: a mental condition is irrelevant to the objective limb.
- *Asmelash* [2013] EWCA Crim 157: voluntary intoxication is irrelevant to the objective standard.

## Debate and critique
- **Imperfect justification v compassionate excuse (Norrie, [2010] Crim LR 275).** Alan Norrie argues that the Law Commission (Partial Defences to Murder, Law Com No 290, 2004) moved from a "compassionate excuse" model to one of "imperfect justification". The old law excused D because D had lost control. The new approach treats anger (or fear) at serious wrongdoing as an ethically appropriate emotion that makes a killing less wicked, though still wrong ("rightfulness in an overall context of wrongfulness"). On that model, he argues, there is no logical reason to require a *loss* of self-control at all. Parliament nevertheless kept the requirement, mixing the two philosophies. He also notes that the 2006 report (Law Com No 304) justified the defence more pragmatically, as mitigation in the face of the mandatory life sentence.
- **A "dog's breakfast" (Jo Miles).** Miles records the parliamentary criticism of the 2009 Act. Lord Lloyd called the reforms "all over the place" and a "dog's breakfast", and Baroness Mallalieu said the description was "a kindness". Points made: combining anger-based and fear-based defences under "loss of control" is incoherent; the sexual infidelity exclusion was badly drafted and of limited use; the complex drafting may confuse juries; the tightened diminished responsibility test may produce more murder convictions in mercy-killing and battered-spouse cases. Lord Lloyd's amendment to let juries add an "extenuating circumstances" rider, giving judges sentencing discretion instead of the mandatory life sentence, attracted wide support but was rejected. Evidence before the Law Commission suggested the public favours more nuanced sentencing than the government assumed.
- **Monitoring the new defences (Mitchell and Mackay, Arch Rev 2011).** Barry Mitchell and Ronnie Mackay summarise why provocation was replaced: decades of criticism that it favoured men; that the loss of self-control requirement was never properly explained; and that the reasonable-person test (from *Camplin*) had become absurdly "anthropomorphised", with conflicting cases on which characteristics count, ending with *Holley* overruling *Smith* [2001]. Battered women were forced to rely on diminished responsibility because the loss-of-control requirement reflected how men respond, whereas abused women "had to choose their moment". Mitchell and Mackay set out to monitor how the new defences work in practice.
- **Abolish and replace with self-preservation? (Elliott, J Crim L).** Catherine Elliott, responding to the Law Commission's 2003 consultation, argued that provocation had fundamental problems and should be abolished and replaced by a partial defence of *self-preservation*. It would apply where D, or someone closely associated with D, had been repeatedly subjected to serious violence or tormenting behaviour, causing a state of severe emotional disturbance at the time of the killing. She criticised the Commission's view that abolishing provocation would require abolishing the mandatory life sentence.
- **Gender.** The fear trigger, and the removal of suddenness, were meant to help women who kill abusers. Critics argue that keeping the loss of control requirement (and *Martin*'s holding that fear or panic is not loss of control) may still exclude women who act deliberately out of fear. Howes, Swaine Williams and Wistrich ("Women Who Kill", [2021] Crim LR) argue that even self-defence rarely works for women who kill abusers. They call for consideration of a specific partial defence for domestic-abuse killings and better jury directions on coercive control (see self-defence.md).
- **Mitchell, "Thinking about Murder" (1992).** Barry Mitchell argues that provocation analysis must take into account the relationship between provoker and provoked. Taunts often exploit the provoked person's weaknesses, so it is unfair to test D's response by a standard that ignores the whole person.
- **The objective test.** Excluding D's mental condition (*Rejmanski*) and intoxication (*Asmelash*) keeps the standard objective, but may disadvantage vulnerable defendants, such as those with cognitive impairments or emotional immaturity. They are pushed towards diminished responsibility instead.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Establish murder first** (actus reus, intent to kill or cause GBH). Only then consider loss of control.
2. **Evidential threshold.** Is there enough evidence on each element for the judge to leave it to the jury (s54(6))? If so, the prosecution must disprove it.
3. **Did D actually lose self-control?** Words like "sees red" or "snapped" help. Calm preparation (hiding behind a door with a knife), long delays, and acting out of fear or panic hurt (*Jewell*; *Martin*). Remember suddenness is not required (s54(2)). Consider s54(4): was this a considered desire for revenge?
4. **Which trigger?**
   - Fear: a genuine fear of *serious* violence from V (e.g. "you will suffer when I get home" from a violent husband). The fear need not be reasonable.
   - Anger: were the circumstances extremely grave, and was D's sense of being seriously wronged justifiable (*Clinton*)? Weight-related taunts and "I wish I'd been with someone slim" may not be "extremely grave" on their own, but a background of emotional abuse may change that.
   - Exclusions: D incited the trigger; sexual infidelity alone.
5. **Objective limb.** Use a person of D's sex and age with normal tolerance and self-restraint, in D's circumstances (history of abuse, the content of the taunt). Ignore intoxication and mental conditions as they affect general self-control (*Asmelash*; *Rejmanski*).
6. **the materials.** The mother was drinking heavily and had a knife for "protection". She intended serious harm, so murder is established. The anger trigger needs an extremely grave circumstance and a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged (the cruel taunt and the bullying of a disabled child). Her intoxication is ignored for the objective test (*Asmelash*). Then consider diminished responsibility (alcoholism, guilt). the materials note records that in *Dowds* Hughes LJ refused to treat voluntary intoxication as a recognised medical condition for diminished responsibility.
7. **the materials problems.** (a) A partner "sees red" after a cumulative history of taunts: discuss the anger trigger and whether the circumstances were grave. (b) An abused wife waits behind the door with a knife: the fear trigger is available, but the delay and preparation suggest no loss of control or a considered plan (*Jewell*). Also consider self-defence and diminished responsibility. (c) A drunk, paranoid neighbour: there is no qualifying trigger, so consider diminished responsibility and intoxication.
8. **Do not forget** suicide pact (s4 HA 1957) where the facts involve an agreement to die together.
