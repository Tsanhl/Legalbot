# Diminished Responsibility

## Overview
Diminished responsibility (DR) is a statutory partial defence to murder under the Homicide Act 1957, s2, as substituted by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, s52. If it succeeds, a murder conviction becomes voluntary manslaughter. This avoids the mandatory life sentence and opens up disposals such as hospital orders. Loss of control asks whether a *normal* person might have reacted as D did. DR starts from the premise that D should not be held to the "normal" standard because a recognised medical condition substantially impaired D's mental abilities. If those abilities were completely destroyed, the issue is insanity instead. The 2009 reform "medicalised" the defence. Commentators debate whether it is clearer or simply narrower, and how it applies to alcohol-dependent defendants.

## The law

### 1. The statutory test: HA 1957 s2(1)-(1B) (as substituted by CJA 2009 s52)
D is not to be convicted of murder if D was suffering from an abnormality of mental functioning which:
1. **arose from a recognised medical condition**;
2. **substantially impaired** D's ability to do one or more of: (a) understand the nature of D's conduct; (b) form a rational judgment; (c) exercise self-control (s2(1A)); and
3. **provides an explanation** for D's acts or omissions in doing or being a party to the killing. It does so if it causes, or is a significant contributory factor in causing, D to carry out that conduct (s2(1B)).

**Burden of proof**: on D, on the balance of probabilities. This contrasts with loss of control, where once the defence is raised the Crown must disprove it.

### 2. The old law
The original s2 required an "abnormality of mind" (arising from arrested or retarded development, inherent causes, or disease or injury) which "substantially impaired his mental responsibility". It was criticised as vague, as relying on experts without statutory guidance, and as stretched by cases such as *Byrne* [1960], which was read as including an inability to control one's actions (according to the Mackay summary in the sources). There was no explicit causal requirement.

### 3. Element 1: abnormality of mental functioning
- The phrase is not defined and is vague. It requires a link between a medical condition and some effect on the mind.
- Expert evidence is relied on to explain the abnormality to the jury, to say how far D's mental functioning departed from the norm, and to give an opinion.
- It is not a clinical term. Baroness Murphy said in debate that it was "better" than "abnormality of mind" but still vague.

### 4. Element 2: recognised medical condition
- The condition may be physical, psychological or psychiatric. Whether it is "recognised" is a question for the court. A physical condition is less likely to satisfy the other elements.
- Guidance comes from the WHO International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), the American diagnostic and statistical manual (DSM), and a reliable body of expert evidence. The Ministry of Justice Circular envisaged practitioners using accepted classificatory lists.
- Examples mentioned in the sources: schizophrenia, PTSD, depression, and physical conditions such as epilepsy or diabetes if linked to impaired functioning. Developmental immaturity in children does not count unless tied to a recognised disorder. The Law Commission's proposal to include it was rejected by the Government.
- **Alcohol**: acute *voluntary* intoxication is not a recognised medical condition (*Dowds* (2012), Hughes LJ). Alcohol dependence syndrome can be (see section 7).

### 5. Element 3: substantial impairment of a specified ability
- The three abilities are: understanding the nature of one's conduct; forming a rational judgment; exercising self-control.
- **"Substantially"**: the Supreme Court in *Golds* [2016] UKSC 61 described it as "weighty", "important" or "significant". *Squelch* [2017] EWCA Crim 204: "less than total and more than trivial". The older test in *Lloyd* [1967] was "more than trivial but less than total". Commentators read *Golds* as raising the bar.
- If impairment is total, consider insanity (insanity.md).

### 6. Element 4: explanation (causation)
- There must be a causal connection between the abnormality and the killing (s2(1B)).
- The abnormality need not be the sole cause (*Dietschmann* [2003] 1 AC 1209). Even with heavy intoxication alongside a depressive condition, the abnormality can be a significant contributory factor.
- The requirement was meant to exclude coincidental abnormalities unrelated to the killing. the materials asks whether it makes any real difference.

### 7. Diminished responsibility and alcohol
- Alcohol features in up to 80% of homicides (Galappathie and Jethwa).
- *Tandy* [1989] set an almost unattainable threshold for alcoholism.
- *Wood* [2008] and *Stewart* [2009] showed greater judicial recognition of alcohol dependence syndrome as a distinct clinical disorder, while maintaining that simple voluntary intoxication is no defence. Galappathie and Jethwa predicted more contested cases.
- *Dowds* (2012): voluntary acute intoxication is not a "recognised medical condition", even though it appears in classificatory lists.
- *Dietschmann*: where D has a qualifying condition *and* is intoxicated, the jury should ask whether the condition substantially impaired D's abilities and was a significant contributory factor, despite the drink.

### 8. Sentencing and disposal
- Sentencing follows the Sentencing Council Definitive Guideline on Manslaughter by reason of Diminished Responsibility. The court assesses the level of responsibility D *retained*. The starting point for a single offence is 7 years, with a range of 3-12 years (as summarised in *Calocane*).
- *R v Calocane* [2024] EWCA Crim 490: D, who had paranoid schizophrenia, killed three people in Nottingham and attempted to murder three others. He pleaded guilty to manslaughter by DR and to attempted murder, and received a hospital order with restrictions (Mental Health Act 1983, ss37 and 41). The Solicitor General's reference for undue leniency was refused. Because D's retained responsibility was low and the offending would not have occurred but for his schizophrenia, the judge was entitled to conclude that no penal element was needed. The key factor is the strength of the link between the impairment and the offending, and the regime that best protects the public. The court noted that, without the mental condition, a whole life term would doubtless have been considered.

## Key authorities
- Homicide Act 1957, s2 (as substituted by CJA 2009, s52): the four-element test. Burden on D.
- *Byrne* [1960]: the old broad reading of "abnormality of mind" (as summarised in the sources).
- *Lloyd* [1967]; *Golds* [2016] UKSC 61; *Squelch* [2017] EWCA Crim 204: "substantial" impairment.
- *Dietschmann* [2003] 1 AC 1209: the abnormality need not be the sole cause, even with intoxication.
- *Tandy* [1989]; *Wood* [2008]; *Stewart* [2009]: alcohol dependence.
- *Dowds* (2012): voluntary acute intoxication is not a recognised medical condition.
- *Brennan* [2014] EWCA Crim 2387: the new criteria relate "entirely to psychiatric matters" (Davis LJ). Juries should not reject unchallenged expert evidence without a proper basis.
- *Calocane* [2024] EWCA Crim 490: sentencing, retained responsibility and hospital orders.

## Debate and critique
- **Clearer but narrower? (R.D. Mackay, [2010] Crim LR 290, CJA 2009 partial defences (2)).** Mackay welcomes the alignment with modern psychiatric classification and the focus on functional impairment. He warns of over-medicalisation, which risks excluding morally sympathetic cases such as mercy killings or borderline disorders. He notes narrower criteria (e.g. distorted perception not affecting the three abilities), a heavier evidential burden from the causal link, and the fact that psychiatric evidence often cannot settle causation. Scotland, by contrast, keeps "abnormality of mind" without an explicit causation requirement.
- **The impairment factors are medico-legal, not purely medical (Mackay, "The Impairment Factors in the New Diminished Responsibility Plea" [2018] Crim LR 462).** After *Golds*, Mackay argues that "substantially" and the three abilities are not psychiatric concepts, even though the courts say the plea emphasises psychiatric issues. "Loss of self-control" in the loss of control defence is plainly not a medical matter, so adding "ability to exercise" cannot make it one. Experts' judgments on these factors are evaluative. Both lawyers and psychiatrists need clearer guidance, and he suggests interpretations that would give experts flexibility. He doubts that the reform achieved its stated goal of "clarification" for the impairment factors.
- **The plea in operation (Mackay, initial findings).** In a CPS study of 90 cases under the new plea, compared with a Law Commission study of 157 under the old law: contested pleas rose from 22.9% to 43.3%; murder convictions rose from 14% to 34.4%; DR findings fell from 80.3% to 65.6%. Most defendants were male (85%) and most victims were known to D (89.8%). This challenges the official view that the reform merely clarified and modernised the law.
- **Moral responsibility in medical clothing (psychiatric evidence article, J Crim L 2018).** This article argues against *Brennan*'s claim that the new criteria relate entirely to psychiatric matters. Understanding, rationality and self-control have moral, social and philosophical dimensions. Whether an abnormality arises from a recognised medical condition is ultimately a question of law. The causal link may be unanswerable by anyone. "Medicalisation" pushes psychiatrists towards the ultimate issue and onto the jury's territory, causing inconsistency and role confusion. It quotes Baroness Murphy that lawyers and psychiatrists "come from different planets", and agrees with Kennefick and with Loughnan and Ward that the "old tensions remain".
- **Kennefick ((2011) MLR, "Introducing a New Diminished Responsibility Defence").** Louise Kennefick examines s52 in its structural context and in its technical detail. She asks whether it answers the critics of the old law and achieves the Government's goals of clarity, fairness and (as the abstract suggests) a better fit with medicine. She criticises the ambiguity created when psychiatrists testify about responsibility and juries try to interpret psychiatric terminology. The annotations flag mercy killers as a group whom the medical model may leave without a defence.
- **Dog's breakfast (Miles).** Critics argue the tighter s2 may increase murder convictions in mercy-killing and battered-spouse cases. The rejection of the developmental-immaturity proposal leaves children aged 10 and over without DR unless they have a recognised condition. An adult with a similar mental age might obtain mitigation that the child cannot.
- **Battered women.** Under the old provocation law, battered women were "effectively forced" to rely on DR because the loss-of-control requirement did not fit their situation (Mitchell and Mackay). This casts them as mentally abnormal rather than as reacting reasonably to abuse. The fear trigger aims to reduce this.
- **Sentencing outcomes.** *Calocane* shows the public controversy when DR leads to a hospital order for multiple killings. The Court of Appeal stressed the medical evidence and public protection over a penal element.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. **Establish murder first.** Then decide whether loss of control and/or DR arise. Both can be run together.
2. **Is there a recognised medical condition?** Look for a diagnosis (depression, schizophrenia, PTSD, alcohol dependence syndrome, "battered woman syndrome" if diagnosed). Paranoia, guilt or grief alone needs expert evidence of a recognised condition. Do not invent a diagnosis: say that expert evidence would be needed.
3. **Which ability was substantially impaired?** Understanding conduct, rational judgment or self-control. "Substantially" means significant, more than trivial (*Golds*).
4. **Explanation.** Was the condition a significant contributory factor? Intoxication alongside a condition does not defeat DR (*Dietschmann*), but acute voluntary intoxication on its own is not a condition (*Dowds*).
5. **Burden.** D must prove the defence on the balance of probabilities.
6. **the materials.** Alcoholism linked to guilt about the crash may be alcohol dependence syndrome, which is potentially a recognised condition (*Wood*; *Stewart*). Her heavy drinking on the day is voluntary intoxication, which is not itself a condition (*Dowds*). Ask whether the dependence (and any depression) substantially impaired her self-control or judgment and contributed significantly to the stabbing (*Dietschmann*).
7. **the materials.** Paranoia may indicate a psychotic disorder, which needs a diagnosis. His drunkenness is set aside unless he has dependence syndrome. If his abilities were *totally* destroyed and he did not know the nature of his act or that it was wrong, consider insanity instead.
8. **Disposal.** Mention that a DR manslaughter conviction allows discretionary sentencing, including hospital orders (*Calocane*).
