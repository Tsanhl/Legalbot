# Criminal Law: answer rules
- Problem answers: no introduction or conclusion; go straight to the offences, party by party; in-text citations, not
  footnotes; no "I/we"; do not hide behind the jury; avoid "likely", "clearly".
- Pick the right offence and analyse it fully rather than surveying many offences superficially.
- Address or rule out **every** element: e.g. direct vs oblique intention; *Woollin*'s two-part test (virtual certainty
  objectively, and D's appreciation); GBH requires "really serious harm"; s 18 needs intent to cause GBH.
- Assault requires V to apprehend immediate unlawful violence: if V did not see it, there is no assault.
- Argue the level of harm with case law and the CPS charging standards (is a broken nose ABH or GBH?).
- Spot **transferred malice** (*Latimer*): same offence only.
- Consent: valid for assault/battery if express or implied and effective; for ABH and above only within a recognised
  exception (*Brown*); sport: *Barnes* factors (type and level of sport, nature of act, degree of force, risk, state of
  mind). Surgery: D intends to wound, so s 18 is in play; the question is consent (*Tabassum* on deception as to nature
  or quality).
- Theft: AR and MR with dishonesty per *Ivey*; consider duress even if it fails.
- Murder/LOSC: apply the facts clearly to each element.
- **Out of scope unless the question raises them** (do not discuss): inchoate offences; secondary/accessorial liability.
- Fitness to plead essays: discuss current and proposed law and medical advances.

## Everyday questions (learned from testing)
- For an 'is it theft?' question, check every element of s 1 Theft Act 1968 before concluding, briefly and in plain words: appropriation (s 3), property (s 4), belonging to another (s 5), dishonesty (s 2, then *Ivey*, applied in criminal cases in *Barton and Booth*) and intention to permanently deprive (s 6). ## Learned from testing
- Loss of control: treat the s 55(3) fear trigger as subjective, so D's genuine fear of serious violence counts even if mistaken or unreasonable; apply 'circumstances of an extremely grave character' and 'justifiable sense of being seriously wronged' only to s 55(4), but still apply the s 55(6) exclusions and the s 54(1)(c) normal-person test. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- Insanity: treat 'disease of the mind' as a legal, not medical, concept (Kemp; Sullivan), so never dismiss insanity because a condition is clinically mild; ask whether it caused a defect of reason, then test both M'Naghten limbs: whether D did not know the nature and quality of the act or, if D did, did not know it was wrong. [2026-11-22 · ref 2026-11-22-1017; 2026-11-22-1855 (see that run's log and grades)]
- Where a mental disorder produces a mistaken belief in an attack, explain how insanity and self-defence interact: the reasonableness of the force is judged objectively and cannot take account of D's insane delusions (Oye) or, save exceptionally, psychiatric conditions affecting perception (Martin (Anthony)), so treat insanity as the route for the delusion; name these cases only under the citation rules and otherwise list them as gaps.
