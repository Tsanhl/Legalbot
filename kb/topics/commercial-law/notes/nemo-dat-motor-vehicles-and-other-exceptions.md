# Nemo Dat - Motor Vehicles on HP (HPA 1964 Part III) and Other Exceptions

## Overview
Because a hirer under hire-purchase has not "agreed to buy" (*Helby v Matthews*), the buyer-in-possession exception
does not protect someone who buys a car from a hirer. Part III of the Hire-Purchase Act 1964 (as re-enacted by the
Consumer Credit Act 1974) fills that gap for **motor vehicles only**, protecting the first **private purchaser** who buys
in good faith without notice of the HP or conditional sale agreement. This note also gathers the remaining
exceptions: market overt (abolished in 1994), special powers of sale and the unpaid seller's resale. Law: England &
Wales.

## The law

### 1. HPA 1964 s 27
"This section applies where a motor vehicle has been bailed ... under a hire-purchase agreement, or has been agreed to
be sold under a conditional sale agreement, and, before the property in the vehicle has become vested in the debtor,
he disposes of the vehicle to another person." Where the disposition is "to a private purchaser" who takes "in good
faith without notice of ... the relevant agreement, that disposition shall have effect as if the creditor's title to
the vehicle had been vested in the debtor immediately before that disposition." Elements:
1. **A hire-purchase or conditional sale agreement** actually exists between creditor and debtor. If the agreement is
   void (or voidable and avoided before the disposition), s 27 does not apply: *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62
   (rogue impersonating Mr Patel; agreement void for mistake of identity; the rogue was not the "debtor" (s 29(4)) so
   Hudson, though a private purchaser in good faith, got nothing).
2. **A motor vehicle**: "a mechanically propelled vehicle intended or adapted for use on roads to which the public has
   access" (s 29(1)). Not other goods: if X has a car and a piano on HP, only the car is covered (revision guide).
3. **A disposition by the debtor before property vests**: s 29(1) - any sale or contract of sale (including a
   conditional sale agreement), any bailment under an HP agreement, any transfer of property under an HP provision, and
   any transaction purporting to be a disposition.
   - *Kulkarni v Manor Credit (Davenham)* [2010] EWCA Civ 69: dealer G took a new car on HP from finance company M and
     the same day sold and delivered it to K (to whom G had earlier given the registration number for insurance). K's
     claim succeeded: the car was not in a deliverable state until plates were fitted, so the sale to K first took
     effect on delivery, when G was already a hirer; s 27 applied.
   - *VFS Financial Services v JF Plant Tyres* (2013): taking a vehicle in settlement of debts is not a "disposition";
     "sale" connotes a money transaction, so s 27 did not protect JF.
4. **To a private purchaser** (s 29(2)): a purchaser who does not, at the time, carry on business as a motor dealer
   (buying vehicles to offer or expose for sale) or a finance company (providing finance by buying vehicles and letting
   them on HP or conditional sale). A part-time dealer is not a private purchaser (*Stevenson v Beverley Bentinck*
   [1976] 2 All ER 606; Lord Denning: the courts must decide where, between two wholly innocent parties, the loss should
   fall); nor is someone buying several cars to sell on for gain (*GE Capital Bank v Rushton* [2005] EWCA Civ 1556). The
   private purchaser may buy or take the vehicle on HP.
5. **First private purchaser**: if the debtor sells to a trade purchaser (dealer) who sells to a private purchaser, the
   first private purchaser in good faith gets the creditor's title though bought from the dealer; the dealer gets no
   title. Once a private purchaser has title, it passes normally to anyone thereafter.
6. **Good faith and without notice** of the HP/conditional sale agreement: *Barker v Bell* [1971] 1 WLR 983 - knowing the
   vehicle had been on HP does not matter if the purchaser believes the finance has been paid off; *Dodds v Yorkshire
   Bank* [1992] CCLR 92 also cited.
7. **Effect**: as if the creditor's title had vested in the debtor immediately before the disposition - so the private
   purchaser takes the creditor's title. Trade or finance purchasers in the chain remain liable to the creditor.

### 2. Market overt (former s 22) - abolished
8. Sales in market overt (open, recognised markets - in the City of London including ground-floor shops open to the
   public - during the hours of daylight and according to market usage) used to give a good faith buyer title even to
   stolen goods. Criticised as archaic, geographically arbitrary and facilitating disposal of stolen goods (Atiyah,
   Diamond). The Law Reform Committee's 12th Report (1966) recommended replacing it with protection for buyers at public
   auctions or trade premises. It was **abolished by the Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1994** (Battersby summary).

### 3. Special powers of sale (s 21(2)(b)) and court orders
9. Common law powers (pledgee's power of sale), statutory powers (enforcement officers selling goods seized under a writ
   of execution), and court-ordered sales under the CPR where it is desirable to sell at once (perishables, falling
   market). See `nemo-dat-rule-and-estoppel.md`.

### 4. Unpaid seller's resale (s 48)
10. An unpaid seller exercising its lien or stoppage in transit who resells passes good title to the new buyer as
    against the original buyer (s 48(2)), and the statutory and contractual resale rights in s 48(3)-(4) rescind the
    first contract. See `remedies-of-the-seller.md`. Combine with s 24 where the seller still has possession.

### 5. Thieves
11. Apart from former market overt, none of the exceptions lets a buyer get title through a thief: s 23 needs a voidable
    title; s 25 cannot defeat an owner who never consented to possession (*National Employers v Jones* [1990] 1 AC 24);
    HPA s 27 needs a genuine HP/conditional sale agreement. The owner's action against a later innocent purchaser may be
    lost six years after that purchase under the Limitation Act 1980 ss 2-4 (Thornely).

## Key authorities
- HPA 1964 ss 27, 29(1), 29(2), 29(4); SGA ss 21(2)(b), 22 (repealed), 25(2), 48(2).
- *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62 - void HP agreement; no s 27 protection.
- *Kulkarni v Manor Credit* [2010] EWCA Civ 69 - timing of disposition; deliverable state.
- *VFS Financial Services v JF Plant Tyres* (2013) - settlement of debt is not a disposition.
- *Stevenson v Beverley Bentinck* [1976] 2 All ER 606; *GE Capital v Rushton* [2005] EWCA Civ 1556 - private purchaser.
- *Barker v Bell* [1971] 1 WLR 983; *Dodds v Yorkshire Bank* [1992] CCLR 92 - notice.
- *Helby v Matthews* [1895] AC 471 - why Part III was needed.

## Debate and critique
- **Davies, "Wrongful Disposition of Motor Vehicles - a Legal Quagmire" [1995] JBL 47.** The protection for vehicles is a
  patchwork: Part III applies only to private purchasers and only to motor vehicles (not caravans, boats or other
  high-value goods) and only to HP and conditional sale (not finance leases or contract hire); reliance on actual rather
  than constructive notice makes wrongful dispositions too easy. Davies argues for a compulsory registration system for
  security interests in vehicles, linked to vehicle identification numbers, with unregistered interests extinguished on
  disposition.
- **Elliott [2004] JBL 381.** After *Shogun*, s 27 gives no protection where a rogue obtains the vehicle by impersonating a
  real person - precisely the fraud most likely to harm private purchasers - an "injustice to innocent purchasers".
- **Thornely [1965] CLJ 24.** Part III protects a buyer from any hirer, whether or not in business, contrasting with the
  narrow s 25 after *Newtons of Wembley*; a sign of the incoherence of the exceptions.
- **Why trade buyers are excluded (one rationale).** Dealers and finance companies can check HPI records and bear losses as a business
  risk; private buyers cannot (the policy behind s 29(2)). *Moorgate v Twitchings* shows that, even so, a dealer misled
  by an incomplete voluntary register keeps the loss.
- **Market overt.** Its abolition strengthened owners' rights; critics noted the Law Reform Committee's alternative
  (protection at auctions/trade premises) was not adopted and there was little empirical research (Battersby, citing
  Atiyah).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is the item a motor vehicle (s 29(1))? If not, Part III is irrelevant - try other exceptions.
2. Is there a valid HP or conditional sale agreement between creditor and debtor (void for mistaken identity -
   *Shogun*)?
3. Did the debtor dispose of it before property vested (sale, contract of sale, HP letting; not a debt settlement -
   *VFS*)? When did the disposition take effect (*Kulkarni*)?
4. Who is the first private purchaser in the chain (exclude dealers, even part-time, and finance companies;
   *Stevenson*, *Rushton*)?
5. Did that purchaser act in good faith without notice of the agreement (*Barker v Bell*)?
6. Effect: the first private purchaser takes the creditor's title; later buyers take from them; trade purchasers
   before them got nothing and may be liable in conversion.
7. For other goods: market overt is gone; consider s 21(2)(b) powers, s 48 resale, and ss 23-25.
Traps: applying s 25 to an HP hirer (*Helby*); giving title to a dealer under s 27; forgetting that a later private
buyer from a dealer can still be protected.

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: saying that if the debtor sells to a trade purchaser (even a part-time dealer, Stevenson v Beverley Bentinck) no later buyer gets title. Under HPA 1964 s 27(3) the first private purchaser in good faith without notice takes the creditor's title. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q09#0]
- Common error: treating a buyer told the car was 'fully paid off' as better placed than in Barker v Bell. It is the Barker v Bell case: protection rests on no actual notice (HPA 1964 s 29(3)) of a subsisting agreement. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q09#2]
