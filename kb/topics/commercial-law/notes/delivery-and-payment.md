# Delivery and Payment

## Overview
the materials that most sale of goods disputes arise from delivery and payment. The SGA sets default
rules - the parties are free to agree otherwise - on what "delivery" means, where and when it happens, the
effect of delivering to a carrier, delivering the wrong quantity, and delivering by instalments. These
rules link directly to the passing of property and risk (s 18 r 5(2) and s 20) and to the buyer's
remedies (rejection, s 30, s 31(2), s 35). Consumer sales have separate rules in CRA 2015 ss 25, 26 and 28.

## The law

### 1. The basic duties
1. **s 27**: the seller must deliver the goods and the buyer must accept and pay for them, in accordance
   with the contract.
2. **s 28**: unless otherwise agreed, delivery and payment are *concurrent conditions*: the seller must be
   ready and willing to give possession in exchange for the price, and the buyer ready and willing to pay
   in exchange for possession. So the buyer is not entitled to credit unless agreed. The seller need not
   actually tender delivery before suing if it can show it was ready and willing and the buyer would have
   refused (*Levey & Co v Goldberg* (1922), revision guide).

### 2. What "delivery" means
3. **s 61(1)**: delivery is the "voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another" - not
   physical carriage. Forms (lecture): *actual* (handing over goods or documents), *symbolic* (handing over
   something representing the goods, e.g. a key) and *constructive* (transferring the right to possession
   of goods held by a third party - attornment, s 29(4)). A change in the capacity in which someone holds
   the goods can amount to delivery (e.g. a hirer exercising an option to buy; the hire-purchase triangle
   where the dealer sells to the finance company but hands the goods to the customer).
   - *Albright & Wilson UK v Biachem* [2002] UKHL 32: a delivery note showed an intention to deliver on
     the seller's behalf; sufficient as (symbolic) delivery.
4. **Who collects (s 29(1))**: depends on the contract, express or implied.
5. **Place (s 29(2))**: absent agreement, the seller's place of business (or residence); for specific goods
   known by both parties to be elsewhere, that place. In other words the default is that the buyer collects.
6. **Time to send (s 29(3))**: where the seller must send but no time is fixed, within a reasonable time
   (a question of fact: s 59). Not applicable to consumer contracts (CRA s 28 governs).
7. **Third-party possession / attornment (s 29(4))**: no delivery until the third party acknowledges to the
   buyer that it holds on the buyer's behalf. A warehouse warrant or receipt does not itself effect delivery
   (*Mercuria Energy Trading v Citibank* (2015), Phillips J); the warehouseman must attorn (*Sterns v
   Vickers* (1923); *Wardar's v W Norwood* (1968)). Goods must be segregated or ascertained, though
   attornment may estop the warehouseman (*Re London Wine Co* (1986)). A right to immediate possession may
   arise where the third party wrongfully refuses to attorn (*Pendragon v Walon* (2005)).
8. **Reasonable hour (s 29(5))**: a demand or tender may be treated as ineffectual unless made at a
   reasonable hour.
9. **Costs (s 29(6))**: unless agreed, the seller bears the costs of putting goods into a deliverable state.
10. **Delivery to the wrong person**: a seller told to deliver at the buyer's premises discharges its duty
    by delivering there, without negligence, to someone apparently authorised (*Galbraith & Grant v Block*
    [1922] 2 KB 155; followed in *Computer 2000 Distribution v ICM Computer Solutions* [2004] EWCA Civ 1634 -
    goods signed for by a security guard and collected by a fraudster; buyer still liable to pay).

### 3. Time of delivery and time of payment
11. **s 10(1)**: unless a different intention appears, time of *payment* is not of the essence. Late payment
    is therefore normally a breach of warranty only; parties may make it of the essence, in which case late
    payment is repudiatory (*Lombard North Central v Butterworth* (1987)).
12. **s 10(2)**: whether any other time stipulation is of the essence depends on the contract. Commercial
    delivery dates are commonly construed as conditions: *Hartley v Hymans* (1920); *Bunge Corp v Tradax*
    [1981] 1 WLR 711 (HL) - where the time is fixed, failure is breach of condition, strictly applied, and
    the degree of inconvenience is irrelevant. The revision guide notes that modern cases treat a term as
    innominate unless the contract makes clear it is a condition or warranty (*Pharmapac v HBS Healthcare*
    (2022)).
13. **Waiver and re-making time of the essence**: a buyer who keeps pressing for delivery after the date
    waives the right to terminate, but can give reasonable notice fixing a new date, making time of the
    essence again (*Charles Rickards v Oppenheim* [1950] 1 KB 616 - car body not ready; buyer's notice
    valid; seller's claim for the price failed). *Virulite v Virulite Distribution* (2015): time runs from
    the notice, not the waived breach. *Alegrow v Yayla Agro* (2021) (citing *Louinder v Leis* (1982)):
    failing to meet such a notice is unreasonable delay amounting to repudiation; beware premature
    termination, which may itself be repudiatory.
14. A seller who misses the delivery period cannot insist the buyer take late delivery (*Hartley v Hymans*,
    McCardie J). A buyer who accepts late delivery loses the right to reject but keeps damages (s 11(4)).

### 4. Delivery to a carrier and transit
15. **s 32(1)**: where the seller is authorised or required to send goods, delivery to a carrier (named by
    the buyer or not) is prima facie delivery to the buyer. This parallels s 18 r 5(2) (unconditional
    appropriation by delivery to a carrier) and matters for risk. It does not apply where the buyer is a
    consumer (s 32(4)); CRA s 29 then governs risk.
16. **s 32(2)**: unless authorised otherwise, the seller must make a reasonable contract of carriage on the
    buyer's behalf; if not and the goods are lost or damaged, the buyer may refuse to treat the delivery to
    the carrier as delivery to itself, or claim damages. *Thomas Young & Sons v Hobson* (1949) 65 TLR 365:
    engines sent "at owner's risk", badly secured, when "company's risk" cost the same - buyer could reject.
17. **s 32(3)**: where goods are sent by a route involving sea transit, the seller must give the buyer
    enough notice to allow it to insure during transit; otherwise the goods are at the seller's risk during
    the sea transit. the materials stresses this.
18. **s 33**: where the seller agrees to deliver at its own risk at a place other than where the goods are
    when sold, the buyer (unless otherwise agreed) takes the risk of deterioration necessarily incident to
    the course of transit.

### 5. Wrong quantity (s 30)
19. **Short delivery (s 30(1))**: the buyer may reject; if it accepts, it pays at the contract rate.
20. **Excess (s 30(2)-(3))**: the buyer may accept the contract quantity and reject the rest, or reject the
    whole; if it accepts the whole it pays at the contract rate. A buyer who rejects can sue for
    non-delivery of those goods.
21. **De minimis**: trivial deviations are not breaches - *Shipton, Anderson v Weil Bros* [1912] 1 KB 574
    (55 lb excess on 4,950 tons of wheat, about 4 shillings, not charged: the buyer could not reject);
    *Margaronis Navigation v Peabody* [1965] 1 QB 300.
22. **s 30(2A)**: a buyer who does not deal as a consumer may not reject under s 30(1)-(2) if the shortfall
    or excess is so slight that rejection would be unreasonable. After CRA Sch 1 amendments the section
    applies to non-consumer sales only; consumer wrong-quantity cases go to **CRA s 25** (reject or accept
    at the contract rate; for excess, accept the contract quantity or reject the whole).

### 6. Instalments (s 31)
23. **s 31(1)**: unless agreed, the buyer need not accept delivery by instalments. A seller who delivers
    short cannot insist on making up the balance later. *Behrend v Produce Brokers* [1920] 3 KB 530: part of
    a cotton seed cargo delivered, the ship left and returned two weeks later with the rest; the buyer
    could keep what was delivered, reject the balance and recover its prepaid price.
24. **s 31(2)**: in a contract for stated instalments separately paid for, where the seller makes defective
    deliveries or the buyer fails to take or pay for instalments, it depends on the terms and circumstances
    whether the breach repudiates the whole contract or is severable (compensation only).
    - *Maple Flock v Universal Furniture Products* [1934] 1 KB 148: one defective instalment out of 20 -
      severable breach; the tests are (i) the quantitative ratio of the breach to the whole contract and
      (ii) the probability of repetition.
    - *Robert A Munro v Meyer* [1930] 2 KB 312: 1,500 tons of meat and bone meal in monthly 125-ton
      instalments; all 631 tons delivered so far were adulterated - buyers need take no further deliveries.
    - *Regent OHG Aisenstadt und Barig v Francesco of Jermyn Street* [1981] 3 All ER 327: one suit short in
      an instalment; where s 31(2) applies it excludes s 30(1), and the purported repudiation was wrongful.
      A slight shortfall would also now fail the s 30(2A) reasonableness test.
    - *Jackson v Rotax Motor and Cycle* [1910] 2 KB 937: accepting earlier instalments does not prevent
      rejection of later defective ones.
    - *Smyth v TD Bailey* (1940): a clause making each instalment a "separate contract" still usually leaves
      one severable contract, so a serious breach can repudiate the whole.
25. Consumer instalments: **CRA s 26** (consumer not bound to accept instalments unless agreed).

### 7. Payment and the price
26. Price is fixed under ss 8-9 (see `scope-of-sga-and-cra.md`). If the buyer has paid and the seller fails
    to deliver, the buyer can recover the price (failure of consideration) with interest (s 54 preserves
    this). If goods perish while at the buyer's risk, the buyer must still pay.
27. **s 36**: a buyer who rightfully refuses goods need not return them; intimating refusal suffices.

### 8. Consumer contracts (CRA)
28. **s 28 CRA**: unless agreed otherwise, the trader must deliver to the consumer; with no agreed time,
    without undue delay and within 30 days of the contract (s 28(3)); an obligation to deliver at the time of
    contract counts as the agreed time (s 28(4)). "Delivery" again means voluntary transfer of possession
    (s 59 CRA).

## Key authorities
- *Albright & Wilson v Biachem* [2002] UKHL 32 - delivery note as symbolic delivery.
- *Bunge v Tradax* [1981] 1 WLR 711 - fixed delivery/time terms in commercial contracts are conditions.
- *Charles Rickards v Oppenheim* [1950] 1 KB 616 - waiver then notice making time of the essence.
- *Galbraith & Grant v Block* [1922] 2 KB 155; *Computer 2000 v ICM* [2004] EWCA Civ 1634 - delivery to an
  apparently authorised person.
- *Thomas Young v Hobson* (1949) - s 32(2) reasonable carriage contract.
- *Shipton, Anderson v Weil* [1912] 1 KB 574; *Margaronis v Peabody* [1965] 1 QB 300 - de minimis.
- *Maple Flock* [1934] 1 KB 148; *Munro v Meyer* [1930] 2 KB 312; *Regent v Francesco* [1981] 3 All ER 327 -
  instalments.
- *Behrend v Produce Brokers* [1920] 3 KB 530 - no obligation to accept instalments.
- SGA ss 10, 27-33, 36, 59, 61; CRA ss 25, 26, 28, 29.

## Debate and critique
- **Strictness of time clauses.** *Bunge v Tradax* favours certainty: commercial parties need to know at
  once whether they can terminate, especially in string contracts. Critics (Stannard, "So what if time is of
  the essence?" (2005) Sing J Legal Stud 114 is set reading) question whether strict conditions let a party
  escape a bad bargain for trivial delay. The revision guide's note on *Pharmapac* (innominate unless clear)
  shows a counter-trend.
- **Wrong quantity.** The common law de minimis rule and s 30(2A) both curb opportunistic rejection where
  the market has moved; but s 30(2A) applies only to non-consumers, reflecting the policy that consumers
  should have clear rights.
- **Instalments.** s 31(2) is open-textured; *Maple Flock*'s two tests give guidance but outcomes are
  fact-sensitive (*Munro v Meyer* v *Maple Flock*). *Regent v Francesco* shows the interaction between ss 30
  and 31 was uncertain before s 30(2A).
- **Risk of fraud in delivery.** *Computer 2000* puts the loss on the buyer whose instructions were
  followed; Dobson (2005) 45 Student Law Review 12 discusses whether that is right.
- **Consumer v business.** The revision guide's suggested essay asks you to evaluate the delivery duty for
  consumer and non-consumer buyers: note the default that the business buyer collects (s 29(2)) contrasts
  with the consumer default that the trader delivers within 30 days (CRA s 28), and that s 32(1) carrier
  delivery does not apply to consumers.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. What did the contract say about delivery (place, time, method)? If silent, apply s 29 defaults.
2. Was there "delivery" in law (s 61)? Check symbolic delivery and attornment (s 29(4)) for goods held by a
   warehouse.
3. Was time of the essence (s 10, *Bunge*)? Did the buyer waive it by pressing for delivery, and then serve
   a valid reasonable notice (*Rickards*)?
4. Carrier involved? s 32(1) delivery (non-consumers); was the carriage contract reasonable (s 32(2))? Sea
   transit notice (s 32(3))? Transit deterioration (s 33)?
5. Wrong quantity? s 30(1)-(3); de minimis; s 30(2A) for non-consumers; CRA s 25 for consumers.
6. Instalments? Were they agreed (s 31(1))? If so, is the breach repudiatory or severable (s 31(2), *Maple
   Flock* ratio and repetition)?
7. Payment: was the buyer ready and willing (s 28)? Late payment usually only a warranty breach (s 10(1)).
8. Then move to remedies: has the buyer accepted the goods (s 35) and lost the right to reject?
Traps: treating delivery as physical handover; forgetting s 30(2A) is non-consumer only; assuming every
late delivery allows termination.
