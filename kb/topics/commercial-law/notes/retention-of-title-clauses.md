# Retention of Title (Romalpa) Clauses

## Overview
A retention (or reservation) of title (ROT) clause is a contractual term under which the seller keeps title to
goods until the price - or, under an "all monies" clause, everything the buyer owes - has been paid, while the
buyer gets possession, use and usually risk. ROT is the unpaid trade supplier's main protection against the
buyer's insolvency: if property never passed, the goods are not part of the insolvent buyer's estate and can be
recovered ahead of unsecured creditors. Simple clauses work; "extended" clauses claiming mixed or manufactured
goods or resale proceeds usually fail because they are characterised as charges created by the buyer that are void
unless registered under the Companies Act. Law: England & Wales, with comparative US (UCC Art 9), German, Australian and New Zealand material.

## The law

### 1. Statutory basis
1. **s 17**: property in specific or ascertained goods passes when the parties intend.
2. **s 19(1)**: where there is a contract for specific goods, or goods are later appropriated, the seller may by
   the terms of the contract or appropriation reserve the right of disposal until conditions are fulfilled; then,
   despite delivery to the buyer, property does not pass until the conditions are fulfilled. The
   obvious condition is payment. ss 17 and 19 together allow ROT clauses (revision guide). An ROT clause is the
   leading example of "contrary intention" displacing s 18 r 1 (*Aluminium Industrie v Romalpa* [1976] 1 WLR 676).
3. CRA s 4(2) applies s 19 to consumer contracts too.
4. ROT may be invoked whenever the buyer is in default, not only on insolvency. The seller bears the burden of
   identifying and proving title to the goods, not the administrators (*Blue Monkey Gaming v Hudson* (2014)).
5. Some clauses, as charges, must be registered under **Companies Act 2006 s 859A** or are void against a
   liquidator/administrator and creditors (lecture 5; earlier law: Companies Act 1948 s 95).

### 2. Types of clause
| Type | Content | Usual result |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | title stays with seller until this price is paid | effective; recover unaltered goods |
| All monies / current account | title stays until all sums owed are paid | effective, not a charge (*Armour v Thyssen*) |
| Mixed / manufactured goods | seller claims products made with its goods | usually fails: identity lost, or a registrable charge |
| Proceeds | seller claims resale proceeds | usually fails: charge, or no fiduciary relationship |

### 3. Leading cases
- **Aluminium Industrie Vaassen v Romalpa Aluminium** [1976] 1 WLR 676 (CA): Dutch seller supplied aluminium foil
  on credit under an extensive clause (clause 13): ownership would pass only when all that was owing was paid;
  products made with the foil would be the seller's property as surety for full payment; the buyer would keep such
  articles as "fiduciary owner" and could sell them in the normal course of business. The buyer went into
  receivership owing more than £120,000, with about £50,000 of foil in stock and about £35,000 in a separate
  account representing resold foil. The CA allowed the seller to recover the unsold foil and the £35,000 by
  equitable tracing, on the basis of a fiduciary/bailment relationship. Crucially, the buyer's counsel conceded the
  fiduciary relationship and that it held goods as bailee.
- **Re Bond Worth** [1980] Ch 228: fibre sold for carpet manufacture; clause retaining "equitable and beneficial
  ownership" while legal property passed - this created a floating equitable charge granted by the buyer, void for
  non-registration.
- **Borden (UK) v Scottish Timber Products** [1981] Ch 25: resin used to make chipboard lost its identity once used in
  manufacture, so the seller's claim failed.
- **Re Peachdart** [1984] Ch 131: leather made into handbags; title could be reserved in the leather, but once made
  into handbags it lost its identity; any interest in the handbags or proceeds was an unregistered charge, so void
  against the receiver and debenture holder.
- **Hendy Lennox v Grahame Puttick** [1984] 1 WLR 485: diesel engines incorporated into generating sets remained
  engines, identifiable by serial numbers and removable with relative ease - recoverable. (The Johnson article also
  cites it as finding fiduciary obligations incompatible with the buyer's freedom to mix goods and funds.)
- **Clough Mill v Martin** [1985] 1 WLR 111 (CA): a simple reservation of title in
  goods sold creates no registrable charge; but a claim to interests in manufactured products could create a charge
  (not decided on the facts). The issue of a "windfall" if the seller could keep goods worth more than the debt is
  discussed.
- **Re Andrabell** [1984] 3 All ER 407: proceeds claim rejected where fiduciary obligations were not
  established (the Davies and Johnson articles say they must be explicit, not implied).
- **Armour v Thyssen Edelstahlwerke** [1991] 2 AC 339 (HL): an all-monies clause did not create a
  security right granted by the buyer; Lord Keith: it gives the seller security "by way of a legitimate retention of
  title, not by virtue of any right over his own property conferred by the buyer". The revision guide notes the HL
  did not consider whether a seller could recover goods already paid for.
- **Proceeds claims void as charges**: *E Pfeiffer v Arbuthnot Factors* [1988] 1 WLR 150; *Compaq Computer v
  Abercorn* [1991]; *Re Weldtech* (1991); *Tatung (UK) v Galex Telesure* (1989) 5 BCC 325 (Phillips J suggested
  *Romalpa* was wrongly decided and the interest should have been a charge); *Re Highway Foods* [1995] 1 BCLC 209.
- **Modelboard v Outer Box** (1993): cardboard sheets made into boxes lost identity.
- **FG Wilson (Engineering) v John Holt & Co** (CA, 2014 per the revision guide): because title was retained, property had not
  passed, so the seller could not sue for the price under s 49(1), which the court treated as exclusive - ironic for
  a clause meant to protect the seller. Advice: make the price payable on a day certain (s 49(2)). the materials materials also refer to *Caterpillar (NI) v John Holt* [2013], described as upholding
  ROT clauses on an issue under s 40 SGA.
- **PST Energy 7 Shipping v OW Bunker Malta (The Res Cogitans)** [2016] UKSC: bunkers supplied on 60-day credit
  with ROT and liberty to consume before payment; the contract was not a contract of sale under s 2(1) (sui generis:
  permission to consume plus transfer of any unconsumed bunkers on payment), so s 49 did not bar recovery of the
  price; the only implied term was that the supplier was entitled to give permission to consume. Lord Mance said
  that had it been a sale he would have overruled *FG Wilson*: s 49 is not a complete code for price claims. *Wood
  v TUI* refused to extend the reasoning to food served at an all-inclusive hotel.
- Other cases listed for research: *Re Hallett's Estate* (1880) (tracing); *Specialist Plant Services v Braithwaite*
  [1987] BCLC 1; *Len Vidgen Ski & Leisure v Timaru Marine* [1986] 1 NZLR 349; *Associated Alloys v CAN 001 452
  106* (2000) 202 CLR 558 (Australia); *CKE Engineering* [2007] BCC 975; *Angara Maritime v OceanConnect* [2011] 1
  Lloyd's Rep 61; *The Saetta* [1994] 1 WLR 1334; *Chaigley Farms v Crawford Kaye* [1996] BCC 957; *Re Weddell NZ*
  (1996); *Pongakawa Sawmill v NZ Forest Products* [1992] 3 NZLR 304; *Carlos Federspiel v Twigg*.

### 4. Limits and third parties
6. Even a valid clause cannot defeat a sub-buyer who takes good title under s 25 SGA / s 9 Factors Act (buyer in
   possession) - see the nemo dat notes. The slides note that where the buyer under ROT transforms goods (timber into
   furniture, resin into chipboard, aluminium into wheels) property usually passes to the buyer.
7. ROT differs from sale or return: under ROT the buyer is committed to buy; Rule 4 does not apply to
   unascertained goods.
8. Risk: under ROT the buyer usually bears risk even without property - an express departure from
   s 20(1).

## Debate and critique
- **Romalpa as "external shock" (Davey & Kelly, "Romalpa and contractual innovation" (2015) 42 JLS 358).** Using
  network theory, they argue *Romalpa* - together with the Brentford Nylons insolvency (suppliers asserting ROT over
  raw materials) and the late-1970s recession - was a tipping point that turned ROT from a rare term into standard
  boilerplate, displacing debenture holders and changing insolvency's "rules of the game". Phase 1 (pre-1974) ROT
  was rare and buyers financed through floating charges; phase 2 saw widespread experimentation, led first by
  smaller firms. Its Dutch origin gave it legal "otherness". Goode called it the case with the greatest impact on
  the commercial world that century and suppliers began stamping "Romalpa" on invoices; Spencer's late-1980s study
  found 59% of surveyed businesses used ROT, many in sophisticated forms; Wheeler stressed enforcement depends on
  bargaining power; De Lacy argued the revolution was overstated given the later retreat; Worthington: ROT fails
  where "the substance of the parties' agreement triumphs over the form".
- **Still an enigma (W Davies (2006) 4(2) Hertfordshire LJ 2).** The Cork Report (1982) described post-*Romalpa* case
  law as a "maze, if not the minefield" and proceeds claims as a "complete waste of time". Diamond highlighted the
  lack of a unified concept of security interests and proposed registration of proceeds clauses; Goode, Cooper and
  Panesar criticise reliance on fiduciary relationships in arm's-length commerce; McCormack calls the case law
  "redolent of formalism". The Law Commission (2004) proposed registration. Germany enforces ROT and proceeds
  clauses without registration or fiduciary relationship; the US treats ROT as a purchase money security interest
  under UCC Art 9, requiring filing.
- **Uniform solution (R B Johnson (1994) 12 Berkeley J Int'l L 99).** English law's ownership-based, fiduciary-based
  approach is confused; Hicks called *Romalpa* a "revolutionary development" since curtailed by recharacterisation
  as charges; Goode and Hicks support a UCC-style security interest model focusing on priority and filing rather
  than ownership.
- **Why fiduciary proceeds claims fail (revision guide):** the concession in *Romalpa* will not be repeated; a
  separate proceeds account is commercially unrealistic because buyers need sale money to run the business; a true
  fiduciary would have to keep goods separate; a fiduciary claim would give the seller all proceeds - possibly more
  than the debt - and if limited to the debt it looks like a charge needing registration.
- **Policy tension.** ROT gives "hidden" security that disadvantages other unsecured creditors and floating charge
  holders, yet registration of every ROT sale would be impracticable and a registered ROT would rank behind prior
  charges. Simple ROT is accepted because it merely stops title passing; anything that looks like the buyer granting
  rights over its own property is treated as a charge (substance over form).
- **Unintended consequences.** *FG Wilson* (no price action) and *Res Cogitans* (contract outside the SGA, losing
  implied terms) show that aggressive drafting can backfire.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was the ROT clause incorporated into the contract (battle of forms, notice)?
2. Construe it: simple, all monies, mixed/manufactured goods, proceeds?
3. Simple/all-monies: are the original goods still identifiable and in the buyer's possession? If so, the seller
   can recover them (s 19; *Armour v Thyssen*). Check identification (serial numbers, *Hendy Lennox*) and whether
   they can be removed without damage.
4. Manufactured/mixed goods: has identity been lost (*Borden*, *Peachdart*, *Bond Worth*, *Modelboard*)? If the
   clause gives the seller an interest in the new product, it is likely a charge granted by the buyer - registered
   under CA 2006 s 859A? If not, void against the administrator/liquidator.
5. Proceeds: is there a genuine fiduciary relationship (separate account, duty to account, no freedom to use
   money)? Usually not (*Andrabell*, *Pfeiffer*, *Compaq*); if limited to the debt it is a charge.
6. Third parties: has a sub-buyer acquired title under s 25 SGA / s 9 FA 1889?
7. Price action: if property has not passed, can the seller sue for the price (s 49(2) day certain; *FG Wilson*
   v the *Res Cogitans* dicta)?
8. Does the clause or liberty to consume take the deal outside s 2(1) (*Res Cogitans*)?
Traps: claiming finished goods without considering identity and registration; assuming *Romalpa* proceeds reasoning
still works; forgetting buyer-in-possession sub-sales.
