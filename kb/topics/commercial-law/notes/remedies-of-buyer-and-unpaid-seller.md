# Remedies of the buyer and of the unpaid seller (SGA 1979; CRA 2015)

## Overview
When a sale goes wrong each side has a menu of remedies. The buyer may claim damages, seek specific performance or reject
the goods (with rejection limited by the rules on acceptance); consumer buyers instead use the tiered scheme in the Consumer
Rights Act 2015 (CRA). The seller who has not been paid has personal remedies (action for the price, damages) and real
remedies against the goods (lien, stoppage in transit, resale). Which remedy is available frequently turns on whether
property has passed.

## The law

### A. Buyer's remedies (business sales, SGA 1979)
1. **Damages for non-delivery (s 51).** Reflects the first limb of *Hadley v Baxendale* (1854) 9 Exch 341: loss arising
   naturally, or reasonably in the contemplation of both parties at contracting. Once loss is not too remote: if there is an
   available market, damages = market price less contract price; if not, the s 51(2) approach applies and depends heavily on
   the purpose for which the goods were bought.
2. **Late delivery.** *Victoria Laundry v Newman* [1949] 2 KB 528 is cited.
3. **Breach of warranty (s 53)** and **consequential loss:** *Godley v Perry* [1960] 1 All ER 36; *Parsons v Uttley Ingham*
   [1978] QB 791.
4. **Specific performance (SGA s 52 per the CRA disapplication list; one heading cites s 51).** Authorities: *Re
   Wait* [1927] 1 Ch 606; *Sky Petroleum v VIP Petroleum* [1974] 1 All ER 954; *Société des Industries Métallurgiques SA v
   Bronx Engineering* [1975] 1 Lloyd's Rep 465.
5. **Rejection.** Questions: when may the buyer reject (note s 15A and s 11(2) qualifications), with what consequences, and how.
   - **Acceptance (s 35).** Deemed on (a) intimating acceptance, or (b) after delivery doing an act inconsistent with the
     seller's ownership; but not until the buyer has had a reasonable opportunity to examine the goods (to check conformity
     and, in a sample sale, compare bulk with sample) (s 35(2)). A consumer cannot lose s 35(2) by agreement or waiver
     (s 35(3)). Also deemed after a reasonable time's retention without intimating rejection (s 35(4)-(5)). Asking for or
     agreeing to repair, or a sub-sale delivered to another, is not itself acceptance (s 35(6)).
   - **Cases on acceptance:** *Kwei Tek Chao v British Traders and Shippers* [1954] 2 QB 459; *Armaghdown Motors v Gray*
     [1963] NZLR 5; *Jones v Gallagher* [2004] EWCA Civ 10; *Bernstein v Pamson Motors* [1987] 2 All ER 220; *Truk (UK) v
     Tokmakidis* [2000] 1 Lloyd's Rep 543; *Clegg v Anderson* [2003] EWCA Civ 320; *JH Ritchie Ltd v Lloyd Ltd* [2007] UKHL 9
     (no holdings are given).
   - **Partial rejection:** s 35A. **Instalment contracts:** *Maple Flock v Universal Furniture Products* [1934] 1 KB 148;
     *Munro v Meyer* [1930] 2 KB 312.
   - Link to s 11(4): once goods are accepted in a non-severable contract, breach of condition is treated as breach of warranty.

### B. Consumer buyer's remedies (CRA 2015)
The CRA revoked the Sale and Supply of Goods to Consumers Regulations 2002 and disapplied SGA ss 35, 51, 52 and 53 for
consumer contracts (replaced by CRA s 19 and following).
1. **s 19(3)** (breach of the terms in ss 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, or non-conformity under s 16): (a) short-term right to reject
   (ss 20, 22); (b) right to repair or replacement (s 23); (c) right to price reduction or the final right to reject
   (ss 20, 24).
2. **s 19(4)** (installation under s 15 or breach of stated requirements): repair or replacement, then price reduction or
   final right to reject.
3. **s 19(5)** (s 12 information): recover costs incurred up to the price paid. **s 19(6)** (s 17(1) right to supply): right
   to reject.
4. **Other remedies (s 19(9)-(11))**: damages, specific performance, relying on the breach against a price claim, and (for
   express terms) treating the contract as at an end remain available, but not so as to recover twice. It is not open to
   treat the contract as ended for breach of the implied terms except as the Chapter allows (s 19(12)).
5. **Six-month presumption (s 19(14)-(15))** (ONLY for the s 19(3)(b)-(c) remedies: repair/replacement, price reduction, final right to reject; it does NOT apply to the short-term right to reject, where non-conformity at delivery must be shown): goods not conforming within six months of
   delivery are taken not to have conformed on delivery, unless shown otherwise or incompatible with the nature of the goods.
6. Related: s 21 (partial rejection), s 25 (wrong quantity), s 26 (instalments), s 28 (delivery time). Digital content:
   ss 42-46 (repair or replacement, price reduction, refund, damage to device).

### C. Unpaid seller's remedies (SGA)
**Definition (s 38(1)).** Seller is unpaid when the whole price has not been paid or tendered, or when a bill or negotiable
instrument taken as conditional payment is dishonoured.

**Personal remedies**
1. **Action for the price (s 49).** (1) Where property has passed and the buyer wrongfully refuses or neglects to pay. (2)
   Where the price is payable on a "day certain" irrespective of delivery, even if property has not passed. "Day certain"
   means a time specified that does not depend on a future or contingent event (*Shell-Mex v Elton Cop Dyeing* (1928) 34 Com
   Cas 39; *Workman, Clark v Lloyd Brazileño* [1908] 1 KB 968 for a specified stage of construction). It is a claim in debt,
   so the damages rules do not bite: the seller proves only own performance, need not prove loss, remoteness and penalty rules
   are irrelevant, there is no duty to mitigate, and summary judgment is often available. Property can pass before delivery
   (for example under FOB terms), and the seller can then sue for the price if ready and willing to deliver (*Maclean v Dunn
   and Watkins* (1828) 4 Bing NC 722), or alternatively for non-acceptance damages under s 50.
2. **Damages for non-acceptance (s 50)**: the estimated loss directly and naturally resulting in the ordinary course of
   events. Usual remedy where property has not passed. Also s 54 (consequential loss) and **s 37** (buyer's failure to take
   delivery within a reasonable time after request: liable for loss and a reasonable charge for care and custody).

**Real remedies (ss 38-39):** lien, stoppage in transit, resale (*RV Ward v Bignall* [1967] 1 QB 534).
1. **Lien (ss 41-43).** Right to retain possession until payment or tender: where goods sold without stipulation as to
   credit; where credit has expired; or where the buyer is insolvent (s 41(1)); *Hammonds v Barclay* (1802) 2 East 227. Part
   delivery leaves a lien on the rest unless it shows waiver (s 42). Lost (s 43) on delivery to a carrier for transmission
   without reserving the right of disposal, when the buyer or his agent lawfully takes possession, or by waiver.
2. **Stoppage in transit (ss 44-46).** Where the buyer becomes insolvent, the seller who has parted with possession may
   resume possession while the goods are in transit and retain them until payment. Transit runs from delivery to a carrier or
   bailee until the buyer or agent takes delivery (s 45(1)). Exercised by taking possession or giving notice to the carrier
   or bailee (s 46), effective notice to a principal needs timing allowing reasonable diligence to inform the agent.
3. **Resale (s 48).** Distinguish *power* to resell (pass title) from *right* to resell (no liability to the first buyer).
   - Power: when the seller still holds property (owner); under s 24 (seller in possession exception to nemo dat; first
     buyer may sue for non-delivery and in tort); or where the seller has exercised lien or stoppage, in which case the second
     buyer gets good title against the original buyer (s 48(2)).
   - Right: statutory where goods are perishable, or the seller gives notice of intention to resell and the buyer does not pay
     or tender within a reasonable time, allowing damages for loss (s 48(3)); or where the seller expressly reserved a right
     of resale on default, in which case resale rescinds the original contract without prejudice to damages (s 48(4)).

## Key authorities
- *Hadley v Baxendale*; *Victoria Laundry v Newman*; *Godley v Perry*; *Parsons v Uttley Ingham* (damages).
- *Re Wait*; *Sky Petroleum*; *Bronx Engineering* (specific performance).
- *Kwei Tek Chao*; *Bernstein v Pamson*; *Clegg v Anderson*; *JH Ritchie v Lloyd* (acceptance).
- *Maclean v Dunn and Watkins*; *Shell-Mex v Elton Cop*; *Workman Clark* (action for the price).
- *RV Ward v Bignall* [1967] 1 QB 534: two cars sold for £850; buyer refused the second (misrepresented age) and offered £500
  for one; seller resold one car and sued for the price. Held: the right of resale rescinded the contract and revested
  property in the seller, so only damages for non-delivery were available.
- *Compagnie de Renflouement v W Seymour Plant Sales* [1981] 2 Lloyd's Rep 466: buyer's several breaches individually did not
  justify termination, but threatening not to take the remaining pipes unless they were cut differently was repudiatory.
- *Hammonds v Barclay* (lien).

## Debate and critique
Reading: Apps, "The Right to Cure Defective Performance" [1994] LMCLQ 525 (seller's right to cure against the
buyer's right to reject). Discussion points from the structure: (i) the action for the price gives the seller significant
advantages over damages (no mitigation, remoteness or penalty control), which is why the property-passing rules and the "day
certain" limit matter so much; (ii) the real remedies give the unpaid seller a form of security against an insolvent buyer,
in tension with the buyer's other creditors; (iii) the CRA replaced a single right to reject with a tiered scheme that pushes
consumers towards repair or replacement first.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Who breached? Seller (buyer remedies) or buyer (unpaid seller remedies)?
2. Consumer or business? Apply CRA s 19 tiers or the SGA.
3. Buyer rejection: was the term breached a condition (ss 13-15)? Has the buyer accepted (s 35: examination opportunity,
   inconsistent act, lapse of reasonable time)? Is it a slight breach (s 15A) or an instalment contract?
4. Damages: market or no market; remoteness (*Hadley*); consequential loss.
5. Unpaid seller: has property passed? If yes, action for the price (or non-acceptance damages). If not, is there a day
   certain? Otherwise damages (s 50).
6. Real remedies: still in possession (lien: is buyer insolvent or credit expired)? In transit (stoppage: insolvency
   required)? Resale: was it by right (perishable, notice, express reservation) and what is its effect on the original contract?

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: citing CRA 2015 s 19(15) for the six-month presumption. The presumption is s 19(14); s 19(15) displaces it (trader proves conformity, or incompatible with goods/defect). It applies only to s 23 and s 24 remedies, not the short-term right to reject. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q09#1]
- Common error: advising a trader to offer repair to forestall rejection. Under CRA 2015 the consumer chooses; within 30 days a repair offer cannot defeat the short-term right to reject (ss 20, 22). A consumer's repair request suspends the period (s 22(6)). [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q09#3]
- Common error: citing CRA 2015 s 19(3)(b)–(c) for when price reduction/final rejection arise. s 19(3) only lists tiers; triggers are s 24(5): one failed repair or replacement, neither available (s 23(3)), or trader failing to repair/replace within a reasonable time without significant inconvenience. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1301/q10#1]
- Common error: assuming any card or PayPal payment gives a CCA 1974 s 75 claim. s 75 needs a regulated credit agreement (e.g. credit card), cash price over £100 up to £30,000; not debit cards, usually not PayPal. Chargeback is separate, non-statutory, own time limits. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-22-1855/q03#1]
