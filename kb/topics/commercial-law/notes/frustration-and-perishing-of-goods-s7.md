# Post-Contractual Loss of Goods and Frustration (s 7 SGA)

## Overview
When goods are lost or destroyed after the contract is made, two linked doctrines decide who bears the loss:
**risk** (whose loss is it?) and **frustration** (are both parties released?). Section 7 SGA provides a
statutory frustration rule for agreements to sell specific goods that perish before risk passes. Outside s 7,
the common law of frustration applies (preserved by s 62(2)), and the consequences then depend on whether
the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 applies - it does not apply to s 7 cases or to contracts for
specific goods frustrated by perishing. The revision lecture frames frustration as arising without fault,
discharging both parties (not making the contract void ab initio), usually raised as a defence. Law: England
& Wales.

## The law

### 1. Risk first
1. s 20(1): unless otherwise agreed, risk passes with property (res perit domino); s 20(2) fault (*Demby
   Hamilton v Barden*); s 20(3) bailee duties; ss 32-33 transit; CRA s 29 for consumers; s 20A co-ownership of
   a bulk. Risk may remain with the seller after property passes (*Head v Tattersall* - the slides draw the
   principle that risk stays with the seller while the buyer has a right of rejection) or pass before property
   (*Sterns v Vickers* - quasi-specific goods from an identified bulk). Under CIF contracts property may pass
   while the seller insures in transit; under FOB the buyer takes risk on shipment.
2. *Mash & Murrell v Joseph Emmanuel*: in a CIF contract the implied quality condition involved a continuing
   obligation that the goods remain in proper condition for a reasonable time after shipment - so deterioration
   in transit may still be the seller's responsibility. Under s 33 the buyer bears only deterioration
   necessarily incident to transit.
3. If goods are destroyed after risk passes, the buyer must pay though the seller cannot deliver; the slides
   note that today the goods will usually be insured, and if the buyer pays the seller may hold the insurance
   proceeds as constructive trustee for the buyer.
4. **Executory contracts** (slide 4): if goods perish at the seller's risk, the buyer need not pay but the
   seller may be liable for non-delivery unless excused by frustration; if at the buyer's risk, the buyer pays
   and may owe damages for non-acceptance unless discharged by frustration.

### 2. Section 7
"Where there is an agreement to sell specific goods and subsequently the goods, without any fault on the part
of the seller or buyer, perish before the risk passes to the buyer, the agreement is avoided." Requirements:
5. An **agreement to sell** (property not yet passed);
6. **Specific goods** (s 61);
7. The goods **perish** (same meaning as s 6: *Asfar v Blundell*, *Horn v Minister of Food*, *Barrow Lane* -
   see s 6 note);
8. **Without fault** of either party;
9. **Before risk passes** to the buyer.
If met, the agreement is avoided (= frustrated): the seller bears the loss of the goods, the buyer need not
pay, and the seller is not liable for non-delivery. Because in a sale of specific goods property and risk
usually pass together (s 18 r 1), s 7 typically bites where property is postponed, e.g. s 18 r 2 (seller must
do something to put goods in a deliverable state). The revision guide adds that frustration may also arise
where performance becomes illegal (*Avery v Bowden* (1855)).
10. **After risk passes, no frustration.** An executed contract cannot be frustrated by perishing (*Re Shipton
    Anderson and Harrison Bros*): if property and risk have passed, or risk passed before property, the loss
    falls on the buyer, who must pay.
11. **Three possible constructions** (slides): (a) implied condition that perishing discharges both; (b) the
    seller promised the goods would not perish (seller liable for non-delivery); (c) the buyer promised to bear
    the loss (buyer pays and may be liable in damages). s 7 is a default and can be excluded (*McRae*).
12. **Partial perishing**: *Barrow Lane v Phillips* - an unseverable contract is frustrated by perishing of
    part; a severable one only as to the part perished. *Sainsbury v Street* [1972] 3 All ER 1127: where only
    part of a crop is produced, the seller may be obliged to offer the remainder to the buyer (MacKenna J:
    seller not liable in damages for the shortfall but had to deliver what was harvested; 275 tons of barley
    from a named farm, only 140 tons grown and sold elsewhere by the seller).

### 3. Common law frustration
13. Definition: "a fundamentally different situation from that originally envisaged" (Viscount Simon,
    *British Movietonews v London and District Cinemas* [1952] AC 166, 185).
14. Lord Simon's elements in *National Carriers v Panalpina* [1981] AC 675 (HL): (i) a supervening event that
    "significantly changes the nature (not merely the expense or onerousness)" of outstanding rights; (ii) no
    fault of either party; (iii) not reasonably contemplated at the time of contracting; (iv) unjust to hold
    the parties to the contract.
15. **Unascertained goods.** Purely generic goods cannot be frustrated by destruction - a whole species does
    not perish; the seller must find other goods:
    - *Blackburn Bobbin v TW Allen* [1918] 1 KB 540: Finnish timber hard to obtain after WWI broke out; an
      unqualified contract; seller took the risk; no frustration.
    - *CTI Group v Transclear (The Mary Nour)* [2008] EWCA Civ 856 (first instance [2007] EWHC 2070): no
      frustration merely because the supplier did not make goods available; commercial impossibility is not
      enough; the seller could have sought goods elsewhere.
    - *Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl* [1962] AC 93: Suez closure did not frustrate a CIF sale of Sudanese
      groundnuts (unascertained goods) to European buyers.
    - *Intertradex v Lesieur-Tourteaux* [1977] 2 Lloyd's Rep 146: supplier default; no frustration.
    - Exception - **specified source**: *Howell v Coupland* (1876) 1 QBD 258 (CA): 200 tons of Regent potatoes
      from a particular field; disease left 80 tons; the buyer took the 80 and sued for the rest; the seller
      was excused - frustration by crop failure. *Re Badische* [1921] 2 Ch 331 (Russell J): unascertained goods
      that both parties knew could only come from Germany - frustrated by the outbreak of war; a rare case.
16. **Goods to be made**: *Appleby v Myers* (1867) LR 2 CP 651: machinery being erected on premises, payment on
    completion; fire destroyed building and machinery; frustrated, and nothing recoverable for work done
    because payment was due only on completion.
17. **Force majeure clauses** (from French law) are express contractual allocations of events beyond the
    parties' control (government restrictions, strikes etc.) allowing cancellation or excuse; unlike
    frustration, which operates by law regardless of the parties' wishes, their effect is what the parties
    agreed.

### 4. Consequences of frustration
18. **Which regime?** Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 s 2(5)(c) excludes (i) any contract to which
    s 7 SGA applies and (ii) any other contract for the sale of specific goods frustrated by the goods
    perishing. The second limb is unclear; the slides give three readings (property and risk both passed;
    risk passed but not property; property passed but goods at seller's risk) and conclude that the Act does
    not apply in each. So for s 7 cases (and specific goods frustrated by perishing) the **common law**
    consequences apply. The Act applies to all contracts for unascertained goods and to specific goods
    frustrated by events other than perishing. Parties' own provisions prevail over the Act (s 2(3)).
19. **Common law consequences**:
    - Both parties are discharged from obligations not yet accrued at the time of the frustrating event; the
      contract is not void ab initio.
    - *Chandler v Webster* (cited in the handout as [1904] AC 32): the loss lay where it fell -
      parties discharged only from future obligations.
    - *Fibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v Fairbairn Lawson Combe Barbour* [1943] AC 32: £1,000 paid in advance for
      machinery; war frustrated the contract before delivery; total failure of consideration, so the payment
      was recoverable, with no set-off for the seller's expenses.
    - Recovery requires a *total* failure of consideration; partial failure is not enough (slide example: 100
      tons of wheat in 10-ton instalments, one instalment delivered, rest destroyed - s 7 applies, the 1943 Act
      does not, and prepayment for the undelivered part is not recoverable at common law because the failure is
      only partial).
    - Entire contracts rule: no payment for part performance of an indivisible obligation without advance
      payment (*Appleby v Myers*). Other cases listed: *Logan v Le Mesurier* (1847); *Whincup v Hughes* (1871);
      *Tyrie v Fletcher* (1777); *Biggerstaff v Rowatt's Wharf* [1896] 2 Ch 93.
    - If goods were delivered, restitution may require their return.
20. **Under the 1943 Act**:
    - s 1(2): money paid is recoverable and sums payable cease to be payable, even on partial failure; the
      court may let the payee keep or recover sums up to its expenses incurred for performance if just.
    - s 1(3): a party who obtained a valuable benefit (other than money) before discharge must pay a just sum
      not exceeding the benefit.
    - *Gamerco v ICM* [1995] 1 WLR 1226 (advance payments under s 1(2)); *BP Exploration v Hunt* [1982] 1 All ER
      925 (just sum under s 1(3)).

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 7, 20, 32-33, 62(2); LR(FC)A 1943 ss 1(2), 1(3), 2(3), 2(5)(c).
- *National Carriers v Panalpina* [1981] AC 675 - elements of frustration.
- *Howell v Coupland* (1876) 1 QBD 258 - specified crop failed: frustration.
- *Sainsbury v Street* [1972] 3 All ER 1127 - partial crop failure; offer what is produced.
- *Barrow Lane v Phillips* [1929] 1 KB 574 - severable v unseverable partial loss.
- *Blackburn Bobbin v Allen* [1918] 1 KB 540; *CTI v Transclear* [2008] EWCA Civ 856; *Tsakiroglou* [1962]
  AC 93 - no frustration for generic goods.
- *Re Badische* [1921] 2 Ch 331 - exceptional frustration of unascertained goods from a known source.
- *Fibrosa* [1943] AC 32; *Chandler v Webster* [1904] - common law consequences.
- *Appleby v Myers* (1867) LR 2 CP 651 - entire contracts.
- *Mash & Murrell v Joseph Emmanuel* - continuing quality obligation in transit.

## Debate and critique
- **Narrowness of s 7.** It needs specific goods, perishing and risk not yet passed. Most commercial sales are
  of unascertained goods and fall to the common law, which almost never frustrates sales of generic goods. The
  revision study materials the SGA rules apply only to specific goods; common law applies to unascertained goods
  or where parties so intend.
- **Unhappy interaction with the 1943 Act.** s 2(5)(c) leaves s 7 cases under the harsh common law (total
  failure only; no allowance for expenses; no recovery for benefits). Thus the regime for the paradigm
  sale-of-goods frustration case is the least developed one. The slides call the second limb of s 2(5)(c)
  unclear ("?!?!"). The revision guide notes *Fibrosa* improved on *Chandler v Webster* but left problems
  (partial failure; payee's expenses) which only the 1943 Act addresses.
- **Howell v Coupland v Sainsbury v Street.** Both are crop failures but one excuses the seller entirely, the
  other requires the seller to offer what was grown; the revision guide reconciles them by asking what the
  parties would have intended had they thought about it - in *Sainsbury* they would likely have given the
  buyer the option of the reduced quantity.
- **Risk and frustration as two separate tools.** Todd's doctoral thesis (1981) analyses perishing, risk and
  frustration separately and then their relationship, and asks whether a sale may be frustrated even after
  property, risk or both have passed - contrary to the orthodox slide position that executed contracts cannot
  be frustrated.
- **Theory of frustration.** A Liverpool Law Review (2022) article in the materials offers a unified account
  of frustration and its remedy in the light of COVID-19, describing doctrinal confusion about what happens
  after a contract is frustrated; useful context for the "loss lies where it falls" critique. Eisenberg
  (secondary materials) proposes relief based on shared tacit assumptions, with restitution so the excused
  party does not keep benefits.
- **Force majeure as the commercial answer.** Because frustration is rare and its consequences crude, parties
  draft force majeure clauses; this supports the view that frustration in sale of goods is a residual
  doctrine.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. When did the loss occur - before or after the contract? Before: s 6 note. After: here.
2. Has risk passed (s 20(1); fault s 20(2); bailee s 20(3); transit ss 32-33; CRA s 29; contractual terms such
   as FOB/CIF)? If risk is on the buyer, no frustration: buyer pays.
3. If risk is still with the seller: are the goods specific, has property not passed (agreement to sell), and
   have the goods "perished" without fault? If yes, s 7 avoids the agreement.
4. If not s 7 (e.g. unascertained goods): is there common law frustration (*Panalpina* elements)? Generic goods -
   almost never (*Blackburn Bobbin*, *CTI*, *Tsakiroglou*); specified source - possibly (*Howell v Coupland*;
   *Re Badische*); partial failure - offer what exists (*Sainsbury v Street*).
5. Check for a force majeure clause or contrary intention.
6. Consequences: s 7 or specific goods perishing - common law (total failure needed; *Fibrosa*); otherwise the
   1943 Act (s 1(2) payments and expenses; s 1(3) valuable benefit).
7. Severable v entire contract on partial loss (*Barrow Lane*).
Traps: applying the 1943 Act to a s 7 case; finding frustration because performance became expensive;
forgetting that the buyer bears the loss once risk has passed.
