# Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1897] AC 22 ([1896] UKHL 1) before citing
> speeches. The sources disagree on the decision date and the full bench, so those are stated cautiously.

## Overview
House of Lords, on appeal from the Court of Appeal, reported [1897] AC 22. One source gives the date of judgment as 16
November 1896 (consistent with the neutral citation [1896] UKHL 1) while another gives 1897, so the year is not
settled; cite it as [1897] AC 22. Lord Halsbury LC, Lord Herschell and Lord Macnaghten are named by all the sources;
other members of the bench are named inconsistently (the sources variously add Lord Davey and others). The House held
unanimously that a company validly incorporated is a legal person distinct from its members, even where one person
holds almost all the shares and controls it.

## Facts
Aron Salomon ran a successful boot and shoe business as a sole trader. In 1892 he turned it into a limited company, A
Salomon & Co Ltd. He sold the business to the company, taking 20,001 of the 20,007 shares issued, together with £10,000 in debentures secured over the company's assets. He
had also borrowed money from a lender (Mr Broderip), secured on the debentures. The company later failed and was
wound up. The secured debts left almost nothing for the unsecured creditors, and the liquidator claimed that Salomon
should indemnify the company for its debts and that the sale to the company should be set aside.

## Held
- **First instance.** Vaughan Williams J accepted that the company was Salomon's agent (an alias) and that he had to
  indemnify it.
- **Court of Appeal.** The court reached the same result on a different basis: the company was a trustee for Salomon
  and the scheme was a device to defraud creditors, since the Companies Act contemplated independent shareholders
  and not a person who dominated the company.
- **House of Lords.** The House unanimously reversed. The company was validly formed under the Companies Act 1862, and
  was a legal person separate from its members, so it was not Salomon's agent or trustee. The Act did not impose any
  limit on how shares could be held, so the courts could not add a requirement of independent members. Salomon was
  therefore not liable for the company's debts, and as a secured creditor he took priority over the unsecured
  creditors. The company's cross-appeal to set the sale aside was dismissed: the sources report that the
  shareholders had knowingly approved the terms and that no fraud on creditors was established.

## Effect
- The orthodox statement of **separate legal personality**: the company's assets and debts are its own, and members
  are liable only as the statute provides (here, limited liability).
- Later exceptions (fraud, sham) have been recognised, including by the Supreme Court, but the basic rule stands.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Check whether the company was validly incorporated. If so, treat the company and its owner or controller as
   different persons for contracts, property and liability.
2. A controlling shareholder or director is not personally liable for the company's debts merely because of control,
   and may lawfully be a secured creditor of his own company.
3. Ask whether any exception applies (fraud or sham used to evade an existing legal obligation, or a statute that
   imposes liability), then say that mere undercapitalisation or domination is not enough.
4. Remember that the principle protects creditors only to the extent that the law (insolvency rules, wrongful trading,
   directors' duties) imposes duties on the directors.
