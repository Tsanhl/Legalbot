# Introduction to Commercial Law

## Overview
English commercial law has no single code. It is identified by its subject matter (sale of goods, agency,
negotiable instruments, security interests, carriage, international sales, arbitration and so on) and by a
set of recurring principles that reflect what the business community needs: certainty, respect for the
bargain, clear security rules and recognition of trade custom. This subject (England & Wales)
builds on Contract Law and focuses mainly on business-to-business sales under the Sale of Goods Act
1979 (SGA), with some coverage of consumer sales under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA). This first
topic gives the historical and conceptual frame (the law merchant, its absorption into the common law,
the 1893 codification) and the principles you should use to explain *why* the courts and Parliament take
the approach they do in every later topic. It is also essay material: whether English commercial law
should be codified (Arden) is a classic question.

## The law
There are few hard rules here; the content is historical and conceptual. Key propositions:

1. **What commercial law is.** It is described as the rules and principles applying between
   merchants in relationships that are purely commercial, covering rights and duties arising from the
   supply of goods and services by way of trade. Whether a transaction is commercial depends on its
   nature, not on who the person is: a merchant who buys a TV for household use makes a consumer
   transaction. It is broader than sale of goods: commercial agency, negotiable instruments (bills of
   exchange, bills of lading, cheques, promissory notes), secured financing (fixed and floating charges),
   conditional sale, hire-purchase, leasing, guarantees, factoring, carriage by sea, rail or air, conflict
   of laws, international sales and arbitration.
2. **No English commercial code.** Unlike other countries, England has no commercial code and no separate
   body of "commercial law"; we recognise it by subject matter and recurring themes. Commentary stresses
   that the rules should take a *transactional* rather than a purely bilateral contractual approach, because
   commercial dealings often involve chains of parties (e.g. sub-buyers, financiers, carriers).
3. **Lex mercatoria (law merchant).** Customary law of medieval European merchants, applied in special
   courts where judge and jury were merchants; it existed because trade was international and conducted
   at fairs and markets, producing reasonably uniform rules (merchant federations such as the Hanseatic
   League are mentioned). Its principles differed from the common law: bills of exchange and bills of
   lading come from the law merchant, whereas the rule that past consideration is no consideration is a
   common law rule.
4. **Absorption into the common law.** 15th-century statutes confined merchant courts to the market and
   fairground; in the 17th century Coke LCJ brought all law under the common law courts, which did not
   adopt the law merchant. Because those courts handled specialist merchant disputes poorly, Holt and
   Mansfield LCJJ in the 17th-18th centuries built a coherent commercial law, using special juries of
   merchants and accepting trade usage as evidence. By the 19th century custom and usage were treated as
   part of the contract, representing the parties' intention.
5. **The 1893 synthesis.** The fusion of common law and law merchant was completed at the end of the 19th
   century; the Sale of Goods Act 1893 is its product. The SGA 1979 consolidates the 1893 Act and its
   amendments. SGA 1979 s 62(2) preserves the rules of the common law (including the law merchant) except
   where inconsistent with the Act, which shows that the Act codifies only one part of the law.
6. **20th-century "new lex mercatoria".** International bodies (UNCITRAL, UNIDROIT) produce conventions and
   principles aimed at harmonisation: the Vienna Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods
   (CISG), the Principles of European Contract Law (PECL) and the UNIDROIT Principles of International
   Commercial Contracts. The key aims include understanding the relationship between
   English law and supranational/international sources.
7. **Interpreting a codifying statute.** *Bank of England v Vagliano Bros* [1891] AC 107, 144-5 (Lord
   Herschell): look first at the natural meaning of the statutory words, "uninfluenced by any
   considerations derived from the previous state of the law", instead of trawling the old cases. This is
   qualified for implied terms by *Ashington Piggeries v Christopher Hill* [1972] AC 441 and for the
   satisfactory quality test by *Rogers v Parish* [1987] QB 933 (see `scope-of-sga-and-cra.md`).

### The principles of commercial law
Use these to explain outcomes throughout.

- **Sanctity of contract.** A commercial party is entitled to the benefit of its bargain and to strict
  performance of conditions. Interventionist statutes (Consumer Credit Act 1974, Unfair Contract Terms Act
  1977) qualify this, and commentary notes they are aimed at protection rather than purely commercial
  contexts. Commentators also mention privity: it blocks third-party beneficiaries and fits badly with
  financial leasing, so parties can now insert a clause giving a third party a right of action.
- **Good faith.** Mainly a civil-law idea, rarely found in common law systems, though it appears in the US
  Uniform Commercial Code (first published 1952) alongside "commercial reasonableness" and
  "unconscionability". In English sale of goods law it appears chiefly in the exceptions to nemo dat (good
  faith purchasers).
- **Facilitating security interests.** Clear rules on taking security over a debtor's assets benefit both
  creditors and debtors; secured credit expands lending and so commerce.
- **Conveyancing principles and priorities.** Three principles compete between rival claimants:
  (a) *security of property* - the first-in-time rule (nemo dat quod non habet, and its equitable partner
  qui prior est tempore potior est jure; *Phillips v Phillips* (1861) 4 De G F & J 208, 215: a transferor
  can dispose only of what he is justly entitled to); (b) *the good faith purchaser* - the statutory
  exceptions to nemo dat (mercantile agents, sellers and buyers in possession, private purchasers of motor
  vehicles on hire-purchase); (c) *ostensible ownership / estoppel* - an owner's representation that the
  non-owner may dispose of the goods (s 21 SGA).
- **Usages and customs.** Commercial courts recognise established trade usage; a continuous course of
  dealing may supply implied terms.
- **Market principle.** The market supplies the benchmark for customs, price and damages (market price
  for damages; mitigation rules where there is an available market).

## Key authorities
- *Bank of England v Vagliano Bros* [1891] AC 107: codifying statute to be read by its natural meaning, not
  by presuming the prior law was preserved. Why it matters: the starting point for reading the SGA.
- *Ashington Piggeries Ltd v Christopher Hill Ltd* [1972] AC 441 (Lord Diplock): the implied-term
  provisions should not be construed so narrowly as to impose promises the parties could not reasonably
  have intended. Qualifies *Vagliano*.
- *Rogers v Parish (Scarborough) Ltd* [1987] QB 933: under the reformed quality test only the words of the
  statute need to be considered.
- *Phillips v Phillips* (1861) 4 De G F & J 208: transferor passes only what he is justly entitled to;
  foundation of security of property.
- SGA 1979 s 62(2): common law and law merchant survive save where inconsistent with the Act.
- SGA 1893: the original codification (drafted by Sir Mackenzie Chalmers according to Arden).

## Debate and critique
**Should English commercial law be codified?** Key reading on this debate: Arden,
"Time for an English Commercial Code" (1997) 56 CLJ 516; Bridge, "What is to be done about sale of
goods" (2003) 119 LQR 173; Lord Irvine, "The Law: An Engine for Trade" (2001) 64 MLR 333; Clarke, "Doubts
from the dark side: the case against codes" [2001] JBL 605; Bridge, "The Future of English private
transactional law" (2002) CLP 191; Rodger, "The codification of commercial law in Victorian Britain"
(1992) 108 LQR 570; Goode, "The Codification of Commercial Law" (1988) 14 Monash U L Rev 135; Goode,
"Insularity or Leadership?" (2001) 50 ICLQ 751; Gilmore, "On the Difficulties of Codifying Commercial
Law" (1948) 57 Yale LJ 1341; Hobhouse, "International Conventions and Commercial Law: The Pursuit of
Uniformity" (1990) 106 LQR 539; Weatherill & Vogenauer (2005) on EC competence to harmonise contract law.
The summary below is of Arden\x27s argument.

**Arden (then Chairman of the Law Commission):**
- *What a code is.* At its most extreme, a code states the whole law of a field so that development is
  by interpretation of or deduction from it (French Code civil, German Commercial Code). England has none
  of that kind, but has "less comprehensive codes" - the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, Sale of Goods Act
  1893, Marine Insurance Act 1906, consumer credit legislation - which are systematic rules on a limited
  subject. Codes need not change the law: the 1882 Act largely restated the common law. She contrasts US
  Restatements (non-binding) and the Uniform Commercial Code (model law adopted by nearly all states, with
  an authoritative commentary).
- *History.* Chalmers drafted the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, the Sale of Goods Act 1893 (introduced by
  Lord Herschell, delayed to take account of Scots law) and the Marine Insurance Act 1906. Chalmers
  complained that sale law was buried in cases from Edward III onwards, with a "plethora" of authority on
  some points and none on elementary ones.
- *The objection.* Common lawyers fear codes freeze the law and stifle judicial development (she cites
  Burrows). Her reply: codify only where principles have already emerged from the case law in reasonably
  settled form; there, judges' room for manoeuvre is already limited by precedent, especially in
  commercial law where certainty is prized, and courts keep an interpretive role.
- *Advantages:* (1) accessibility to non-lawyers; (2) quicker, cheaper answers, reducing litigation and
  advice costs (aided by *Vagliano*, which discourages recourse to prior law); (3) the chance to modernise
  and remove "blots" (her example is the absurdity of codifying the rule in Pinnel's case); (4) avoids
  waiting decades for a point to be litigated (Chalmers' example of a point first ruled on in 1753 and not
  decided again for 129 years); (5) resolves conflicts and gaps in authority; (6) cuts excessive case law -
  Chalmers' image that the "leaves" of case law hide the trunk and branches, and his "old wine into new
  bottles".
- *Process requirements:* real commercial demand; expert drafting with practitioners; consultation with the
  responsible department; Parliamentary Counsel working with experts in clear language that still permits
  creative interpretation; wide public consultation; suitable drafting conventions (illustrations,
  commentary); expert evidence to the Parliamentary committee; monitoring and amendment.
- She is careful to say the views are her own, not the Commission's, and that resources may be lacking.

**Tensions to use in essays.**
- Certainty (sanctity of contract, strict conditions) versus fairness (UCTA, CRA). The choice to
  focus on business sales reflects a view that commercial parties need predictable rules.
- Security of property versus protection of the good faith purchaser: the core policy fight in nemo dat.
- The SGA as a "code" that does not codify general contract law (s 62(2)), creating gaps with closely
  related areas (each interpretation can widen the gap between sale law and
  related personal property law).
- Harmonisation: international instruments (CISG, UNIDROIT, PECL) versus English insularity (Goode's
  "Insularity or Leadership?").

## Applying it to a problem question
This topic rarely forms a problem question on its own, but it frames every answer:
1. Characterise the transaction: commercial (SGA, UCTA for exclusions) or trader-to-consumer (CRA)?
   A business person buying for private use may be a consumer.
2. Identify the statute first (SGA/CRA/Factors Act), read the words (*Vagliano*), then use case law;
   remember s 62(2) lets common law fill gaps.
3. When advising rival claimants to goods, identify which conveyancing principle each relies on (security
   of property v good faith purchase v estoppel). Commentary stresses that who you advise shapes
   the argument.
4. In evaluation sections, explain results by the commercial principles (certainty, market, custom).

Method for problem questions: identify the facts raising the issue; identify the legal
issue; state the law (statute and cases); apply it, including the other side's defences; conclude on
what happens to the contract/goods and the remedy.
