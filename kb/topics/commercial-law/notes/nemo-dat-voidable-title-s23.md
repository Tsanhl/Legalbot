# Nemo Dat Exception - Sale under a Voidable Title (s 23 SGA) and Mistaken Identity

## Overview
The classic fact pattern: a rogue tricks an owner into selling goods (usually paying with a worthless cheque or
lying about who he is), then resells to an innocent buyer and disappears. If the rogue's contract with the owner was
merely *voidable* for fraud, the rogue got a title that he can pass to a good faith buyer before the owner rescinds
(s 23). If the contract was *void* (typically for mistake as to the rogue's identity), the rogue got nothing and the
owner wins. The battleground is therefore (i) void v voidable - the law of mistaken identity, settled (contentiously)
by *Shogun Finance v Hudson* - and (ii) whether the owner rescinded in time (*Car & Universal Finance v Caldwell*).
Law: England & Wales.

## The law

### 1. Section 23
"When the seller of goods has a voidable title to them, but his title has not been avoided at the time of the sale,
the buyer acquires a good title to the goods, provided he buys them in good faith and without notice of the seller's
defect of title." Elements:
1. The seller (rogue) has a **voidable** (not void) title.
2. Not yet **avoided** at the time of the sale.
3. A **sale** to the buyer.
4. Buyer in **good faith without notice** - burden on the original owner to disprove (*Whitehorn Bros v Davison*
   [1911] 1 KB 463, CA), a reversal compared with FA s 2, s 8/s 24 and s 9/s 25.

### 2. Void or voidable? Mistaken identity
5. **Fraud as to attributes** (creditworthiness, a false cheque) makes a contract voidable; a fundamental mistake as
   to the identity of the other party can make it void.
6. **Face-to-face presumption**: where parties deal in person, the law presumes the seller intends to deal with the
   person physically present, even if he gives a false name (Lords Nicholls and Millett in *Shogun*).
   - *Phillips v Brooks* [1919] 2 KB 243: rogue in a jeweller's shop claimed to be a prominent aristocrat, paid by
     cheque and took a ring, which he pledged to a pawnbroker for £350. The contract was voidable, so the pledge was
     valid and the pawnbroker kept the ring.
   - *Lewis v Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198 (CA): Lewis sold his car to a rogue posing as the actor Richard Greene, paid by a
     worthless cheque; the rogue sold to Averay. Voidable - Averay won. Lord Denning would have abandoned the
     identity/attributes distinction and treated such contracts as voidable generally.
   - *Ingram v Little* [1961] 1 QB 31 (CA): elderly sisters selling a car checked the rogue's claimed name ("P G M
     Hutchinson") and address in a directory before accepting his cheque; held void - they intended to deal only with
     the real Hutchinson. Pearce LJ treated the facts as special. **The House of Lords in *Shogun* said *Ingram v
     Little* was wrongly decided** (Elliott).
7. **Written contracts**: where the contract is made in writing, the identity of the parties is a matter of
   construction of the document. *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62: a rogue at a Leicester dealer's showroom
   agreed to take a Mitsubishi Shogun on HP, signing the finance form in the name of Mr Durlabh Patel and producing
   Patel's stolen driving licence; the finance company checked Patel's credit and approved; the rogue paid a 10%
   deposit, drove away, sold the car to Hudson for £17,000 and vanished. The majority held the HP agreement was with
   Patel, as named in the written agreement (Lord Phillips: identity is determined by construing the putative written
   contract; Lord Hobhouse approving *Hector v Lyons*), so it was void - the rogue was not the "debtor" under HPA 1964
   s 27 and could pass no title. The dealer was a mere go-between, not the finance company's agent, so the face-to-face
   principle did not apply. The decision rests on *Cundy v Lindsay* (1878) 3 App Cas 459 (written dealings with a
   rogue posing as a known firm - void). A simple alias, with no real person impersonated, does not void the contract:
   *King's Norton Metal v Edridge Merrett* (rogue "Hallam & Co" - contract with the letter-writer).
8. *Lake v Simmons* [1927] AC 487 (Lord Haldane) limited *Phillips v Brooks* where identity was crucial (Turpin).

### 3. Avoidance before the resale
9. Normally rescission must be communicated to the other party. But *Car & Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525
   (CA): where the rogue deliberately absconds so that he cannot be told, communication is "a useless formality"; the
   owner rescinds by doing everything possible to evince the intention - e.g. informing the police and the AA/
   motoring organisations. A sale after that passes no title under s 23.
10. But *Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560 (CA): the dealer sold a car to A against a cheque (property only
    to pass on clearance); the cheque bounced; the dealer told the HP Information Bureau and police (rescission per
    *Caldwell*). Weeks later A sold the car for cash in Warren Street, an established street market for used cars, to
    B, who sold to Williams. B nevertheless got title under FA s 9 / SGA s 25 (buyer in possession): A had obtained
    possession with the seller's consent; the withdrawal of consent did not matter (FA s 2(2)); selling in an
    established car market was acting as a mercantile agent would. Thornely: this "considerably restricts" *Caldwell*,
    since a rogue in possession will rely on s 25 rather than s 23.

### 4. Pledges and other dispositions
11. s 23 speaks of a "seller" and "buyer" and so covers sales only; but the common law reaches the same result for
    pledges: *Cundy v Lindsay* states that property passes under a voidable contract, so the rogue can pass good title to
    a good faith taker for value unless the contract has been avoided; *Phillips v Brooks* (pledge valid).

## Key authorities
- SGA s 23; *Whitehorn Bros v Davison* [1911] 1 KB 463 - burden on owner.
- *Shogun Finance v Hudson* [2003] UKHL 62 - written contract with a named person: void; *Ingram v Little* wrong.
- *Phillips v Brooks* [1919] 2 KB 243; *Lewis v Averay* [1972] 1 QB 198 - face to face: voidable.
- *Ingram v Little* [1961] 1 QB 31 - void (disapproved).
- *Cundy v Lindsay* (1878) 3 App Cas 459; *King's Norton Metal v Edridge Merrett* - written dealings; alias.
- *Car & Universal Finance v Caldwell* [1965] 1 QB 525 - rescission without communication.
- *Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560 - s 25 rescues buyer after rescission.

## Debate and critique
- **Elliott, "No Justice for Innocent Purchasers of Dishonestly Obtained Goods" [2004] JBL 381.** *Shogun* "continues
  an injustice" to innocent purchasers. The courts prized certainty and the written document ("the bargain is the
  document") but the result is artificial: the finance company was treated as contracting with someone who never knew
  of the agreement, while the rogue it actually dealt with (through the dealer) was ignored. The revision guide notes
  Elliott's point that *Shogun* removes from HPA s 27 any case where a rogue impersonates a real person. Elliott places
  *Shogun* alongside *Great Peace Shipping* as risking injustice by over-emphasising commercial certainty and the
  contract's terms.
- **Foster (2004) 9 Cov LJ 1.** *Shogun* shows the courts preferring strict legal analysis (void v voidable) to fairness,
  and the inadequacy of the statutory exceptions where fraud and mistaken identity are involved; the exceptions are
  piecemeal and reform is needed to protect good faith purchasers.
- **Turpin, "Mistake of Identity" (1972) 30 CLJ 19.** Criticises the unclear identity/attributes framework, notes the Law
  Reform Committee's 12th Report proposal that contracts induced by mistaken identity be voidable rather than void (so
  third parties are protected), and discusses taking account of the first victim's fault, including apportioning loss
  (contributory negligence), while noting practical difficulties. Lord Denning in *Lewis v Averay* similarly favoured
  voidability.
- **Form over substance.** Outcomes turn on whether the rogue dealt face to face or in writing, although in both
  cases there are two innocent parties and the owner (here the finance company) may be better placed to detect the
  fraud. Devlin LJ in *Ingram v Little* had proposed giving courts power to apportion the loss.
- **Caldwell** favours owners - rescission without communication means a buyer cannot discover from the rogue that
  title has gone - but *Newtons of Wembley* shows s 25 can undo this, producing a tangled interplay of exceptions
  (Thornely).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. How did the rogue get the goods? Fraud as to creditworthiness (voidable) or mistaken identity?
2. If identity: was the deal face-to-face (presumption of intention to deal with the person present - voidable:
   *Phillips*, *Lewis*) or in writing naming a real third person (void: *Shogun*, *Cundy*)? A mere alias - voidable
   (*King's Norton*). *Ingram v Little* is no longer good law.
3. If void: rogue had no title; s 23 fails; also HPA s 27 fails (no "debtor"). Owner wins unless another exception
   applies.
4. If voidable: had the owner avoided before the resale (communication, or *Caldwell* steps such as notifying police)?
5. If not avoided: buyer in good faith without notice? Owner must prove otherwise (*Whitehorn*).
6. If avoided: consider s 25 SGA / s 9 FA - did the rogue get possession with consent as a buyer, and resell as a
   mercantile agent would (*Newtons of Wembley*)?
7. Pledge rather than sale? Common law (*Phillips v Brooks*).
Traps: forgetting s 25 after a successful rescission; citing *Ingram v Little* as authority; putting the good faith
burden on the buyer under s 23.
