# Nemo Dat Exceptions - Seller in Possession and Buyer in Possession

## Overview
Possession and ownership often separate in sales. A seller may keep goods after property has passed (s 24/s 8 FA),
or a buyer may get the goods before property passes - because of a retention of title clause, a dishonoured cheque or
a conditional sale (s 25/s 9 FA). In each case the person in possession looks like the owner, and the Acts protect a
good faith second buyer who takes delivery. These exceptions are commercially important (they can defeat ROT sellers
and pre-paying buyers, including s 20A co-owners) and are heavily litigated, often around cars. The SGA and Factors
Act versions are almost identical; FA s 8 is slightly wider than SGA s 24 (revision guide). Law: England & Wales, with
Privy Council, Australian and New Zealand authority.

## The law

### A. Seller in possession - SGA s 24 / FA s 8
FA s 8: "Where a person, having sold goods, continues, or is, in possession of the goods or of the documents of title
to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person, or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or
documents of title under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof or under any agreement for sale, pledge or
other disposition thereof, to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of the previous sale,
shall have the same effect as if the person making the delivery or transfer were expressly authorised by the owner of
the goods to make the same." (s 24 SGA is materially the same.)

Pattern: S sells to B1 (property passes), keeps the goods, then sells/pledges to B2 and delivers. B2 wins; B1 sues S.

1. **"Having sold"** - there must have been a sale to B1 (property passed).
2. **"Continues, or is, in possession"** - continuity of *physical* possession; the capacity in which S holds is
   irrelevant.
   - *Staffs Motor Guarantee v British Wagon* [1934] 2 KB 305 and *Eastern Distributors v Goldring* had required
     possession "as seller"; the Privy Council in *Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits (Hire Finance)* [1965] 2 All ER
     105 (Lord Pearce) said they were wrong: a dealer who sold cars to a finance company but kept them on a "display
     agreement" was still "in possession"; only physical possession counts.
   - *Worcester Works Finance v Cooden Engineering* [1972] 1 QB 210 (CA) followed it. Lord Denning: it does not matter
     what private arrangement exists (e.g. seller as bailee); what matters is continuity of physical possession; a
     substantial break (delivery to the buyer who keeps them for a while, then the seller gets them back) may take the
     case outside the section.
   - *Mitchell v Jones* (1905) 24 NZLR 932: horse sold and delivered to B1, leased back to S a few days later; S sold to
     B2; the break in possession was fatal to B2.
   - *Fadallah v Pollak* [2013] EWHC 3159 (QB): if S only comes into possession *after* the sale, as bailee, and never
     held as seller, s 24 does not protect B2.
3. **"Delivery or transfer" to B2** of goods or documents of title.
   - *Nicholson v Harper* [1895] 2 Ch 415: wine stored with a warehouseman; merchant sold it to B and later pledged it to
     the warehouseman; no title - physical delivery to B2 required.
   - *Gamer's Motor Centre v Natwest Wholesale* (1987) 163 CLR 236 (High Court of Australia) rejected *Nicholson*:
     constructive delivery suffices.
   - *Michael Gerson (Leasing) v Wilkinson* [2000] 2 All ER (Comm) 890 (CA) adopted *Gamer's*: in a sale and leaseback where machinery
     never left the premises, a change in the character of S's possession (now holding for B2) is enough.
4. **"Sale, pledge or other disposition"** (or agreement for any of them): a "disposition" is a transfer of an interest
   in property; merely giving possession is not enough (*Worcester Works*).
5. **Good faith and no notice of the previous sale** - burden on B2 (*Heap v Motorists' Advisory Agency* [1923] 1 KB
   577).
6. **Effect**: as if S were expressly authorised by B1 (the owner). B2 takes B1's title; B1 has a personal claim against
   S. The Burns article notes s 24 still threatens buyers of shares in a bulk under s 20A.

### B. Buyer in possession - SGA s 25 / FA s 9
FA s 9: "Where a person, having bought or agreed to buy goods, obtains with the consent of the seller possession of the
goods or the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer, by that person or by a mercantile agent acting
for him, of the goods or the documents of title under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof or under any
agreement for sale, pledge or other disposition thereof, to any person receiving the same in good faith and without
notice of any lien or other right of the original seller in respect of the goods, shall have the same effect as if
the person making the delivery or transfer were a mercantile agent in possession of the goods or documents of title
with the consent of the owner."

Pattern: S agrees to sell to B1 (property not yet passed - ROT, cheque condition), B1 gets possession with S's consent,
B1 sells/pledges to B2 and delivers. B2 may win.

7. **"Bought or agreed to buy"**:
   - Hire-purchase is out - the hirer has an option, not an agreement to buy: *Helby v Matthews* [1895] AC 471 (piano
     pledged by a hirer).
   - Conditional sale was in: *Lee v Butler* [1893] 2 QB 318 (hirer bound to complete purchase). Now **s 25(2)** (and
     the amended s 9): a buyer under a conditional sale agreement that is a consumer credit agreement under the CCA 1974,
     with at least part of the price by instalments, is not a person who has bought or agreed to buy (motor vehicles are
     then dealt with by HPA Part III).
   - An agent entrusted to sell is not a person who has agreed to buy (but FA s 2 may apply); not sale or return or a
     services contract (revision guide). *Edwards v Vaughan* (1910) is also cited.
   - *Shaw v Commissioner of Police* [1987] 1 WLR 1332: dealer L had never bought or agreed to buy the Porsche, so a
     buyer from L could not use s 25(1).
8. **Possession with the consent of the seller** - consent obtained by fraud still counts (*Du Jardin v Beadman Bros*
   [1952] 2 QB 712, per Thornely); withdrawal of consent does not matter if B2 has no notice (FA s 2(2), applied in
   *Newtons of Wembley*).
   - Constructive possession: *Four Point Garage v Carter* [1985] 3 All ER 12 (commentators use it to ask whether constructive
     delivery between S and B1 suffices).
   - Nature of possession: *Marten v Whale* [1917] 2 KB 480 (goods lent temporarily to B1 - still in possession under
     s 25); cf *Pacific Motor Auctions* (physical possession is what counts).
9. **Delivery or transfer to B2** - a conscious voluntary act by B1: *Forsythe International v Silver Shipping* [1993] 2
   Lloyd's Rep 268 (B2 seizing goods from B1 is not delivery). Delivery by a mercantile agent acting for B1 suffices;
   constructive delivery suffices (revision guide).
10. **Sale, pledge or other disposition, or agreement** - *Worcester Works* (meaning of disposition); *Re Highway Foods
    International* [1995] BCC 271 and *Shenstone v Hilton* [1894] 2 QB 452 (agreements).
11. **Good faith, no notice of any lien or other right of the original seller** - burden on B2.
12. **"As if a mercantile agent"** - the effect is that of FA s 2(1), so B1's disposition must be made in the way a
    mercantile agent acting in the ordinary course of business would act:
    - *Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560 (CA): rogue A bought a car with a bad cheque (property only on
      clearance), the dealer rescinded per *Caldwell*, but A sold for cash in Warren Street, an established street market
      for used cars, to B. Treated as acting in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent; B (and so
      Williams) got title.
    - *Lambert v G & C Finance* (1963) Sol Jo 666: sale of a car without its logbook - not in the ordinary course.
    - Commonwealth cases: *Langmead v Thyer Rubber* (1947, South Australia); *Jeffcott v Andre Motors* [1966] NZLR 721.
13. **Thieves - the owner's title is not defeated**: *National Employers' Mutual General Insurance Association v Jones*
    [1990] 1 AC 24 (HL): H's car was stolen and sold successively to L, T, A Ltd, M Ltd (dealers acting in the ordinary
    course) and Jones, all later buyers innocent. Jones argued that on a literal reading M Ltd had "agreed to buy" and had
    possession with the consent of its "seller" A Ltd, so his purchase took effect as if made by a mercantile agent with
    the consent of "the owner". Held: s 9/s 25 operate only to defeat an owner who has entrusted possession to the buyer
    (consent of *the owner* to possession is crucial); goods traced to a thief carry no title capable of passing. Lord
    Goff relied on legislative history.
14. **Effect**: B2 takes the title of the person whose consent B1 had (the original seller), not better.

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 24, 25(1)-(2); FA 1889 ss 2(2), 8, 9.
- *Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits* [1965] 2 All ER 105 (PC); *Worcester Works v Cooden* [1972] 1 QB 210 -
  continuity of physical possession.
- *Mitchell v Jones* (1905) NZ; *Fadallah v Pollak* [2013] EWHC 3159 - breaks in possession.
- *Nicholson v Harper* [1895] 2 Ch 415; *Gamer's Motor Centre* (1987) 163 CLR 236; *Michael Gerson v Wilkinson* [2000] 2 All ER (Comm) 890 -
  constructive delivery.
- *Helby v Matthews* [1895] AC 471; *Lee v Butler* [1893] 2 QB 318 - bought or agreed to buy.
- *Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB 560; *Lambert v G & C Finance* (1963) - notional mercantile agent.
- *National Employers v Jones* [1990] 1 AC 24 - no title through a thief.
- *Four Point Garage v Carter* [1985] 3 All ER 12; *Marten v Whale* [1917] 2 KB 480; *Forsythe v Silver Shipping*
  [1993] 2 Lloyd's Rep 268.

## Debate and critique
- **Thornely [1965] CLJ 24 on *Newtons of Wembley*.** It is "startling" that the case implies the development of
  hire-purchase (approved in *Helby v Matthews* to escape s 9) was largely unnecessary: since the buyer-in-possession's
  disposition must satisfy the "ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent" test, a private buyer in possession
  will rarely pass title unless he is in trade or sells in peculiar circumstances like Warren Street. The decision both
  restricts *Caldwell* (rogues will rely on s 25 rather than s 23) and narrows s 25(2) - ironically just as Parliament
  enacted HPA Part III to protect private purchasers of cars from any hirer. Contrast the seller-in-possession provision,
  whose different wording has no ordinary course requirement.
- **Thornely [1988] CLJ 15 on *Jones* (CA).** The majority (May and Croom-Johnson LJJ) avoided a literal reading by
  holding there is no "sale" unless the seller has (or had) power to transfer ownership; Sir Denys Buckley, dissenting,
  read the section literally: it is rational to let the owner recover from the thief's immediate buyer (who can enquire)
  but not from later innocent buyers, leaving the probably insured owner to bear the loss. Thornely agrees with the
  dissent's criticism of the majority's ratio (it would make s 12(1) otiose and exclude many sales of future goods) but
  thinks equating "seller" and "owner" (as in NZ and Alberta cases) less disruptive. He calls for a thorough overhaul of
  the duplicated FA/SGA provisions and their "absurd fictions".
- **Battersby (1991) on the HL decision in *Jones*.** Lord Goff relied on legislative history to hold that ss 24-25 were
  not designed to facilitate disposal of stolen goods; Palmer and Merkin criticised the rigid historical focus and
  neglect of commercial reality; the insurance argument favours the innocent purchaser.
- **Merrett, "The Importance of Delivery and Possession in the Passing of Title" (2008) 67 CLJ 376** (revision guide):
  evaluates ss 24-25, the meaning of "continues in possession", the practical problems of requiring continuous physical
  possession, and argues "delivery" and "possession" should have consistent meanings throughout the Act.
- **Physical v constructive delivery.** *Nicholson v Harper* (physical) v *Gamer's*/*Michael Gerson* (constructive):
  the trend favours commercial reality (sale-and-leaseback), but it widens the risk to the first buyer.
- **ROT and bulk buyers.** s 25 can defeat a retention of title seller whose buyer resells; s 24 can defeat a pre-paying
  buyer or s 20A co-owner whose seller resells (Burns).

## Applying it to a problem question
**Seller in possession:**
1. Did S sell to B1 so property passed (check ss 17-18)? If not, S is still owner and B2 wins anyway.
2. Did S stay in continuous physical possession (capacity irrelevant: *Pacific Motor*, *Worcester*)? Any break
   (*Mitchell v Jones*) or possession only acquired later as bailee (*Fadallah*)?
3. Did S deliver goods/documents to B2 (physical or constructive: *Michael Gerson*) under a sale, pledge or other
   disposition?
4. B2 in good faith without notice of the first sale (B2 proves)?
**Buyer in possession:**
1. Had B1 bought or agreed to buy (not HP: *Helby*; not a consumer-credit conditional sale: s 25(2); not a mere agent)?
2. Did B1 obtain possession of goods/documents with the seller's consent (fraud irrelevant; withdrawal irrelevant
   without notice; constructive possession: *Four Point*)?
3. Did B1 voluntarily deliver to B2 (*Forsythe*) under a sale, pledge, disposition or agreement?
4. B2 in good faith without notice of the original seller's rights?
5. Did B1 act as a mercantile agent in the ordinary course would (*Newtons of Wembley* v *Lambert*)?
6. Is there a thief anywhere upstream (*NEM v Jones* - no title)?
Then state the effect and the loser's personal remedies (B1 v S for non-delivery; S v B1 for the price).
