# Consumer credit (Consumer Credit Act 1974 and the FCA regime)

## Overview
Consumers who buy on credit have two contracts: one with the supplier of the goods or services, and one with the lender. At common law each party is liable only on its own contract. The Consumer Credit Act 1974 (CCA), as amended by the Consumer Credit Act 2006, gives borrowers under "regulated agreements" strong protections: formalities, cancellation, an unfair relationship jurisdiction, and, above all, joint liability of the lender for the supplier's misrepresentation or breach (ss 56, 75, 75A). Since 1 April 2014 regulation is by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA), though the CCA remains in force for substantive rights. This note follows the Baskind revision guide chapter (Commercial Law Concentrate, 6th edn, ch 11).

## The law (England and Wales)
**1. Regulatory structure.**
- Until 1 April 2014 the Office of Fair Trading licensed lenders under the CCA, and unlicensed trading was a criminal offence. The OFT closed and the FCA took over.
- By the FSMA (Regulated Activities) (Amendment) (No 2) Order 2013 ("FSMA RAO"), entering consumer credit or hire agreements, credit brokerage and debt collection are regulated activities; carrying them on by way of business requires FCA authorisation (or interim permission), or the "general prohibition" in FSMA s 19 may be breached.
- Terminology changed: debtor/creditor became borrower/lender; "debtor-creditor-supplier" (D-C-S) became "borrower-lender-supplier" (B-L-S), and D-C became B-L; "individual" became "relevant recipient of credit". Conduct rules are in the FCA Consumer Credit Sourcebook (CONC); a private person who suffers loss from breach of CONC has a damages action (FSMA s 138D).

**2. Regulated agreements.** CCA s 189(1): a regulated agreement is a consumer credit agreement or consumer hire agreement that is not exempt. A credit agreement is one between an individual or relevant recipient of credit (a small partnership of two or three persons not all bodies corporate, or an unincorporated body) and another person for credit of any amount (FSMA RAO art 60B(3)). Credit is a cash loan or other financial accommodation, including hire purchase. The £25,000 ceiling was removed for agreements made on or after 6 April 2008. *Nram plc v McAdam* (2016): a lender's mistaken statement that an agreement was regulated did not incorporate the Act's protections by contract.
- Consumer hire agreement (art 60N): bailment of goods to an individual which is not hire purchase and is capable of subsisting for more than three months; *Dimond v Lovell* (2002): an agreement stating it could not last more than 28 days was not one.
- Non-commercial agreements (not made in the course of a business, e.g. between friends or family) are defined in s 189(1); *Hare v Schurek* [1993] CCLR 47: a car dealer's one-off hire purchase to a friend did not make him a consumer credit business (s 189(2)).

**3. Exempt agreements (grouped).** Examples listed in the source: regulated mortgage contracts and home purchase plans; credit above £25,000 wholly or predominantly for the borrower's business purposes; certain land-related agreements (where under 40 per cent of the land is used as a dwelling); agreements with specified lenders (local authorities, building societies, charities and so on); low-number-of-payments agreements (for example, not more than four payments within 12 months, interest-free or secured on land); credit unions where the total charge for credit does not exceed 42.6 per cent; low-rate agreements offered to a class of borrower; and high net worth individuals who sign the prescribed declaration for loans secured on land or above £60,260.

**4. Unfair relationship (CCA ss 140A-140D).** The extortionate credit bargain provisions (ss 137-140, "grossly exorbitant") were replaced by the unfair relationship test, applicable to all new consumer lending, not only regulated agreements.
- s 140A(1): court may act if the relationship arising out of the agreement is unfair to the borrower because of any term of the agreement or related agreement, the way the lender has exercised or enforced rights, or any other thing done or not done by or on behalf of the lender before or after the agreement. The question is whether the relationship as a whole is unfair (*Patel v Patel* (2009)); a lender's failure to supply a copy of the agreement in time made it temporarily unenforceable but did not make the relationship unfair (*Carey v HSBC Bank* (2009)). The relationship may have ended (s 140A(4)). Once unfairness is alleged, the lender must prove it is not unfair (s 140B(9)).
- s 140B powers: repayment of sums, requiring or prohibiting action, reducing or discharging sums payable, return of security to a surety, setting aside duties, altering terms, and directing accounts.
- *Plevin v Paragon Personal Finance Ltd* [2014] UKSC 61: PPI on a £34,000 loan carried 71.8 per cent commission (£1,870 to the broker, £2,280 to the lender), undisclosed. The Supreme Court (Lord Sumption) held the non-disclosure made the relationship unfair under s 140A(1)(c), although the failure to assess suitability did not; at some size commission must be disclosed; Paragon was responsible as the only party that knew both commissions. *Plevin* overruled the Court of Appeal's *Harrison v Black Horse* (2012). *Scotland v British Credit Trust* (2014) concerned a salesman's misrepresentation about PPI and an undisclosed procuration fee, with the borrower entitled to recover it.

**5. Formalities (ss 60-61, 65, 127).** The documents must make the borrower aware of rights and duties, the total charge for credit including APR, and the protections and remedies available (s 60(1); Consumer Credit (Agreements) Regulations 2010). To be properly executed (s 61(1)), the agreement must be in prescribed form containing prescribed terms, signed in the prescribed manner by both parties, embody all express terms, and be readily legible. An improperly executed agreement is enforceable against the borrower only by court order (s 65); under s 127 the court has discretion, considering prejudice to any person, the degree of culpability, and its power to reduce or discharge sums. Before 6 April 2007 there was no discretion for certain failures (repealed s 127(3)-(5)), producing harsh results in *Wilson v First County Trust (No 2)* (2004) (HL: no breach of Art 6 or Article 1 of Protocol 1 ECHR; the HRA was not yet in force) and *Dimond v Lovell* (2002). The borrower may still enforce such an agreement against the lender or supplier.

**6. Cancellation (ss 67-68).** A regulated agreement is cancellable if antecedent negotiations included oral representations in the borrower's presence, it was not secured on land, and it was signed by the borrower away from the lender's or supplier's trade premises. There is a cooling-off period, generally five days (sometimes 14) from receiving a copy of the signed agreement. Small agreements (s 17(1): credit not exceeding £50, other than hire purchase or conditional sale, or hire with payments not exceeding £50, unsecured or secured only by guarantee or indemnity) escape most formalities and cancellation rules.

**7. Types of agreement and connected lender liability.**
- B-L-S agreement (art 60L(1)): credit financing a transaction between borrower and lender, or between borrower and supplier under pre-existing or contemplated arrangements between lender and supplier (or an unrestricted-use agreement made under such arrangements knowing it will finance the transaction). B-L: credit to finance a transaction with a supplier without such arrangements, refinancing, or unrestricted use without such knowledge. The practical difference: with a B-L-S agreement both supplier and lender are potentially liable for the supplier's misrepresentation or breach.
- **s 75:** where the borrower has a claim against the supplier for misrepresentation or breach of contract, he has a "like claim" against the lender, jointly and severally liable with the supplier. Requirements: a regulated B-L-S agreement (s 12(b)/(c)), a single item with cash price more than £100 and less than £30,000 (s 75(3)(b)), and lender and supplier being different parties. Applies even if only part of the price is paid on credit. Cannot be excluded (s 173(1)). The lender may claim an indemnity from the supplier and join it to proceedings (s 75(2), (5)). Liability is not limited to the credit advanced. Applies to purchases made abroad with a UK credit agreement (*OFT v Lloyds TSB Bank* (2008) HL). Whether a second (authorised) cardholder is covered is undecided. It is especially useful when the supplier is insolvent or untraceable.
- *Durkin v DSG Retail Ltd and HFC Bank plc* (2014, Supreme Court): a borrower who rescinds the supply contract can rescind the credit agreement, not through s 75's "like claim" but because the law implies a term that the credit agreement is conditional on the survival of the supply agreement (Lord Hodge).
- **s 56:** the supplier is deemed the lender's agent in antecedent negotiations (from first communication, including advertisements, s 56(4)), so the lender is liable for the supplier's statements, and the borrower may rescind and give notice to the supplier (s 102). Not excludable (s 173(1)). *Forthright Finance Ltd v Ingate* (1997): a dealer's promise to settle the buyer's old finance was an antecedent negotiation relating to the new purchase. Unregulated agreements: no agency unless exceptional (*Branwhite v Worcester Works Finance* (1969)).
- **s 75A (from 1 February 2011):** secondary liability of the creditor in "linked credit agreements" (credit that exclusively finances specific goods or services where the lender uses the supplier's services or the goods are specified in the agreement); credit cards are usually excluded. Covers breach of contract, not misrepresentation; applies to agreements outside s 75 but within the Consumer Credit Directive; it does not apply where the cash value is £30,000 or less, the credit exceeds £60,260, or the agreement is for business purposes; the borrower must first take reasonable steps against the supplier (supplier untraceable, unresponsive, insolvent, or fails to satisfy) before claiming against the lender.

**8. Termination and enforcement.**
- Protected goods (s 90): after one-third of the total price has been paid and title remains with the lender, repossession of goods under regulated hire purchase or conditional sale requires a court order. Recovery in breach terminates the agreement, releases the borrower and entitles him to recover sums paid (s 91).
- Early settlement (s 94): the borrower can discharge his indebtedness at any time on notice and payment; s 97 entitles him to a statement of the amount required.

## Key authorities
- CCA 1974 ss 8-12 (via FSMA RAO), 56, 60-61, 65, 67-68, 75, 75A, 90-91, 94, 127, 140A-140D, 173, 189; Consumer Credit Act 2006; FSMA 2000 and RAO 2013.
- *Plevin v Paragon* [2014] UKSC 61; *Durkin v DSG Retail* (2014); *OFT v Lloyds TSB* (2008); *Wilson v First County Trust (No 2)* (2004); *Nram v McAdam* (2016); *Dimond v Lovell* (2002); *Patel v Patel* (2009); *Carey v HSBC* (2009); *Forthright Finance v Ingate* (1997).

## Debate and critique
- **Policy of strict formalities.** The *Wilson* and *Dimond* outcomes gave borrowers windfalls for technical errors, and the 2006 amendments restored court discretion so such cases would likely be decided differently now (source's view).
- **Connected lender liability.** Controversial for imposing on lenders liability for suppliers' wrongs; it is a major consumer protection where suppliers are insolvent or abroad. Section 75's gaps (second cardholders, price thresholds, credit cards for s 75A) are points for critique.
- **Unfair relationship.** More flexible than extortionate bargain, evaluated holistically, with the burden on the lender; *Plevin* extends it to undisclosed commissions and overruled *Harrison*.
- **Regulatory shift.** The CCA no longer provides the full regulatory framework; the FCA regime governs authorisation and conduct, which creates a split between CCA rights and FCA rules.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there a regulated agreement? Credit to an individual (or small partnership) of any amount, not exempt (business above £25,000, mortgage, other exemptions)? For hire: more than three months?
2. Identify the structure: B-L-S (credit card, hire purchase, point-of-sale finance) or B-L (loan from a bank unconnected to the supplier).
3. Defective goods or misrepresentation by the supplier: s 75 (cash price between £100 and £30,000, regulated B-L-S, joint and several liability) and s 56 for misrepresentation in negotiations. Consider s 75A for linked credit not within s 75. Remember these provisions cannot be excluded.
4. If the supply contract is terminated, consider *Durkin* (conditional credit agreement).
5. Documentation problems: was the agreement properly executed (s 61)? If not, only enforceable by court order; consider the s 127 factors.
6. Doorstep or off-premises signing with oral representations: cancellation rights and the cooling-off period (s 67-68), unless a small agreement.
7. Unfair terms, undisclosed commissions, aggressive enforcement: s 140A relationship test; burden on the lender.
8. Repossession after one-third paid: s 90; early settlement under s 94.
