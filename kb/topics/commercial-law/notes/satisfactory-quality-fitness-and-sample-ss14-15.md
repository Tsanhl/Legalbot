# Satisfactory quality, fitness for purpose and sales by sample (SGA ss 14-15; CRA ss 9-10, 13)

## Overview
Beyond title (s 12) and description (s 13), a buyer of goods is protected by implied terms about the *quality* of what
is delivered. In a business-to-business sale under the Sale of Goods Act 1979 (SGA) the terms are found in s 14(2)
(satisfactory quality), s 14(3) (fitness for a particular purpose) and s 15 (sales by sample). In a trader-to-consumer
contract the SGA provisions are switched off and the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA) supplies near-identical terms in ss 9,
10 and 13. These terms are the main route by which a buyer complains about defective goods, and their status as conditions
means that breach can justify rejection (subject to the acceptance rules in the remedies note).

## The law

### 1. Satisfactory quality: s 14(2) SGA
1. **Trigger.** The seller must sell "in the course of a business". the materials lists *R & B Customs Brokers v UDT Finance
   Ltd* [1988] 1 All ER 847, *Stevenson v Rogers* [1999] QB 1028 and *Feldaroll Foundary v Hermes* [2004] EWCA Civ 747 as the
   authorities on that phrase (the handout gives no holdings; see the Further Reading to L Sealy, "When is a sale made in the
   course of a business" (1999) 58 CLJ 276). A purely private seller is therefore outside s 14(2) and (3).
2. **Term.** The goods "supplied under the contract" must be of satisfactory quality. This covers everything delivered
   under the contract, not just the thing bargained for: *Geddling v Marsh* [1920] 1 KB 668 and *Wilson v Rickett* [1954]
   1 QB 598 are cited on this point (in the materials's list for goods supplied under the contract).
3. **Status.** By s 14(6), in England and Wales (and Northern Ireland) the terms implied by s 14(2) and (3) are conditions.
4. **The standard (s 14(2A)).** Goods are satisfactory if they meet the standard a reasonable person would regard as
   satisfactory, taking account of any description, the price (if relevant) and all other relevant circumstances.
   Authorities listed: *Bartlett v Sidney Marcus Ltd* [1965] 2 All ER 753 and *Friarwood Ltd v Champagne Cattier SA* [2006]
   EWCA Civ 1105.
5. **Aspects of quality (s 14(2B)).** Quality includes state and condition, and (among others, where appropriate): (a)
   fitness for all the purposes for which goods of that kind are commonly supplied; (b) appearance and finish; (c) freedom
   from minor defects; (d) safety; (e) durability. Cases the materials groups under this subsection: *Thain v Anniesland Trade
   Centre* 1997 SLT 102; *Clegg v Anderson* [2003] EWCA Civ 320; *Medivance v Gaslane* [2002] EWCA Civ 500; *Brocket v DSG*
   (unreported, 26 February 2003); *Bramhill v Edwards* [2004] EWCA Civ 403.
6. **Exceptions (s 14(2C)).** The term does not extend to a matter making the quality unsatisfactory (a) which was
   specifically drawn to the buyer's attention before the contract; (b) where the buyer examined the goods before the
   contract, which that examination ought to have revealed; or (c) in a sale by sample, which would have been apparent on
   reasonable examination of the sample.

### 2. Fitness for a particular purpose: s 14(3) SGA
1. **Trigger.** Sale in the course of a business, and the buyer, expressly or by implication, makes known to the seller (or
   credit-broker) any particular purpose for which the goods are being bought. Purpose-cases listed: *Griffiths v Peter
   Conway Ltd* [1939] 1 All ER 685; *Slater v Finning* [1997] AC 473; *Ashington Piggeries Ltd v Christopher Hill Ltd*
   [1972] AC 441.
2. **Term.** The goods supplied must be reasonably fit for that purpose, whether or not it is a purpose for which such
   goods are commonly supplied. Authorities on "reasonably fit": *Leicester Circuits v Coates* [2003] EWCA Civ 290 and
   *Balmoral Group Ltd v Borealis (UK) Ltd* [2006] EWHC 1900.
3. **Reliance exception.** The term does not apply where the circumstances show that the buyer does not rely, or that it
   is unreasonable for him to rely, on the skill or judgment of the seller or credit-broker (*Jewson v Kelly* [2003] EWCA Civ
   1030 is the listed case). Reliance is therefore the pivot of s 14(3), whereas s 14(2) needs no communication of purpose.
4. **Status.** A condition (s 14(6)).
5. The "business" and "supplied under the contract" points are the same as under s 14(2).

### 3. Sales by sample: s 15 SGA
1. **Meaning.** A contract is by sample where there is an express or implied term to that effect (s 15(1)).
2. **Implied terms (s 15(2)).** (a) The bulk will correspond with the sample in quality; and (b) the goods will be free from
   any defect making their quality unsatisfactory that would not be apparent on reasonable examination of the sample.
3. **Status.** A condition in England and Wales (s 15(3)).
4. ***Godley v Perry* [1960] 1 All ER 36.** A defective catapult (described as a most dangerous toy for the juvenile market) caused an accident. Edmund Davies J treated the defect (not
   apparent on reasonable examination of the samples) as breaching s 15(2)(c) as it then stood, and the liability ran
   through the whole chain of sales: retailer, wholesaler, importer. the materials also cites it for consequential loss.

### 4. Consumer contracts: CRA 2015
- SGA s 14(9) and s 15(5) disapply the SGA terms where Chapter 2 of Part 1 CRA applies.
- **s 9 CRA**: every contract to supply goods includes a term that quality is satisfactory. The test and the list of aspects
  of quality mirror s 14(2A)-(2B). Exceptions (s 9(4)) mirror s 14(2C) (brought to attention, ought to have been revealed by
  examination, sample).
- **Public statements (s 9(5)-(7)).** The "relevant circumstances" include any public statement about the specific
  characteristics of the goods by the trader, producer or their representative, especially in advertising or labelling. The
  statement is disregarded if the trader shows (a) it was not, and could not reasonably have been, aware of it; (b) it was
  publicly withdrawn or corrected before the contract; or (c) it could not have influenced the consumer's decision. The SGA
  has no equivalent.
- **s 9(8) / s 10(5)**: terms about quality or fitness may also be treated as included by custom.
- **s 10 CRA**: fitness for a particular purpose made known to the trader before contracting (or to a credit-broker where
  the goods were previously sold to the trader by that broker and the price is payable by instalments). Reliance exception in
  s 10(4).
- **s 13 CRA** (goods to match a sample seen or examined by the consumer): goods must match the sample except for
  differences brought to the consumer's attention, and be free from any defect making quality unsatisfactory that would not be
  apparent on reasonable examination of the sample. s 14 CRA has the equivalent rule for models.
- **s 18 CRA**: no other requirement to treat a term about quality or fitness as included (so the statutory terms occupy the
  field). Remedies for breach are in s 19 (see the remedies note).
- **Digital content**: ss 34 and 35 CRA replicate satisfactory quality and fitness (the list of aspects omits appearance and
  finish) with a trial-version exception in s 34(4)(c); remedies via s 42. ss 39 and 40 deal with supply by transmission and
  content subject to modifications.

## Key authorities
- SGA s 14(2), (2A), (2B), (2C), (3), (6); s 15(1)-(3); s 11(2) and (4) (condition treated as warranty: the buyer may waive
  the condition or elect to treat breach as breach of warranty; and where the contract is not severable and the goods have
  been accepted, breach of condition can only be treated as breach of warranty unless the contract says otherwise).
- *Bartlett v Sidney Marcus*; *Friarwood v Champagne Cattier* (standard of satisfactory quality).
- *Leicester Circuits v Coates*; *Balmoral v Borealis* (reasonable fitness); *Slater v Finning*; *Griffiths v Peter Conway*;
  *Ashington Piggeries* (particular purpose); *Jewson v Kelly* (reliance).
- *Godley v Perry* (sample; the dangerous catapult; liability down the chain).
- CRA 2015 ss 9, 10, 13, 14, 18, 34, 35.

## Debate and critique
The handout points the student to academic commentary rather than stating it: I Brown, "The meaning of merchantable quality
in sales of goods: quality or fitness for purpose?" [1987] LMCLQ 400 (the title contrasts quality with fitness for purpose); C Twigg-Flesner, "The relationship between satisfactory quality
and fitness for purpose" (2004) 63 CLJ 22; and C Twigg-Flesner, "Examination prior to purchase: a cautionary note" (2005) 121
LQR 205 (on the s 14(2C)(b) exception). The structural debate you can develop from the statutory text: (i) two regimes with
nearly identical wording exist for business and consumer buyers, and the CRA adds the public-statement rule only for
consumers; (ii) s 14(3) depends on reliance and a communicated purpose, so a buyer who buys from a specialist but does not
disclose the purpose is left with s 14(2), whose "fitness for all common purposes" limb is narrower; (iii) conditions give
a strong rejection remedy, which is then cut back by acceptance and by s 15A of the SGA (see the remedies note).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Who is the buyer? Consumer (CRA ss 9-10, 13) or business (SGA ss 14-15)? Check the scope note first.
2. Was the seller selling in the course of a business? If private, s 14(2)/(3) do not apply (but s 13 may, if a sale by
   description).
3. s 14(2): apply the reasonable-person standard using description, price and all circumstances; run through the s 14(2B)
   aspects (fitness for common purposes, appearance, minor defects, safety, durability). Price is an express factor in the test.
4. Test the exceptions: was the defect pointed out? Did an examination actually take place, and would it have revealed the
   defect?
5. s 14(3): was a particular purpose made known (expressly or impliedly)? Did the buyer rely on the seller, and was reliance
   reasonable? An unusual purpose not communicated defeats the claim.
6. Sample: was there an express or implied term that the sale is by sample? Would the defect have been apparent on reasonable
   examination of the sample?
7. All are conditions: move on to remedies (rejection, acceptance, s 35, consumer short-term right to reject).
8. Check any exclusion clause (see the exclusion clauses note): ss 13-15 liability can be excluded only if reasonable (UCTA
   s 6(1A)) and not at all against consumers (CRA s 31).
