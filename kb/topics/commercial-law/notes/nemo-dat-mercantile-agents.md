# Nemo Dat Exception - Sale by a Mercantile Agent (Factors Act 1889 s 2)

## Overview
Owners commonly hand goods to dealers and agents to display, obtain offers or sell. If the agent sells or pledges
without authority, the Factors Act 1889 s 2(1) (preserved by SGA s 21(2)(a)) protects a third party who deals in good
faith with a mercantile agent in possession with the owner's consent, acting in the ordinary course of business. The
exception embodies the commercial policy that people who deal with professional intermediaries should be able to
rely on appearances the owner helped create. It also matters because the buyer-in-possession exception (s 9 FA /
s 25 SGA) works by treating the buyer "as if" a mercantile agent. Law: England & Wales; many cases concern cars and
their registration documents.

## The law

### 1. The provision
FA 1889 s 2(1): "Where a mercantile agent is, with the consent of the owner, in possession of goods or of the documents
of title to goods, any sale, pledge, or other disposition of the goods, made by him when acting in the ordinary course
of business of a mercantile agent, shall ... be as valid as if he were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods
to make the same; provided that the person taking under the disposition acts in good faith, and has not at the time of
the disposition notice that the person making the disposition has no authority to make the same."
Result: the innocent third party gets the goods. Elements:

### 2. A "mercantile agent"
1. FA s 1(1) (also SGA s 26): a mercantile agent "having in the customary course of his business as such agent
   authority either to sell goods, or to consign goods for the purpose of sale, or to buy goods, or to raise money on
   the security of goods". Interpreted broadly:
   - *Weiner v Harris* [1910] 1 KB 285: a retail jeweller who also travelled selling goods for others, to whom a
     manufacturing jeweller sent stock for sale - a mercantile agent; his pledge bound the owner.
   - *Lowther v Harris* [1927] 1 KB 393: a dealer in pictures and furniture on commission, though normally dealing on
     his own account and acting for a single principal - a mercantile agent.
2. A person who is a mere employee, or a person entrusted as a bailee for some unrelated purpose, is not acting as a
   mercantile agent (see consent below). Thornely (1988) observes that case law makes it hard to say with confidence
   who is a mercantile agent today.

### 3. "In possession" of goods or documents of title
3. FA s 1(2): possession exists where goods or documents are in his actual custody or held by another subject to his
   control or for him or on his behalf.
4. Possession is required *at the time* of the disposition, not merely in the past: *Beverley Acceptances v Oakley*
   [1982] RTR 417 (bills of sale over cars executed when O was no longer in possession; no protection). The court also
   held a car logbook is not a document of title.
5. FA s 1(3): "goods" includes wares and merchandise. s 1(4): "document of title" is wide - bill of lading, dock
   warrant, warehouse-keeper's certificate, warrant or order for delivery, and any other document used in the
   ordinary course of business as proof of possession or control or authorising transfer or receipt of the goods.

### 4. "With the consent of the owner"
6. Consent is effective even if obtained by fraud or a trick (*Pearson v Rose & Young* [1951] 1 KB 275 for goods;
   the revision guide notes consent may be established even where the owner was tricked). But in *Pearson* the owner
   took his car to a dealer's showroom to get offers; the dealer obtained the registration book by a trick and sold the
   car with it. On the facts there was no intention to leave the book in the dealer's possession, and Denning LJ's
   capacity test (below) comes from this case.
7. Consent must be to possession **in the agent's capacity as a mercantile agent** or for a purpose connected with
   that business - Denning LJ in *Pearson*: if an owner leaves furniture with a repairer who happens also to be a
   dealer, the repairer cannot deprive the owner of it by selling; "why should that incidental knowledge deprive the
   true owner of his goods?" So possession for repair does not suffice. Possession for display, to get offers or to put
   the goods in the showroom does (Hornby, citing Denning LJ).
   - *Astley Industrial Trust v Miller* [1968] 2 All ER 36: the owner (a finance company) released a car to D Co, a
     car-hire firm and second-hand dealer, which sold it to an innocent buyer M; the question was the capacity in which
     D Co had possession (slide diagram only).
8. **Statutory presumptions** (FA s 2(2)-(4)):
   - s 2(2): once in possession with consent, a disposition valid had consent continued is valid despite withdrawal
     of consent, if the taker has no notice of the withdrawal (applied in *Newtons of Wembley v Williams* [1965] 1 QB
     560 after the owner avoided the rogue's title).
   - s 2(3): consent to possession of the documents of title is presumed from consent to possession of the goods.
   - s 2(4): consent is presumed in the absence of evidence to the contrary.

### 5. "Sale, pledge or other disposition"
9. **Sale** requires consideration (FA s 5). A gift is not covered. An *agreement* to sell is not expressly covered and
   is probably not a "disposition" (transfer of title).
10. **Pledge** (s 1(5)) includes any contract pledging or giving a lien or security on goods for an advance or
    pecuniary liability. s 4: a pledge for an antecedent debt gives the pledgee no more than the rights the agent
    could have enforced at the time.

### 6. "Ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent"
11. *Oppenheimer v Attenborough & Son* [1908] 1 KB 221 (Buckley LJ): acting as a mercantile agent would act - within
    business hours, at a proper place of business, and otherwise in the ordinary way, so that nothing leads the
    disponee to suspect wrongdoing.
12. *Stadium Finance v Robbins* [1962] 2 QB 664 (CA): the owner left his car with a dealer to obtain offers, kept the
    ignition key, but accidentally left the registration book locked in the glove compartment. The dealer got another
    key, retrieved the book and sold the car (via HP finance). Held: no consent to possession of the car with its book
    (Willmer LJ: absence of the key was symbolic of retaining the right of disposal) and a sale of a car without key and
    book was not in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent (Ormerod LJ).
13. *Pearson v Rose & Young* - a car sold without a registration book is not sold in the ordinary course of business.

### 7. Good faith and no notice
14. Question of fact; the **burden is on the third party** relying on the exception (contrast s 23).
    - *Heap v Motorists' Advisory Agency* [1923] 1 KB 577: car sold through a friend at a price well below its value,
      with a request for an "open" cheque - a sale at an undervalue may indicate dishonesty.
    - *Summers v Havard* [2011] EWCA Civ 1164 at [17]: deliberately avoiding enquiries in suspicious circumstances may
      indicate bad faith.

## Debate and critique
- **Hornby, "Mercantile Agents and Car Registration Books" (1962) 25 MLR 719.** Hornby criticises *Pearson* and
  especially *Stadium Finance*. Leaving a car with a dealer to get offers is precisely consent to possession as a
  mercantile agent (Denning LJ's own test in *Pearson*: display, obtaining offers, putting goods in the showroom), so
  Willmer LJ's reasoning on consent is contrary to principle and authority. On "ordinary course of business" the court
  wrongly looked at how the dealer obtained the book rather than at the time of sale, when key and book were in fact
  available. The effect is that a buyer of a second-hand car from a dealer runs a serious risk of getting no title if
  the dealer obtained the book without consent, which undermines the Act's purpose of facilitating commerce. He hoped
  the House of Lords would revisit the cases.
- **Fictional "mercantile agent" tests elsewhere.** Thornely (1965) shows how s 9 FA's reference to a mercantile agent
  forced the court in *Newtons of Wembley* to ask whether a private rogue selling in a street market acted in the
  ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent; Thornely (1988) calls these "absurd fictions" and asks why the
  Factors Act and SGA still duplicate provisions with subtle differences.
- **Balance.** The exception protects those dealing with professionals, consistent with ostensible ownership, but the
  case law's focus on keys and logbooks shows how much turns on fine factual detail. Davies ([1995] JBL 47, motor
  vehicles) criticises the piecemeal patchwork of exceptions for vehicles.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Was the seller/pledgor a mercantile agent (business of selling/buying/consigning/raising money on goods for others;
   *Weiner*, *Lowther*)?
2. Was he in possession of the goods or documents of title at the time of the disposition (*Beverley Acceptances*)?
3. Did the owner consent to that possession, in his capacity as mercantile agent (display, offers - yes; repair - no;
   *Pearson*)? Consent obtained by fraud still counts; withdrawn consent still counts if the buyer had no notice (s
   2(2)).
4. Was there a sale, pledge or other disposition for consideration (not a gift; probably not a mere agreement)? For a
   pledge for an existing debt, s 4.
5. Was the disposition in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent (*Oppenheimer*; car with key and
   documents, business hours, proper premises; *Stadium Finance*)?
6. Can the third party prove good faith and no notice (*Heap*; *Summers v Havard*)?
7. If all satisfied, the disposition is as valid as if authorised: owner's remedy is against the agent.
Traps: confusing the burden of proof with s 23; forgetting that the registration document is not a document of title
but its absence affects ordinary course of business.
