# Rowland v Divall [1923] 2 KB 500 (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the judgments were not read. Check [1923] 2 KB 500 before citing. The exact
> date of judgment and the exact period the car was in use are not confirmed (see below).

## Overview
Court of Appeal, Bankes, Scrutton and Atkin LJJ, 1923. A buyer who bought a car from a seller with no title could recover the whole price as a total failure
of consideration, even though he had used the car for some months before it was taken back by the true owner. The case
is the leading illustration of the implied condition as to title in the Sale of Goods Act (now s 12(1) of the Sale of
Goods Act 1979).

## Facts
The defendant sold a car to the claimant, a car dealer, for £334. Neither party knew the car had been stolen. The
claimant repainted it, displayed it and sold it to a customer for £400. Some months after the original sale (sources
give periods of about two to four months for the claimant's and his customer's use together), police took possession
of the car and it was returned to the true owner. The seller had no title to pass. The claimant sued the defendant to
recover the £334 he had paid.

## Held
- The Court of Appeal allowed the buyer's appeal. The seller was in breach of the implied condition that he had a right
  to sell. Because the buyer had not received what he contracted for, namely the property in the car, the
  consideration for his payment had totally failed.
- The buyer could therefore **recover the full price**, in the way a buyer recovers money paid on a consideration that
  has totally failed, not just damages. His use of the car did not bar recovery, nor reduce the sum, as the
  contract was for the property in the car and he had never had that.

## Effect
- A seller who has no right to sell is in breach of the condition as to title and the buyer can claim the whole price
  back; use of the goods does not reduce the claim.
- The decision has been criticised by commentators (Atiyah called it a fallacy) because the buyer had the use of the
  car for some months. Later cases on its limits (for example where the buyer received some of what he bargained for)
  were not verified for this note.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Check the seller's right to sell at the time of the contract, under s 12(1) SGA 1979 (the condition cannot be
   excluded in a contract: see UCTA 1977, s 6).
2. If the seller had no title (a stolen or subject-to-hire-purchase car, for example), identify the breach of the
   implied condition.
3. Claim the price as money paid for a total failure of consideration, unreduced by the value of the buyer's use
   (*Rowland v Divall*), and consider damages for other losses.
4. Check whether title has since been perfected, or the buyer has affirmed, which may limit the claim.
5. Apply the nemo dat rule: who is the true owner, and could any exception give the seller or buyer title?
