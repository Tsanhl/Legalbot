# The Nemo Dat Rule, Owner's Consent, Estoppel and Special Powers of Sale

## Overview
*Nemo dat quod non habet* - no one can give what they do not have. When a non-owner (a thief, a rogue, an agent
exceeding authority, a seller who has already sold) sells goods, the law must choose between two innocent
parties: the original owner and the good faith purchaser. The SGA starts from protection of property (s 21(1)) but
recognises a series of exceptions that protect commerce. Lectures 10-13 cover the exceptions in turn: (1)
authority/consent of the owner, (2) estoppel, (3) special powers of sale (this note); (4) mercantile agents, (5)
voidable title, (6) seller in possession, (7) buyer in possession, (8) private purchasers of motor vehicles (other
notes). This is a core exam topic for both essays (is the balance right?) and problems (who wins the goods?). Law:
England & Wales.

## The law

### 1. The rule
1. **s 21(1)**: "Subject to this Act, where goods are sold by a person who is not their owner, and who does not sell
   them under the authority or with the consent of the owner, the buyer acquires no better title to the goods than
   the seller had, unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to
   sell." See also *Cundy v Lindsay* (1878) 3 App Cas 459 and *Jerome v Bentley* [1952] 2 All ER 114.
2. The slides split s 21(1) into a *negative part* (a non-owner cannot pass title) and a *positive part* (the
   estoppel proviso). Two problem phrases: "where goods are sold" and "consent of the owner".
3. "Where goods are sold" excludes mere agreements to sell: *Shaw v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [1987]
   1 WLR 1332 (see below). Several commentators think it wrongly decided (handout); Thornely [1988] CLJ 15 thought
   the restriction "seems correct", especially where a buyer claims ownership without having paid.
4. Nemo dat is wider than s 21 (slide 8): it also covers an owner who sells subject to an encumbrance in favour of a
   third party (e.g. a pledgee who returns goods to the pledgor for a limited purpose such as warehouse clearance
   keeps its rights; but not if the goods were returned with unrestricted authority to sell, or if the pledgor is a
   mercantile agent, when FA 1889 s 2(1) may protect the buyer). Equitable interests yield to the bona fide
   purchaser of the legal title for value without notice.
5. Policy: Lord Denning in *Bishopsgate Motor Finance v Transport Brakes*: two principles "have striven for
   mastery" - protection of property and protection of commercial transactions; the first has held sway but has been
   modified by common law and statute.
6. Subject to the exceptions, the original owner can recover the goods (or damages in conversion) from anyone in the
   chain; the innocent buyer's remedy is against its seller under s 12 (see `implied-term-as-to-title-s12.md`).

### 2. Exception 1 - authority or consent of the owner
7. Goods sold with the owner's actual authority pass title under normal agency rules (s 62(2) preserves agency law).
   Apparent (ostensible) authority overlaps with estoppel.
8. **s 47(1)**: the unpaid seller's lien or right of stoppage is not affected by any sale or other disposition by the
   buyer unless the seller has assented to it. If the seller assents to a sub-sale, the sub-buyer takes free of the
   lien/stoppage. Unlike estoppel, the seller's assent need only be communicated to the buyer, not the sub-buyer.

### 3. Exception 2 - estoppel (s 21(1) proviso)
9. **Effect**: the owner is precluded from asserting that the sale was unauthorised, so the sale takes effect as if
   authorised and transfers real ownership. Devlin J in *Eastern Distributors v Goldring* [1957] 2 QB 600: unlike
   "equitable estoppel", it transfers "a real title and not merely a metaphorical title by estoppel".
10. **Requirements common to all estoppels** (slides; revision lecture): a representation (i) of fact, (ii)
    unambiguous, (iii) acted upon (reliance, to the buyer's detriment), (iv) voluntary - one made under duress does
    not count even if relied on (*Debs v Sibec Developments*). No need to prove fraud or intent to deceive.
11. **Estoppel by representation - words**:
    - *Henderson & Co v Williams* [1895] 1 QB 521 (CA): the party with control of the goods was estopped from
      denying the claimant's right to them (the handout refers to Atiyah & Adams p 302 for the facts).
    - *Shaw v Commissioner of Police*: N advertised his Porsche; dealer L said he wanted it for a client; induced by a
      worthless post-dated cheque, N signed a letter and the registration transfer slip saying he had sold to L. L
      agreed to sell to Shaw for a banker's draft and cash, but vanished without payment when the bank refused cash.
      The representations were unambiguous and would have given Shaw title by estoppel had L *sold* to him, but L had
      only *agreed to sell* (property not to pass until payment), so s 21(1) did not apply; s 25(1) failed because L
      had never "bought or agreed to buy" (Thornely). The slides argue s 21 mirrors common law estoppel and should
      protect anyone who acted to their detriment; Shaw's claim should rather have failed because he had not paid -
      i.e. no detrimental reliance.
    - *Eastern Distributors v Goldring*: M owned a van and, with dealer G, deceived finance company E by forms saying G
      owned the van and M wanted it on HP. E bought from G and let it to M; M defaulted and sold to X. M was estopped
      by his representation that G owned the van, so E got good title from G; M had none to pass to X.
12. **Estoppel by conduct - mere possession is not enough**:
    - *Farquharson Bros v King* [1902] AC 325 (HL): a timber importer's clerk exceeded his authority and fraudulently
      sold timber, sending delivery orders to buyers. The owners were not estopped: entrusting the clerk with
      possession/control was no representation, and the buyer had not acted on any representation by the owners.
    - *Mercantile Bank of India v Central Bank of India* [1938] AC 287: allowing merchants to hold railway receipts
      represented no more than possession of the goods themselves would; no estoppel. There must be some act which
      positively misleads.
    - *Central Newbury Car Auctions v Unity Finance* [1957] 1 QB 371 (CA): owner let a rogue take a car and its
      registration book; no estoppel - the book is not a document of title but a warning that the registered keeper
      may not be the owner; entrusting possession is not enough.
    - *Lloyds & Scottish Finance v Williamson*: if the buyer knows the seller is only an agent, there is no estoppel as
      to ownership (only ostensible agency questions).
    - *Motor Credits (Hire Finance) v Pacific Motor Auctions* [1965] AC 867: a representation may be limited by the
      circumstances.
13. **Estoppel by negligence** - severely restricted. There must be (i) a duty of care owed to the buyer, (ii)
    breach, (iii) the breach must be the proximate cause of the loss. A duty is hard to establish; the requirement
    excludes estoppel where the owner parted with goods under a contract void for mistake.
    - *Mercantile Credit v Hamblin* [1965] 2 QB 242 (CA): Mrs Hamblin signed and handed over documents she did not
      read to an apparently trustworthy dealer (Sealy's note); not estopped - trusting him was not unreasonable and, even if careless, the proximate cause of loss was the
      dealer's fraud (Sealy's note in [1965] CLJ adds that the title rules often have nothing to do with the justice of
      the case and refers to Devlin LJ's suggestion of apportionment).
    - *Moorgate Mercantile v Twitchings* [1977] AC 890 (HL, majority): finance company failed to register an HP
      agreement with HPI (voluntary scheme); a dealer checked HPI, was told nothing was registered, and bought from the
      hirer. Neither estoppel by representation (HPI was not the company's agent for that) nor by negligence (no duty to
      register) succeeded. Lord Fraser: an owner "is entitled to be careless with it if he likes". Lord Wilberforce:
      inaction or silence is "colourless" and only acquires positive content where there is a duty to speak or act owed
      to the person prejudiced or a class including them.
14. **Reliance**: *Farquharson* (buyer had not acted on any representation by the owner).

### 4. Exception 3 - special powers of sale (s 21(2)(b))
15. Nothing in s 21 affects the validity of a sale under (i) common law powers - e.g. a pledgee's power of sale (for
    regulated pledges, the Consumer Credit Act 1974 applies); (ii) statutory powers - e.g. enforcement officers selling
    goods seized under a writ of execution; (iii) an order of the court, where for "any just and sufficient reason" it
    is desirable to sell at once (CPR 1998), e.g. perishable goods or a falling market.
16. s 21(2)(a) preserves the Factors Acts and other enactments enabling the apparent owner to dispose of goods as if
    true owner (see mercantile agents note).

### 5. Map of the remaining exceptions
| Exception | Provision | Who must prove good faith |
|---|---|---|
| Mercantile agent | FA 1889 s 2(1) (s 21(2)(a) SGA) | third party |
| Voidable title | SGA s 23 | owner must prove bad faith (*Whitehorn v Davison*) |
| Seller in possession | SGA s 24 / FA s 8 | second buyer |
| Buyer in possession | SGA s 25 / FA s 9 | second buyer |
| Motor vehicles on HP/conditional sale | HPA 1964 Part III s 27 | private purchaser must be in good faith without notice (see that note) |
| Market overt | SGA s 22 - abolished by Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1994 | - |

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 21(1), 21(2), 47(1), 62(2).
- *Bishopsgate Motor Finance v Transport Brakes* - the two competing principles.
- *Shaw v Commissioner of Police* [1987] 1 WLR 1332 - "sold" excludes agreements to sell.
- *Eastern Distributors v Goldring* [1957] 2 QB 600 - estoppel passes real title.
- *Henderson v Williams* [1895] 1 QB 521 - estoppel by words.
- *Farquharson v King* [1902] AC 325; *Mercantile Bank of India* [1938] AC 287; *Central Newbury v Unity Finance*
  [1957] 1 QB 371 - possession alone insufficient.
- *Mercantile Credit v Hamblin* [1965] 2 QB 242; *Moorgate v Twitchings* [1977] AC 890 - negligence.
- *Debs v Sibec* - involuntary representation.

## Debate and critique
- **Which innocent party should lose?** Battersby ("The Sale of Stolen Goods: a Dilemma for the Law" (1991) 54 MLR
  752) frames the tension as ease of transfer v security of ownership. Arguments noted in the materials: the owner is
  often insured (so loss-spreading favours the purchaser); Atiyah called for empirical research on the volume of
  stolen goods and how often innocent purchasers suffer; market overt was archaic. Foster (2004) 9 Cov LJ 1 and Riley
  (2017) 8 QMLJ 99 criticise the exceptions as piecemeal, restrictive and inconsistent; Riley advocates reforms based
  on relative culpability and a broader contextual approach to estoppel, while noting the law's all-or-nothing
  approach rejects proportional apportionment as impractical.
- **Apportionment.** Devlin LJ in *Ingram v Little* proposed that courts be able to divide the loss between two
  innocent victims; Sealy (commenting on *Hamblin*) thought this might soon be law; it was never enacted. The Law
  Reform Committee's 12th Report (1966) considered the nemo dat rule. Turpin (1972) also discussed apportionment
  (contributory negligence) but noted practical difficulties.
- **Estoppel is narrow.** *Moorgate* shows that even careless owners keep their title: a strong preference for
  property. Critics say this ignores that the owner was best placed to prevent the fraud (registering with HPI was
  cheap). Supporters say the law should not impose duties on owners to the world at large.
- **Shaw.** Whether s 21(1) should cover agreements to sell: slides say it should mirror common law estoppel and turn
  on detriment; Thornely accepts the restriction, noting buyers under agreements for unique goods may rely on
  ordinary apparent authority.
- **Relativity of title.** Thornely notes English law treats title to chattels as relative (Battersby & Preston (1972)
  35 MLR 268): exceptions usually pass only the title the relevant owner had.
- **Codification and reform.** Thornely calls for a thorough overhaul of the duplicated Factors Act and SGA provisions,
  pointing to the US modernisation of commercial law.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. List the parties in order (owner -> non-owner -> buyer(s)); the revision lecture stresses identifying whom you
   advise and anticipating the other side's arguments.
2. Did the owner authorise or consent to the sale (actual/apparent authority; s 47 assent)?
3. Estoppel: did the owner make an unambiguous, voluntary representation of fact (words, documents like a signed
   transfer slip, *Eastern Distributors*) on which the buyer relied to its detriment? Was there more than mere
   possession (*Farquharson*, *Central Newbury*)? For negligence, can you find a duty owed to the buyer, breach and
   proximate cause (*Hamblin*, *Moorgate* - usually fails)?
4. Was it a sale, not merely an agreement to sell (*Shaw*)?
5. Special power of sale (pledgee, enforcement officer, court order)?
6. Then work through the statutory exceptions (mercantile agent; s 23; s 24; s 25; HPA Part III).
7. If no exception: owner wins; the buyer sues its seller under s 12 for total failure of consideration/damages.
Traps: treating handing over a registration document as a representation of ownership; forgetting the burden of
proof differs between exceptions.
