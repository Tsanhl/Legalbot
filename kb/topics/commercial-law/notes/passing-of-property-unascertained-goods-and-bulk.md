# Passing of Property in Unascertained Goods and Goods Forming Part of a Bulk

## Overview
Most commercial sales are of unascertained goods - generic goods, goods yet to be made or grown, or an
unidentified part of a bulk. The basic, mandatory rule is that no property can pass until the goods are
ascertained (s 16). Property then passes on unconditional appropriation (s 18 r 5) or by exhaustion of a bulk
(r 5(3)-(4)). Before 1995 this left pre-paying buyers of part of a bulk as unsecured creditors when the seller
became insolvent (*Re Wait*, *Re London Wine*, *Re Goldcorp*). The Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1995, following
Law Com No 215 (1993), added ascertainment by exhaustion and ss 20A-20B, giving pre-paying buyers an undivided
share as owners in common. These rules apply also to consumer sales (CRA s 4). Law: England & Wales (Law Com
215 was a joint report with the Scottish Law Commission).

## The law

### 1. Kinds of unascertained goods (week 9 slide 12)
- Goods to be manufactured or grown by the seller;
- Purely generic goods (seller free to source anywhere);
- An unidentified portion of a specified bulk or whole ("ex-bulk"; risk may pass at a different time).
Lord Mustill in *Re Goldcorp* (case summaries) distinguished generic goods from goods sold ex-bulk, which must
come from a fixed and pre-determined source.

### 2. Section 16
1. "Subject to section 20A below, where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods no property in the
   goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained."
2. It is mandatory and prevails over the parties' intention (slides; Law Com summary: cannot be contracted out of).
3. "Ascertained" is undefined; Atkin LJ in *Re Wait* [1927] 1 Ch 606: identified in accordance with the agreement
   after the contract is made. Lord Mustill in *Goldcorp*: common sense dictates the buyer cannot acquire title until
   it is known to what goods the title relates; it makes no difference what the parties intended if it is
   impossible.
4. *Karlshamns v Eastport* (Millett LJ, per the slides): apart from r 5(1), two essentials - the goods must be ascertained, and the parties must intend property to pass.

### 3. Rule 5(1) - unconditional appropriation with assent
"Where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained or future goods by description, and goods of that
description and in a deliverable state are unconditionally appropriated to the contract, either by the seller
with the assent of the buyer or by the buyer with the assent of the seller, the property in the goods passes to
the buyer; and the assent may be express or implied, and may be given either before or after the appropriation."
Elements:
5. **Goods of the contract description in a deliverable state** (see Rule 1 cases in the specific goods note).
6. **Unconditional appropriation** - goods irrevocably attached or earmarked to this contract:
   - *Carlos Federspiel v Charles Twigg* [1957] 1 Lloyd's Rep 240: bicycles made to order, packed in crates marked
     with the buyer's name, price paid, shipping arranged, but the seller went into receivership before shipment.
     Property had not passed: the intention was that ownership pass on shipment; no correspondence showing an
     earlier intention; no actual or constructive delivery; no intention to put goods at the buyer's risk before
     shipment; no insistence on insurance. The seller's responsibility for shipping was decisive. Setting goods
     aside is not enough while the seller can still change its mind.
   - *Hendy Lennox v Grahame Puttick Engines* [1984] 2 All ER 152: property passed when generators were ready and
     the buyers received invoices and delivery notes bearing the serial numbers.
   - *Healy v Howlett & Sons* [1917] 1 KB 337: 190 boxes of mackerel sent; railway told to earmark 20 for the
     buyer; fish deteriorated. No property passed - before earmarking nothing was identified and the earmarking
     instruction was not irrevocable.
   - *Aldridge v Johnson* (1857) 7 E & B 885: seller filled sacks sent by the buyer with barley - property passed
     though the sacks stayed in the seller's possession.
   - *Langton v Higgins* (1859) 4 H & N 402: buyer's bottles filled with oil - appropriation; the buyer could sue
     a third party to whom the seller sold them. "When a vendee sends his ship, or cart, or cask, or bottle ... and
     he puts the article sold into it, that is a delivery to the vendee."
   - *Noblett v Hopkinson* [1905] 2 KB 214: beer put into the seller's own bottle and set aside overnight - no
     appropriation.
   - *Wardar's v W Norwood* [1968] 2 QB 663: 600 of 1,500 cartons of ox kidneys in a cold store; the delivery
     order was handed to the store and the cartons loaded; they deteriorated afterwards. Property and risk had
     passed when the order was handed over (at the latest when the third party set the goods aside), so the
     buyer bore the loss.
   - *Customs & Excise v Everwine* (see specific goods note) - release notes covering the whole stock of a
     description.
7. **Conditional appropriation** - if the seller reserves rights (e.g. goods released only on payment), property
   does not pass (and see s 19 on reservation of the right of disposal).
8. **Assent** - express or implied, before or after appropriation: *Pignataro v Gilroy* [1919] 1 KB 459 (140 bags of
   rice sold by sample; seller notified the buyer to collect 125 bags in one collection and 15 in another; assent
   to that appropriation was inferred; the buyer did not collect the 15 bags; property had passed and the risk of
   loss was on the buyer).
9. **Rule 5(2) - delivery to a carrier**: where the seller delivers goods to the buyer or a carrier for transmission
   to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal, it is taken to have unconditionally appropriated them.
   But goods shipped still mixed with others cannot pass (still unascertained) - *Healy v Howlett*.

### 4. Rule 5(3)-(4) - ascertainment by exhaustion (added 1995)
10. Where an identified bulk is reduced so that the only goods left are those due to the buyer, they are treated
    as appropriated to the buyer's contract and property passes (the materials: ascertainment "if they are the only
    goods left"; Burns: property passes once the bulk is reduced to the buyer's share). Codifies *Wait & James v Midland Bank* (1926) 24 Ll L Rep 313 (other buyers took delivery
    leaving only the claimant's wheat). See also *The Elafi* [1982] 1 All ER 208.
11. *Re London Wine Co (Shippers)* (1986) BCC 121: company sold wine but kept it; customers got "certificates of
    title", paid storage, but there was no earmarking or segregation. No appropriation by exhaustion (impossible to
    tell which wine was whose); claims that customers had bought the whole stock, or a whole brand, failed; trust
    and equitable arguments also failed (Sealy note).

### 5. No equitable short cut
12. *Re Wait* [1927] 1 Ch 606: W bought 1,000 tons of wheat on a named vessel and sold 500 tons to X, who paid; one
    bill of lading for the whole; W went bankrupt. Majority of the CA: no property passed (insufficient
    appropriation) and Atkin LJ said the SGA, governing transfer of property between buyer and seller, left no room
    for equitable relief (X's claim to an equitable assignment or charge over the 500 tons failed).
13. *Re Goldcorp Exchange* [1995] 1 AC 74 (Privy Council per the handout and Sealy; the case summaries label it
    House of Lords): customers bought non-allocated bullion stored by the company, receiving certificates; the
    company was insolvent with too little gold and a bank holding a floating charge. No legal or equitable property
    passed: the goods were never ascertained; arguments for a trust, fiduciary duty, estoppel or remedial
    constructive trust were rejected.
14. *Re Stapylton Fletcher* [1995] 1 All ER 192: wine segregated from trading stock for storage for customers, though
    not labelled per customer; held ascertained by segregation and customers took as tenants in common
    proportionately; property passed by common intention, not r 5. The slides call it "much criticised"; the
    handout asks whether it is correct and would be decided differently after ss 20A-20B.

### 6. Sections 20A and 20B (Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1995)
15. **s 20A conditions**: (a) a contract for a specified quantity of unascertained goods; (b) the goods form part of
    a bulk identified in the contract or by subsequent agreement; (c) the buyer has paid for some or all of the
    goods.
16. **Effect**: unless otherwise agreed, property in an undivided share passes and the buyer becomes an owner in
    common of the bulk, with "such share as the quantity of goods paid for and due to the buyer out of the bulk
    bears to the quantity of goods in the bulk" (s 20A(3)). Shares adjust proportionately if the bulk shrinks
    (Burns' example: 1,000 tons sold 500/400/100 but only 800 tons - shares become 400/320/80).
17. Risk: the slides note s 20 does not apply to risk of loss where goods are held in co-ownership under s 20A.
18. **s 20B** (deemed consent by a co-owner to dealings in bulk goods - title as listed in CRA s 4(2)): per the
    Burns summary, it allows delivery to co-owners on a first-come, first-served basis, and a co-owner who takes
    delivery is not liable to the other co-owners for a shortfall.
19. Remaining gaps (Burns summary): a buyer can still lose out to a good faith purchaser under s 24 and must sue for
    non-delivery; no statutory scheme for apportioning losses among co-owners if one is insolvent.

## Key authorities
- SGA s 16; s 18 r 5(1)-(4); ss 20A-20B; s 61; CRA s 4.
- *Re Wait* [1927] 1 Ch 606 - no property, no equity.
- *Re Goldcorp* [1995] 1 AC 74 - unascertained bullion; no proprietary claim.
- *Re London Wine* (1986) - no segregation, no title.
- *Re Stapylton Fletcher* [1995] 1 All ER 192 - segregation; tenancy in common; criticised.
- *Carlos Federspiel v Twigg* [1957] 1 Lloyd's Rep 240 - setting aside is not unconditional appropriation.
- *Hendy Lennox* [1984] 2 All ER 152 - serial numbers on documents.
- *Healy v Howlett* [1917] 1 KB 337; *Wardar's v Norwood* [1968] 2 QB 663; *Pignataro v Gilroy* [1919] 1 KB
  459; *Aldridge v Johnson*; *Langton v Higgins*; *Noblett v Hopkinson*.
- *Wait & James v Midland Bank* (1926); *The Elafi* [1982] - exhaustion.
- *Sterns v Vickers* [1923] 1 KB 78 - risk passed before property.

## Debate and critique
- **Why reform was needed (Law Com 215; Burns, "Better Late than Never" (1996) 59 MLR 260).** s 16 meant buyers who
  had paid, and even held shipping documents, had no property: they lost both goods and money on the seller's
  insolvency (*Re Wait*; Scottish *Hayman v McLintock* (1907)), could not sue in tort (*The Aliakmon*) and could
  not use the goods as security. Bills of lading do not by themselves pass ownership (*Sewell v Burdick* (1884)).
  Larger ships and trading of undivided cargo shares in transit increased the problem; *The Gosforth* (1985)
  sub-buyers holding delivery orders lost to an unpaid seller. Traders began choosing German or US law, threatening
  London's shipping, insurance and arbitration business; GAFTA lobbied for reform. The US Uniform Commercial Code
  (Llewellyn) had abandoned "property" as the central organising concept and let buyers of an undivided bulk become
  co-owners (UCC s 2-105(4)); Canada followed. T B Smith criticised the rigid statutory scheme displacing Scots
  common law flexibility, and the piecemeal nature of the reform.
- **Comparative (Law Com 215)**: France passes property in specific goods on agreement but requires
  individualisation for goods sold by weight or measure; German law requires delivery (actual or constructive);
  Dutch law is also delivery-based.
- **Limits of ss 20A-20B**: protection depends on prepayment and an identified bulk; generic sales and unpaid
  buyers remain unsecured; s 24 still exposes co-owners; no insolvency apportionment scheme. Whether *Stapylton
  Fletcher* would now be decided under s 20A is a set question.
- **Property v obligation.** Goode, "Ownership and Obligation in Commercial Transactions" (1987) 103 LQR 433,
  McKendrick (1994) LQR 509 and Sealy [1994] CLJ 443 (set reading) debate whether equity should give pre-paying
  buyers proprietary relief. *Re Wait* and *Goldcorp* say no: the statute is exclusive and proprietary rights
  should not be created to rescue particular creditors at the expense of others (Sealy notes the Privy Council
  refused remedial constructive trusts merely because of insolvency). Campbell [1996] JBL 199 is also listed.
- **Appropriation tests are fact-sensitive.** *Carlos Federspiel* (crates marked, price paid, no property) v *Hendy
  Lennox* (documents with serial numbers suffice) v *Langton v Higgins* (filling the buyer's bottles) - the key is
  irrevocability and who controls the next step.
- **Merrett [2008] CLJ 376** on delivery and possession is set reading; delivery to the buyer or carrier is often the
  practical moment of appropriation.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Are the goods unascertained? (Generic, future, or unidentified part of a bulk.) If specific, use the other note.
2. Has anything happened that ascertains them: unconditional appropriation with assent (r 5(1)); delivery to a
   carrier without reservation (r 5(2)) - but not if still mixed; exhaustion of an identified bulk (r 5(3)-(4))?
3. Was the appropriation irrevocable (*Federspiel*, *Healy*) or merely internal setting aside? Were goods
   identified in documents (*Hendy Lennox*)? Did the buyer supply the containers (*Aldridge*, *Langton*)?
4. Was there assent (express/implied, before/after; *Pignataro*)?
5. If not ascertained: is there an identified bulk, a specified quantity and prepayment? Then s 20A gives an undivided
   share (calculate proportion, s 20A(3)); s 20B for deliveries to other co-owners.
6. If none applies, the buyer has only a personal claim (*Re Wait*, *Goldcorp* - no equitable rescue); consider
   whether risk nonetheless passed (*Sterns v Vickers*).
7. Consequences: insolvency (who gets the goods), risk (s 20; s 20A co-ownership), price action.
Traps: assuming payment passes property; assuming marking goods with the buyer's name suffices; forgetting the
prepayment requirement in s 20A; arguing equitable title after *Goldcorp*.
