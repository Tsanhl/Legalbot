# Meaning and Significance of Property and Risk

## Overview
"Property" in the SGA means general property - ownership - as opposed to possession or a special property
(s 61). The moment property passes is one of the most important events in a sale because so much hangs on
it: who bears accidental loss (risk follows property under s 20(1)), whether the seller can sue for the
price (s 49), who wins on either party's insolvency, who can sue third parties in tort, whether the
contract can be frustrated (s 7), and who can pass title on resale. This note explains *why* passing of
property matters and the risk rules; *when* property passes is in the two passing-of-property notes. Law:
England & Wales; for trader-to-consumer sales CRA s 29 displaces the property-based risk rule.

## The law

### 1. Meaning of property
1. Property = "general property in goods" (s 61): title or ownership. It is what the buyer bargains for,
   not possession or use. Property rights bind third parties; contract rights bind only the parties.
2. Three things to keep apart: the object (the goods), the right to immediate control
   (possession) and the general interest (ownership). Battersby & Preston (1972) 35 MLR 268 and Ho (1997) 56
   CLJ 571 are set reading on the concept.
3. *Sale* (property passes under the contract) v *agreement to sell* (property to pass later); an agreement
   becomes a sale when the time passes or condition is met (s 2(6)).
4. Passing of property does not by itself give the buyer a title good against the world or a right to
   possession against the seller: under s 28 the buyer's right to possession depends on paying the price
   (or being given credit), not on property.
5. The importance of ownership is shown by total failure of consideration: if the buyer does not get the
   ownership bargained for, it can recover the whole price even after considerable use (see
   `implied-term-as-to-title-s12.md`).

### 2. Consequences: sale v agreement to sell
| | Sale (property passed) | Agreement to sell (property not passed) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | executed | executory |
| Buyer's rights | in rem; buyer can sue third parties | in personam; only seller sues third parties |
| Buyer's remedy | damages for non-delivery (s 51), and in tort | damages for non-delivery (s 51) |
| Seller's remedy | damages for non-acceptance (s 50) and action for the price (s 49) | damages for non-acceptance (s 50) only; seller still responsible for goods |
| Resale | seller cannot pass good title (subject to s 24); buyer can | seller can pass title; buyer can only agree to sell |
| Risk (s 20) | buyer | seller |
| Perishing | no frustration | may be frustrated (s 7) |
| Requisition | loss on buyer; compensation to buyer | loss on seller; compensation to seller |
| Seller insolvent | buyer can claim the goods | buyer only a creditor for money paid and damages |
| Buyer insolvent | seller cannot reclaim goods, only prove for price | seller can keep/claim back goods |

Qualifications:
- **Retention of title (ROT/Romalpa clauses)** let possession and risk pass while title stays with the
  seller until full payment - protecting the seller on the buyer's insolvency. Some, as charges, are void
  unless registered under Companies Act 2006 s 859A. Simple clauses are usually enforceable; extended ones
  raise problems (see `retention-of-title-clauses.md`).
- **Seller in possession resells**: s 24 lets the seller pass good title to a bona fide transferee even after
  property has passed (*Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits*; *Worcester Works Finance v Cooden
  Engineering* [1972] 1 QB 210 - capacity in which the seller keeps possession is immaterial). Not where the
  buyer took possession and returned the goods to the seller as a bailee.
- **Seller insolvent with goods still in its possession**: the buyer's rights may be affected by the Bills
  of Sale Acts (individual seller) or the Companies Act registration regime (company seller).
- **Documents of title**: if the buyer transfers documents of title to a third party, the seller's rights may
  be defeated.

### 3. Risk: the default rule (s 20)
6. **s 20(1)**: unless otherwise agreed, goods remain at the seller's risk until property is transferred;
   once property passes, they are at the buyer's risk whether or not delivered ("res perit domino": the loss
   falls on the owner).
7. Risk means the risk of accidental destruction, loss or damage (theft, fire, flood, sinking). The parties
   can allocate it differently by agreement (e.g. force majeure clauses), and fault displaces it.
8. **s 20(2) - delay by fault**: where delivery is delayed through the fault of either party, the goods are
   at the risk of the party at fault as regards any loss that might not have occurred but for the fault.
   - *Demby Hamilton v Barden* [1949] 1 All ER 435: 30 tons of apple juice to be collected weekly; the buyer
     failed to collect, the juice went off; loss on the buyer.
9. **s 20(3) - bailees**: nothing in s 20 affects the duties of either party as bailee of the goods for the
   other. *Wiehe v Dennis Bros* (1913) 29 TLR 250: pony injured while in the seller's custody; the seller,
   as bailee who had shown no care, was liable.
10. **Effect between the parties**: if risk has passed and the goods are lost, the buyer must pay
    the full price and cannot sue for non-delivery; if risk has not passed, the seller cannot compel payment
    or acceptance of the remains.
11. **Risk separated from property by agreement or circumstances.**
    - *Head v Tattersall* (1871): horse sold with a wrong description, with a right to return by a set time;
      injured before return. Buyer could return it and recover the price: property had vested only
      provisionally.
    - *Sterns v Vickers* [1923] 1 KB 78: 120,000 gallons of white spirit from a larger tank; the buyer took a
      delivery warrant and left the spirit in store; it deteriorated before separation. Property had not
      passed (unascertained), but the Court of Appeal held risk had passed to the buyer.
    - *Inglis v Stock* (1884) 12 QBD 564: under FOB terms the buyer bore risk from shipment though goods
      were unascertained.
    - Unascertained generic goods: Commentary states transfer of risk generally depends on prior
      ascertainment; risk in a specified bulk is the main exception (ss 20A-20B; see bulk note).
12. **Delivery and transit**: s 32(1) delivery to a carrier (non-consumers); s 32(2) unreasonable carriage
    contract; s 32(3) sea transit - the seller must give notice enabling the buyer to insure, or the goods
    are at the seller's risk during the sea transit; s 33 buyer bears deterioration necessarily incident to
    transit where the seller agrees to deliver at its own risk elsewhere.
13. **Consumers - CRA s 29**: goods remain at the trader's risk until they come into the physical possession
    of the consumer or a person identified by the consumer (s 29(2)); exception where the consumer arranges
    the carrier and the trader did not offer it (s 29(3)-(4)). This replaced the former s 20(4) SGA. For all
    other contracts s 20 SGA applies.

### 4. Action for the price (s 49)
14. The seller can sue for the price once property has passed and the buyer wrongfully fails to pay
    (s 49(1)), or where the price is payable on a day certain irrespective of delivery (s 49(2)). The seller
    must be ready and willing to deliver (s 28). If neither property nor possession has passed and the buyer
    repudiates, the remedy is damages for non-acceptance (s 50) - for example losses on contracts with third
    parties.

### 5. Suing third parties
15. A right of property is needed to sue in tort for damage to goods; a purely contractual right gives
    only pure economic loss. *Leigh & Sillavan v Aliakmon Shipping* (The Aliakmon): goods at the buyer's
    risk from shipment but property had not passed; the buyer could not sue the shipowner in negligence for
    damage in transit. The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1992 now gives the buyer a contractual right against
    the carrier.

### 6. Frustration link
16. After a sale has been concluded the contract is not frustrated if the goods perish: it is executed and
    risk has passed. During an agreement to sell the contract may be frustrated (s 7). Common law frustration
    still applies via s 62(2) (Lord Simon's elements in *National Carriers v Panalpina* [1981] AC 675). See
    `frustration-and-perishing-of-goods-s7.md`.

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 2(4)-(6), 20(1)-(3), 28, 32, 33, 49, 50, 51, 61; CRA s 29.
- *Demby Hamilton v Barden* [1949] 1 All ER 435 - buyer's delay; risk on buyer (s 20(2)).
- *Wiehe v Dennis Bros* (1913) 29 TLR 250 - seller as careless bailee liable (s 20(3)).
- *Head v Tattersall* (1871) - provisional property; buyer could return injured horse.
- *Sterns v Vickers* [1923] 1 KB 78 - risk passed before property in undivided bulk.
- *Inglis v Stock* (1884) 12 QBD 564 - FOB risk from shipment for unascertained goods.
- *The Aliakmon* - no tort claim without property; now CoGSA 1992.
- *Pacific Motor Auctions v Motor Credits*; *Worcester Works Finance v Cooden* [1972] 1 QB 210 - s 24.
- Companies Act 2006 s 859A - registration of charges (ROT).

## Debate and critique
- **Should risk follow property?** Linking risk to property (res perit domino) is criticised because
  property passes at abstract moments (often the moment of contract for specific goods under s 18 r 1),
  whereas the party able to insure and look after the goods is the one in possession. Sealy, "Risk in the
  Law of Sale" [1972] CLJ 225 is set reading. The secondary source on the timing of risk asks precisely
  whether risk should be linked to property or to the situation (possession/control) of the goods. It
  argues English law takes a mixed approach: the primary rule is really party intention ("unless otherwise
  agreed"), with res perit domino as the default and ss 20(2) and 32 operating as exceptions where risk
  passes separately from property. It notes cases pull in different directions (*Sterns v Vickers* and
  *Head v Tattersall* both separate risk from property, on different bases).
- **Consumer reform as evidence.** CRA s 29 ties consumer risk to physical possession, effectively accepting
  the critique for consumers and leaving businesses with the property-based default (which they routinely
  contract around).
- **International practice.** Enakireru and Airen (Nigerian commercial law journal, 2020) stress the harshness of risk rules and recommend parties use INCOTERMS in international
  sales; they describe risk chiefly as "price risk" (whether the buyer must pay despite loss) and
  distinguish it from liability of a party in breach. They call for a modern, fit-for-purpose sale statute.
- **Property as a bundle of consequences.** Commentators suggest the best way to understand property is by
  its consequences. This invites criticism that one event (passing of property) is made to answer many
  unrelated questions (risk, insolvency, tort standing, frustration), whereas other systems separate them.
  Merrett [2008] CLJ 376 on the importance of delivery and possession in passing title is set reading.
- **Insolvency and ROT.** Keeping property to protect against insolvency is commercially vital but creates
  hidden security, hence the registration debate (ROT note).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Classify the goods (specific/unascertained; in a bulk?) and the buyer (consumer or not).
2. Consumer? Apply CRA s 29: risk with the trader until physical possession (check carrier exception).
3. Business sale: did the contract allocate risk expressly or by trade terms (FOB/CIF; force majeure)?
4. If not, when did property pass (ss 16-19, 20A)? Risk follows (s 20(1)).
5. Was delivery delayed by anyone's fault (s 20(2), *Demby Hamilton*)? Was either party a careless bailee
   (s 20(3), *Wiehe*)?
6. Carriage issues: s 32(2) reasonable contract; s 32(3) sea transit insurance notice; s 33 transit
   deterioration.
7. Consequence: if risk on buyer - buyer pays and cannot claim non-delivery; if on seller - no price, and
   consider frustration (s 7) for specific goods.
8. Other consequences to mention as relevant: action for price (s 49), insolvency, standing to sue third
   parties (*The Aliakmon*), ability to resell.
Traps: assuming risk passes on delivery in a B2B sale; forgetting CRA s 29; treating passing of property as
giving a right to possession without payment.
