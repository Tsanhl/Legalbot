# Implied Terms as to Title and Quiet Possession (s 12 SGA; s 17 CRA)

## Overview
The most basic thing a buyer pays for is ownership. Section 12 SGA implies (1) a condition that the seller has the
right to sell, and (2) warranties that the goods are free from undisclosed charges or encumbrances and that the buyer
will enjoy quiet possession. Where the parties intend only a limited title to be transferred, s 12(3)-(5) substitute
narrower terms. Breach of s 12(1) gives the buyer a powerful remedy: recovery of the whole price for total failure of
consideration, even after long use (*Rowland v Divall*). Liability under s 12 cannot be excluded (UCTA s 6(1)); for
consumers, s 17 CRA replaces s 12 and cannot be excluded (s 31 CRA). This is the buyer's main remedy when it loses the
goods to the true owner under the nemo dat rule. Law: England & Wales.

## The law

### 1. Classification of terms
1. Conditions v warranties v innominate terms. Breach of condition allows the buyer to treat the contract as repudiated;
   breach of warranty gives damages only, no right to reject (s 11(3)). s 12(5A) classifies: s 12(1) is a condition;
   s 12(2) terms are warranties.

### 2. s 12(1) - right to sell (condition)
"In a contract of sale, other than one to which subsection (3) ... applies, there is an implied term on the part of the
seller that in the case of a sale he has a right to sell the goods, and in the case of an agreement to sell he will
have such a right at the time when the property is to pass."
2. **Right to sell is wider than title.**
   - *Niblett v Confectioners' Materials* [1921] 3 KB 387 (CA): condensed milk labelled "Nissly" infringed Nestlé's
     trademark, so Nestlé could obtain an injunction to restrain its sale. The seller had no right to sell even though
     it owned the tins (Atkin LJ: the seller was liable to be restrained by injunction), and there was also a breach of
     quiet possession because the buyers had to strip the labels before using the goods. The case summary records that
     *Monforts v Marsden* was disapproved.
   - The PQ guide notes, from *Niblett*, the unsettled point that a seller may not be in breach if the buyer acquires
     a right to sell notwithstanding the seller's own inability.
3. **A seller need not itself own the goods** if it can procure a transfer: Mustill J in *The Elafi* [1982] 1 All ER
   208 - a contract can be performed by a seller who never has title, by causing a third party to transfer it directly
   to the buyer.
4. **Exceptions to nemo dat** - even where a buyer gets good title through an exception, the seller who was not the
   owner may still be in breach of s 12(1).
5. **Remedy: total failure of consideration.** *Rowland v Divall* [1923] 2 KB 500 (CA): a dealer bought a car from D,
   repainted it, displayed it and sold it to a customer; two months later police took it as stolen and returned it to
   the owner. The dealer refunded its customer and recovered the whole £334 from D. Atkin LJ: the buyer did not receive
   "any part of that which he contracted to receive - namely, the property and right to possession"; use of the car
   made no difference; total failure of consideration. Bankes LJ: the buyer got no portion of what it agreed to buy.
   Scrutton LJ: when the title condition is broken the buyer can rescind and recover the price unless, knowing the
   facts, it affirmed; the seller cannot rely on the impossibility of restitutio in integrum since that results from
   its own lack of title. Atkin LJ: no obligation to return the car, as the seller had no right to receive it.
6. **Feeding title.** *Butterworth v Kingsway Motors* [1954] 1 WLR 1286: a hirer under HP mistakenly thought she could
   sell while paying the instalments; the car passed through several hands to C, who used it for almost a year before
   the finance company claimed it. C wrote to rescind and claim the price; within a week the hirer paid off the HP, so
   title passed to her and "fed" the defective titles down the chain. Held: C's rescission before the title was fed was
   valid; C recovered the full price despite extensive use.
7. **Express title terms** - *Barber v NWS Bank* [1996] 1 WLR 641 (CA): under a conditional sale, an express term that
   the car was the seller's sole property free of encumbrance was a condition (like s 12); the car was in fact subject to
   a prior HP agreement; the buyer could rescind and recover the deposit and all instalments.
8. *Heathrow Truck Centre v Motability Operations* (2021, County Court; case summaries): a possessory, inferior title can
   be passed down a chain, and payment can feed the superior title down the chain per *Butterworth*.
9. **Defence**: the PQ guide notes the seller may raise change of position (*Lipkin Gorman*) where restitution of the
   price would be inequitable.

### 3. s 12(2) - freedom from encumbrances and quiet possession (warranties)
"(a) the goods are free, and will remain free until the time when the property is to pass, from any charge or
encumbrance not disclosed or known to the buyer before the contract is made, and (b) the buyer will enjoy quiet
possession of the goods except so far as it may be disturbed by the owner or other person entitled to the benefit of any
charge or encumbrance so disclosed or known."
10. s 12(2)(a): *Lloyds & Scottish Finance v Modern Cars* [1966] 1 QB 764.
11. s 12(2)(b) is a continuing warranty, so it can be broken by events after the sale:
    - *Mason v Burningham* [1949] 2 KB 545: stolen typewriter; buyer sued under s 12(2)(b) - shows the overlap with
      s 12(1).
    - *Microbeads v Vinhurst Road Markings* [1975] 1 All ER 529 (CA): road-marking machines sold when a third party's
      patent applications were pending; patents granted later. No breach of s 12(1) (seller had a right to sell at the
      time) but the seller could be in breach of s 12(2)(b) when the buyer's possession was disturbed after the patent
      was published.
    - *The Playa Larga* [1983] 2 Lloyd's Rep 171: seller ordered the ship not to unload the sugar - wrongful disturbance
      by the seller itself.
    - *Rubicon Computer Systems v United Paints* (2000) 2 TCLR 454: supplier secretly installed a time-lock that made the
      computer system unusable in a dispute over its obligations - breach of s 12(2)(b).
12. Scope: the buyer is protected against wrongful disturbance by the seller, and against lawful disturbance by
    a third party if due to the seller's fault or a superior right impairing the buyer's title or possession; not against
    unlawful disturbance by a stranger (e.g. a trespasser).

### 4. s 12(3)-(5) - sale of a limited title
13. s 12(3): applies where it appears from the contract or is inferred from the circumstances that the seller should
    transfer only such title as he or a third person may have (e.g. a finder's or possessory title).
14. s 12(4): implied term that all charges or encumbrances known to the seller and not known to the buyer have been
    disclosed.
15. s 12(5): implied term that the buyer's quiet possession will not be disturbed by (a) the seller, (b) where the title
    of a third person is being transferred, that person, or (c) anyone claiming through or under the seller or that third
    person otherwise than under a disclosed or known charge or encumbrance.

### 5. Exclusion and consumers
16. **UCTA s 6(1)**: liability for breach of s 12 cannot be excluded or restricted by any contract term - void.
17. **s 12(7)** SGA (as amended by the CRA): s 12 does not apply to contracts within Chapter 2 of Part 1 CRA; **s 17 CRA**
    applies: the trader must have the right to sell/transfer the goods when ownership is to be transferred (or, for hire,
    to transfer possession for the hire period); goods free from undisclosed charges/encumbrances and quiet possession;
    s 17(4)-(7) limited title equivalents. Remedies: s 19 CRA. **s 31 CRA**: liability under s 17 cannot be excluded or
    restricted. Digital content: s 41 CRA.

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 11(3), 12(1)-(5A), 12(7); UCTA s 6(1); CRA ss 17, 19, 31.
- *Rowland v Divall* [1923] 2 KB 500 - total failure of consideration despite use.
- *Niblett v Confectioners' Materials* [1921] 3 KB 387 - right to sell includes freedom from injunction.
- *The Elafi* [1982] 1 All ER 208 - seller may procure third-party transfer.
- *Butterworth v Kingsway Motors* [1954] 1 WLR 1286 - feeding title; rescission before feeding valid.
- *Barber v NWS Bank* [1996] 1 WLR 641 - express title term as condition.
- *Microbeads v Vinhurst* [1975] 1 All ER 529 - later disturbance by patent.
- *Mason v Burningham* [1949] 2 KB 545; *Rubicon v United Paints* (2000); *The Playa Larga* [1983] - quiet possession.
- *Lloyds & Scottish Finance v Modern Cars* [1966] 1 QB 764 - encumbrances.

## Debate and critique
- **Windfall to the buyer?** *Rowland v Divall* and *Butterworth* let a buyer recover the whole price after months of
  use. Commentators highlight that in *Butterworth* the buyer recovered the full price "even though extensive use of car" and
  even though title was fed within a week of the rescission letter - the buyer escaped what had become a bad bargain.
  Supporters (Atkin LJ) say the buyer bargained for property and right to possession, not mere use; the seller who sold
  what it did not own should bear the loss. The PQ guide notes change of position as a possible limit.
  The property note makes the same point: the importance of ownership under the Act is shown by recovery of the whole price
  as total failure of consideration even after possession and use for a considerable time.
- **Right to sell v title.** *Niblett* extends s 12(1) beyond ownership to freedom from legal restraint (IP rights), which
  protects buyers but can make sellers liable for third-party rights they did not know of. *Microbeads* channels
  post-sale IP problems into the warranty of quiet possession instead.
- **Interaction with nemo dat.** Where an exception gives the buyer title, the buyer usually has no loss; where the owner
  recovers the goods, s 12 is the buyer's only route - against a seller who may itself be an innocent intermediary
  (*Rowland v Divall*), producing chains of claims back to the rogue.
- **Non-excludability** (UCTA s 6(1), CRA s 31) reflects the view that title is fundamental.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is it a B2B sale (s 12 SGA) or trader-consumer (s 17 CRA)?
2. Did the seller lack the right to sell at the time property was to pass - stolen goods, goods on HP, goods infringing IP
   (*Niblett*)? Could the seller have procured a transfer (*The Elafi*)?
3. If breach of s 12(1): condition - the buyer can reject/rescind and recover the whole price for total failure of
   consideration (*Rowland*), even after use; has the buyer affirmed with knowledge? Was title later fed, and did the buyer
   rescind first (*Butterworth*)?
4. Undisclosed charge/encumbrance (s 12(2)(a)) or later disturbance of possession by the seller or a third party with a
   superior right (s 12(2)(b); *Microbeads*, *Rubicon*, *Playa Larga*)? Warranty - damages only.
5. Was only a limited title sold (s 12(3)-(5))?
6. Any exclusion clause is void (UCTA s 6(1); CRA s 31).
