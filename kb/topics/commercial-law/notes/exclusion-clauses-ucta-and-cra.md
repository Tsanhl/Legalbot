# Exclusion and limitation clauses in sales (common law control, UCTA 1977, CRA 2015)

## Overview
Sellers commonly try to exclude or cap liability for breach of the implied terms and for related claims. English law
controls such clauses in three layers: the common law (incorporation and construction), the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977
(UCTA) for business contracts, and, for consumers, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA), which now governs consumer contracts
entirely. The sale-of-goods provisions (UCTA s 6; CRA s 31) are the most heavily protected, and the title term in s 12 SGA
is the most protected of all.

## The law

### 1. Definitions
- An **exclusion (exemption) clause** excuses a party from liability that would otherwise arise under or in connection with
  the contract; in consumer sales it is nearly always the seller who relies on one.
- A **limitation clause** restricts liability, for example by capping damages (*George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds* [1983]
  2 AC 803) or restricting enforcement of the other side's rights.

### 2. Common law control
1. **Incorporation.** The clause must be part of the contract: by signature (*L'Estrange v Graucob* [1934] 2 KB 394) or by
   giving reasonable notice (*Parker v South Eastern Railway* (1877) 2 CPD 416; *Olley v Marlborough Court* [1949] 1 KB 532;
   *Interfoto v Stiletto* [1988] QB 433, cited by the handout for onerous terms and notice).
2. **Construction.** Does the wording cover the breach that happened? Examples listed: *Photo Production v Securicor*
   [1980] AC 827; *Houghton v Trafalgar Insurance* [1954] 1 QB 247. *Interactive E-Solutions JLT v O3B Africa* [2018] EWCA Civ
   62: the Court of Appeal upheld a widely drafted limitation clause which stopped short of excluding liability for fraud.

### 3. UCTA 1977 (business contracts)
1. **Scope.** s 1(3): applies to terms excluding or limiting "business liability". Schedule 1 lists excluded contract types
   (including insurance, contracts creating or transferring interests in land, patents or trade marks, and company formation).
2. **s 2**: negligence liability (the handout only names s 2; for consumers, CRA ss 62 and 65 replace it).
3. **s 3**: where one party deals on the other's standard terms of business, the other cannot, as against that party,
   exclude or restrict liability for its own breach, or claim to render a substantially different performance or none at all,
   except so far as the term satisfies the requirement of reasonableness.
4. **s 6 (sale and hire purchase).**
   - Liability under s 12 SGA (title) cannot be excluded or restricted by any contract term (s 6(1)).
   - Liability under ss 13, 14 and 15 SGA (description, quality/fitness, sample) cannot be excluded or restricted except so
     far as the term satisfies reasonableness (s 6(1A)).
   - s 6(4): these liabilities are covered whether or not they are "business liability" under s 1(3), i.e. they apply to any
     contract of sale or hire purchase.
5. **s 7**: for contracts where possession or ownership of goods passes but which are not sales or hire purchase (the handout
   notes the Supply of Goods and Services provisions): description/sample/quality/fitness liability only excludable if
   reasonable (s 7(1A)); liability under s 2 of the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982 (title) cannot be excluded (s 7(3A));
   right to transfer and quiet possession otherwise only if reasonable (s 7(4)).
6. **s 13**: anti-avoidance. Where UCTA prevents exclusion of liability it also prevents making the liability or its
   enforcement subject to restrictive or onerous conditions, excluding or restricting the right or remedy, or restricting
   rules of evidence or procedure; ss 2, 6 and 7 extend to terms and notices that exclude the underlying obligation.
7. **Reasonableness (s 11 and Sch 2).** The term must have been "fair and reasonable" to include, having regard to the
   circumstances known or in the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made. For s 6 the Schedule 2 guidelines
   apply: (a) relative bargaining strength and alternatives; (b) any inducement to agree or opportunity to contract
   elsewhere without the term; (c) whether the customer knew or ought to have known of the term (trade custom, course of
   dealing); (d) where the term depends on compliance with a condition, whether compliance was practicable; (e) whether the
   goods were made, processed or adapted to the customer's special order.
8. **Reasonableness cases (listed):** *George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds* [1983] 2 AC 803; *St Albans CDC v International
   Computers* [1996] 4 All ER 481; *Britvic Soft Drinks v Messer* [2002] EWCA Civ 548; *Bacardi-Martini Beverages v Hardy*
   [2002] EWCA Civ 549. The handout lists them without holdings.

### 4. Consumer contracts: CRA 2015, Part 2
The CRA revoked the Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Regulations 1999 and amended UCTA: ss 2, 3, 6 and 7 UCTA no longer
apply to consumer contracts (replaced by CRA ss 62, 65 and 31).
1. **s 31 (goods contracts): blacklisted exclusions.** A term is not binding on the consumer to the extent that it would
   exclude or restrict the trader's liability under: s 9 (quality), s 10 (fitness), s 11 (as described), s 12 (pre-contract
   information), s 13 (sample), s 14 (model), s 15 (installation), s 16 (digital content non-conformity), s 17 (right to
   supply), s 28 (delivery) or s 29 (risk). Also caught: terms excluding rights or remedies, imposing onerous conditions,
   disadvantaging a consumer who pursues a remedy, or restricting evidence/procedure; and terms that prevent an obligation
   arising or limit its extent. An agreement to arbitrate is not an exclusion for this purpose. Hire contracts have a modified
   rule for s 17. s 47 repeats this for digital content (ss 34-37 and 41).
2. **s 61**: Part 2 covers consumer contracts and consumer notices, including notices that purport to exclude liability,
   whether or not expressed to apply to consumers, if it is reasonable to assume a consumer will see or hear them.
3. **s 62: fairness.** An unfair term or notice is not binding on the consumer (who may choose to rely on it). A term is
   unfair if, contrary to the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and
   obligations to the consumer's detriment. Fairness is assessed by reference to the nature of the subject matter, all
   circumstances when the term was agreed, and all other terms of the contract (or any contract on which it depends). s 62
   does not affect ss 31, 47, 57 and 65.
4. **s 63 and Sch 2 Part 1 (grey list).** Non-exhaustive list of terms that may be unfair, still tested under s 62. Examples
   quoted: para 2 (inappropriately excluding or limiting legal rights on non-performance or inadequate performance, including
   set-off); para 4 (trader keeps sums if consumer does not proceed, with no equivalent compensation where trader cancels);
   para 5 and para 6 (disproportionately high compensation payable by a consumer who withdraws or defaults).
5. **s 64: core terms.** Terms specifying the main subject matter, or concerning the appropriateness of the price, escape the
   fairness test, but only if transparent (plain, intelligible, legible) and prominent (such that an average, reasonably
   well-informed, observant and circumspect consumer would be aware of it). The exemption does not apply to Sch 2 Part 1
   terms.
6. **s 65**: no exclusion or restriction of a trader's liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence.
7. **s 67**: if a term is unfair, the rest of the contract continues. **s 68**: notices must be transparent. **s 69**:
   ambiguity is resolved in the consumer's favour.

## Key authorities
- *L'Estrange v Graucob*; *Parker v SE Rly*; *Olley v Marlborough Court*; *Interfoto v Stiletto* (incorporation).
- *Photo Production v Securicor*; *Houghton v Trafalgar*; *Interactive E-Solutions v O3B* (construction).
- *George Mitchell v Finney Lock Seeds* (limitation clause and reasonableness); *St Albans v ICL*; *Britvic v Messer*;
  *Bacardi-Martini v Hardy* (reasonableness).
- UCTA ss 1(3), 2, 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, Sch 1, Sch 2; CRA ss 31, 47, 61-65, 67-69, Sch 2.

## Debate and critique
The handout refers to Beale, "Legislative Control of Fairness" (in Beatson and Friedman, *Good Faith and Fault in Contract
Law*, 1995), Willett on good faith and consumer terms, and the CMA's unfair terms guidance (CMA 37, 2015) as further reading.
Points to draw out: UCTA's reasonableness test is a case-by-case discretion (a clause can pass in one deal and fail in
another), whereas the CRA uses a "good faith / significant imbalance" test plus a
fixed blacklist in s 31 which removes discretion for the core sale terms. The consumer regime thus has three tiers:
blacklisted (s 31, s 65), grey-listed (Sch 2), and generally unfair (s 62). The transparency-and-prominence condition on core
terms is a policy compromise between freedom of contract in price and subject matter and consumer protection.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify which term was breached: s 12 (cannot be excluded, business sale), ss 13-15 (reasonableness), or something else
   (s 3 UCTA or common law only).
2. Is the buyer a consumer? If yes, go to CRA s 31/s 62/s 65 and ignore UCTA. If no, use UCTA.
3. Is the clause incorporated (signature, notice, onerous terms)? Does it, on construction, cover the breach?
4. Is the clause a limitation rather than an exclusion? Reasonableness still applies; consider the cap relative to the loss
   and insurance (use Sch 2 factors and the cases listed).
5. Standard terms? s 3 UCTA depends on one party dealing on the other's standard terms.
6. Watch for clauses that disguise exclusion (remedy restrictions, onerous time limits): s 13 UCTA and s 31(2) CRA.
