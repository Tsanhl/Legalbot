# Passing of Property in Specific Goods (ss 17-18 Rules 1-4 SGA)

## Overview
When does ownership move from seller to buyer? The SGA answers in layers: s 16 (unascertained goods cannot
pass until ascertained), s 17 (for specific or ascertained goods, property passes when the parties intend),
and s 18 (five presumptive rules used only when intention is unclear). This note covers specific goods
(Rules 1-3) and goods on approval or sale or return (Rule 4); unascertained goods (Rule 5, s 20A) are in the
companion note. The timing matters for risk, insolvency, the action for the price and frustration (see
`property-and-risk.md`). The same rules apply to trader-to-consumer contracts: CRA s 4(2) points to SGA ss 16,
17, 18, 19, 20A and 20B.

## The law

### 1. Method (week 8 slide 4)
1. **s 16** - are the goods ascertained? If not, no property can pass (subject to s 20A).
2. **s 17** - if specific/ascertained, when did the parties intend property to pass? s 17(2): regard is had to
   the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case.
3. **s 18** - only if intention cannot be found, apply the relevant Rule ("unless a different intention
   appears").

### 2. Specific goods
4. s 61: goods "identified and agreed upon at the time a contract of sale is made". Future goods cannot be
   specific. The revision lecture adds: the seller cannot substitute other goods that fit the description;
   examples are goods identified by serial number (*Hendy Lennox*) or pointed out by the buyer in the shop.
   - *Kursell v Timber Operators*: all trees in a Latvian forest meeting certain measurements - not
     sufficiently identified (not all trees were to pass), so property did not pass.
   - *Joseph Reid v Schultz*: all millable/marketable hardwood timber on a site - specific goods.
   - *Customs and Excise Commissioners v Everwine*: wine held by a warehouse (X); release notes faxed to X on
     each sale. Where the note covered the whole of X's stock of that description that day, property passed;
     where it did not, the goods were not sufficiently specified.

### 3. Rule 1 - unconditional contract, specific goods, deliverable state
"Where there is an unconditional contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state the property in
the goods passes to the buyer when the contract is made, and it is immaterial whether the time of payment or
the time of delivery, or both, be postponed."
5. **Unconditional.** *Varley v Whipp* [1900] 1 QB 513 treated a contract as conditional because the implied
   conditions of description (s 13) and merchantable quality remained unfulfilled. The slides warn this would
   make it impossible to know whether property had passed until the goods were inspected; the modern meaning
   is "not subject to any condition upon the fulfilment of which the passing of property depends". The handout
   warns against reading *Varley* too literally. Stoljar (1953) 69 LQR 485 is set reading.
   - *Dennant v Skinner and Collom*: car sold at auction; property passed on the fall of the hammer; the
     buyer's later signature on a document saying title would not pass until his cheque cleared came too late.
   - Payment by cheque is a conditional discharge of the payment obligation and does not stop Rule 1 operating
     (*Anderson v Havana Horse*, per slides).
6. **Deliverable state.** s 61(5): goods are in a deliverable state when they are in such a state that the
   buyer would under the contract be bound to take delivery of them. The slides note that defects do not
   prevent property passing, and that the phrase concerns physical readiness the buyer agreed to accept.
   - *Underwood v Burgh Castle Brick and Cement Syndicate* [1922] 1 KB 343: a condensing engine cemented to the
     floor had to be detached and dismantled (the seller's job under an FOB contract): not deliverable; property
     had not passed.
   - *Head v Showfronts* [1970] 1 Lloyd's Rep 140: carpet to be laid by the seller, in a heavy bundle, stolen
     before laying: not deliverable; property had not passed.
   - *Kulkarni v Manor Credit (Davenham)* [2010] EWCA Civ 69: a new car was not deliverable until number plates
     were fitted so it could lawfully be driven.
7. **Contrary intention** (displacing Rule 1):
   - (a) *Express agreement*, e.g. retention of title: *Aluminium Industrie v Romalpa* [1976] 1 WLR 676. Absent
     express terms the onus is on the seller to displace the presumption.
   - (b) *Consumer/retail sales*: *R V Ward v Bignall* [1967] 1 QB 534 (Diplock LJ): "in modern times very
     little is needed" to infer that property in specific goods passes only on delivery or payment. Shop: after
     mode of payment agreed; supermarket: on payment (*Davies v Leighton*); petrol station: when petrol enters
     the tank (*Edwards v Ddin*).
   - (c) *Postponed delivery/payment* is not itself contrary intention (Rule 1 says it is immaterial).
     *Clark v Michael Reilly*: old car taken in part-exchange but kept by the customer until the new car arrived;
     property and risk in the old car had passed to the dealer. Criticised: odd to put risk on the dealer while
     the customer keeps using the car.
   - (d) *Risk terms*: if risk stays with the seller, that may suggest property has not passed; *Re Anchor
     Line* (1936): a clause putting risk on the buyer implied property had not passed (otherwise unnecessary).
     *Carlos Federspiel v Twigg*. The inference depends on the circumstances (s 17(2)).
   - (e) *Insurance*: who must insure indicates risk and so property, but the inference depends on context (a
     seller with a lien may require the buyer to insure; if the seller would have no interest once property
     passed but still requires buyer insurance, property and risk have probably not passed).
   - (f) *Goods sold with land* (house and furnishings): property passes on conveyance.
   - (g) *Supply and install* contracts where goods become fixtures: property passes on completion of
     installation.

### 4. Rule 2 - seller must put goods into a deliverable state
"Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods and the seller is bound to do something to the goods
for the purpose of putting them into a deliverable state, the property does not pass until the thing is done
and the buyer has notice that it has been done."
8. Two requirements - the act **and** notice to the buyer (the second is often forgotten).
9. Does not apply where the seller has merely agreed to *repair* the goods (handout and slides).
10. Examples: *Underwood v Burgh Castle*; *Head v Showfronts*.
11. If a conditional contract falls outside Rules 2-4 and s 19, go back to s 17 and the parties' presumed
    intention.

### 5. Rule 3 - seller must weigh, measure or test to fix the price
"Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state but the seller is bound to
weigh, measure, test, or do some other act or thing with reference to the goods for the purpose of
ascertaining the price, the property does not pass until the act or thing is done and the buyer has notice."
12. Only acts by the **seller** count:
    - *Turley v Bates* (1863) 2 H & C 200: buyer was to weigh fireclay; property passed at contract; buyer liable
      for the price of the goods not taken.
    - *Nanka-Bruce v Commonwealth Trust* [1926] AC 77: cocoa resold and weighed by the sub-buyer to check
      amounts; weighing did not make the contract conditional; property passed before the price was fixed.
13. The slides call this a weaker presumption than Rules 1-2.

### 6. Rule 4 - approval or sale or return
"When goods are delivered to the buyer on approval or on sale or return or other similar terms the property
passes: (a) when he signifies approval or acceptance to the seller or does any other act adopting the
transaction; (b) if he does not signify approval or acceptance but retains the goods without giving notice of
rejection, then, if a time is fixed for return, on its expiry, and if not, on expiry of a reasonable time."
14. Sale on approval may be a sale with a condition subsequent; sale or return is initially an offer with
    delivery, needing acceptance. Rule 4 treats both alike, including deliveries to a dealer expected to resell
    or return (*Poole v Smith's Car Sales* [1962] 1 WLR 744). *The Vesta* [1921] 1 AC 774 is also cited.
15. **Adopting the transaction**: any act inconsistent with the buyer's free power to return - e.g. pledging or
    reselling. *Kirkham v Attenborough* [1897] 1 QB 201: jewellery on sale or return pledged to a pawnbroker -
    adoption; property passed and the seller could not recover it from the pawnbroker; irrelevant whether the
    act came to the seller's attention.
16. **Contrary intention overrides Rule 4**: a term that property stays with the seller until payment protects
    the seller. *Weiner v Harris*: jewellery sent with a term that property remained with the sender until sold
    or paid for; the recipient pledged it. The Court of Appeal treated the recipient as a commercial agent with
    power to pledge, so the pledgee won (see the slides' question: sale or return or agency?).
17. **Retention beyond time**: property passes on expiry of the fixed or a reasonable time. *Poole v Smith's
    Car Sales*: failure to return within a reasonable time because of the buyer's employees' default passed
    property though the buyer had told them to return the car. Not so if the goods are held by the buyer's
    creditors, over whom the buyer has no control (slides).
18. **Rejection**: *Atari Corp v Electronics Boutique Stores* [1998] 1 All ER 1010 (CA): absent a contractual
    method, any notice that the buyer does not wish to buy suffices, with goods made available for collection
    within a reasonable time; it need not identify the goods exactly if they can be identified (Adams (1998) 61
    MLR 432). Sale or return contracts are bailments until property passes, so goods remain at the seller's
    risk even if the buyer has paid.
19. Rule 4 v ROT: ROT usually concerns unascertained goods (ss 17, 19) and the buyer is committed to buy; under
    sale or return it is not.

## Debate and critique
- **Abstract timing.** Rule 1 passes property at contract even if the goods stay with the seller and nothing is
  paid. Combined with s 20(1), this puts risk on a buyer who has neither possession nor insurance. Courts and
  commentators therefore find contrary intention readily (Diplock LJ in *Ward v Bignall*), which shrinks Rule 1
  in practice. Merrett [2008] CLJ 376 (set reading) argues for the importance of delivery and possession in the
  passing of title.
- **"Unconditional"**: the *Varley v Whipp* reading confuses conditions in the contractual sense (essential terms)
  with conditions on which passing of property depends. The modern reading is preferred.
- **"Deliverable state"**: s 61(5) defines it by reference to whether the buyer would be bound to take
  delivery, which seems to suggest defective goods are not deliverable; the slides reject that - defects do not
  stop property passing. *Kulkarni* shows legal (registration) readiness matters too.
- **Clark v Michael Reilly** illustrates the mismatch between property, risk and use.
- **Rule 4 and third parties.** *Kirkham* protects pledgees; *Weiner v Harris* shows careful drafting (and agency
  characterisation) can protect sellers or not. The question is who should bear the risk of a dishonest
  dealer - a nemo dat theme.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Identify goods: specific (identified and agreed at contract) or not? If not, go to the unascertained note.
2. Look for express or implied intention (s 17): ROT clause, payment-before-property terms, retail context,
   risk or insurance clauses, installation.
3. If none: is the contract unconditional and the goods deliverable? Rule 1 - property passes at contract, even
   with delivery/payment postponed.
4. Must the seller do something to make them deliverable? Rule 2 - act + notice. (Repairs are not covered.)
5. Must the seller weigh/measure/test to fix the price? Rule 3 - act + notice; not where the buyer does it.
6. Goods on approval/sale or return? Rule 4: adoption (e.g. pledge, resale), approval, or retention beyond the
   time; check a contrary term (property only on payment) and agency (*Weiner v Harris*).
7. State the consequences: risk (s 20), insolvency, action for price (s 49), frustration (s 7), third parties.
Traps: forgetting the notice requirement in Rules 2-3; applying Rule 4 when the contract reserves title until
payment; overlooking s 17 and jumping straight to s 18.
