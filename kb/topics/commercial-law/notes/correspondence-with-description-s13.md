# Correspondence with Description (s 13 SGA; s 11 CRA)

## Overview
Where goods are sold "by description", s 13 implies a condition that they correspond with it. Liability is strict:
the seller is liable even if not at fault and even if the goods are perfectly usable (*Arcos v Ronaasen*). Because
breach of condition lets the buyer reject, s 13 has historically been used by buyers to escape bad bargains on
technical grounds, and the modern courts have narrowed it in three ways: (i) only words identifying the goods (a
"substantial ingredient of the identity" of what is sold) count, not words about quality (*Ashington Piggeries*,
*Reardon Smith*); (ii) the description must be intended as a term and, in practice, relied upon (*Harlingdon &
Leinster*); and (iii) non-consumer buyers cannot reject for slight breaches (s 15A). For consumers, CRA s 11 applies.
Law: England & Wales (s 13(1A) makes the term a condition for England & Wales and Northern Ireland).

## The law

### 1. The provisions
1. **s 13(1)**: "Where there is a contract for the sale of goods by description, there is an implied term that the goods
   will correspond with the description." **s 13(1A)**: for England & Wales and NI, a condition.
2. Remedies are qualified by s 11(2) (buyer may waive or treat as warranty), s 11(4) (acceptance turns condition into
   warranty) and **s 15A** (non-consumer: breach so slight that rejection would be unreasonable is treated as breach of
   warranty).
3. **s 13(2)**: in a sale by sample as well as description, it is not enough that the bulk matches the sample if the goods
   do not also match the description.
4. **s 13(3)**: a sale is not prevented from being by description merely because the goods, being exposed for sale or
   hire, are selected by the buyer (self-service).
5. **s 13(5)** (as amended): s 13 does not apply to CRA Chapter 2 contracts; see CRA s 11.
6. Interpretation: the implied terms should not be read so narrowly as to impose on parties promises they could not
   reasonably have intended (Lord Diplock, *Ashington Piggeries*).

### 2. When is a sale "by description"?
7. Sales of future or unascertained goods are necessarily by description.
8. Specific goods can be: *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills* [1936] AC 85 (PC, Lord Wright): there is a sale by
   description even of a thing displayed on the counter, "so long as it is sold not merely as the specific thing but as a
   thing corresponding to a description" (woollen undergarments, a hot-water bottle). (Facts: underwear with excess
   sulphite gave the buyer dermatitis.)
9. *Scancom International v RWH Enterprises* [2003]: goods selected by reference to examples shown at an exhibition -
   sale by description, not by sample.
10. The slides' example: an advertisement for a "2007 Honda Civic automatic, only one owner, 10,000 miles, headrests,
    radio, excellent condition" (Goode) - which words are description?

### 3. Intention that the description be a term; reliance
11. *Harlingdon & Leinster v Christopher Hull Fine Art* [1991] 1 QB 564 (CA, Stuart-Smith LJ dissenting): both parties
    were London art dealers; the sellers (not experts in German art) had a painting described in an auction catalogue as
    by Gabriele Münter and said they knew little about it; the buyers specialised in German expressionists and inspected
    it; they paid £6,000; it was a forgery worth under £100. Held: not a sale by description.
    - Nourse LJ: the description must be influential in the sale; reliance by the buyer is the "natural index" of a sale
      by description; there cannot be a sale by description where it is not within the parties' reasonable
      contemplation that the buyer relies on it; an objective test; courts should be "exceedingly wary" of giving an
      attribution of a painting contractual effect.
    - Slade LJ: the question is whether the court can impute to the parties a common intention that correspondence with
      the description be a term; s 13 then makes it a condition. No actual reliance need be proved to sue on a term, but
      absence of reliance is powerful evidence that no term was intended. The buyer who fails "through the front door of
      s 13(1)" cannot succeed "through the back door of s 14".
    - Stuart-Smith LJ (dissent): every item in a description constituting a substantial ingredient of identity is a
      condition; the identity of the artist can be such an ingredient; a buyer need not prove reliance on a term; a
      disclaimer of expertise is not clear words excluding the condition; a near-worthless fake is not of merchantable
      quality for resale as a Münter at £6,000.
12. *Drake v Agnew* [2002] EWHC 294: art dealer bought a painting described as by Van Dyck from a specialist seller whose
    references were clearly stated to be opinion only - mere opinion; not a sale by description.
13. **Representations v terms**: *Oscar Chess v Williams* [1957] 1 All ER 325; cf *Beale v Taylor* [1967] 1 WLR 1193 (which
    Brown describes as the "apotheosis" of extending s 13 to specific goods, arguably making all descriptive statements
    conditions). See also *T & J Harrison v Knowles & Foster* (1918) (written particulars stating 460 tons deadweight were
    an innocent representation, not part of the contract) and *Heilbut Symons v Buckleton* (1913) (intention decides
    whether a statement is a warranty).

### 4. Which words are "description"? Identity v attributes
14. *Ashington Piggeries v Christopher Hill* [1972] AC 441 (HL): "Norwegian herring meal fair average quality of the
    season" contaminated with a substance fatal to mink. No breach of s 13 - "fair average quality of the season" is an
    attribute (quality), not identity; the herring meal was still herring meal. (Liability arose under s 14.)
15. *Re Moore & Co and Landauer* [1921] 1 KB 519: tinned fruit to be packed in cases of 30 tins was delivered partly in
    cases of 24. The packing was part of the description; the buyer could reject the whole
    consignment.
16. *Reardon Smith Line v Hansen-Tangen* [1976] 3 All ER 570 (HL): a charterparty described the vessel as "Yard No 354 at Osaka"; it
    was actually built by a subcontracting yard (Oshima) with a different number. The charterers, wanting to get out of the
    contract for another reason, argued misdescription. Held: the words merely identified/labelled the vessel; they were not words of
    description under s 13. Lord Wilberforce: courts must consider the commercial purpose and "factual matrix"; words
    identifying an essential part of the description get contractual force, while words merely enabling the goods to
    be found are construed liberally. *Re Moore* was called "excessively technical". The test: is the item a "substantial
    ingredient of the identity of the thing sold"?
17. *Arcos v Ronaasen* (1933) (HL): staves of Russian redwood and whitewood specified as half an inch thick; most
    were thicker but still fit for making cement barrels. The buyers could reject. Lord Atkin: "A ton does not mean about
    a ton, or a yard about a yard"; a seller wanting a margin must stipulate for it; negligible deviations may be
    ignored but otherwise conditions must be strictly performed; fitness for purpose is irrelevant to s 13. Lord Buckmaster: the buyer may reject though the goods are the
    commercial equivalent.
18. Classic vehicles: *Brewer v Mann* (2012, CA; Wilson, "Battle of the Bentley" (2012) 162 NLJ 510): a 1930 Bentley
    "Speed Six" with a later-fitted engine. The CA held an authentic, continuous documentary history is not the same as
    description; a car rebuilt from part of the original numbered chassis still merits the name; originality affects
    value, not description. (The claim against the finance company failed: it had not misdescribed anything.)

### 5. Strictness and the s 15A limit
19. Liability is strict and independent of fault or fitness (*Arcos*). For **non-consumer** buyers, s 15A prevents
    rejection where the breach is so slight that rejection would be unreasonable; the breach is treated as breach of
    warranty (damages). The de minimis principle also applies (*Arcos*, Lord Warrington: negligible deviations).

### 6. Consumers - CRA s 11
20. Every contract to supply goods by description includes a term that the goods will match the description (s 11(1));
    sample plus description (s 11(2)); selection by the consumer does not prevent supply by description (s 11(3));
    pre-contract information on main characteristics under the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and
    Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 is a term (s 11(4)) and cannot be changed without express agreement (s 11(5));
    remedies s 19. Digital content: s 36 (match description; a trial version is not enough if the content does not match
    the description), s 39 (supply by transmission), s 40 (modifications). **s 14 CRA** (new): goods must match a model
    seen or examined, except for differences brought to the consumer's attention; no SGA equivalent. Exclusion barred by
    s 31.

## Key authorities
- SGA ss 11(2), 11(4), 13(1)-(5), 15A; CRA ss 11, 14, 19, 31, 36, 39, 40.
- *Grant v Australian Knitting Mills* [1936] AC 85 - specific goods can be sold by description.
- *Harlingdon & Leinster v Christopher Hull* [1991] 1 QB 564 - reliance/intention; no sale by description.
- *Ashington Piggeries v Hill* [1972] AC 441 - identity v quality.
- *Re Moore & Landauer* [1921] 1 KB 519 - packing as description (now criticised).
- *Reardon Smith v Hansen-Tangen* [1976] - "substantial ingredient of identity"; labels.
- *Arcos v Ronaasen* (1933) - strict compliance.
- *Drake v Agnew* [2002] EWHC 294 - opinion not description.
- *Beale v Taylor* [1967] 1 WLR 1193; *Oscar Chess v Williams* [1957] 1 All ER 325 - representations v terms.

## Debate and critique
- **Brown, "Forgery, Fine Art and the Sale of Goods" (1990) 106 LQR 561.** s 13 was conceived for commercial sales of
  unascertained or future goods. Its 20th-century extension to specific goods (culminating in *Beale v Taylor*) was largely
  a device to get round the old s 14(2), which applied only to sales by description - an artifice made unnecessary when the
  Supply of Goods (Implied Terms) Act 1973 removed that limit. *Ashington* and *Reardon Smith* then cut s 13 back to a
  "substantial ingredient" of identity, which invites "metaphysical" distinctions between subject matter and attributes:
  is a fake "Münter" still "a painting"? *Harlingdon* narrows s 13 further via reliance. Brown proposes a sliding scale
  of reliance depending on the goods and width of description, with reliance presumed unless disproved (by analogy with
  s 14(3), where the seller must prove lack of reliance), so that only a sale of specific goods "as such" with no
  description falls outside s 13.
- **Reliance controversy within *Harlingdon*.** Nourse LJ (reliance as index), Slade LJ (reliance as evidence of intention),
  Stuart-Smith LJ (reliance irrelevant to a term). Critics say importing reliance confuses terms with representations;
  supporters say it reflects commercial reality between experts. Note the market context: art attributions are inherently
  arguable.
- **Technical rejection.** *Arcos* and *Re Moore* allowed buyers to reject for trivial deviations, often to escape a falling
  market. *Reardon Smith* (charterers wanting out for other reasons) and s 15A respond to that abuse; but strictness gives
  certainty, especially in string contracts and documentary sales.
- **Wilson** shows how description disputes about classic goods turn on market understanding of what the name means.
- **s 13 v s 14 overlap.** the materials asks how description interacts with quality and fitness (the antique chest described
  as an "18th Century masterpiece" bought at a flea market by an antiques dealer - would it differ if the buyer ran a
  vintage car business?).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Consumer or business buyer? (CRA s 11 / SGA s 13.)
2. Was there a description, and was it intended to be a term (not mere opinion or a representation): *Harlingdon*, *Drake v
   Agnew*, *Oscar Chess*? Who had expertise? Did the buyer inspect or rely on its own judgment?
3. Does the description go to identity (substantial ingredient) rather than quality (*Ashington*, *Reardon Smith*)?
4. Do the goods correspond? Strictly (*Arcos*) - but ignore microscopic deviations.
5. Remedy: condition - reject (unless accepted, s 11(4)/s 35). Non-consumer: is the breach so slight that rejection would
   be unreasonable (s 15A)? If so, damages only. Consumer: CRA s 19 remedies.
6. Also consider s 14(2)/(3) - but note Slade LJ's warning about the "back door".
7. Sample and description together: goods must match both (s 13(2)).
Traps: treating every adjective as description; forgetting s 15A; assuming specific goods cannot be sold by description.
