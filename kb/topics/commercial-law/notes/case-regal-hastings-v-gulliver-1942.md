# Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1942] 1 All ER 378; [1967] 2 AC 134n (case note)

> Built from secondary summaries only; the speeches were not read. Check [1942] 1 All ER 378, [1967] 2 AC 134n
> ([1942] UKHL 1) before citing. The judgments below were not reported at the time, so details of the lower courts
> are limited.

## Overview
House of Lords, 20 February 1942. Panel (named by two sources): Viscount Sankey, Lord Russell of Killowen, Lord
Macmillan, Lord Wright and Lord Porter. Lord Russell and Lord Wright are the speeches usually cited. The House reversed
the trial judge and the Court of Appeal and held that the four directors who took up shares had to account to the
company for their profit, whatever their good faith.

## Facts
Regal owned a cinema in Hastings and wished to acquire the leases of two more. It formed a subsidiary, Hastings
Amalgamated Cinemas Ltd, to take the leases. The landlord would not grant the leases without a personal guarantee
from the directors, who refused to give one, and instead required the subsidiary's paid-up share capital to be
£5,000. Regal could afford to subscribe only £2,000. The other £3,000 was raised by four directors taking 500 shares
each, 500 shares taken by the company's solicitor at the board's request, and 500 shares arranged by the chairman,
Gulliver, from outside subscribers. Later the subsidiary's shares were sold at a profit of just under £3 per share
(£2 16s 1d, per one source). Regal, through its changed ownership, claimed that those who had profited had to account
for it because of their position. The trial judge (Wrottesley J, per one source) found for the defendants, the
Court of Appeal dismissed Regal's appeal, and Regal appealed to the House of Lords. The lower-court judgments were not
reported.

## Held
- The four directors who had subscribed for shares in their own right were liable to account to Regal for the profit
  they made. They obtained the shares by reason of the fact that they were directors and in the course of their
  office.
- Per Lord Russell, liability does not depend on fraud, bad faith, or on whether the company could have taken up the
  opportunity itself or suffered any loss; it arises from the making of the profit through the fiduciary position.
- Per Lord Wright, a director's honest belief that the company could not take up the opportunity does not release him
  from the duty not to acquire it for himself.
- **Gulliver**, who took no shares himself but found outside subscribers, was not liable to account, and nor was the
  company's **solicitor**, Garton, who took shares at the board's request and with its consent (reported by several
  secondary sources; not checked against the speeches).
- The directors could have protected themselves by obtaining the approval of the shareholders in general meeting
  with full disclosure; they did not do so.

## Effect
- Strict rule: a fiduciary who makes a profit from the fiduciary position must account, regardless of honesty or
  loss to the principal. The case is the usual authority on the no-profit rule for directors and is a companion
  to the conflict rule.
- Now reflected in company law in the Companies Act 2006, s 175 (duty to avoid conflicts of interest, which expressly
  covers exploiting any property, information or opportunity, whether or not the company could take advantage of it),
  read with s 176 (benefits from third parties).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Ask whether the defendant was a director or other fiduciary, and whether the profit came by reason of that
   position or the knowledge or opportunity gained in office.
2. Do not accept as defences that the director acted in good faith, that the company could not have taken the
   opportunity, or that the company suffered no loss.
3. Look for informed consent: approval by the board under the constitution, or by the members, given with full
   disclosure.
4. Consider each individual separately: a director who took no personal benefit, or a non-director who acted at the
   company's request, may not be liable to account.
5. State the remedy: an account of profits (liability to hand over the profit), not compensation for loss.
