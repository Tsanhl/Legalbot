# Non-Existent and Perished Goods at the Date of the Contract (s 6 SGA)

## Overview
Sometimes, unknown to the parties, the goods contracted for have already been destroyed - or never existed -
when the contract is made. Section 6 SGA deals with one slice of this problem: specific goods that have
perished, without the seller's knowledge, before the contract. Everything else (goods that never existed,
unascertained goods, cases where the seller promised the goods existed) is governed by the common law of
mistake and by construction of the contract. This is a "pre-contractual impossibility" topic, to be
contrasted with s 7 (post-contractual perishing - frustration). The meaning of "perish" is shared by both
sections and is a frequently tested issue. Law: England & Wales (with Australian authority in *McRae*). The
CRA is silent, so the revision guide assumes the same position applies to consumer sales.

## The law

### 1. Section 6
"Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods, and the goods without the knowledge of the seller
have perished at the time when the contract is made, the contract is void." Elements:
1. **Specific goods** - identified and agreed on at the time of contract (s 61). Not unascertained goods, nor
   goods ascertained after the contract.
2. **Perished** - see below. Goods must once have existed; s 6 does not apply to goods that never existed.
3. **At the time the contract is made** (pre-contract perishing; post-contract perishing is s 7).
4. **Without the seller's knowledge** - a seller who knew cannot rely on s 6.
5. **Effect**: the contract is void - neither party is liable; money paid is recoverable.

### 2. The four common law possibilities
The question is one of construction: what did the parties intend should happen if the goods did not exist?
1. **The buyer bought a chance** - *Couturier v Hastie* (cited as (1852) 22 LJ Ex 97; the revision guide
   refers to the House of Lords decision of 1856): a cargo of corn shipped from Salonika had, before the
   sale, been lawfully sold by the ship's master, so there was no corn to deliver. The sellers sued the
   agent, whose liability depended on the buyer's. On its true construction the contract was for existing
   goods, so the buyer (and so the agent) was not liable. Commentary presents this as construction rather
   than mistake; the buyer had not bought "a chance". s 6 is said to confirm this case.
2. **The contract is void for common mistake** - *Bell v Lever Bros* [1932] AC 161: common mistake voids a
   contract only if fundamental to the identity of the subject matter. On its facts no avoidance: the parties
   were mistaken only as to the value of what was bought (release from employment contracts), not what was
   bought. If specific goods have perished unknown to the seller, the contract is void; if goods are
   unascertained, the seller must find them elsewhere.
3. **The seller promised the goods existed** - *McRae v Commonwealth Disposals Commission* (1950) 84 CLR 377
   (High Court of Australia): the Commission sold an oil tanker said to be on Jourmand Reef; McRae spent money
   on a salvage expedition; there was no tanker and never had been. The Commission was liable for breach: it
   had promised there was a tanker at the specified place; there was no implied condition precedent. s 6 did
   not apply because goods that never existed cannot have perished. The court also said ss 6-7 can be
   excluded by contrary intention (commentators link this to s 55(1), permitting variation of implied terms).
   - *Goldsborough v Carter*: sale of "about" 4,000 sheep on certain land, buyer to pay for the actual number
     if fewer; only 890 found. There was an implied condition that the sheep existed at the time for
     performance, but since the seller did not know the number and the buyer accepted the risk of a shortage,
     the contract was only for the sheep that existed on muster.
4. **The contract is subject to a condition precedent** - *Associated Japanese Bank v Credit du Nord* [1989] 1
   WLR 255: AJB bought four machines for £1m from Mr B and leased them back; Credit du Nord guaranteed B's
   obligations; the machines never existed (B was a fraudster). Credit du Nord was not liable: the guarantee
   was subject to a condition precedent that the machines existed. The takeaway: in a sale and
   leaseback, the seller guarantees the goods' existence.

### 3. s 6 as a rule of construction
- Commentators note the normal expectation would be that a seller impliedly undertakes the goods exist; s 6
  instead creates a presumption that the seller is *not* contracting that they exist. *Joseph Constantine SS v
  Imperial Smelting*: the only effect of s 6 is that presumption - a rule of construction, not a rigid rule of
  law. If on its true construction the seller promised existence, s 6 yields and the seller is liable for
  non-delivery.
- Key point: where the goods never existed (*McRae*, *AJB*), s 6 cannot apply, and the presumption may be that
  the seller *was* promising existence.

### 4. Meaning of "perish" (applies to ss 6 and 7)
5. **Physical destruction** obviously counts.
6. **Commercial change of identity**: *Asfar v Blundell* [1896] 1 QB 123: dates on a ship sunk in the Thames
   were raised, still looked like dates but were fermented and contaminated by sea and sewage (unfit for
   eating, though fit for distillation). Held a total loss (in an insurance/freight context). Lord Esher MR:
   if for business purposes a thing becomes something else - "an unmerchantable thing, which no buyer would
   buy and no honest seller would sell" - its nature has altered.
7. **Narrow view**: *Horn v Minister of Food* [1948] 2 All ER 1036: Morris J said (obiter, under s 7 of the 1893
   Act) that potatoes which had rotted in a clamp until worthless had not perished - they were still potatoes.
   Commentary calls this a very narrow reading that has attracted much academic criticism; the revision
   guide says it "appears to be wrong" and out of line with other authority. A seller of such potatoes would
   instead be liable for breach of the quality term - unless s 6 applies.
8. **Theft**: *Barrow Lane & Ballard v Phillip Phillips* [1929] 1 KB 574: specific lot of 700 bags of nuts;
   unknown to both, 109 had been stolen before the contract (and more later; 150 were delivered before the
   buyer learned of it). Wright J: with no real prospect of recovery, stolen goods are treated as perished; the
   contract was for an indivisible lot of 700 and, part having perished, s 6 applied and the contract was
   void. The buyer had not contracted to buy 591 bags. Had the contract been severable, only the part
   covering the missing goods would fail (revision guide).
9. **Never-grown crops**: *Sainsbury v Street* [1972] 3 All ER 1127: barley to be grown on a particular farm
   was not specific goods; the part of the crop that never grew had not "perished". The case is governed by
   the common law (see s 7 note).
10. Todd's thesis suggests the *Asfar* test is really about identity, not merchantability: goods perish when
    so altered that, commercially, their nature/identity has changed; mere unmerchantability is not enough.

### 5. Unascertained goods
11. ss 6-7 apply only to specific goods. Purely generic goods (e.g. "100 tons of Santini tomatoes") cannot
    perish - the seller takes the risk of finding goods: *Intertradex v Lesieur-Tourteaux* [1977] 2 Lloyd's
    Rep 146 (seller's supplier of Mali groundnuts failed; seller had assumed the basic risks of shortage and
    price rises; not excused); *Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl* [1962] AC 93 (Suez closure did not frustrate sale
    of Sudanese groundnuts).
12. Goods to be manufactured: if a nearly finished yacht burns, the builder starts again at its own risk unless
    otherwise agreed.
13. Goods from a specified source/bulk: failure of the source may make performance impossible - *Howell v
    Coupland* (1876) 1 QBD 258 (200 tons of potatoes from a particular field; crop failed, only 80 tons; seller
    excused for the balance). This is frustration, discussed in the s 7 note.

## Key authorities
- SGA s 6; s 61 (specific goods); s 55 (exclusion by agreement).
- *Couturier v Hastie* - construction: contract presupposed existing goods; buyer not liable.
- *Bell v Lever Bros* [1932] AC 161 - narrow common mistake.
- *McRae v Commonwealth Disposals Commission* (1950) 84 CLR 377 - seller promised existence; s 6 inapplicable
  to never-existing goods; ss 6-7 excludable.
- *Associated Japanese Bank v Credit du Nord* [1989] 1 WLR 255 - condition precedent of existence.
- *Goldsborough v Carter* - "about" 4,000 sheep; contract only for those found.
- *Joseph Constantine SS v Imperial Smelting* - s 6 a presumption/rule of construction.
- *Asfar v Blundell* [1896] 1 QB 123; *Horn v Minister of Food* [1948] 2 All ER 1036; *Barrow Lane v Phillips*
  [1929] 1 KB 574 - meaning of perish.
- *Sainsbury v Street* [1972] 3 All ER 1127; *Howell v Coupland* (1876) 1 QBD 258 - crops.

## Debate and critique
- **Is s 6 about mistake or construction?** Commentators present s 6 as a presumption about
  allocation of risk (who promised what), following the construction reading of *Couturier v Hastie* and
  *Joseph Constantine*. The revision guide treats s 6 as a "mistake/impossibility" provision. *McRae* shows the
  construction approach is more flexible: it can impose liability where the seller assumed responsibility.
  Set reading: Chandler, Devenney & Poole, "Common Mistake: Theoretical Justification and Remedial
  Inflexibility" [2003] JBL 34 - the "void" outcome is all-or-nothing, giving no room to apportion loss.
- **Rigid voidness.** s 6 renders the contract void even where the buyer might have wished to take the
  surviving goods (*Barrow Lane*: no option to take 591 bags). Contrast *Sainsbury v Street* at common law,
  where the seller had to offer what was harvested.
- **Meaning of perish.** *Horn* (identity of species) v *Asfar* (commercial identity) produce opposite answers
  for worthless but recognisable goods. Most commentators side with *Asfar*; Todd argues the test is change of
  commercial identity, so unmerchantability alone is insufficient. The practical effect: under *Horn*, a seller
  of rotten specific goods is liable on s 14 instead of being released by s 6.
- **Theft as perishing** stretches the ordinary meaning; *Barrow Lane* justifies it where recovery is not
  realistically possible, which is commercially sensible but uncertain at the margins.
- **Never-existing goods**: excluding them from s 6 seems odd (a seller of non-existent goods is in a worse
  position than a seller of goods that once existed), but follows from the statutory word "perished" and from
  the risk-allocation logic of *McRae*.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Did the problem arise before or after the contract? Before - s 6 / common law; after - s 7 / frustration.
2. Were the goods specific (s 61)? If unascertained/generic, s 6 cannot apply; the seller normally bears the
   risk of supply (*Intertradex*), unless from a specified source (*Howell v Coupland*).
3. Did the goods ever exist? If not, s 6 is out; go to common law: chance (*Couturier*), mistake (*Bell*),
   promise of existence (*McRae* - damages for breach), condition precedent (*AJB*).
4. Have they "perished"? Physical destruction; commercial change of identity (*Asfar*); compare the narrow
   *Horn* view; theft with no prospect of recovery (*Barrow Lane*); partial perishing of an indivisible lot.
5. Did the seller know? If so s 6 does not protect it.
6. Is there contrary intention (express term or construction that the seller guaranteed existence)? ss 6-7 can
   be excluded (*McRae*; s 55).
7. Outcome: void - no liability either way, recover payments; or seller liable for breach if it promised
   existence.
Traps: applying s 6 to a contract for crops "to be grown" (*Sainsbury*); forgetting that a contract for an
indivisible lot fails entirely on partial perishing.
