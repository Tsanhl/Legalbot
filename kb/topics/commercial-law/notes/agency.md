# Agency (creation, authority, relationships, commercial agents)

## Overview
Agency lets one person (the agent) bring another (the principal) into a direct contract with a third party. The agent negotiates, but the contract is between principal and third party, in line with the maxim qui facit per alium facit per se. The law of England and Wales cares above all about the agent's authority: it decides whether the principal is bound. The topic then covers the rights and duties among the three parties, how agency ends, and the special regime for commercial agents. The source for these notes is the Baskind revision guide (Commercial Law Concentrate, 6th edn) chapters 12 and 13.

## The law
**1. Meaning and creation.**
- "Agent" has a technical meaning: an estate agent, or a shop displaying "Sony agent", usually is not an agent in the strict sense. Lord Herschell in *Kennedy v De Trafford* (1897) complained that no word is more abused.
- Agency usually arises from express or implied agreement, often contractual but not necessarily so; it can arise by law or from circumstances, and can be negated by them (*Spearmint Rhino Ventures v HMRC* (2007): lap dancers were principals, not agents for the club). Also by necessity or ratification (below).
- Generally no formalities, even if the act to be done requires writing. The principal must have capacity; the agent need not (a minor can be appointed agent).

**2. Types of authority.**
- *Express actual authority.* By agreement, scope construed as a contract, including trade usage and course of dealing (Diplock LJ in *Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd* [1964] 2 QB 480).
- *Implied actual authority.* Inferred from conduct or circumstances: *Hely-Hutchinson v Brayhead Ltd* [1968] 1 QB 549 (a chairman acting as managing director bound the company; appointing a managing director impliedly authorises what falls within the usual scope of the office). No implied authority to act contrary to express instructions (*Waugh v HB Clifford & Sons* [1982] Ch 374).
- *Apparent (ostensible) authority.* A representation by the principal to the third party that the agent has authority, which the third party relies on, works as an estoppel (*Freeman & Lockyer*; Toulson J in *ING Re (UK) Ltd v R&V Versicherung AG* (2006)). Requirements (Slade J in *Rama Corporation v Proved Tin* (1952)): (i) a representation by the principal or someone authorised by him (not the agent: *Armagas Ltd v Mundogas SA* (1986)); (ii) reliance; (iii) alteration of position, now satisfied by entering the contract. Apparent authority is lost if the third party knows, or (orthodox position) should suspect the lack of authority and fails to make reasonable inquiry. In *Thanakharn Kasikorn Thai Chamkat v Akai Holdings* (2010, HK) Lord Neuberger limited loss of reliance to actual knowledge or dishonest or irrational belief, but the Privy Council in *East Asia Co Ltd v PT Satria Tirtatama Energindo* (2020) reaffirmed the orthodox approach. *First Energy (UK) Ltd v Hungarian International Bank* [1993] 2 Lloyd's Rep 194: a manager had apparent authority to communicate head office's lending decisions, not to grant the loan himself.
- *Usual authority.* Authority usually attached to a position in a trade or profession; mostly an extension of implied actual and apparent authority (*Panorama Developments (Guildford) Ltd v Fidelis Furnishing Fabrics Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 711: a company secretary's apparent authority covered hiring cars). *Watteau v Fenwick* [1893] 1 QB 346 treated usual authority as independent: the undisclosed owner of a pub was bound by the manager's cigar purchase despite an express prohibition. The case is much criticised and "puzzling" (Bingham J in *The Rhodian River*), never overruled, and unlikely to be followed; confined to cases where the third party does not know there is a principal, the agent was forbidden to act, and the contract was usual for such an agent. There is no usual authority contrary to an express limit, and it may be limited to ordinary-course transactions on reasonable terms (*Taylor v Rhino Overseas* (2021)).
- Tip from the source: consider actual authority first, then apparent, then usual.

**3. Agency of necessity.** Imposed in an emergency where the agent controls the property, intervention is necessary, communication with the principal is impossible or not reasonably practicable, the agent acts bona fide and reasonably in the principal's interests, and the principal is competent. *Springer v Great Western Railway* [1921] 1 KB 257: no agency of necessity where the agent could have contacted the owner before selling perishing tomatoes. Modern communications make it rarer; courts have kept it largely maritime, though *Surrey Breakdown v Knight* (1999) left the door open.

**4. Ratification.** An unauthorised act done in the name of a principal can be adopted, and then is treated as authorised from the outset (*Koenigsblatt v Sweet* (1923)). Conditions:
- Whole transaction or none (*Smith v Henniker-Major* (2003)).
- Only a principal identified at the time can ratify; an undisclosed principal cannot (*Keighley, Maxted & Co v Durant* [1901] AC 240).
- The principal must have existed at the time (*Kelner v Baxter*: no ratification by a company not yet incorporated).
- Capacity at the time of the act (*Boston Deep Sea Fishing v Farnham* [1957] 1 WLR 1051: alien enemy) and at the time of ratification (*Grover & Grover v Mathews* [1910] 2 KB 401: insurance after the fire).
- Within a reasonable time, no waiting to see if it is advantageous (*SEB Trygg Holding v Manches* (2005)); a void contract cannot be ratified.

**5. Rights and liabilities on the contract.**
- Disclosed principal, actual authority: principal and third party can enforce against each other. Apparent authority only: third party can enforce against the principal, but the principal cannot enforce against the third party (no representation by the third party), unless the principal ratifies.
- Undisclosed principal with actual authority can sue and be sued on the contract once revealed, justified by "convenience" (Lord Lindley in *Keighley*). Exclusions: an express term, or an implied term (*Humble v Hunter* (1848): agent signed as owner), or where the third party wanted to contract with the agent personally or the principal's identity matters (*Said v Butt* [1920] 3 KB 497: theatre critic barred from the first night; undisclosed principal could not enforce).
- Third party paying a disclosed principal's agent with actual or apparent authority discharges the debt. Paying an undisclosed agent counts as settlement with the principal even if the agent absconds (explained by estoppel, which the source calls unconvincing).
- Third party and undisclosed principal: after discovery the third party can elect to sue principal or agent. Issuing proceedings is strong evidence of election (*Clarkson Booker v Andjel* (1964)), unless the contract makes them jointly liable (*Tattersalls v McMahon* (2021)).
- Agent's own liability: a disclosed agent generally neither sues nor is sued (*Montgomerie v UK Mutual Steamship Assn* (1891)), subject to contrary intention. An agent for an undisclosed principal is liable while the principal is undisclosed and remains liable after revelation. Breach of warranty of authority: an agent who claims authority he lacks is liable to the third party (*Yonge v Toynbee* [1910]). Del credere agents (guarantee the third party's performance) are now rare.

**6. Agent's rights against the principal.** Remuneration only if agreed (express or implied; courts reluctant to imply a term: *AG of Belize v Belize Telecom* (2009)) or quantum meruit, calculated on unjust enrichment principles (*Benedetti v Sawiris* (2014)). Indemnity for expenses, losses and liabilities properly incurred within actual authority (*Thacker v Hardy*; *Adamson v Jarvis*). A particular lien over the principal's property in the agent's possession, not general (*Bock v Gorrissen*; *Bryans v Nix*).

**7. Agent's duties.** Contractual: obey lawful instructions (*Turpin v Bilton* (1843); not owed under a unilateral arrangement or by a gratuitous agent, who may still owe a tort duty of care), act with reasonable care and skill (implied by Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982 s 13 for business services; subject to UCTA 1977), and perform personally unless delegation is authorised, customary, necessary for the nature of the agency, forced by unforeseen circumstances or purely ministerial. Fiduciary (paid or not): avoid conflicts of interest (*Aberdeen Rail v Blaikie*; *Armstrong v Jackson* [1917] 2 KB 822: stockbroker sold his own shares to the principal, who could rescind), no secret profit or bribes (*Hippisley v Knee Brothers* [1905] 1 KB 1; *Imageview Management v Jack* [2009] EWCA Civ 63; *Boardman v Phipps*), account and keep money separate, and confidentiality (*Bolkiah v KPMG*). Remedies for fiduciary breach: dismissal without notice, recovery of profits, rescission against the third party, recovery of bribes (as a debt or on constructive trust: *AG for Hong Kong v Reid* [1994] 1 AC 324; *FHR European Ventures v Cedar Capital Partners* [2015] gives the principal a choice of personal or proprietary remedy). See fiduciary-duties.md.

**8. Termination.** By agreement, completion of task or expiry, or by operation of law: death (*Campanari v Woodburn* (1854): agent unaware of death not entitled to commission but to expenses), mental incapacity of either party (*Drew v Nunn*; *Boughton v Knight*), insolvency of the principal (and usually the agent), or frustration. Termination of actual authority does not necessarily end apparent authority vis-à-vis third parties unaware of it.

**9. Commercial Agents (Council Directive) Regulations 1993.** Apply only if reg 2(1) is met: a self-employed intermediary with continuing authority to negotiate (or negotiate and conclude) the sale or purchase of goods for a principal. Services, one-off transactions (*Poseidon Chartering v Marianne Zeeschip* C-3/04), company officers and partners are excluded; whether the authority is continuing depends on the contract (*Nagel v Pluczenik Diamond* (2018)); software licences were held not to be goods by the Court of Appeal in *Computer Associates v Software Incubator* (2021), though the CJEU on reference treated downloaded software on a perpetual licence as goods (the case went back to the Supreme Court). The Regulations govern only principal-agent relations; the third-party position remains common law. Key rules: duties of the agent (reg 3: good faith, proper efforts, information, reasonable instructions) and principal (reg 4: good faith, documents, information, tell the agent of acceptance or refusal), not excludable (reg 5); remuneration and commission (regs 6-12); written terms (reg 13); fixed-term agreements continuing become indefinite (reg 14); notice of one, two and three months for the first, second and later years (reg 15); immediate termination for default or exceptional circumstances (reg 16); on termination, compensation (default) or indemnity (reg 17; indemnity capped at one year's average remuneration over five years), claim notified within one year; contracting out to the agent's detriment prohibited. *Green Deal Marketing v Economy Energy* (2019): reg 17 compensation differs from damages and can be claimed without breach, but double recovery is barred; *Tigana v Decoro* (2003): compensation also applies on expiry.

## Key authorities
- *Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park* [1964]: definitions of actual and apparent authority.
- *Hely-Hutchinson v Brayhead* [1968]: implied authority.
- *Watteau v Fenwick* [1893]: usual authority.
- *Keighley, Maxted v Durant* [1901]: no ratification by undisclosed principal.
- *Said v Butt* [1920]: personal elements.
- *Armstrong v Jackson* [1917]; *Boardman v Phipps*; *Reid*; *FHR*: fiduciary breach.
- Commercial Agents Regulations 1993.

## Debate and critique
- **Apparent authority.** It is "no more than an illusion of authority" created by the principal's representation; the debate (Ian Brown, JBL 1995 and 2004, as listed in the source) considers whether the concept has distorted the basis of principal liability and the decline of general authority.
- **Watteau v Fenwick** is defended by Tettenborn (CLJ 1998) only by treating it as an estoppel or business-owner case outside agency.
- **Undisclosed principal doctrine.** The justification is convenience; the settlement rule's estoppel explanation is doubtful since an undisclosed principal makes no representation.
- **Reasonableness of third party's inquiry.** *Akai* versus *East Asia*.
- **Limits of agency of necessity** in a connected world.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Is there an agency relationship at all, in the legal sense, and who are principal, agent and third party? Disclosed or undisclosed?
2. Authority in order: express actual, implied actual, apparent (representation by the principal, reliance, no notice), usual (only *Watteau*-type facts), then agency of necessity, then ratification. State whether each exists.
3. If no authority and no ratification: the principal is not bound; the agent is liable for breach of warranty of authority.
4. Who can sue whom? Apply the disclosed/undisclosed tables and exclusions (personal element, contract terms).
5. Agent's breach: contractual (instructions, care, delegation) or fiduciary (conflict, secret profit, bribe) and remedies.
6. Is the agent a "commercial agent" for goods? If so, the Regulations (notice, commission, compensation) override the common law position.
7. Link to property cases: a mercantile agent selling goods without the owner's authority raises Factors Act exceptions (see nemo-dat-mercantile-agents.md).
