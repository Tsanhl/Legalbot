# Child maintenance - Child Support Act 1991 and the Child Maintenance Service

## Overview
Both parents are responsible for maintaining their children (Child Support Act 1991 s 1). Where a parent lives apart from the child, "child support maintenance" is calculated by the Child Maintenance Service (CMS), an executive function of the Secretary of State for Work and Pensions, by a formula based on the paying parent's gross weekly income. The family courts have a limited role: they cannot make or vary child periodical payments where the CMS has jurisdiction, except in specified cases (consent orders, "top-up" for high earners, educational expenses, disability), though they retain wide powers over lump sums and property for children (Children Act 1989 Sch 1; MCA 1973) and over spousal provision.

## The law
**Duty and jurisdiction.** CSA 1991 s 1(1): each parent of a qualifying child is responsible for maintaining him; s 1(2): a non-resident parent is taken to have met that responsibility by making periodical payments of maintenance in accordance with the Act. A "qualifying child" is under 16, or under 20 and in full-time non-advanced education (or under 20 and in approved training), and has at least one parent who is a non-resident parent. The CMS has jurisdiction where the child, the person with care and the non-resident parent are habitually resident in the UK (and in certain other cases).

**Applications and options.** A parent with care (the "receiving parent") or the non-resident parent ("paying parent") may apply to the CMS. Parents are encouraged to make their own "family-based" arrangement (with or without a court consent order), and the CMS gateway requires a conversation with Child Maintenance Options before an application, though not in cases of domestic abuse. Payments can be made directly ("Direct Pay") or through the CMS ("Collect and Pay"). The £20 application fee was abolished from 26 February 2024; Collect and Pay carries collection fees (20 per cent added to the paying parent's payments and 4 per cent deducted from the receiving parent's). The Government has announced that Direct Pay will be abolished, with all cases moving to a single collection service and lower fees for compliant parents, but this requires primary legislation and had not been enacted by November 2026.

**Calculation (the 2012 scheme).** The paying parent's "gross weekly income" is taken from HMRC data (Real Time Information on earnings, plus self-employment), and other forms of income (including pensions, benefits in some cases). Allowances: pension contributions, "relevant other children" in the paying parent's household (the income is reduced by a percentage for each, 11 per cent for one, 14 per cent for two, 16 per cent for three or more), and shared care. Rate bands (gross weekly income): 
- Nil rate: below £7, or where the paying parent is in prison, a full-time student or a minor, or receiving specified benefits.
- Flat rate: £7 a week where income is between £7 and £100, or where the paying parent receives specified benefits.
- Reduced rate: between £100.01 and £199.99, using a formula with a flat amount plus a percentage of income above £100.
- Basic rate: £200 to £800 a week, as a percentage of gross income: 12 per cent for one child, 16 per cent for two, 19 per cent for three or more.
- Basic plus: for income above £800 up to £3,000, the basic rate on the first £800 plus 9, 12 or 15 per cent (one, two, three or more children) of the remainder (CSA 1991 Sch 1 para 2(2)). Income over £3,000 a week is ignored (Sch 1 para 10(3)).
- Default rate where the paying parent's income is unknown or not provided: £38 (one child), £51 (two), £64 (three or more) per week.
GOV.UK presents the basic and basic plus bands together as a single "basic rate" for £200 to £3,000; the percentages above are those in CSA 1991 Sch 1 para 2.

**Shared care.** A paying parent who has the child overnight reduces maintenance per child: for 52 to 103 nights a year, one-seventh (14.29 per cent); 104-155 nights, two-sevenths (28.57 per cent); 156-174 nights, three-sevenths (42.86 per cent); 175 nights or more, one half (50 per cent) plus a further £7 a week reduction. A minimum of £7 a week is payable (unless the nil rate applies). The paying parent on the flat rate who receives benefits and has 52 or more nights pays nothing for that child. Nights are counted according to a court order or agreed arrangement; if parents agree 52 or more nights but not the number, 52 is assumed.

**Variations.** The paying parent's or receiving parent's request can lead to a variation where there are special expenses (for example, costs of contact with a disabled child; boarding school fees), or "additional cases" such as unearned income or assets, or a lifestyle inconsistent with declared income. The CMS can also decide in extreme cases that income is understated.

**Enforcement.** The CMS may deduct payments from earnings (deduction from earnings orders, DEOs), or order deductions from bank accounts (Deduction Order), use a liability order from the magistrates' court (CSA 1991 s 33) which then allows bailiff action, charging orders, and enforcement; and, from the order, disqualification from driving and committal to prison (s 39A and s 40). The CMS can add collection fees. Arrears may be written off only in limited cases.

**Reviews and appeals.** A person aggrieved can request a mandatory reconsideration (reviews of decisions) and then appeal to the First-tier Tribunal (Social Entitlement Chamber). Annual reviews of calculations occur and a change in circumstances (income change of 25 per cent or more) can lead to a revision.

**Role of the courts (s 8).** The CSA 1991 s 8(3): where the CMS would have jurisdiction, no court may exercise any power to make, vary or revive a maintenance order in relation to the child and non-resident parent. Exceptions: (a) s 8(5): the court may make a consent order (a maintenance order in the same terms as a written agreement between the parents); (b) s 8(6): "top-up" maintenance where a CMS maintenance calculation is in force and the non-resident parent's gross weekly income exceeds the statutory cap (£3,000 a week, so £156,000 a year); (c) s 8(7): educational or training expenses; (d) s 8(8): disability expenses; (e) s 8(10): orders against a person with care. A written maintenance agreement does not prevent either parent applying to the CMS (s 9(3)-(4)); and where a court consent order for child maintenance made on or after 3 March 2003 has been in force for at least a year, either parent may apply to the CMS (s 4(10)(aa)), after which the CMS calculation replaces the order. The court retains power to make lump sum and property transfer orders for children (MCA 1973 s 23(1)(d) and (f), Sch 1 CA 1989 para 1) as these are not periodical payments, though in practice cases of this kind are limited. The court also has power over step-children (MCA 1973 s 25(4)), for whom the CSA does not generally provide, and children where the CMS has no jurisdiction (e.g. non-resident parent living abroad).

## Key authorities
- *Child Support Act 1991*, esp. ss 1, 8, 11, 33, 39A, 40 and Sch 1 (formula); Child Support Maintenance Calculation Regulations 2012.
- *R (Kehoe) v Secretary of State for Work and Pensions* [2005] UKHL 48, [2006] 1 AC 42: a parent with care complained that she could not herself take court proceedings to enforce arrears against the non-resident parent. The House of Lords (Baroness Hale dissenting) held that the Child Support Act 1991 gave her no such right: enforcement is exclusively for the Secretary of State, and because she had no civil right to enforce, art 6 ECHR was not engaged.

## Applying it
For a problem question: (1) Is there a qualifying child and a non-resident parent? Is the CMS the right forum (s 8(3))? (2) Calculate: gross weekly income to the right band, apply the percentage for the number of qualifying children, adjust for other children and shared care; give figures only after checking current rates. (3) If the income is over £3,000 a week, consider a court top-up order under s 8(6). (4) Where school fees are in issue, consider s 8(7). (5) Where the parents agree an amount, the court can only embody it in a consent order (s 8(5)) or the parents can make their own agreement (CMS can still be applied to after the agreement lapses; an agreement cannot oust the CMS: s 9(4)). (6) Enforcement: DEO, liability orders, driving disqualification. (7) Housing and lump sum: Sch 1 CA 1989 claim by the parent with care (unmarried parents), or MCA 1973 claims for married parents. Common traps: saying a court can set child periodical payments in a divorce financial order (it cannot, if the CMS has jurisdiction, except by consent or the stated exceptions); applying the old (2003) scheme figures (net income) rather than the 2012 scheme (gross income); forgetting the nil/flat rate and shared care rules. Everyday: "The Child Maintenance Service calculates payments from the paying parent's income; courts only step in for top-ups, school fees, disability costs or to approve an agreement."

## Debates and reform
- Effectiveness of the CMS: arrears, collection costs, fees to parents, and the reliability of HMRC data for the self-employed; the Work and Pensions Committee has repeatedly criticised delays and enforcement.
- Whether collection fees deter applicants, particularly victims of domestic abuse, and whether they should be waived for compliant parents and abuse survivors.
- Domestic abuse: the CMS can itself be used as a form of economic abuse (for example through non-payment or manipulation of declared income); the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 s 1(4) recognises economic abuse in the statutory definition.
- The gap between CMS awards and the cost of raising a child; whether the formula adequately recognises shared care.

## Recent changes and points to check
- The rate bands, flat rate, default rates and shared care fractions above match GOV.UK as at November 2026; the basic and basic plus percentages and the other-children reductions are in CSA 1991 Sch 1 para 2.
- 26 February 2024: CMS application fee abolished. Collection fees remain (20 per cent paying parent, 4 per cent receiving parent).
- Announced but not yet enacted: abolition of Direct Pay and a single collection service with reduced fees. Liability orders are still obtained from the magistrates' court (s 33).
