# Financial remedies on divorce - MCA 1973 ss 23-25A, sharing, needs, compensation and non-matrimonial property

## Overview
On divorce, dissolution or nullity the court has wide discretionary powers over income, capital and pensions under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 (MCA 1973), guided by the s 25 factors (with first consideration to the welfare of any minor child) and by three principles developed in *White*, *Miller* and *McFarlane*: needs, sharing and compensation. Matrimonial property is generally shared equally unless there is good reason; non-matrimonial property is protected from the sharing principle but may be reached to meet needs. *Standish v Standish* [2025] UKSC 26 is now the leading authority on when non-matrimonial property is treated as matrimonial. The courts must also consider a clean break (s 25A).

## The law
**Powers.** Financial provision orders (s 23): periodical payments, secured periodical payments, lump sums (for a spouse, and for children, including lump sums for children). Property adjustment orders (s 24): transfer of property, settlement of property, variation of ante- or post-nuptial settlements, and order for sale under s 24A. Pension sharing orders (s 24B) and pension attachment orders (ss 25B-25D): see the pensions note. Orders may be made on or after the divorce order, and some take effect only when the divorce order is final.

**Section 25.** The court must have regard to all the circumstances, first consideration being given to the welfare of any minor child of the family under 18 (s 25(1)). Section 25(2): (a) income, earning capacity, property and other financial resources which each party has or is likely to have in the foreseeable future, including any increase in earning capacity it would be reasonable to expect a party to take steps to acquire; (b) financial needs, obligations and responsibilities; (c) standard of living before breakdown; (d) age of each party and duration of the marriage; (e) physical or mental disability; (f) contributions to the welfare of the family, including looking after the home or caring for the family; (g) conduct, if inequitable to disregard it; (h) the value of any benefit (for example a pension) which a party will lose the chance of acquiring through divorce. There is no hierarchy among (a)-(h); the "yardstick of equality" cross-check in *White v White* (existing case note) was refined in *Miller*/*McFarlane* (existing case note). Section 25(3) sets child-specific factors for orders in favour of children.

**Section 25A: clean break.** On or after making a divorce order, the court must consider whether to exercise its powers so that the financial obligations of each party towards the other end as soon after the divorce order as the court considers just and reasonable (s 25A(1)); where it makes periodical payments in favour of a spouse it must consider whether payments should be limited to a term sufficient to enable the payee to adjust without undue hardship (s 25A(2)); if the court considers that no continuing obligation should be imposed, it may dismiss the claim with a direction that no further application be made (s 25A(3)). Unsecured periodical payments to a spouse end on the death of either party or the remarriage (or new civil partnership) of the payee (s 28(1)(a)); so the payer's death also ends them, which is why life insurance or a secured order may be needed, and on making a term order the court may direct that the payee may not apply under s 31 to extend the term (s 28(1A)).

**The three principles (as developed in the House of Lords).** *Needs*: the primary driver in most cases, especially where resources are limited, and a standard of generosity in larger cases. *Sharing*: marriage as a partnership of equals, shared equally unless there is good reason to depart, applied to "matrimonial property" (assets generated by the parties' joint endeavour during the marriage). *Compensation*: where a party has suffered a relationship-generated disadvantage (typically through giving up a career) not met by needs or sharing (*McFarlane*). Compensation is rarely awarded as a free-standing head and needs to be shown on the facts.

**Matrimonial and non-matrimonial property.** Matrimonial property typically means the family home, savings and assets acquired during the marriage by joint effort; non-matrimonial property includes assets owned before the marriage, gifts and inheritances received by one party, and perhaps post-separation accruals, subject to being "matrimonialised" by how the parties treated them. *Standish v Standish* [2025] UKSC 26 (Supreme Court, 2 July 2025, a single unanimous judgment): in 2017, about three years before the marriage broke down, the husband transferred investment funds then worth about £77.8 million (about £80 million at trial), mostly his pre-marital wealth, into the wife's sole name as an inheritance tax scheme; the intention was that she would settle them on discretionary trusts for the children, which never happened. Moor J treated the whole £80 million as matrimonial and awarded the wife £45 million; the Court of Appeal held that 75 per cent of the 2017 assets remained non-matrimonial, reduced the sharing award to about £25 million and remitted the case for a needs assessment. The Supreme Court dismissed the wife's appeal and upheld the Court of Appeal's orders. It held: (1) the distinction between matrimonial and non-matrimonial property turns on the source of the asset, not on who holds title; (2) "the sharing principle only applies to matrimonial property and does not apply to non-matrimonial property", although non-matrimonial property remains available to meet needs and compensation (paras 48-49, rejecting the contrary view in *Charman v Charman (No 4)* [2007] EWCA Civ 503); (3) matrimonial property should normally be shared equally, which is the principled starting point (para 50); (4) non-matrimonial property can become matrimonial ("matrimonialisation"), which is neither a narrow nor a wide concept: the question is whether the parties, over a period long enough to be regarded as settled, have treated the asset as shared between them. Wilson LJ's three situations in *K v L* [2011] EWCA Civ 550 are illustrations, not exclusive categories (paras 51-55). On the facts there was no matrimonialisation, because the transfer was made to save tax and for the benefit of the children, not to share the assets with the wife.

**Maintenance and variation: *Mills v Mills*** [2018] UKSC 38: a variation case (MCA 1973 s 31). Under a 2002 consent order the wife received about £230,000 capital (enough to house herself) and joint lives periodical payments. Through a series of unwise property transactions she exhausted the capital and by 2015 was renting with no capital. On cross-applications, Judge Everall QC refused the husband's applications to discharge or term the payments and refused the wife's application to increase them to cover her rent. The Court of Appeal increased the payments; the Supreme Court (Lord Wilson, unanimous) allowed the husband's appeal and restored the judge's order. The question was whether a court is entitled to decline to increase periodical payments to fund rent where the payee was given capital for a home and dissipated it; the answer was yes. The court is entitled, not obliged, to decline; but "a court would need to give very good reasons" for requiring a spouse to fund the other's rent in those circumstances, since an obligation to duplicate housing provision already made "is most improbable" (para 40). The husband's challenge to the refusal to discharge or term the order was not before the Court because the Court of Appeal had refused him permission. Treat *Mills* as authority on needs the payee has created by dissipating capital provision, within the wide discretion under s 31(1) and (7).

**Disclosure.** Parties must give full and frank financial disclosure (Form E; *Livesey v Jenkins* [1985] AC 424). A consent order made without proper disclosure may be set aside if the non-disclosure was material, that is, if it led the court to make an order substantially different from the order it would have made with full disclosure (*Livesey*). Where the non-disclosure was fraudulent, the burden shifts: in *Sharland v Sharland* [2015] UKSC 60 (husband lied about plans to float his company) the Supreme Court held that a consent order obtained by fraud will be set aside unless the party resisting it shows that the court would not have made a substantially different order had the truth been known, and set the order aside. In *Gohil v Gohil* [2015] UKSC 61 (husband later convicted of money laundering) the Court held that the same approach applies to deliberate non-disclosure, and allowed the wife's appeal against the Court of Appeal's decision not to set the consent order aside. *Barder v Barder* [1988] AC 20: unforeseen events after an order within a short time may justify appeal out of time.

**Third-party assets.** *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] UKSC 34: MCA 1973 s 24 allows transfer only of the property of the spouse; piercing the corporate veil is restricted (evasion principle), but a company's assets held on resulting trust for the spouse can be transferred.

**Procedure.** Family Procedure Rules 2010 Part 9 (Form A, First Appointment, Financial Dispute Resolution appointment, final hearing; Form E; mediation information and assessment meeting (MIAM) required before applying). Consent orders need court approval.

## Key authorities
- *White v White* [2001] 1 AC 596 (HL): existing case note; no bias in favour of the money-maker; yardstick of equality.
- *Miller v Miller; McFarlane v McFarlane* [2006] UKHL 24 (HL): existing case note; needs, sharing, compensation.
- *Standish v Standish* [2025] UKSC 26: non-matrimonial property and the sharing principle.
- *Mills v Mills* [2018] UKSC 38: variation; court entitled to refuse to fund needs the payee created by dissipating capital provision.
- *K v L* [2011] EWCA Civ 550 (CA): non-matrimonial property, and when it is matrimonialised.
- *Charman v Charman (No 4)* [2007] EWCA Civ 503, [2007] 1 FLR 1246 (CA): sharing in big money cases and the "special contribution" debate. Its statement (para 66) that the sharing principle applies to all the parties' property, including non-matrimonial property, was expressly rejected in *Standish* (paras 48-49) and is no longer good law.
- *Prest v Petrodel* [2013] UKSC 34; *Sharland v Sharland* [2015] UKSC 60; *Gohil v Gohil* [2015] UKSC 61.
- *Barder v Barder* [1988] AC 20 (HL).

## Applying it
For a problem question: (1) Assets: list all resources (home, savings, business, pensions, trusts). (2) Child first: housing for the primary carer and child (s 25(1)). (3) Needs: income and housing need for each; are they met? Is the pot large enough for more? (4) Matrimonial/non-matrimonial: identify the origin of each asset and whether the parties' treatment merged it (*Standish*). (5) Apply sharing to matrimonial property: start from equality and consider departure (special reasons are rare). (6) Compensation: only if one party gave up career for the family and suffers a disadvantage. (7) Clean break and term maintenance (s 25A). (8) Pensions: see the pensions note. (9) Procedure points: disclosure and the effect of conduct (rarely relevant unless gross: s 25(2)(g)). (10) Variation and appeals: *Mills*, *Barder*. For an everyday question: the court looks at needs, home for the children, and equal sharing of what was built together; inheritances and pre-marriage wealth are often kept separate.

## Debates and reform
- Predictability: the Law Commission's scoping report, *Financial remedies on divorce and dissolution* (Law Com No 417, 18 December 2024), concluded the current law lacks certainty and accessibility and reform is needed, but made no recommendation and set out four models (from codification of the case law to default rules); the Government has since consulted: see the recent changes section.
- Spousal maintenance (term limits, long-term dependency) and the "meal ticket for life" criticism; the compensation principle's lack of use; the treatment of non-matrimonial property after *Standish*.
- Conduct and domestic abuse: whether s 25(2)(g) should be reformed so that abuse is taken into account; also the problem of financial abuse in disclosure.
- Costs and access: litigant-in-person difficulties.

## Recent changes and points to check
- Standish v Standish [2025] UKSC 26 (2 July 2025).
- The Ministry of Justice consultation "A fairer end to relationships" (CP 1581, 5 June to 14 August 2026) proposes a "codification-plus" model: an overarching objective of a fair outcome through the sharing and needs principles (compensation becoming a factor rather than a core principle); equal sharing of matrimonial property as the starting point, departed from only to meet needs; statutory definitions of matrimonial and non-matrimonial property reflecting *Standish*; a staged needs assessment with children's needs first; a specific duty to consider pensions accrued during the marriage; reconsideration of how conduct, including economic abuse, is treated; and binding "qualifying nuptial agreements". It does not propose changing the types of order available. These are proposals only; no Bill had been published by November 2026, and the current law applies. See also the nuptial agreements and cohabitants notes.

## Common errors to avoid

- Error: treating only the marriage-date value of a pre-marital business as non-matrimonial. It can include latent/springboard value plus passive growth (Jones v Jones [2011] EWCA Civ 41); apportionment is broad and evaluative, not arithmetic (Hart v Hart [2017] EWCA Civ 1306). - Error: saying unsecured spousal periodical payments end only on the payee's death or remarriage. Under MCA 1973 s 28(1)(a) they also end on the payer's death; only a secured order (s 28(1)(b)) survives it, hence life cover or securing the order. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q17#2]
- Error: describing Standish v Standish [2025] UKSC 26 as concerning inherited funds. The £80m was mostly the husband's pre-marital wealth; 'inheritance' refers only to the purpose of the 2017 transfer (an inheritance tax scheme), not the source. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q17#3]
