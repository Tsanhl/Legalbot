# Miller v Miller; McFarlane v McFarlane [2006] UKHL 24 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. Estimates of Mr Miller's wealth vary and are left
> approximate. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 24 May 2006 (Lords Nicholls, Hoffmann and Hope, Baroness Hale and Lord Mance). Two appeals heard
together. The House identified three strands of fairness on divorce: **needs**, **compensation** and **sharing**.

## Facts
- **Miller:** a short, childless marriage of about two years and nine months. The husband, an asset manager in the City, was very
  wealthy (a press estimate of about £30 million is reported; other figures circulate, so it is left approximate). The wife received £5 million in
  capital (the matrimonial home and a lump sum). The House dismissed the husband's appeal and the award stood.
- **McFarlane:** a long marriage with three children. Both spouses had had successful careers; the wife gave up her
  career as a solicitor to look after the children. The capital (about £3 million, according to one source) was
  divided broadly equally and the dispute concerned the husband's ongoing payments. The Court of Appeal ([2004] EWCA
  Civ 872) had ordered periodical payments of £250,000 a year but limited them to five years. The House of Lords
  **removed the five-year limit** and restored an order of £250,000 a year for the parties' joint lives, recognising
  that periodical payments can serve to compensate as well as to maintain; the husband could still apply to vary.

## Principles
- Fair financial provision at the end of a marriage is guided by: (1) meeting financial **needs** generated by the
  relationship; (2) **compensation** for relationship-generated economic disadvantage; and (3) **sharing** the fruits of
  the matrimonial partnership.
- *McFarlane* is treated as the paradigm for compensation: Lord Nicholls's example was a case where one spouse's
  earning capacity was advanced by the marriage while the other's was left significantly handicapped, giving a
  significant prospective economic disparity.
- Equal sharing of matrimonial property is treated as the yardstick (building on *White v White*) subject to
  exceptions, and in *Miller* it applied even though the marriage was short. The origin of property and the length of the
  marriage may affect what it is fair to share.
- Compensation and needs overlap, so double-counting must be avoided, and there is no hard rule on whether to begin with
  sharing and depart for need or compensation (reported as Baroness Hale's point).

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Work through the section 25 factors, then organise the analysis around needs, compensation and sharing.
2. Separate matrimonial property (shared equally unless there is good reason) from non-matrimonial property
   (inherited or pre-marital), whose treatment depends on need and on the marriage's length.
3. If a spouse gave up a career for the family, argue compensation for lost earning capacity (*McFarlane*), and avoid
   counting the same loss as need.
4. In a short marriage, argue that equal sharing still applies to matrimonial assets built up during it (*Miller*).
