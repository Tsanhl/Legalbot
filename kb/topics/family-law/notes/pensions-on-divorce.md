# Pensions on divorce - sharing, attachment, offsetting and the PAG guidance

## Overview
Pensions are often the largest or second largest asset on divorce but are frequently overlooked. A court dealing with financial remedies must take pension rights into account (MCA 1973 s 25(2)(a) and (h), s 25B), and has three main tools: pension sharing (a "clean break" transfer of a percentage of the pension value to the other spouse, MCA 1973 s 24B), pension attachment (formerly "earmarking", ss 25B-25D, payments from the member's pension when they come into payment), and offsetting (trading pension value against other assets, such as the home). Pension sharing is now the usual first choice where it suits the case, supported by the guidance of the Pension Advisory Group (PAG).

## The law
**Pensions as a resource.** Section 25(2)(a) (resources "which each of the parties has or is likely to have") includes benefits under a pension arrangement (s 25B(1)(a)); s 25(2)(h) (the value of a benefit that a party will lose the chance of acquiring by reason of dissolution) includes pension benefits lost (s 25B(1)(b)). The court has power to order disclosure of pension information; a Cash Equivalent Transfer Value (CETV) is the usual measure (WRPA 1999 s 30: cash equivalents), and the scheme must provide valuations within a prescribed period. The state pension is covered separately (see below).

**Pension sharing.** MCA 1973 s 21A defines a pension sharing order as an order that provides that one party's shareable rights under a specified pension arrangement (or shareable state scheme rights) are subject to pension sharing for the benefit of the other party, and specifies the percentage value to be transferred. Section 24B: on or after making a divorce or nullity order, the court may, on application, make one or more pension sharing orders in relation to the marriage; the order is not to take effect unless the divorce order has been made final (s 24B(2)). Orders can be made against rights under occupational and personal pensions and shareable state scheme rights. Pensions already in payment can be shared, but a pension sharing order cannot be made in relation to an arrangement that is already subject to a pension sharing order, or to a pension attachment order, in relation to the same marriage (s 24B(3)-(5)); rights under overseas schemes cannot be shared by an English order. The Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999 (WRPA 1999) Part IV Chapter I (ss 27-46) provides the mechanism: s 28 (activation of pension sharing), s 29 (creation of pension debits and credits) so that the member's pension is reduced (a "pension debit") and the former spouse gets a "pension credit", s 30 (cash equivalents), s 31 (reduction of the member's benefit), ss 33-35 (time and mode of discharge by the scheme within the four-month "implementation period" in s 34), s 37 (pension credit benefit), s 41 (charges). The percentage is of the CETV; the receiving spouse can take an internal transfer (becoming a member of the scheme with the credit) or an external transfer to another approved arrangement. Pension sharing for the benefit of the other spouse creates their own pension, avoids the member's need to retire, and gives a clean break; sharing of state scheme rights (WRPA Part IV Chapter II, s 47 onwards) applies to shareable state scheme rights such as the additional state pension and, for those under the new state pension, the protected payment. The basic state pension and the flat-rate new state pension are not shareable. A pension compensation sharing order (s 21B and s 24E) applies to Pension Protection Fund compensation.

**Pension attachment (earmarking).** MCA 1973 s 25B(4) and (7): the court can require the person responsible for the pension arrangement to pay part of any benefit that becomes due to the member to the former spouse, expressed as a percentage of the payment (s 25B(5)); the order can also direct that a lump sum due on retirement be paid to the other party (s 25C: lump sums, including on death of the member before retirement). Attachment orders bite only when the member's benefits come into payment. An attachment of pension income takes effect as a periodical payments order, so it ends on the death of either party or the recipient's remarriage or new civil partnership (s 28(1)); attachment of lump sums (including death benefits under s 25C) is not ended by remarriage; the member retains control of timing (when to retire), and the arrangement leaves the parties financially entangled, so they are now rarely made, except where sharing is unavailable (some foreign or unfunded schemes) or the other spouse needs income immediately. WRPA 1999 Part III (s 19 and Sch 3) amended ss 25B-25D into their current form; WRPA s 24 deals with charges by pension arrangements for earmarking orders. MCA 1973 s 25D contains supplementary provisions (including what happens when the member transfers rights to another arrangement, and regulation-making powers). Attachment orders can be varied under s 31.

**Offsetting.** The court may take the value of one party's pension into account by giving the other a larger share of other assets (typically the home) in exchange, instead of a pension share. Offsetting is a matter of valuation: a CETV is not a reliable measure of the "real" value of a pension that is a defined benefit scheme (public sector schemes where CETV may undervalue) and may differ from the "fund" value in a defined contribution pension. The court will often instruct a pension on divorce expert (PODE), a jointly instructed actuary, in complex cases or where the pension assets are significant. Offsetting is appropriate where the cash for it is available and the parties do not wish to remain linked; but it risks unfairness if the pension and the cash assets have different liquidity and tax treatments (pension money is taxed on income; capital in a house is not). 

**Pension Advisory Group guidance.** The Pension Advisory Group (PAG), a multi-disciplinary group set up under the auspices of the Family Justice Council, published "A Guide to the Treatment of Pensions on Divorce" (the "PAG report") in July 2019, and a second edition ("PAG2") was published online in December 2023 and launched in January 2024, endorsed by the President of the Family Division and the Family Justice Council. Its main themes: the marital element of pensions is generally shared on the same principles as other matrimonial property (so non-matrimonial accruals, such as pre-marriage service and post-separation accrual, are treated differently, and *Standish* is relevant); the need to distinguish a sharing exercise from a needs (income in retirement) exercise; the choice between an approach that equalises capital (sharing CETVs) and one that equalises the income each party will receive, which can produce a different result for defined benefit schemes; when a joint expert (PODE) is needed; and the treatment of public sector and other special schemes and the state pension. The guidance has no statutory force; courts treat it as persuasive and practitioners use it widely.

**Practical points.** A pension sharing order requires the court to have the scheme's information (the s 23 WRPA information regulations) and to serve notice on the pension scheme before the order is made so the scheme can raise implementation issues. A "pension sharing annex" (form P1) is sent to the scheme. Remarriage does not affect a pension share but ends attachment of pension income. An error in a pension sharing order can result in a delay; a pension sharing order can be varied only on an application made before it takes effect and before the divorce order is made final (s 31(2)(g), (4A)), and otherwise only by appeal. Time limits for implementation are four months from the later of the order or the information being provided (WRPA s 34). Disclosure includes CETVs for all pensions, including the state pension forecasts.

## Key authorities
- *Brooks v Brooks* [1996] AC 375 (HL): a pension scheme was held to be a post-nuptial settlement the court could vary, which allowed the court to protect the wife (in a time when pension assets could not be shared); now largely superseded by sharing orders.
- *White v White* [2001] 1 AC 596 and *Miller; McFarlane* [2006] UKHL 24 (existing case notes): the sharing principle and needs principle underpin pension awards.
- *Standish v Standish* [2025] UKSC 26: non-matrimonial property and the sharing principle; relevant to pre-marriage and post-separation pension accruals (see the financial remedies note).
- Other pension case law (for example on the use of CETVs and PODEs) is set out in the PAG report.

## Applying it
Steps: (1) List all pensions: type (defined benefit, defined contribution, state), value (CETV), accrual period, whether in payment. (2) Decide the objective: income in retirement versus capital now. (3) Check ages and needs: retirement dates, other assets, mortgage-free housing. (4) Apply s 25 and the *White/Miller* principles: share the marital element equally unless there is a reason to depart; consider non-marital accruals (pre-marriage service, post-separation). (5) Choose the tool: sharing (clean break; most cases), offset (where cash assets suitable), attachment (rare). (6) Expert evidence: PODE where the values are large or complex. (7) Timing: the sharing order takes effect only on the final order; implement within four months; ensure that the divorce is not finalised before the pension is dealt with (especially loss of survivor benefits). (8) Costs and tax: lifetime allowance abolished from April 2024 (replaced by lump sum allowances), so a pension debit/credit has limited tax significance. Common traps: forgetting pensions entirely; treating the CETV as a true value; ignoring public sector schemes; failing to deal with death benefit loss. Everyday: "A court can transfer part of your pension to your ex, or give them more of other assets instead; get a CETV for every pension before agreeing a settlement."

## Debates and reform
- Valuation: CETVs may be inaccurate for defined benefit schemes; PAG recommends using PODEs; the 2019 and 2023/24 editions encourage uniform methodology.
- Gender pension gap: the pension gap between women and men; whether the law should require pension sharing more often in long marriages and whether the "sharing principle" fully addresses the gap; Law Commission scoping report 2024 on financial remedies (Law Com No 417) noted pension issues and the case for guidance; the Ministry of Justice consultation "A fairer end to relationships" (June to August 2026) proposes a specific duty on the court to consider pension needs and pensions accrued during the marriage, but not default equal sharing of pensions (proposals only).
- Cohabitants: pension sharing and attachment orders under the MCA 1973 are available only on divorce, nullity or dissolution, so cohabitants have no equivalent; whether any future cohabitant scheme should reach pensions is open.
- Complexity and cost: lack of consistent treatment and the need for experts; calls for a pensions "calculator" and simplified approach in modest cases.

## Recent changes and points to check
- PAG2 (second edition, December 2023 / January 2024) is the current PAG guide.
- The lifetime allowance was abolished from 6 April 2024 and replaced by the lump sum allowance and the lump sum and death benefit allowance. Under the Finance Act 2026 (Royal Assent 18 March 2026), most unused pension funds and pension death benefits will form part of a person's estate for inheritance tax for deaths on or after 6 April 2027 (death-in-service benefits are excluded), which is relevant to the value of lost death benefits on divorce.
- From 6 April 2016 the new state pension applies to those reaching state pension age after that date, which has only a "protected payment" shareable element.
- The 2026 consultation on financial remedies is a proposal only.

## Common errors to avoid

- Error: implying the Pensions Act 1995 let courts divide pensions. It introduced earmarking/attachment (MCA 1973 ss 25B-25D), which only redirects payments; pension sharing (s 24B) came with the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999, for proceedings begun on or after 1 December 2000.
