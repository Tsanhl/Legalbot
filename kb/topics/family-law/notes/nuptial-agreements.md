# Nuptial agreements - Radmacher and the reform proposals

## Overview
In England and Wales a pre-nuptial or post-nuptial agreement is not automatically binding, because the court's jurisdiction over financial remedies on divorce (MCA 1973 ss 23-25) cannot be ousted by private agreement. Since *Radmacher v Granatino* [2010] UKSC 42, however, the court will give decisive weight to an agreement freely entered into by each party with a full appreciation of its implications, unless it would not be fair in the circumstances to hold the parties to it. The Law Commission recommended legislation for "qualifying nuptial agreements" in 2014; Parliament has not enacted it, but the Government's 2026 consultation revives the idea.

## The law
**Starting point: the court's jurisdiction cannot be ousted.** Agreements which purport to prevent a spouse applying to court on divorce are contrary to public policy (*Hyman v Hyman* [1929] AC 601, HL). An agreement is therefore a factor under s 25 MCA 1973 (the "all the circumstances" requirement), not a contract enforceable as such. Agreements may also be governed by general contract and family-law concepts of fairness (duress, undue influence, misrepresentation, and, for a spouse's capacity to understand, independent advice).

**Radmacher.** *Radmacher (formerly Granatino) v Granatino* [2010] UKSC 42, [2011] 1 AC 534 (Supreme Court, 8-1): the wife was a wealthy German heiress and the husband a French national who had worked in banking and later became a researcher; they signed a pre-nuptial agreement in Germany before marrying, providing that neither would claim against the other's property on divorce. The first-instance judge gave it reduced weight and made a substantial award; the Court of Appeal gave it decisive weight and limited the husband's provision to what he needed in his role as father of the children (housing to revert to the wife when the children no longer needed it); the Supreme Court (8-1) dismissed the husband's appeal and upheld that order. The test (Lord Phillips, giving the majority judgment, para 75): "The court should give effect to a nuptial agreement that is freely entered into by each party with a full appreciation of its implications unless in the circumstances prevailing it would not be fair to hold the parties to their agreement."
- *Free entry*: factors such as duress, fraud, misrepresentation and undue pressure; independent legal advice is relevant, but the court looks to the substantive reality.
- *Full appreciation*: each party must understand the effect, with disclosure appropriate to the circumstances. The agreement need not have been preceded by full financial disclosure, but each party must have understood the main effects.
- *Fairness at the date of the application*: the agreement's weight is reduced where the terms leave one party in need, or without proper provision for children, or where circumstances at the time of the divorce were not foreseen. The court will not let the agreement prejudice the reasonable requirements of any children of the family. A party's needs generated by the relationship (including childcare responsibilities) will normally justify departure. In *Radmacher* the husband was able to earn a good living and his needs were not generated by the marriage, so the agreement was given effect save for the provision referable to his role as the children's father.
- *Needs, compensation and sharing*: an agreement is most likely to be upheld where it excludes sharing (for example, ring-fencing pre-marital or inherited wealth); it will rarely be fair to hold a party to an agreement that leaves them in real need or uncompensated for relationship-generated disadvantage (*Radmacher*, paras 81-82).
- Baroness Hale was the sole dissentient: she rejected a presumption in favour of the agreement, would have asked simply whether, in the circumstances, it would be fair to hold the parties to it, stressed the gendered effects of such agreements, and thought reform of this kind should be for Parliament after Law Commission work (which was then under way).
- Foreign agreements: English law applies on divorce in England whatever law governs the agreement, but the fact that the agreement was intended to be binding under the law the parties chose (here German law) is relevant to showing that they intended it to be effective.

**Procedure and formalities.** There is no statutory form. In practice, prudent practice includes: each side taking independent legal advice; full financial disclosure; execution well before the wedding (the Law Commission suggested at least 28 days); a clear statement that the parties understand it; and, for a post-nuptial agreement, a contract or deed with consideration where needed. Pre- and post-nuptial agreements apply by analogy to civil partnerships.

## Key authorities
- *Radmacher (formerly Granatino) v Granatino* [2010] UKSC 42: governing test; weight depends on freedom, appreciation and fairness.
- *Hyman v Hyman* [1929] AC 601 (HL): agreements cannot oust the court's jurisdiction.
- *MacLeod v MacLeod* [2008] UKPC 64, [2010] 1 AC 298 (Privy Council): the Privy Council (on appeal from the Isle of Man) distinguished pre-nuptial from post-nuptial agreements and was more cautious about the former; *Radmacher* later rejected that distinction for England and Wales, so *MacLeod* is of limited use on this point.
- *Edgar v Edgar* [1980] 1 WLR 1410 (CA): separation agreements: weight attached to a prior agreement, with a spouse with competent advice held to a bad bargain unless there is undue pressure or an unfair result.
- *Miller; McFarlane* [2006] UKHL 24 and *White v White* [2001] 1 AC 596 (existing case notes): the underlying principles that an agreement must be assessed against, notably the needs principle.

## Applying it
Problem question steps: (1) Identify the type of agreement (pre-nuptial, post-nuptial, or separation) and when and where made. (2) Apply the *Radmacher* test: was it freely entered into? Was there duress or pressure close to the wedding? Did each party have advice and an understanding of the terms (not necessarily full disclosure)? (3) Circumstances at the time of the hearing: Does the agreement leave a party unable to meet needs, or fail to provide for children? Were there changes the parties did not foresee (children, illness, a career given up for the family)? (4) Result: give decisive weight to the terms that are fair, but depart to the extent needed to meet needs, compensate for relationship-generated disadvantage, or protect children. Common traps: saying the agreement is "binding"; forgetting the needs override; neglecting to ask whether the agreement was signed shortly before the wedding. Everyday: "A prenup is not automatically enforceable in England and Wales, but a properly made one will usually be followed unless it is unfair, especially if it leaves someone or the children in need."

## Debates and reform
- Law Commission, *Matrimonial Property, Needs and Agreements* (Law Com No 343, February 2014): recommended that "qualifying nuptial agreements" (made by deed, at least 28 days before the wedding, with disclosure of material information and independent legal advice) should be enforceable and not be subject to the court's discretion, except that they cannot contract out of a spouse's financial needs; the Law Commission also recommended guidance on financial needs. The Government did not implement these recommendations.
- Argument for: autonomy, predictability and costs; argument against: gender inequality, the vulnerable spouse, bargaining power and the risk of unfairness.
- Law Commission scoping report on financial remedies (Law Com No 417, 18 December 2024): it also covered nuptial agreements and described reform options without recommending one.
- Ministry of Justice consultation, "A fairer end to relationships" (CP 1581, 5 June to 14 August 2026): proposes "qualifying nuptial agreements" allowing couples to make binding financial arrangements in advance, with safeguards to ensure the decision is fully informed and freely made and that needs are met. This is a proposal only.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No statute yet makes nuptial agreements binding; *Radmacher* remains the law.
- The 2026 consultation closed on 14 August 2026; no Government response or Bill had been published by November 2026.
- Later first-instance and Court of Appeal decisions apply *Radmacher* to particular facts; the weight of an agreement always depends on the circumstances at the date of the hearing.
