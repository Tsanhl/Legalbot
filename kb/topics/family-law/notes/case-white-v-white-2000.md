# White v White [2001] 1 AC 596 (case note)

> Built from secondary sources only; the speeches were not read. Do not quote paragraph numbers from this note.

## Overview
House of Lords, 26 October 2000 (neutral citation [2000] UKHL 54; reported at [2001] 1 AC 596). Panel: Lord Nicholls
of Birkenhead, Lord Cooke of Thorndon, Lord Hoffmann, Lord Hope of Craighead and Lord Hutton. Lord Nicholls gave the
leading speech, which is the one quoted in all the sources. The case moved ancillary relief in "big money" divorces away from
the wife's "reasonable requirements" and introduced the **yardstick of equality** as a cross-check on fairness.

## Facts
Martin and Pamela White married in 1961 and were farmers who built up and ran the farming business in effect as
partners; they had children. The assets, including the farms, were worth about £4.5 to £4.6 million. When the marriage
ended each sought ancillary relief on a clean-break basis. At first instance Mrs White was awarded £800,000, aimed at
housing and income needs, and Mr White kept the farming business and the farms. The Court of Appeal increased the
award to £1.5 million, recognising her contribution to the farm and family.

## Decision
- The House of Lords dismissed both Mr White's appeal (seeking to restore the first-instance order) and Mrs White's
  cross-appeal (seeking an equal division), so the Court of Appeal's £1.5 million award stood.
- The "reasonable requirements" approach was rejected as the basis for awards in cases with large assets.
- There should be no discrimination between husband and wife and their respective roles (the earner and the home-maker
  and carer).
- Judges should check their tentative conclusions against the **yardstick of equality of division**. Equality is
  not a presumption or starting point (the House did not create a rule that assets are shared equally) but departure
  from equality needs good reason.
- Fairness remains the guiding aim under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973.

## Later development
Commentators note that the later case of *Cowan v Cowan* stressed that *White* does not create a rule of equality.
*Miller; McFarlane* (2006) then developed the principles of needs, compensation and sharing.

## Applying it to a problem question
1. Where assets exceed needs, do not stop at the claimant's "reasonable requirements".
2. Value the contributions of both spouses as equally important, whether earner or carer.
3. Cross-check the proposed division against equality; justify any departure with a reason (for example, special
   contributions, non-matrimonial property, or needs).
4. Add that equality is a check, not a presumption.
