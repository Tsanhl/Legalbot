# Cohabitants - property claims, Schedule 1 Children Act claims and reform

## Overview
There is no "common law marriage" in England and Wales: living together, however long, gives a couple no marriage-like financial rights. A cohabitant must rely on property law (trusts, proprietary estoppel, contract) to claim a share of a home, and on Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989 for provision for a child. The leading cases on shared homes are *Stack v Dowden* and *Jones v Kernott*. Reform proposed by the Law Commission in 2007 was never enacted; the Government consulted again from June to August 2026 on a statutory framework for eligible cohabitants, but the proposals are not law.

## The law
**No marriage rights.** Cohabitants have no statutory right to financial provision on separation under the MCA 1973; no automatic intestacy rights; limited rights under the Family Law Act 1996 (occupation orders for cohabitants are available but more restricted: ss 36 and 38, with a time limit of six months, renewable once, for non-entitled applicants).

**Property: the family home.**
- *Sole name, no express trust.* The claimant must establish a beneficial interest through a resulting, constructive or common intention trust: *Lloyds Bank plc v Rosset* [1991] 1 AC 107 (HL) set a strict approach (an express agreement or direct financial contribution to the purchase price for common intention), which *Stack* and *Jones* have softened for joint-name cases.
- *Joint names, no declaration of trust.* *Stack v Dowden* [2007] UKHL 17, [2007] 2 AC 432 (HL): where a home is conveyed into joint names, the presumption is that beneficial ownership follows the legal title (joint beneficial owners, equally); the burden is on a party who says otherwise to show that the parties had a different common intention; the court asks what the parties intended, inferred from their whole course of conduct in relation to the property (not only the financial contributions), including any relevant factors (Baroness Hale's list). In *Stack* the couple had kept their finances separate and the female partner had contributed much more to the purchase price; she was held entitled to 65 per cent.
- *Quantification and imputation.* *Jones v Kernott* [2011] UKSC 53, [2012] 1 AC 776 (Supreme Court): where parties have joint legal title and where their intentions as to shares cannot be inferred, the court may impute an intention to them, to reach what is fair having regard to the whole course of dealing; the starting point is that the parties intended joint ownership, and the burden of rebutting this is on the person who says otherwise; the first approach is to infer the common intention (actual intention), and only if that cannot be inferred does imputation arise. In *Jones*, the parties had separated, the house was bought jointly, and the Supreme Court restored the first-instance division of 90/10 in the woman's favour (she had remained in the home with the children and paid the outgoings, while the man had stopped contributing after separation), treating the parties' intentions as having changed at that point. *Stack* and *Jones* concern joint legal title. In a sole-name case the claimant must first prove an actual (express or inferred) common intention that they should have a beneficial interest, plus detrimental reliance; that intention cannot be imputed. Once it is established, if the parties had no actual intention about the size of the shares, the court may impute one by reference to the whole course of dealing (*Jones v Kernott*, para 52; *Abbott v Abbott* [2007] UKPC 53). The strict *Rosset* view that only direct contributions to the price can found an inferred intention has been doubted (Lord Walker in *Stack*, para 26).
- *Proprietary estoppel.* A claimant who was assured by the owner that they would have an interest in the property and acted to their detriment in reliance may be given a remedy that satisfies the equity (*Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18). In *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27 the majority (Lord Briggs, 3-2) held that the aim of the remedy is to prevent the unconscionability of the promisor going back on the assurance; the natural starting point is to fulfil the claimant's expectation (or its monetary equivalent), adjusted for accelerated receipt and checked so that it is not out of all proportion to the detriment. Lords Leggatt and Stephens, dissenting, favoured a remedy measured by compensating the detriment. The remedy is flexible and can take the form of a money award.
- *Contracts and declarations.* Couples can agree property shares by a cohabitation agreement or declaration of trust (a written declaration for land, in accordance with the Law of Property Act 1925 s 53(1)(b)); cohabitation agreements are treated as contracts in the ordinary way, subject to public policy. Resulting trusts apply to purchase money contributions.

**Children: Schedule 1 to the Children Act 1989.** A parent or guardian or a person with a residence (child arrangements) order may apply for (para 1(1)): periodical payments (including secured), lump sums, settlement of property, and transfer of property for the benefit of the child. Para 4 factors: all the circumstances, including the income, earning capacity, property and other financial resources of the applicant, parents and others; the financial needs, obligations and responsibilities of those persons; the financial needs of the child; the income and resources of the child; any physical or mental disability of the child; the manner in which the child was being, or was expected to be, educated or trained (para 4(1)). Para 4(2) adds factors regarding a person who is not a parent (whether that person assumed responsibility). Welfare of the child is not paramount but is a relevant factor (*Re P (A Child) (Financial Provision)* [2003] EWCA Civ 837). The usual way of housing the child is a settlement of property (or a lump sum to buy a home) for the child's minority or until the end of full-time education, with the property then reverting to the paying parent (*A v A (A Minor: Financial Provision)* [1994] 1 FLR 657); provision is for the child's benefit, so the carer does not receive capital outright for their own benefit, although an order can incidentally benefit the carer, as where a joint council tenancy was transferred to the mother for the benefit of the children (*K v K (Minors: Property Transfer)* [1992] 2 FLR 220 (CA)). The jurisdiction is limited to children; the child maintenance exclusion in the Child Support Act 1991 s 8 restricts periodical payments (see the child maintenance note). Order against a person with sufficient resources. Orders for periodical payments end at 18 in general but may continue to cover education.

**Other claims.** The Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 s 1(1A)-(1B) gives cohabitants who lived with the deceased for at least two years before death a claim for reasonable financial provision from the estate. Cohabitants may have claims under the Domestic Abuse Act regime and have standing for non-molestation orders under the FLA 1996 Part IV (associated persons: s 62(3)(b)). Tax: no spousal exemptions for inheritance tax and capital gains tax.

**Reform history.** Law Commission Report, *Cohabitation: The Financial Consequences of Relationship Breakdown* (Law Com No 307, 2007): proposed a scheme for eligible cohabitants (couples with a child or who lived together for a minimum period of two to five years), allowing claims where the applicant had contributions or economic advantage or disadvantage; the Government did not implement it, and in 2011 it announced it would not do so in that Parliament, as it would await evidence from the Scottish experience (Family Law (Scotland) Act 2006 ss 25-29, which gives a cohabitant limited claims). A private member's Cohabitation Rights Bill has been before Parliament in different forms but has not been enacted.

## Key authorities
- *Stack v Dowden* [2007] UKHL 17 (HL): joint names; presumption of equality rebuttable by whole course of dealing.
- *Jones v Kernott* [2011] UKSC 53 (Supreme Court): inference and imputation of intention.
- *Lloyds Bank plc v Rosset* [1991] 1 AC 107 (HL): sole-name homes; strict common intention requirement.
- *Thorner v Major* [2009] UKHL 18 (HL): proprietary estoppel by assurance and reliance.
- *Re P (A Child) (Financial Provision)* [2003] EWCA Civ 837 (CA): Sch 1 and the child's welfare.
- *Guest v Guest* [2022] UKSC 27: proprietary estoppel remedies, where the assurance relates to inheritance of property.

## Applying it
For a problem question: (1) Is the couple married or civil partners? If not, MCA 1973 does not apply. (2) Legal title: sole or joint name? (3) If joint: *Stack* presumption of equal shares, and then ask whether it is rebutted; apply *Jones* to infer or impute a different intention. (4) If sole: ask whether there is a common intention constructive trust (express discussion or conduct, and detrimental reliance), or a proprietary estoppel (assurance, reliance, detriment), or a resulting trust (direct contribution to the purchase price). (5) Children: Sch 1 claim for a lump sum or housing for the child; check the CMS role. (6) Inheritance if one dies: 1975 Act claim by cohabitant of two years or more; intestacy gives no share. (7) Practical advice: cohabitation agreement, declaration of trust, will. Common traps: assuming common law marriage; applying *Stack* to a sole-name home; confusing the presumption of equity with the quantification stage. Everyday: "A long-term partner has no right to maintenance, and a share of the home depends on the deeds and the couple's intention."

## Debates and reform
- Calls for reform from campaigners and parliamentary committees, and the argument that the "common-law marriage myth" leaves women (particularly mothers who give up work) financially vulnerable. Counter-argument: respect for choice not to marry; difficulty of defining cohabitation.
- The Law Commission's 2007 scheme was a restricted discretion, not equal sharing; the Scottish scheme uses a similar approach.
- Ministry of Justice consultation, "A fairer end to relationships" (CP 1581, 5 June to 14 August 2026): proposes a statutory scheme for eligible cohabitants (adults in a committed, interdependent relationship who have lived together for at least three years, or who live together and share a child), applying automatically unless both opt out. Legal ownership would be the starting point, with no sharing principle; the court could depart from it only to meet defined needs (narrower than on divorce), with children's welfare the first consideration, a strong emphasis on clean breaks and maintenance only in exceptional, time-limited cases; cohabitants could not do better than a comparable spouse. It also proposes intestacy rights for qualifying cohabitants and removing the two-year requirement for 1975 Act claims where the couple had a child together. These are proposals only.

## Recent changes and points to check
- No cohabitation Act has been passed. The consultation closed on 14 August 2026; no Government response or Bill had been published by November 2026.
- The 1975 Act's two-year cohabitation requirement is unchanged (the consultation proposes relaxing it where the couple had a child).
