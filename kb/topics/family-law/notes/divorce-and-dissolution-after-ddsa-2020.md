# Divorce and dissolution after the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020

## Overview
Since 6 April 2022 divorce, dissolution of civil partnership and judicial separation in England and Wales are "no-fault": the only requirement is a statement that the marriage (or civil partnership) has broken down irretrievably, which the court must treat as conclusive. The old five facts (adultery, behaviour, desertion, two years' consent, five years' separation) are gone, and an application can be made by one or both spouses. There is a minimum period of 20 weeks from the start of proceedings to the application for a conditional order, and a further six weeks from conditional order to final order, so at least 26 weeks in all.

## The law
**Ground and application (Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 s 1, as substituted by the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020).**
- s 1(1): either or both parties may apply for a "divorce order" on the ground that the marriage has broken down irretrievably.
- s 1(2): the application must be accompanied by a statement by the applicant(s) that the marriage has broken down irretrievably.
- s 1(3): the court must take the statement to be conclusive evidence that the marriage has broken down irretrievably, and make a divorce order.
- s 1(4): a divorce order is in the first instance a "conditional order" (formerly decree nisi), which may not be made final (a "final order", formerly decree absolute) before the end of six weeks from its making.
- s 1(5): the court may not make a conditional order unless the applicant (sole application) or both applicants (joint application) confirm they wish the application to continue; confirmation may not be given before the end of 20 weeks from the start of proceedings.
- s 1(6)-(9): the Lord Chancellor may by statutory instrument (affirmative procedure) change the periods, provided the two periods together do not exceed 26 weeks (s 1(7)); the court may by order shorten either period in a particular case (s 1(8)).
- s 3: no application for a divorce order within one year of the marriage. (Civil partnerships: Civil Partnership Act 2004 s 44, as amended, makes the same no-fault provision for dissolution orders, and s 41 bars an application within one year of the formation of the civil partnership.)

**Sole and joint applications.** A sole applicant serves the application on the respondent (the court usually serves by post or email, or the applicant can arrange personal service). The respondent files an acknowledgement of service. A joint application is made by both on one form, each confirming the statement. A joint application can be converted to a sole application if one party withdraws (s 1(10), and rules). The respondent cannot defend on the basis that the marriage has not broken down; there is no "defended divorce" on the merits. The court's role is limited to checking jurisdiction, validity of the marriage and service.

**Challenges that remain.** A respondent can dispute the court's jurisdiction, the validity or subsistence of the marriage, or the service of the application, or argue the applicant's circumstances (for example, that the marriage was void). Divorce can be challenged if the application was made by fraud or mistake. *Owens v Owens* [2018] UKSC 41 (a petition refused for failing to establish the "behaviour" fact) prompted the reform and is no longer relevant to the new ground, since the old facts have been abolished.

**Procedure.** Online service (MyHMCTS "apply for a divorce") is the standard route. A court fee is payable (set by the Family Proceedings Fees Order and revised periodically; help with fees is available). The sequence is: apply; service; acknowledgement; wait 20 weeks; apply for conditional order; court pronounces a conditional order; six weeks; apply for final order (applicant, or respondent after a further three months if the applicant does not apply). The final order dissolves the marriage and ends the status of spouse. If a spouse dies between conditional and final order, the marriage still subsists.

**Financial remedies timing.** Financial orders under MCA 1973 ss 23-24B can be made on or after the conditional order, before or after the final order (s 23(1)), but spousal periodical payments, secured periodical payments and lump sums made on or after the conditional order (s 23(5)), property adjustment orders (s 24(3)) and pension sharing orders (s 24B(2)) do not take effect until the divorce order is made final. Interim maintenance (maintenance pending suit) is available before then. Under s 9(2), where a sole applicant has not applied for the final order within three months of the earliest date they could have done, the respondent may apply. Under s 10(2)-(4), where the conditional order was made on a sole application (or one joint applicant has withdrawn), the respondent may ask the court to consider their financial position after the divorce, and the court must not make the order final unless satisfied that no financial provision should be required or that the provision made is reasonable and fair or the best that can be made (unless there is a reason for urgency and a satisfactory undertaking is given). Section 10A allows the court to refuse to make the order final until religious divorce steps have been taken. The usual practice is to advise a spouse not to apply for the final order until financial remedy proceedings are concluded, as a spouse who is entitled to widow's or spouse's pension benefits, or a spouse who inherits on intestacy, loses those rights when the marriage is dissolved. Financial remedy proceedings are governed by the Family Procedure Rules 2010 Part 9 and PD 9A.

**Judicial separation and nullity.** The decree of judicial separation is now a "judicial separation order" (MCA 1973 s 17). Either or both parties may apply; the application must be accompanied by a statement that the applicant (or applicants) seek to be judicially separated, and the court must make the order (s 17(1)-(1B)). Irretrievable breakdown is not required, there is no one-year bar and there are no 20-week or six-week periods. Nullity (void and voidable marriages: MCA 1973 ss 11-12) is unaffected in substance, apart from renaming to a "nullity of marriage order", which is conditional in the first instance and then made final.

**Jurisdiction.** The English court can hear a divorce if the Domicile and Matrimonial Proceedings Act 1973 s 5(2) conditions are met (habitual residence of both, of one for a year, or of the applicant for six months in some cases, or domicile of either). After EU withdrawal the Brussels IIa residual jurisdiction rules were replaced by domestic rules in the Jurisdiction and Judgments (Family) (Amendment etc.) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019. A "forum" analysis (stay of proceedings) is for cases where a foreign court is also seised.

## Key authorities
- *Owens v Owens* [2018] UKSC 41, [2018] AC 899 (Supreme Court): under the old law a wife petitioned on the ground of unreasonable behaviour (MCA 1973 s 1(2)(b)); the courts below found that the behaviour alleged was not enough to show that she could not reasonably be expected to live with the husband, and the Supreme Court dismissed her appeal, holding that the statutory test was correctly applied and that any change to the law was for Parliament. The case directly prompted the legislative reform; it is now of historical interest.
- *Sharland v Sharland* [2015] UKSC 60: non-disclosure and setting aside financial orders (see the financial remedies note).
- *Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd* [2013] UKSC 34: court's powers over assets held through companies (see the financial remedies note).

## Applying it
For a question on divorce: (1) Is the marriage valid and has a year passed (s 3)? (2) Does the court have jurisdiction (DMPA 1973 s 5(2) criteria)? (3) Applicant (sole or joint), statement of irretrievable breakdown. (4) Timeline: 20 weeks to confirm, conditional order, six weeks to apply for final order; the earliest total is therefore 26 weeks. (5) Defences: none on the merits; only jurisdiction, validity and service. (6) Do not finalise before finances are resolved, and explain why (loss of pension survivor rights, inheritance, tax). Common errors: referring to "decree nisi" or "five facts" as current law; saying one can divorce within the first year; saying the other party can contest the breakdown. Everyday answer: "You can start a divorce after one year of marriage by saying it has broken down irretrievably; your spouse cannot stop it, and the quickest it can complete is about six months."

## Debates and reform
- The Law Commission and Family Justice campaigners had long argued the fault-based facts forced couples to blame each other (Resolution's campaign for no-fault divorce). The Act's critics argue it makes divorce too easy and removes a period for reflection; the 20-week reflection period was added to meet this.
- The "overarching 26 weeks" is a statutory maximum for any changes by order (s 1(7)), so the period can be changed only within it.
- Financial remedies reform, which is not covered by the 2020 Act, is now the main reform conversation; see the financial remedies note.
- Domestic abuse: there is no automatic fast-track for abuse victims, although the court may shorten the periods in a particular case (s 1(8)).
- Comparison: Scotland retains irretrievable breakdown established by adultery, behaviour or separation (one year with consent, two years without), so England and Wales now has the more liberal regime.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 6 April 2022: DDSA 2020 came into force (sole/joint applications, conditional and final orders, no contesting).
- Court fees and online process details change; quote the fee only from the current HMCTS fee list.
- The Ministry of Justice consultation "A fairer end to relationships" (5 June to 14 August 2026) proposed reform of financial remedies, not of the divorce process; no Bill had been published by November 2026.
