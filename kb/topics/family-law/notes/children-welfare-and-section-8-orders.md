# Children - welfare principle, section 8 orders, parental responsibility and the presumption of parental involvement

## Overview
When a court determines any question about a child's upbringing, the child's welfare is its paramount consideration (Children Act 1989 s 1(1)). The court uses the s 1(3) welfare checklist for contested s 8 orders, applies the no-delay principle and the no-order principle, and (until it is repealed) presumes that the involvement of each parent in the child's life will further the child's welfare, unless the contrary is shown (s 1(2A)). The Government announced on 22 October 2025 that it would repeal that presumption; the repeal is in the Courts and Tribunals Bill, which was at Lords report stage (set for 1 December 2026) and had not received Royal Assent in November 2026, so the presumption remains law for now.

## The law
**Welfare principle (s 1).**
- s 1(1): when a court determines any question with respect to the upbringing of a child, or the administration of a child's property, the child's welfare shall be the court's paramount consideration.
- s 1(2): any delay in determining the question is likely to prejudice the child's welfare.
- s 1(2A): in proceedings for s 8 orders where the question is whether a parent is to be involved in the child's life, the court is to presume, unless the contrary is shown, that involvement of that parent in the child's life will further the child's welfare (inserted by the Children and Families Act 2014 s 11); s 1(2B): "involvement" means involvement of some kind, either direct or indirect, but not any particular division of a child's time. The presumption applies to a parent only if that parent can be involved in a way that does not put the child at risk of suffering harm, unless there is some evidence before the court to suggest that involvement of that parent in any form would put the child at risk of suffering harm (s 1(6)).
- s 1(3): checklist (applies, under s 1(4), where the court is considering whether to make, vary or discharge a s 8 order that is opposed, a special guardianship order, or an order under Part IV (care and supervision)): (a) ascertainable wishes and feelings of the child (considered in light of age and understanding); (b) physical, emotional and educational needs; (c) likely effect of any change in circumstances; (d) age, sex, background and any relevant characteristics; (e) any harm suffered or at risk of suffering; (f) how capable each parent and any other relevant person is of meeting the child's needs; (g) the range of powers available to the court.
- s 1(5): the no-order principle: the court shall not make an order unless it considers that doing so would be better for the child than making no order.

**Paramountcy.** *J v C* [1970] AC 668 (HL): welfare is the first and paramount consideration, so that the claims of natural parents are weighed but do not displace it. *Re B (A Child)* [2009] UKSC 5, [2009] 1 WLR 2496 (Supreme Court): in a residence dispute between a father and the maternal grandmother who had cared for the child, the Court restored the magistrates' decision in the grandmother's favour; the child's welfare is paramount, and there is no presumption or "right" in favour of a biological parent, although the importance of the parental relationship is a factor in the welfare assessment.

**Section 8 orders.** s 8(1): a child arrangements order (who the child lives with, spends time or otherwise has contact with; replacing residence and contact orders since April 2014), a prohibited steps order (prevents a step being taken without court consent), a specific issue order (determines a specific question, such as a school or medical treatment). Who may apply (s 10): parents, guardians, special guardians, persons with a child arrangements order in their favour, and others with leave of the court (a child's carers, step-parents with certain periods of living with the child, and so on). Where a child arrangements order names a father (or a female second parent) as a person with whom the child is to live, the court must also make a parental responsibility order if they do not already have PR (s 12(1)); a non-parent named as a person with whom the child lives gets PR while the order lasts (s 12(2)). The court can make an order of its own motion. s 9(1): no s 8 order (other than a child arrangements order about residence) can be made in respect of a child in local authority care. s 91(14): the court may bar further applications without leave. Relocation: the court applies the welfare principle with the checklist (*Re F (A Child) (International Relocation Cases)* [2015] EWCA Civ 882 and *Re C (Internal Relocation)* [2015] EWCA Civ 1305 for internal moves; *Payne v Payne* [2001] EWCA Civ 166 is now regarded only as guidance and not a presumption in favour of the primary carer).

**Parental responsibility (PR).** s 3: all the rights, duties, powers, responsibilities and authority which by law a parent has in relation to the child and the child's property. s 2: a mother has PR from birth; a father has it if married to the mother at the time of the birth, or, for births registered on or after 1 December 2003, if he is registered as the father on the birth certificate (s 4(1)(a), as amended); otherwise he can acquire it by agreement with the mother, by court order (both under s 4(1), as amended), by a child arrangements order naming him as the person the child lives with (s 12(1), which requires a PR order to be made), or by becoming a guardian; step-parents can acquire PR by agreement or order (s 4A); same-sex female partners under the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008 are legal parents and may acquire PR. A parent with PR cannot surrender or transfer it (s 2(9)), though some or all of it may be delegated; it is lost in practice only on adoption or by order of the court in limited cases. Persons with PR may act alone unless the matter is one that requires consent of all (for example change of name, major medical issues, removal from the jurisdiction: *Re C (Welfare of Child: Immunisation)* [2003] EWCA Civ 1148 on immunisation and the court's role). Children's own views: *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1986] AC 112 (HL): a child under 16 who has sufficient understanding can consent to treatment; the court should give increasing weight to a child's wishes.

**Domestic abuse and contact.** The Court of Appeal in *Re H-N and Others (Children) (Domestic Abuse: Finding of Fact Hearings)* [2021] EWCA Civ 448 reviewed Practice Direction 12J (child arrangements and contact orders: domestic abuse and harm) and the Harm Panel's report to the Ministry of Justice (June 2020), and emphasised that the court must identify abuse early, decide whether a fact-finding hearing is needed and consider the impact on the child; the statutory definition of domestic abuse in the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 informs the court's approach; the presumption does not apply where there is a risk of harm. The Harm Panel recommended an urgent review of the s 1(2A) presumption, which led to the 2025 review. The Domestic Abuse Act 2021 also prohibits cross-examination in person by an alleged abuser in family proceedings (inserted Part 4B of the Matrimonial and Family Proceedings Act 1984), and requires courts to consider special measures.

**Procedure.** A MIAM (mediation information and assessment meeting) is generally required before applying (Family Procedure Rules 2010 Part 3), with exceptions for domestic abuse and urgency; Cafcass prepares a safeguarding letter and may prepare reports (s 7 report). Children may be separately represented under FPR Part 16 in complex cases. The Private Law Pathfinder model (piloted in Dorset and North Wales from 2022 and since extended to further court areas) uses a more investigative, child-focused approach with early information gathering.

## Key authorities
- *J v C* [1970] AC 668 (HL): paramountcy of welfare.
- *Re B (A Child)* [2009] UKSC 5: biological parent no presumptive priority; welfare is paramount.
- *Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech AHA* [1986] AC 112 (HL): child's capacity and consent.
- *Re H-N and Others* [2021] EWCA Civ 448 (CA): domestic abuse and child arrangements; PD 12J.
- *Payne v Payne* [2001] EWCA Civ 166 and *Re F (International Relocation Cases)* [2015] EWCA Civ 882: relocation: welfare is paramount; no presumption.
- *Re C (Welfare of Child: Immunisation)* [2003] EWCA Civ 1148: immunisation; best interests.

## Applying it
For a problem question on contact or residence: (1) Who has PR (the unmarried father's position)? (2) What orders are sought and who is able to apply (s 10)? (3) Apply s 1(1) paramountcy; delay and no-order principles; (4) checklist (a)-(g) item by item to the facts: the child's age, wishes, needs, harm (e.g. domestic abuse), capability of each parent; (5) the presumption in s 1(2A): it applies unless the contrary is shown, and not where there is risk of harm, and involvement can be direct or indirect (so it is not an assumption of equal time); (6) remedy: child arrangements order (lives with, spends time); prohibited steps order (for example to stop removal from the jurisdiction); specific issue (school). (7) Also consider mediation, CAFCASS, and enforcement. Common traps: treating the welfare checklist as applying to all questions (it applies to contested s 8 and Part IV orders, not every case); saying the mother has the right to decide; forgetting PD 12J where abuse is alleged. Everyday: both parents can ask for arrangements; courts apply the child's welfare, not parents' rights, and ordinarily prefer a child to keep a relationship with both parents unless that would be harmful.

## Debates and reform
- *Presumption of parental involvement.* The Harm Panel (2020) said the presumption might cause courts to prioritise contact over safety; the Ministry of Justice published the final report of its review in October 2025 and announced on 22 October 2025 that the presumption would be repealed; the review found that it is rarely relied upon in judgments, and that it risks diluting individualised welfare assessment. The repeal clause ("Welfare of the child: repeal of presumption of parental involvement", clause 17 at Commons committee stage in April 2026) is in the Courts and Tribunals Bill, introduced on 25 February 2026, which passed the Commons on 13 October 2026 and was at Lords report stage (1 December 2026) at the end of November 2026. Supporters of the presumption, including fathers' groups, say it protects children's relationships with safe parents; others say the presumption is unnecessary because welfare is paramount in any event. Until Royal Assent and commencement, s 1(2A)-(2B) remain in force.
- *Children's voice and participation*: whether children should be heard more directly (Article 12 UNCRC), and the use of Cafcass and guardians.
- *Delay and the family court system*: the Private Law Pathfinder; the Public law outline for care cases is separate.
- Whether PR should be extended automatically to unmarried fathers or be tied to registration and conduct.
