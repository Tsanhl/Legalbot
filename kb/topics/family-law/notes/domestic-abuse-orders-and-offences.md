# Domestic abuse - Domestic Abuse Act 2021, FLA 1996 Part IV orders and coercive control

## Overview
Civil protection against abusive partners and family members comes mainly from Part IV of the Family Law Act 1996 (FLA 1996): non-molestation orders (s 42) protect the applicant or a child from molestation, and occupation orders (ss 33-41) regulate who may live in the family home. The Domestic Abuse Act 2021 (DAA 2021) gave domestic abuse a single statutory definition and created new orders and offences, and controlling or coercive behaviour is a criminal offence under the Serious Crime Act 2015 s 76. Domestic Abuse Protection Notices and Orders (DAPNs and DAPOs) under the DAA 2021, piloted in selected areas since November 2024, are being rolled out across England and Wales from 25 November 2026; non-molestation and occupation orders remain available alongside them.

## The law
**Statutory definition (DAA 2021 s 1).** Behaviour of a person (A) towards another (B) is "domestic abuse" if A and B are each aged 16 or over and are "personally connected", and the behaviour is abusive (s 1(1)). Behaviour is abusive if it consists of any of: physical or sexual abuse; violent or threatening behaviour; controlling or coercive behaviour; economic abuse (adverse effect on ability to acquire, use or maintain money, property or goods or obtain services); psychological, emotional or other abuse; it may be a single incident or a course of conduct (s 1(3)). "Personally connected" (s 2) includes spouses and civil partners, ex-partners, those who have agreed to marry, those in an intimate personal relationship (current or former), relatives, and those who have, or have had, a parental relationship in relation to the same child. A child who sees, hears or experiences the effects of abuse and is related to A or B is also treated as a victim (s 3). FLA 1996 Part IV uses its own concepts ("associated persons", "molestation") rather than this definition.

**Non-molestation orders (FLA 1996 s 42).** The court may make an order prohibiting a respondent from molesting an associated person, and/or a relevant child (s 42(1)). It may do so on application by an associated person, or of its own motion in any family proceedings if it considers the order should be made for the benefit of a party or a child (s 42(2)). In deciding whether to make the order and in what terms, the court must have regard to all the circumstances including the need to secure the health, safety and well-being of the applicant (or person for whose benefit it is made) and any relevant child (s 42(5)). "Associated persons" (s 62(3)): spouses and civil partners (current and former), cohabitants and former cohabitants, people who live or have lived in the same household (other than as employees, tenants, lodgers or boarders), relatives, people who have agreed to marry or enter a civil partnership (s 44 requires evidence of the agreement, and an application must be made within three years of its termination: s 42(4)), people who have or have had an intimate personal relationship with each other of significant duration (s 62(3)(ea)), parents of a child or persons with parental responsibility for the same child, and parties to the same family proceedings (other than Part 4 proceedings themselves). "Molestation" has no statutory definition: it covers violence, intimidation, harassment, pestering and conduct which interferes with the applicant's peace and privacy, causing serious interference with their well-being (*Vaughan v Vaughan* [1973] 1 WLR 1159 (CA), a pre-1996 authority). Orders can be made without notice where it is just and convenient (s 45(1)); the court considers all the circumstances, including any risk of significant harm to the applicant or a relevant child if the order is not made immediately, whether the applicant is likely to be deterred or prevented from pursuing the application if it is not, and whether the respondent is deliberately evading service to the serious prejudice of the applicant or child (s 45(2)). The respondent must be given a full hearing as soon as just and convenient (s 45(3)). Breach is a criminal offence (s 42A), triable either way; maximum five years' imprisonment on indictment, or the general limit in a magistrates' court on summary conviction (s 42A(5)). A person convicted of the offence for particular conduct cannot also be punished for contempt for that conduct, and vice versa (s 42A(3)-(4)); the police arrest for the offence, since a power of arrest is no longer attached to the order. Orders have a fixed duration or until further order. The court may accept an undertaking instead (s 46), but breach of an undertaking is contempt, not a s 42A offence, and the court must not accept one where the respondent has used or threatened violence and a non-molestation order is needed so that any breach is punishable under s 42A (s 46(3A)).

**Occupation orders (FLA 1996 ss 33-41).** These govern the right to occupy the family home and exclude a respondent. They differ by category of applicant:
- s 33: entitled applicants, who have an estate or interest or home rights (spouses and civil partners with matrimonial home rights under Part IV, s 30). Court may declare the entitlement, enforce rights, require the respondent to allow the applicant to enter and remain, regulate occupation, and exclude the respondent. s 33(6): the court must have regard to all the circumstances, including: (a) housing needs and housing resources of each party and any relevant child; (b) the financial resources of each; (c) likely effect of any order, or of any decision not to make an order, on the health, safety or well-being of the parties and any relevant child; (d) the conduct of the parties in relation to each other and otherwise. s 33(7): the "balance of harm" test: where the court finds that the applicant or a relevant child is likely to suffer significant harm attributable to the respondent's conduct if an order is not made, the court must make the order unless the respondent or child is likely to suffer significant harm if the order is made and that harm is as great or greater than the harm to the applicant or child.
- s 35: one former spouse (or civil partner) with no existing right to occupy; s 36: one cohabitant or former cohabitant with no existing right; ss 37 and 38: neither spouse (or cohabitants) entitled. For the non-entitled former spouse (s 35) and cohabitant (s 36) the court has regard to a similar list of factors (s 35 adds the time since the parties lived together and since the marriage ended; s 36 adds the nature of the relationship, in particular the level of commitment involved, the length of cohabitation and whether there are children). For s 35 the balance of harm test is mandatory, as under s 33(7) (s 35(8)); for s 36 the balance of harm questions are only factors the court must consider in deciding whether to include exclusion provisions (s 36(7)-(8)), so the court has a discretion even where significant harm is shown. Duration: a s 35 order is limited to a specified period of up to six months, extendable on one or more occasions by up to six months each (s 35(10)); a s 36 order is limited to six months and may be extended only once (s 36(10)); a s 37 order (neither former spouse entitled) may be extended on one or more occasions (s 37(5)); a s 38 order (neither cohabitant entitled) may be extended only once (s 38(6)).
- *Chalmers v Johns* [1999] 1 FLR 392 (CA): the court must first ask whether the s 33(7) balance of harm test is met; if it is not, it has a discretion under s 33(6). An order overriding a respondent's property rights is "draconian" and justified only in exceptional cases; on the facts an occupation order was set aside.
- Applications under Part IV (non-molestation and occupation orders) are made to the family court under Part 10 of the Family Procedure Rules 2010.

**Controlling or coercive behaviour (Serious Crime Act 2015 s 76).** An offence is committed if A repeatedly or continuously engages in behaviour towards B that is controlling or coercive; at the time A and B are personally connected (as amended by DAA 2021 s 68 with effect from 5 April 2023, removing the former requirement that family members and ex-partners live together, so that ex-partners and relatives are covered whether or not they live together); the behaviour has a serious effect on B; and A knows or ought to know it will have a serious effect on B (s 76(1)). "Serious effect" means causing B to fear on at least two occasions that violence will be used against B, or causing B serious alarm or distress which has a substantial adverse effect on B's day-to-day activities (s 76(4)). A person "ought to know" if a reasonable person in possession of the same information would think so (s 76(5)). Defence (s 76(8)-(10)): A believed they were acting in B's best interests and the behaviour was, in all the circumstances, reasonable; the defence is not available where the behaviour causes B to fear violence. Maximum penalty: five years' imprisonment on indictment (or a fine); twelve months on summary conviction. Home Office statutory guidance describes typical patterns of behaviour. The DAA 2021 also created the non-fatal strangulation and suffocation offence (DAA 2021 s 70, inserting s 75A into the Serious Crime Act 2015; in force 7 June 2022; maximum five years' imprisonment on indictment).

**Domestic Abuse Protection Orders (DAPOs).** Under DAA 2021 Part 3, police issue DAPNs and courts make DAPOs, which can impose prohibitions and positive requirements (including electronic monitoring); DAPOs may be made in criminal, civil and family proceedings, and breach is a criminal offence. Pilots began on 27 November 2024 (Greater Manchester, three London boroughs and the British Transport Police, later Cleveland and North Wales); the pilot period (SI 2025/319) ends on 24 November 2026 and national rollout across England and Wales was announced for 25 November 2026, with DAPOs replacing DVPNs and DVPOs. The Crime and Policing Act 2026 s 142, in force 25 November 2026 (SI 2026/1158), amends the DAA 2021 so that a DAPO may require an assessment for, and then participation in, a programme of activities (DAA s 35(5A)-(5B)), and lets magistrates' courts and the Crown Court vary their own DAPOs of their own motion; the programme provision (s 142(2)) is not yet in force for civil or family proceedings. NMOs and occupation orders are unaffected.

**Other protections.** The DAA 2021 bars cross-examination in person between alleged abusers and victims in family proceedings (MFPA 1984 Part 4B), provides special measures, created the Domestic Abuse Commissioner and imposes a safe accommodation duty on local authorities (Part 4); see also Clare's law and the Protection from Harassment Act 1997.

## Key authorities
- *Vaughan v Vaughan* [1973] 1 WLR 1159 (CA): "molestation" covers conduct that is a serious interference with the applicant's well-being; need not be violence.
- *Chalmers v Johns* [1999] 1 FLR 392 (CA): balance of harm first, then s 33(6) discretion; occupation orders are draconian.
- *Re H-N and Others* [2021] EWCA Civ 448 (CA): domestic abuse in child arrangements, PD 12J and coercive control; the statutory definition informs family court practice.

## Applying it
Problem question: (1) Are the parties "associated" (s 62) or "personally connected" (DAA s 2)? (2) Does the conduct amount to "molestation" or fall within the DAA categories? (3) Remedy: non-molestation order (s 42), with or without notice (s 45); occupation order if the home is in issue: identify the category (ss 33, 35-38), then apply the factors and the balance of harm test in the form that section uses; criminal route: s 42A breach, SCA 2015 s 76, Protection from Harassment Act 1997. (4) Immediate safety: police, DAPN or DAPO, emergency accommodation. Traps: thinking a non-molestation order needs violence; applying s 33(7) to a s 36 applicant; saying DAPOs have replaced non-molestation orders; forgetting the "serious effect" element of s 76. Everyday: "You can ask the family court for a non-molestation order, with or without notice, if the other person is a partner, ex, relative or someone you live with; breaching it is a crime."

## Debates and reform
- Whether civil orders offer enough protection: breach rates, enforcement and cost (legal aid for private family cases requires prescribed evidence of abuse under LASPO 2012 Sch 1 para 12; the evidence rules were widened in 2018).
- Overlap of orders (NMO, DVPO, DAPO), and whether DAPOs will replace NMOs in practice in the family court.
- Coercive control: under-prosecution; the role of the definition in family courts.
- Economic abuse and child maintenance (see the child maintenance note).
- Domestic abuse and the presumption of parental involvement: see the children note; the Harm Panel (2020).
