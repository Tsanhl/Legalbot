# Tenancy deposits and the private rented sector after the Renters' Rights Act 2025

## Overview
Landlords and agents in England who take a deposit on an assured tenancy must protect it in an approved scheme within 30 days and give the tenant prescribed information (Housing Act 2004 ss 212 to 215). Failure exposes the landlord to a penalty of one to three times the deposit and blocks most possession claims. Under the Renters' Rights Act 2025, the main reforms started on 1 May 2026: section 21 "no-fault" evictions ended, assured shorthold tenancies (including fixed terms) became assured periodic tenancies, and rent can be increased only once a year by a statutory notice.

## The law

### Tenancy deposit protection (Housing Act 2004, Part 6 Chapter 4)
- **Schemes (s 212):** a deposit paid in connection with an assured tenancy (before 1 May 2026, an assured shorthold tenancy) must be held in an authorised scheme: custodial (deposit held by the scheme) or insured (landlord holds the deposit and pays a premium). In England the approved schemes are the Deposit Protection Service, mydeposits and the Tenancy Deposit Scheme.
- **Requirements (s 213):** within 30 days of receiving the deposit, the landlord (or agent) must comply with the scheme's initial requirements and give the tenant (and anyone who paid the deposit on their behalf) the "prescribed information" (Housing (Tenancy Deposits) (Prescribed Information) Order 2007, SI 2007/797): details of the scheme, how to get the deposit back, the landlord's contact details, and how to dispute.
- **Deposit cap:** under the Tenant Fees Act 2019, the deposit is capped at five weeks' rent (six weeks where the annual rent is £50,000 or more). Holding deposits are capped at one week's rent.
- **Penalty (s 214):** if the deposit has not been protected or the prescribed information has not been given in time, the tenant may apply to the county court. The court must order the landlord either to repay the deposit to the tenant or to pay it into a custodial scheme (or, if the tenancy has ended, may order repayment), and must order the landlord to pay the tenant a sum of between one and three times the deposit (s 214(4)). Applications can be made during or after the tenancy. The court has a discretion on the multiple, guided by the seriousness of the breach; protecting the deposit late does not remove the penalty.
- **Section 215 and possession:** before 1 May 2026 an unprotected deposit (or missing prescribed information) prevented a valid section 21 notice. As amended by the 2025 Act, the restriction now bites on possession claims generally: the court will make a possession order only if the deposit is protected and the prescribed information given, or the deposit has been returned (in full or with agreed deductions), or a s 214 claim has been decided, settled or withdrawn. The restriction does not apply to the anti-social behaviour grounds. A landlord can therefore cure the problem by returning the deposit before seeking possession.
- **History:** *Superstrike Ltd v Rodrigues* [2013] EWCA Civ 669 held that a deposit taken before April 2007 had to be protected when a statutory periodic tenancy arose later; the Deregulation Act 2015 reversed this and clarified the rules for renewals.
- **Deposit disputes:** at the end of the tenancy the landlord can deduct only amounts the tenant agrees or the scheme adjudicator (or a court) awards, for example rent arrears, damage beyond fair wear and tear, or cleaning to the check-in standard. The schemes' dispute resolution is free and the disputed amount stays protected pending the outcome.

### Renters' Rights Act 2025: what changed and when
- Royal Assent: 27 October 2025. New council investigatory powers began on 27 December 2025. Phase 1 started on 1 May 2026. Phase 2 (the private rented sector database, rolling out from late 2026, and a landlord ombudsman, with mandatory membership expected in 2028) and Phase 3 (a Decent Homes Standard for private rented homes and Awaab's Law, with dates still subject to consultation) follow.
- **From 1 May 2026 (Phase 1):**
  - Section 21 of the Housing Act 1988 abolished. A landlord who served a section 21 notice before 1 May 2026 had to start court proceedings by 31 July 2026 (or earlier if the notice's own validity ran out first); claims issued in time continue. That window has now closed.
  - All existing and new assured shorthold tenancies became assured periodic tenancies with no fixed term. Existing tenants had to be given the government's Information Sheet by 31 May 2026. The tenant can end the tenancy by two months' written notice; the landlord needs a statutory ground under section 8 and the correct notice (usually four months, shorter for some grounds).
  - Grounds: new mandatory Grounds 1 (landlord or family moving in) and 1A (sale), each needing four months' notice, unavailable in the first 12 months of a tenancy, and followed by a 12-month ban on re-letting or marketing. Ground 8 (rent arrears) now needs at least three months' rent (13 weeks for weekly or fortnightly rent) owed both when notice is served and at the hearing, with four weeks' notice (previously two months' arrears and two weeks' notice). Anti-social behaviour grounds carry shorter notice and proceedings can start immediately. There is a student ground for HMO lets.
  - Rent increases: only once a year (and not in the first 12 months), only by a section 13 notice in the prescribed form (Form 4A) with at least two months' notice, proposing a market rent. The tenant can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), which cannot set a rent above the landlord's proposed figure. Rent review clauses no longer work.
  - Rent in advance: landlords cannot require or accept rent before the tenancy agreement is signed, and between signing and the start of the tenancy can require at most one month's rent (28 days where rent is paid more often). Rental bidding is banned: a written advert must state an asking rent and the landlord must not encourage or accept higher offers.
  - Discrimination: landlords and agents cannot refuse to let, or set less favourable terms, because a tenant has children or receives benefits.
  - Pets: a tenant can ask to keep a pet; the landlord must not unreasonably refuse and must give reasons for a refusal.
- **Enforcement:** local authorities can impose civil penalties of up to £7,000 for initial or minor breaches (for example rental discrimination, rental bidding, misuse of possession grounds), and up to £40,000 for serious, persistent or repeat breaches (or prosecute instead, with an unlimited fine). Breaches of the Tenant Fees Act rules on rent in advance carry penalties of up to £5,000. Rent repayment orders now cover more offences and can be for up to two years' rent.
- The deposit protection regime in the Housing Act 2004 continues to apply to the converted periodic tenancies.

### Other points on landlord obligations
- Gas safety certificate, EPC, the prescribed information and electrical safety checks remain duties; sanctions that used to work through s 21 no longer do, but local authority enforcement, civil penalties and rent repayment orders (Housing and Planning Act 2016, as extended) continue.

## Key authorities
- *Superstrike Ltd v Rodrigues* [2013] EWCA Civ 669 (CA): deposits and periodic tenancies (reversed by the Deregulation Act 2015).
- Statute is the main source: Housing Act 2004 ss 212 to 215; Housing Act 1988 ss 8 and 13 and Sch 2 (as amended); Tenant Fees Act 2019; Renters' Rights Act 2025.

## Applying it
1. Was a deposit taken? Was it protected within 30 days, with the prescribed information given?
2. If not, the tenant can claim 1 to 3 times the deposit under s 214 and repayment or protection; and the landlord cannot get a possession order (except on anti-social behaviour grounds) until the deposit is returned or the claim resolved.
3. At the end: deductions and disputes through the scheme.
4. Possession: since 1 May 2026, does the landlord have a statutory ground (section 8) and have they served the correct notice for that ground? Pre-May 2026 section 21 notices can no longer be used to start proceedings.
5. Rent increases: a valid section 13 notice, two months' notice, once a year, tribunal challenge.
6. Common traps: advising that s 21 can still be used; forgetting the penalty claim stands even after the deposit is returned late; assuming rent can be increased by a clause in the tenancy; assuming a fixed term still binds the tenant; using a selling or moving-in ground in the first year.
7. Everyday answer: "My landlord did not protect my deposit." You can apply to the county court for 1 to 3 times the deposit and for the deposit to be returned or protected; the landlord also cannot evict you on most grounds until it is dealt with.

## Debates and reform
- The Act was debated over the balance between security for tenants and landlords' ability to recover possession; concerns include court delays and landlords leaving the sector.
- Rent controls: the Act does not cap rents but limits increases to once a year at market rent.
- Deposit-replacement products and the cost of deposits for tenants remain live policy topics.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 1 May 2026: Phase 1 in force (section 21 abolished; periodic tenancies; new grounds; rent increase, rent-in-advance and bidding rules).
- 31 July 2026: last date for proceedings on pre-May section 21 notices (passed).
- Late 2026 onwards: PRS database rollout; ombudsman membership expected 2028; Decent Homes Standard and Awaab's Law for private renting still subject to consultation on timing.
- These rules are for England; Wales has its own regime (Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016).
