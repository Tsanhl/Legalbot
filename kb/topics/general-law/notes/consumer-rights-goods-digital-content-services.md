# Consumer rights for goods, digital content and services, and section 75 Consumer Credit Act

## Overview
The Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA) gives consumers statutory rights against traders when buying goods, digital content or services. A consumer who receives faulty goods can reject them within 30 days, then ask for repair or replacement, and finally a price reduction or refund; faults appearing in the first six months are presumed to have been present at delivery. For purchases of £100 to £30,000 on credit, section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes the card issuer jointly liable with the retailer.

## The law

### Who is protected
A consumer is an individual acting for purposes wholly or mainly outside their trade, business, craft or profession (CRA s 2(3)); a trader acts for purposes relating to their trade etc (s 2(2)). Rights cannot be excluded or restricted by contract term or notice (ss 31, 57; and see Part 2 on unfair terms). Business-to-business sales are under the Sale of Goods Act 1979 instead.

### Goods (Chapter 2, ss 9 to 32)
- **Implied terms:** goods must be of satisfactory quality (s 9: the standard a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, taking into account description, price and other relevant circumstances, including appearance and finish, freedom from minor defects, safety and durability); fit for any particular purpose made known (s 10); as described (s 11); match any pre-contract information that is taken into account (s 12); match a sample (s 13) or model (s 14); installation, where it is part of the contract, must be proper (s 15); and the trader must have the right to supply the goods (s 17).
- **Remedies** (s 19, with ss 20 to 24): the consumer's rights are the short-term right to reject (s 20; time limit of 30 days from ownership, delivery or completion of installation, whichever is later: s 22), repair or replacement (s 23), and the final right to reject or a price reduction (s 24).
  - Short-term right to reject: within 30 days, the consumer can reject and get a full refund (refund within 14 days of the trader agreeing: s 20). The consumer can choose repair or replacement instead.
  - Repair or replacement (s 23): the consumer chooses, and the trader must act within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience, at the trader's cost; unless one is disproportionate compared with the other.
  - Price reduction or final right to reject (s 24): available if repair or replacement is impossible or disproportionate, or the trader has failed to do it in a reasonable time or without significant inconvenience, or after one repair or replacement attempt the goods still do not conform. A deduction for use may be made against a refund where the final right to reject is exercised more than six months after delivery; within six months no deduction is allowed, except for motor vehicles, where a deduction for use can be made at any time (s 24(8)-(10)).
- **Six-month presumption** (s 19(14) and (15)): if goods are found not to conform within six months of delivery, they are taken not to have conformed on delivery, unless the trader proves otherwise or the presumption is incompatible with the nature of the goods or the lack of conformity. After six months the burden is on the consumer to prove that the fault was there at delivery (for faults such as defects of manufacture).
- **Delivery and risk** (ss 28, 29): unless otherwise agreed, goods must be delivered within 30 days of the contract; the goods remain at the trader's risk until they come into the consumer's physical possession.
- **Other remedies:** damages, including consequential loss (s 19(10) preserves rights to claim damages); limitation period of six years (Limitation Act 1980 s 5; in Scotland five years).

### Digital content (Chapter 3, ss 33 to 47)
- Digital content (data in digital form, such as software, games, apps, e-books, streamed media) must be of satisfactory quality (s 34), fit for a particular purpose (s 35), as described (s 36) and conform to pre-contract information (s 37).
- Remedies (s 42): repair or replacement (s 43); then a price reduction, which may be up to the full price (s 44), if repair or replacement is impossible or fails; a refund (s 45) where the trader had no right to supply the content (s 41); and a remedy for damage to the consumer's device or other digital content caused by the trader's failure to use reasonable care and skill (s 46). There is no 30-day short-term right to reject for digital content.
- Liability cannot be excluded (s 47).

### Services (Chapter 4, ss 48 to 57)
- Service to be performed with reasonable care and skill (s 49); information given by or on behalf of the trader that the consumer takes into account is binding (s 50); where price is not fixed, a reasonable price is payable (s 51); where no time is fixed, performance must be within a reasonable time (s 52).
- Remedies (s 54): the consumer has the right to require repeat performance (s 55) (within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience, at no cost), and a price reduction (s 56) where repeat performance is impossible or not done in a reasonable time; plus damages.
- Liability cannot be excluded or restricted (s 57).

### Unfair terms
CRA Part 2 requires terms and notices to be fair and transparent; unfair terms are not binding on the consumer (s 62); a "grey list" in Sch 2 gives examples. The Competition and Markets Authority and the courts can enforce these rules.

### Unfair commercial practices and other statutory rules
From 6 April 2025, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCCA) Part 4 Chapter 1 prohibits unfair commercial practices (replacing the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008) and gives consumers redress rights. The subscription-contract regime in the DMCCA (Part 4 Chapter 2: clear pre-contract information, reminder notices, easy cancellation and cooling-off rights) is not yet in force; after several delays the government said it would apply from January 2027, but the commencement regulations had not been made by autumn 2026.

### Section 75 Consumer Credit Act 1974
- s 75(1): if the debtor under a debtor-creditor-supplier agreement (such as a credit card purchase) has a claim against the supplier in respect of misrepresentation or breach of contract, the debtor has a like claim against the creditor, who with the supplier is jointly and severally liable.
- Financial limits: the single item must have a cash price of more than £100 and not more than £30,000 (s 75(3)(b)). The claim need not be limited to the item's price: the card issuer's liability can reach the full loss, including consequential loss, and the amount of the claim is not limited to the amount charged to the card.
- Scope: covers purchases abroad (*Office of Fair Trading v Lloyds TSB Bank plc* [2007] UKHL 48 (HL)): the House held that s 75 applies to overseas purchases using UK cards. It applies where there are three parties: debtor, creditor, supplier, with the arrangements between creditor and supplier. It does not apply to debit cards (there is the separate "chargeback" scheme, which is voluntary under card scheme rules).
- Where only part of the price is paid on the card (for example a deposit), the issuer is still liable for the whole claim provided the single item's cash price falls within the range.
- For linked credit agreements (for example point-of-sale loans) over £30,000 and up to £60,260, s 75A gives a separate, secondary right against the lender, usable only after reasonable steps against the supplier.
- The consumer should first notify the supplier and then the card issuer, who may deal with it through the Financial Ombudsman Service if it refuses.

## Key authorities
- *Office of Fair Trading v Lloyds TSB Bank plc* [2007] UKHL 48 (HL): s 75 applies to purchases made abroad.
- Case law on satisfactory quality under the Sale of Goods Act 1979 is still relevant by analogy: *Bernstein v Pamson Motors (Golden Square) Ltd* [1987] 2 All ER 220 (a car engine failure after 140 miles: not of satisfactory/merchantable quality; the buyer lost the right to reject after accepting; damages) and *Clegg v Andersson* [2003] EWCA Civ 320 (rejection of a yacht where the keel was heavier than specified; the buyer had not lost the right to reject).
- *Rogers v Parish (Scarborough) Ltd* [1987] QB 933 (CA): satisfactory quality takes account of new car's appearance, finish and durability.

## Applying it
1. Is the buyer a consumer and the seller a trader? What was bought (goods, digital content, service)?
2. Which statutory term was breached: quality, purpose, description, information, sample, installation, reasonable care and skill?
3. When did the fault appear? Within 30 days (short-term right to reject), within six months (presumption), or later (burden on the consumer)?
4. Choose the remedy in order: reject; repair or replacement; price reduction or final right to reject; plus damages.
5. If paid by credit card (£100 to £30,000), consider a s 75 claim against the card issuer in addition to the retailer; for debit cards, chargeback.
6. Common traps: telling the consumer they must accept a repair first within 30 days (they may reject); ignoring the 30-day period; saying "no refunds" notices are binding; treating the trader's own warranty as limiting statutory rights; forgetting that s 75 does not apply to debit cards or to items of £100 or less.
7. Everyday answer: "My new laptop broke after two months." The seller must repair or replace it, and if that fails or is disproportionate, you can ask for a price reduction or final rejection and refund; the six-month presumption helps you.

## Debates and reform
- The six-month presumption and the 30-day period are shorter than the periods in some European regimes; there is recurring pressure to lengthen them.
- Digital content remedies are weaker than for goods; critics call for a short-term right to reject.
- Section 75 and the chargeback gap for debit cards, and ending the £100 minimum, have been the subject of lobbying.
- The DMCCA 2024 shifts consumer enforcement to direct fining by the CMA (up to 10% of global turnover).

## Recent changes and points to check
- 6 April 2025: DMCCA Part 4 Chapter 1 (unfair commercial practices; CMA direct enforcement) in force.
- Subscription contracts regime: expected January 2027; not in force at November 2026.
- s 75 limits (£100 and £30,000) are unchanged.
