# Making a contract in everyday life: offer, acceptance, consideration, intention, written terms and cancellation rights

## Overview
A contract in English law needs agreement (offer and acceptance), consideration, and an intention to create legal relations. Most everyday contracts can be made orally, in writing, online or by conduct, with no formalities, except contracts for the sale of land and some regulated agreements. Consumers who buy online, by phone or off-premises usually have a 14-day right to cancel under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013.

## The law

### Offer and acceptance
- **Offer:** a definite statement of willingness to be bound on specified terms if accepted. It can be made to the world (*Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 (CA): an advertisement promising £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using the smoke ball as directed was a unilateral offer, accepted by performance without notifying the advertiser; the deposit of £1,000 at the bank showed seriousness).
- **Invitation to treat:** displaying goods in a shop (*Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (Southern) Ltd* [1953] 1 QB 401 (CA): the offer is made by the customer at the till, accepted by the cashier); price-tagged window displays (*Fisher v Bell* [1961] 1 QB 394 (DC)); most advertisements (*Partridge v Crittenden* [1968] 1 WLR 1204 (DC)); and auctions. Online, a website listing is usually an invitation to treat and the customer's order is the offer; when the trader accepts depends on its terms (often on dispatch rather than on an automatic order acknowledgment).
- **Acceptance:** must be an unqualified agreement to the terms offered, communicated to the offeror. A counter-offer rejects the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench* (1840) 3 Beav 334). A request for information is not a counter-offer (*Stevenson, Jaques & Co v McLean* (1880) 5 QBD 346). Silence is not acceptance (*Felthouse v Bindley* (1862) 11 CBNS 869).
- **Postal rule:** acceptance by post is effective when posted (*Adams v Lindsell* (1818) 1 B & Ald 681). For instantaneous communications (telephone, telex), acceptance takes effect when and where it is received (*Entores Ltd v Miles Far East Corp* [1955] 2 QB 327 (CA); *Brinkibon Ltd v Stahag Stahl* [1983] 2 AC 34 (HL)). There is no appellate ruling on email, but first-instance dicta treat it as effective on receipt, not on sending (*Thomas v BPE Solicitors* [2010] EWHC 306 (Ch)). The offeror can waive or exclude the rule by specifying how acceptance must be made.
- **Certainty:** the terms must be sufficiently certain; an "agreement to agree" is not enforceable.
- **Battle of the forms** in business-to-business dealings is usually resolved by the last-shot rule (*Butler Machine Tool Co Ltd v Ex-Cell-O Corp* [1979] 1 WLR 401 (CA)); it rarely arises in consumer contracts.

### Consideration
- Each party must give something of value; it need not be adequate but must be sufficient (*Chappell & Co Ltd v Nestlé Co Ltd* [1960] AC 87 (HL): chocolate wrappers were part of the consideration; *Currie v Misa* (1875) LR 10 Ex 153: a benefit to one party or a detriment to the other).
- Past consideration is no consideration (*Re McArdle* [1951] Ch 669 (CA)).
- Performance of an existing duty: *Stilk v Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp 317 (no new consideration where a crew was promised extra pay for doing what they were already bound to do); *Williams v Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd* [1991] 1 QB 1 (CA): a promise of extra payment for the same performance can be enforced where the promisor obtains a practical benefit and there is no duress or fraud. Part payment of a debt is not consideration for discharge of the whole (*Foakes v Beer* (1884) 9 App Cas 605 (HL)), subject to the doctrine of promissory estoppel (*Central London Property Trust Ltd v High Trees House Ltd* [1947] KB 130, a shield and not a sword, suspending rights).
- A deed does not need consideration (it must be signed, witnessed and delivered: Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 s 1).

### Intention to create legal relations
- Social and domestic agreements are presumed not to be intended to create legal relations (*Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571 (CA)), but the presumption is rebuttable where the parties' arrangement is more commercial (*Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211 (CA)). In *Jones v Padavatton* [1969] 1 WLR 328 (CA), an arrangement between a mother and daughter was held not to be intended to be legally binding.
- Commercial agreements are presumed to be binding; a clause making an arrangement "binding in honour only" can negate it (*Rose and Frank Co v JR Crompton & Bros Ltd* [1925] AC 445 (HL)); advertisements and competitions are assessed by the circumstances (*Carlill*).

### Written terms and incorporation
- No general requirement for writing. Exceptions: contracts for the sale or other disposition of land must be in writing, signed by both parties (Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1989 s 2); regulated consumer credit agreements; guarantees must be evidenced in writing signed by the guarantor or an authorised agent (Statute of Frauds 1677 s 4).
- Electronic signatures and online "click-wrap" acceptance are generally valid: the Law Commission's report *Electronic Execution of Documents* (Law Com No 386, 2019) concluded that an electronic signature can validly execute a document, including a deed (though a deed still needs a witness physically present), where any formality otherwise is met. Electronic Communications Act 2000 s 7 makes e-signatures admissible in evidence.
- **Incorporation of terms:** terms are binding if the person has signed (*L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394 (DC), even if unread, subject to misrepresentation or non est factum), or if reasonable notice was given before or at the time of contract (*Parker v South Eastern Railway Co* (1877) 2 CPD 416 (CA); *Olley v Marlborough Court Ltd* [1949] 1 KB 532 (CA): notice given after the contract is too late); particularly onerous or unusual terms need to be fairly and reasonably brought to the other party's attention (*Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking Ltd* [1971] 2 QB 163 (CA); *Interfoto Picture Library Ltd v Stiletto Visual Programmes Ltd* [1989] QB 433 (CA)).
- **Consumer contracts:** terms in consumer contracts must also satisfy transparency and fairness requirements in Part 2 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (unfair terms), and implied terms apply (see the consumer rights note).
- **Misrepresentation and mistake** can allow a consumer to escape the contract (Misrepresentation Act 1967).

### Cancellation rights for distance, online and off-premises contracts
- **Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 (SI 2013/3134)** apply to contracts between a trader and a consumer made at a distance (online, phone, mail order), off-premises (for example at the consumer's home), and on-premises (information requirements only).
- **Information:** the trader must give prescribed pre-contract information (identity, main characteristics, total price, delivery, cancellation rights, and so on) before the consumer is bound. If the trader fails to inform the consumer of the right to cancel, the cancellation period is extended by up to 12 months (reg 31).
- **Right to cancel (reg 29):** a consumer can cancel a distance or off-premises contract without giving a reason within 14 days. For goods, the period starts the day after the consumer (or a nominated person) receives the goods (reg 30); for services, from the day after the contract is entered into; for digital content, from the day after the contract is made.
- **How to cancel:** by clear statement (the trader may supply a model cancellation form; any unequivocal statement suffices); the notice is effective when sent.
- **Refund:** the trader must refund within 14 days of receiving the cancellation notice (and may withhold a refund until goods are returned or evidence of return is given), including standard delivery costs; the consumer returns the goods within 14 days and normally bears return costs if the trader told them in advance; the consumer must take reasonable care of goods and can be liable for diminished value caused by handling beyond what is needed to establish the goods' nature, characteristics and functioning.
- **Services:** if the consumer asks for services to start during the cancellation period, they must pay a proportionate amount for what has been provided before cancelling; if the trader did not provide the information, the consumer need not pay.
- **Exceptions (reg 28):** goods made to the consumer's specification or clearly personalised; perishable goods; sealed goods unsuitable for return for health or hygiene reasons once unsealed; sealed audio or video recordings or software once unsealed; newspapers and magazines (except subscriptions); goods mixed with other items on delivery; digital content once downloading or streaming has begun with the consumer's prior express consent and acknowledgement that the right is lost; services fully performed with the consumer's consent; urgent repair services requested by the consumer; accommodation, transport, car hire, food delivery and leisure services for a specific date.
- **Other duties:** hidden charges and pre-ticked boxes for extras are banned (reg 40), and the consumer must be told clearly before ordering that the order obliges them to pay. The Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002 (SI 2002/2013) also require information for online contracts.
- **Subscriptions:** the DMCCA 2024's subscription contract rules (including cooling-off periods, reminder notices and easy exit) were not in force at November 2026; the government has said they will apply from January 2027.

## Key authorities
- *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* [1893] 1 QB 256 (CA): unilateral offers; acceptance by performance.
- *Pharmaceutical Society v Boots* [1953] 1 QB 401 (CA): display is an invitation to treat.
- *Entores v Miles Far East* [1955] 2 QB 327 (CA): instantaneous communication.
- *Williams v Roffey Bros* [1991] 1 QB 1 (CA): practical benefit as consideration.
- *Balfour v Balfour* [1919] 2 KB 571 (CA); *Merritt v Merritt* [1970] 1 WLR 1211 (CA): intention.
- *Thornton v Shoe Lane Parking* [1971] 2 QB 163 (CA): notice of onerous terms.

## Applying it
1. Was there an offer (not just an invitation to treat)? Who made it and when?
2. Was there acceptance, communicated, on the offered terms? When and where was the contract made?
3. Consideration: what did each give?
4. Intention: social or commercial?
5. Terms: were they incorporated (signature, notice, course of dealing)? Are they fair (CRA Part 2)? Any statutory implied terms?
6. If the consumer bought at a distance or off-premises: 14 days to cancel; check exceptions; the 12-month extension if no information; refund within 14 days.
7. Common traps: calling a shop display an offer; applying the postal rule to email; treating a promise to pay more as unenforceable without noting *Williams v Roffey*; claiming that a contract must be in writing; missing that cancellation rights do not apply to in-store purchases (statutory returns rights apply instead, such as the CRA rights).
8. Everyday question: "I ordered shoes online and changed my mind." You can cancel within 14 days of receiving them and send them back (you may have to pay return costs); the trader must refund within 14 days of getting them back or proof of posting.

## Debates and reform
- Whether *Williams v Roffey* undermines *Stilk v Myrick* and *Foakes v Beer*. In *MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd v Rock Advertising Ltd* [2018] UKSC 24 the Supreme Court held that a no-oral-modification clause is effective, and declined to decide whether *Foakes v Beer* should be reconsidered in the light of *Roffey*.
- The postal rule is criticised as outdated; reformers suggest a simple receipt rule.
- Electronic contracts and smart contracts: the Law Commission's work on electronic execution and smart legal contracts.
- Consumers' rights against subscription traps (DMCCA 2024).

## Recent changes and points to check
- DMCCA 2024 subscription contract regime: not in force at November 2026; expected January 2027.
- 6 April 2025: DMCCA unfair commercial practices rules in force (replacing the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008).
