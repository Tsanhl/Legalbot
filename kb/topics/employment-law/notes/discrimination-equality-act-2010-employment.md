# Discrimination at work under the Equality Act 2010

## Overview
The Equality Act 2010 (EqA) protects workers and job applicants from direct and indirect discrimination, harassment and victimisation connected with nine protected characteristics, and imposes a duty to make reasonable adjustments for disabled people. There is no qualifying service, no cap on compensation, and a shifting burden of proof (s 136). Claims go to the employment tribunal after Acas early conciliation, within a time limit of three months, or six months where the act complained of (or the end of conduct extending over a period) is on or after 1 October 2026, subject to a just and equitable extension.

## The law
**Protected characteristics (s 4):** age; disability; gender reassignment; marriage and civil partnership; pregnancy and maternity; race; religion or belief; sex; sexual orientation. In *For Women Scotland Ltd v The Scottish Ministers* [2025] UKSC 16, the Supreme Court held that, in the EqA, "sex", "woman" and "man" refer to biological sex; trans people (with or without a gender recognition certificate) remain protected under the gender reassignment characteristic, and can still claim sex discrimination by perception or association. Disability (s 6): a physical or mental impairment with a substantial (more than minor or trivial) and long-term (lasting or likely to last 12 months, or recurring) adverse effect on normal day-to-day activities; the effect of medical treatment or aids is disregarded when assessing the impairment (Sch 1 para 5).

**Scope of employment duties (s 39):** an employer must not discriminate in recruitment, terms, promotion, transfer, training, benefits, dismissal (including the end of a fixed term and constructive dismissal), or by subjecting the person to any other detriment; similarly for victimisation and harassment. "Employment" in s 83(2) is wider than the ERA employee definition (it includes a contract personally to do work).

**Direct discrimination (s 13).** A person (A) discriminates against another (B) if, because of a protected characteristic, A treats B less favourably than A treats or would treat others. Elements: less favourable treatment; because of the characteristic (the reason may be conscious or unconscious, and the characteristic needs to be a significant influence, not the sole cause); a real or hypothetical comparator whose circumstances are not materially different (s 23). Motive is irrelevant. Age is the only characteristic where direct discrimination can be justified (s 13(2): proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim). Pregnancy and maternity discrimination is dealt with in s 18 (see the pregnancy and maternity note). Section 14 (combined discrimination) is not in force.

**Indirect discrimination (s 19).** A applies a provision, criterion or practice (PCP) which is discriminatory in relation to a protected characteristic if: it applies equally to all but puts or would put persons sharing the characteristic at a particular disadvantage compared with others; it puts B at that disadvantage; and A cannot show it is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. The disadvantage can be shown by group statistics or other evidence of group disadvantage. Marriage and civil partnership is covered only for direct discrimination; pregnancy and maternity are dealt with under s 18; all other characteristics are covered for indirect discrimination.

**Discrimination arising from disability (s 15).** Unfavourable treatment because of something arising in consequence of B's disability (sickness absence, for instance), unless A shows the treatment is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim, or that A did not know and could not reasonably have been expected to know B had the disability. It is a distinct form of claim without a comparator.

**Reasonable adjustments (ss 20-21).** Where a PCP, a physical feature or the absence of an auxiliary aid puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage compared with non-disabled people, the employer must take reasonable steps to avoid the disadvantage. The employer is not liable if it did not know and could not reasonably be expected to know of the disability and the likelihood of disadvantage (Sch 8 para 20). Failure to comply is discrimination (s 21). Factors: effectiveness, practicability, cost, resources, disruption.

**Harassment (s 26).** Unwanted conduct related to a relevant protected characteristic (other than pregnancy and maternity, and marriage and civil partnership) which has the purpose or effect of violating B's dignity, or creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for B. In deciding whether conduct has that effect, the tribunal takes into account B's perception, the other circumstances, and whether it is reasonable for the conduct to have that effect (s 26(4)). Sexual harassment (s 26(2)) covers unwanted conduct of a sexual nature; less favourable treatment for rejecting it (s 26(3)). An employer's duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of employees (s 40A) has applied since 26 October 2024 (Worker Protection (Amendment of Equality Act 2010) Act 2023); since 30 October 2026 the Employment Rights Act 2025 has raised the standard to "all reasonable steps" (s 20) and, by new EqA s 40(1A)-(1C) (s 21), made it unlawful for an employer to permit a third party (for instance, a customer) to harass an employee in the course of employment, which it does if it failed to take all reasonable steps to prevent it; this covers harassment related to any relevant characteristic, not only sexual harassment. Section 22 allows regulations to specify what counts as reasonable steps (none made yet). All three sections were commenced on 30 October 2026 by SI 2026/1065. A tribunal can uplift compensation by up to 25% for breach of the s 40A duty (s 124A).

**Victimisation (s 27).** Subjecting a person to a detriment because they have done a protected act (bringing proceedings, giving evidence, making allegations in good faith that the EqA has been breached, or doing something under the Act) or because the employer believes they have done or may do so. No comparator is required.

**Liability.** Employers are vicariously liable for acts of employees done "in the course of employment", whether or not done with the employer's knowledge or approval, subject to the defence that it took all reasonable steps to prevent the employee from doing the act (s 109); employees and agents are personally liable too (s 110).

**Burden of proof (s 136).** If there are facts from which the tribunal could decide, in the absence of any other explanation, that a person contravened the provision, the tribunal must hold that the contravention occurred unless the respondent shows that it did not. The claimant must establish a prima facie case; the burden then shifts to the employer to show a non-discriminatory explanation.

**Time limits (s 123).** For a discriminatory dismissal, time runs from the date the dismissal takes effect, not when notice is given (*Lupetti v Wrens Old House Ltd* [1984] ICR 348 (EAT)); earlier acts such as an 'at risk' selection run from their own dates. A claim must be brought within three months starting with the date of the act, or such other period as the tribunal thinks just and equitable. The Employment Rights Act 2025 (s 152, Sch 12) changed three months to six months where the act (or, for conduct extending over a period, the end of it) is on or after 1 October 2026 (SI 2026/954 reg 4); earlier acts keep the three-month limit. Conduct extending over a period is treated as done at the end of the period (s 123(3)(a)), and a failure to do something is treated as occurring when the person decided on it. Early conciliation extends time (stop the clock).

**Remedies (s 124).** A declaration; compensation (uncapped, with no statutory limit) for financial loss and injury to feelings, personal injury, aggravated or exemplary damages (rare), interest; and appropriate recommendations (limited to the claimant, since the 2015 repeal of wider recommendations). For injury to feelings, the *Vento* bands as updated by the Presidential Guidance apply: for claims presented from 6 April 2026, the lower band is £1,300 to £12,600, the middle band £12,600 to £37,700, the upper band £37,700 to £62,900, and awards above that are exceptional (the figures are revised every April). In unfair dismissal cases there is no duplication of awards.

## Key authorities
- *Igen Ltd v Wong* [2005] EWCA Civ 142, [2005] ICR 931 (Court of Appeal): guidance on the two-stage burden of proof under the predecessor to s 136; claimants must prove facts from which discrimination could be inferred; the employer must prove on the balance of probabilities that it did not commit the act. Approved in the Supreme Court in *Hewage v Grampian Health Board* [2012] UKSC 37.
- *Madarassy v Nomura International plc* [2007] EWCA Civ 33, [2007] ICR 867 (CA): a difference in status and treatment alone is not enough to shift the burden; there must be "something more" from which discrimination could be inferred.
- *Efobi v Royal Mail Group Ltd* [2021] UKSC 33 (UK Supreme Court): the s 136 burden applies to the totality of the evidence, and tribunals may draw inferences from the facts, including those the respondent has adduced.
- *Shamoon v Chief Constable of the Royal Ulster Constabulary* [2003] UKHL 11 (HL): comparator and "less favourable treatment" approach; where reason for treatment is clear, a tribunal can decide the reason first.
- *R (E) v Governing Body of JFS* [2009] UKSC 15 (UK Supreme Court): motive or good intention is irrelevant to direct discrimination where the criterion used is the protected characteristic.
- *Essop v Home Office* [2017] UKSC 27 (UK Supreme Court): in indirect discrimination the claimant need not explain why the group suffers a disadvantage; a causal link between the PCP and the disadvantage is enough.
- *Archibald v Fife Council* [2004] UKHL 32 (HL): reasonable adjustments may require reassigning a disabled employee to a different post, even a higher one without competition.
- *Environment Agency v Rowan* [2008] ICR 218 (EAT): the proper approach to reasonable adjustments identifies the PCP, comparators and the substantial disadvantage.
- *Pnaiser v NHS England* [2016] IRLR 170 (EAT): approach to s 15: identify the unfavourable treatment, its cause, whether it arose from disability, and justification.
- *Richmond Pharmacology v Dhaliwal* [2009] ICR 724 (EAT): the harassment definition; conduct should be assessed against the s 26(4) factors and not every unwanted comment will amount to harassment.
- *Hendricks v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis* [2002] EWCA Civ 1686 (CA): the continuing act test looks at whether there is an ongoing state of affairs, not a series of discrete acts.
- *Vento v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police (No 2)* [2002] EWCA Civ 1871, [2003] ICR 318 (CA): the three bands for injury to feelings.
- *Chief Constable of West Yorkshire v Khan* [2001] UKHL 48 (HL): in victimisation, "by reason that" asks for the real reason for the treatment; a defendant's wish to protect its position in litigation can be a non-discriminatory reason.

## Applying it
1. Is the claimant covered (s 83) and which characteristic is in play? For disability, run the s 6 test, including knowledge.
2. Identify the conduct and the type of claim: direct, indirect, s 15, adjustments, harassment, victimisation (the claimant can plead several in the alternative).
3. Apply the elements. Direct: comparator and reason. Indirect: PCP, group disadvantage, individual disadvantage, justification (legitimate aim, rational connection, necessity, proportionality balance). Adjustments: PCP, substantial disadvantage, steps, reasonableness, knowledge. Harassment: unwanted conduct, relationship, purpose or effect, s 26(4). Victimisation: protected act, detriment, reason.
4. Burden: show primary facts and inference (s 136) and the employer's explanation.
5. Time and procedure: act date or end of the continuing act; early conciliation; just and equitable extension (the discretion is wide: *Robertson v Bexley Community Centre* [2003] EWCA Civ 576 says time limits are exercised strictly and extensions are the exception; *Abertawe Bro Morgannwg University Local Health Board v Morgan* [2018] EWCA Civ 640 says the tribunal's discretion is wide and no fixed checklist is needed).
6. Remedy: injury to feelings band; financial loss; interest; any liability of individuals; employer defences (s 109(4)).
7. Everyday question: "I'm being treated worse because of my age/race/disability; what can I do?" Answer: record the incidents, raise a grievance, contact Acas for early conciliation promptly: the limit is six months for acts on or after 1 October 2026, three months for earlier acts.

Traps: forgetting that the s 15 defence needs knowledge; treating indirect discrimination as needing intent; missing the s 109(4) reasonable steps defence; forgetting the comparator is hypothetical in direct discrimination; assuming the cap on unfair dismissal applies to EqA compensation (it does not).

## Debates and reform
- Whether combined (dual) discrimination should be brought into force (s 14 is not).
- The sex definition after *For Women Scotland* and its effect on guidance for employers and single-sex services (Equality and Human Rights Commission guidance and codes were under revision in 2025-26).
- The adequacy of preventive duties, as against enforcement after harm.
- Whether "disability" definitions and the burden of proving knowledge are too tough for claimants.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 26 October 2024: s 40A duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment.
- 30 October 2026: "all reasonable steps" standard and liability for third-party harassment (ERA 2025 ss 20-22; SI 2026/1065).
- 6 April 2026: Vento bands increased (Ninth Addendum).
- 1 October 2026: six-month time limit for acts on or after that date (SI 2026/954).
- Confidentiality terms (NDAs) about harassment and discrimination (ERA 2025 s 24, new ERA s 202A): not yet in force; government timeline gives September 2027.

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: calling the respondent's s 136 EqA burden 'evidential'. Once facts are shown from which discrimination could be inferred, the legal burden shifts to the respondent to prove the treatment was in no sense whatsoever because of the characteristic (Igen v Wong [2005] EWCA Civ 142). - Common error: running EqA time for a discriminatory dismissal from the notice date. Time runs from when the dismissal takes effect (Lupetti v Wrens Old House Ltd [1984] ICR 348, EAT); separate earlier detriments, such as at-risk notice or selection, run from their own dates.
