# Automatically unfair dismissal and the burden of proof

## Overview
Certain reasons for dismissal are made unfair by statute, whatever the employer's process, and in most cases no qualifying service is needed. The main ones are family-related reasons (pregnancy, maternity and other family leave), health and safety activity, whistleblowing, asserting a statutory right, and trade union reasons. The central practical issue is proving the reason: the employer bears the burden where the claimant has qualifying service, but a claimant without service must produce evidence that the reason was an inadmissible one, and the Court of Appeal in *Kuzel* explained how the proof works. Interim relief can be sought at once in the key cases.

## The law
**No qualifying service.** ERA 1996 s 108(3) disapplies the qualifying period for dismissals within, among others, ss 99, 100, 101A(1)(d), 102(1), 103, 103A, 104, 104A, 104C and several other sections. The tests below are therefore usable on day one. Where the reason or principal reason is one of these, the dismissal is "regarded as unfair" and the ordinary reasonableness inquiry under s 98(4) does not apply.

**Pregnancy, childbirth and family reasons (s 99).** A dismissal is automatically unfair where the reason or principal reason is of a prescribed kind connected with pregnancy, childbirth, maternity, paternity, adoption, parental leave, shared parental leave, neonatal care or time off for dependants. The detail is in regulations: for maternity, Maternity and Parental Leave etc Regulations 1999 reg 20 (reason connected with pregnancy, childbirth or maternity leave; or redundancy where the employer has failed to offer a suitable available vacancy as required by reg 10). The same dismissal is a pregnancy or maternity discrimination under EqA s 18 (see the pregnancy and maternity note), which has no service requirement and uncapped compensation.

**Health and safety (s 100).** Dismissal is automatically unfair if the reason or principal reason is that the employee: (a) carried out, or proposed to carry out, designated health and safety duties; (b) was a safety representative or committee member performing those functions; (c) brought harmful or potentially harmful circumstances to the employer's attention by reasonable means, where there was no representative or committee or it was not reasonably practicable to raise the matter through them; (d) left or refused to return to the workplace in circumstances of serious and imminent danger the employee reasonably believed in; or (e) took appropriate steps to protect themselves or others from danger. Detriment is separately covered by s 44.

**Asserting a statutory right (s 104).** Dismissal is automatically unfair if the reason or principal reason is that the employee brought proceedings against the employer to enforce a relevant statutory right, or alleged that the employer had infringed it. It does not matter whether the employee actually has the right or whether it has been infringed, provided the claim to the right and its infringement are made in good faith (s 104(2)); the allegation need not specify the right (s 104(3)). The relevant rights include those under ERA (notice, written statements, deductions, rest breaks, and so on) and the Working Time Regulations. Section 104A (national minimum wage) and s 104C (flexible working) are separate versions.

**Whistleblowing (s 103A).** Dismissal is automatically unfair if the reason (or principal reason) is that the employee made a protected disclosure (see the whistleblowing note). A worker who is not an employee cannot claim under s 103A but may claim detriment under s 47B.

**Other categories.** Trade union reasons (TULRCA 1992 ss 152-153), employee representatives (s 103), working time (s 101A), flexible working (s 104C) and, since 18 February 2026 (ERA 2025 s 77, amending TULRCA s 238A), stronger protection against dismissal for taking part in protected industrial action. The 2025 Act also adds a further category: dismissal where the employer sought a "restricted variation" of the contract (for instance, a cut in pay or hours) which the employee refused, or dismissed to re-engage on varied terms for the same duties (new ERA s 104I, subject to a narrow financial-difficulty exemption, with a separate version for local authorities), and dismissal to replace the employee with a non-employee (new s 104K). Neither is in force; the government timeline gives March 2027 (see the contract variation note).

**The burden of proof.**
- Employee with qualifying service: the employer must show the reason for dismissal. If the claimant asserts an automatically unfair reason, the claimant must produce some evidence to put it in issue (the evidential burden), but the legal burden to prove the reason it relied on remains with the employer. If the tribunal rejects the employer's reason, it may, but need not, find that the reason was the one the claimant asserts.
- Employee lacking the qualifying period for ordinary unfair dismissal: the claimant bears the legal burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that the reason (or principal reason) for dismissal was the inadmissible one (*Smith v Hayle Town Council* [1978] ICR 996 (CA)), because without qualifying service the employer has no obligation under s 98(1) to show a fair reason. The claimant may rely on inferences from timing, inconsistencies and the employer's explanation. (*Kuzel* distinguished *Smith v Hayle* on exactly this basis; it did not change the rule for claimants without service.)
- Where the claim is under s 103A and the claimant does have enough service, the question is whether the protected disclosure was the reason or principal reason, and the "rejection of the employer's reason" route may work in the claimant's favour.
- The qualifying period that matters is the one for ordinary unfair dismissal at the EDT: two years where the EDT is before 1 January 2027, six months where it is on or after that date.

**Interim relief.** ERA ss 128-132 allow an employee who claims dismissal for one of the listed reasons (ss 100(1)(a) and (b) only (designated health and safety duties and safety representatives, not the other s 100 limbs), 101A(1)(d), 102(1), 103, 103A, 104F, and trade union reasons under TULRCA s 161) to apply to the tribunal for an order that pay and benefits continue or that the contract continues pending the final hearing. The application must be presented within seven days of the EDT. The tribunal must be satisfied that the claimant is "likely" to succeed at the full hearing (s 129(1)).

## Key authorities
- *Kuzel v Roche Products Ltd* [2008] EWCA Civ 380, [2008] ICR 799 (Court of Appeal, Mummery LJ): the claimant (who had the qualifying service then required) said she was dismissed for making protected disclosures; the employer relied on conduct and then "some other substantial reason" (breakdown of trust). Held: where the employee has the qualifying period, s 98(1) puts the legal burden of showing the reason on the employer; an employee who positively asserts an inadmissible reason must produce some evidence of it (an evidential burden), but need not prove it; if the employer fails to prove its reason, the tribunal may, but is not obliged to, find the employee's reason, and may find a reason advanced by neither side (here, a manager's loss of temper). The Court treated *Smith v Hayle* as a case about employees lacking qualifying service, which is why the burden there was on the employee.
- *Smith v Hayle Town Council* [1978] ICR 996 (CA): an employee without the qualifying service who alleges an inadmissible reason (there, trade union activities) bears the burden of proving it; the principle applies to the other automatically unfair reasons.
- *Fecitt v NHS Manchester* [2012] EWCA Civ 1190 (Court of Appeal): for whistleblowing detriment (s 47B), the protected disclosure must materially (more than trivially) influence the employer's treatment; for dismissal under s 103A, the disclosure must be the reason or principal reason.
- *Royal Mail Group Ltd v Jhuti* [2019] UKSC 55 (UK Supreme Court): where a manager with an invented reason manipulates the process and the dismissing officer is innocently unaware, the tribunal should look behind the decision-maker and attribute the manager's real reason to the employer.
- *Mennell v Newell & Wright (Transport Contractors) Ltd* [1997] ICR 1039 (CA): under what is now s 104, the employee need not prove they actually had the right asserted or that it was infringed; it is enough that the claim to the right was genuine and made in good faith.
- *Taplin v C Shippam Ltd* [1978] ICR 1068 (EAT): "likely" for interim relief means a pretty good chance of success; refined in *Ministry of Justice v Sarfraz* [2011] IRLR 562 (EAT), which requires a significantly higher degree of likelihood than more likely than not.

## Applying it
1. Check whether the claimant is an employee and whether the reason asserted is a listed s 108(3) category (and its own conditions: for s 100, which limb; for s 104, a right and a good-faith assertion; for s 103A, a qualifying disclosure).
2. Identify the employer's stated reason and the circumstances: timing (dismissal soon after the protected act), inconsistency, absence of process, a denial of knowledge, comparator treatment. Use these to satisfy the evidential burden.
3. Apply the correct burden: employer shows the reason if the claimant has the qualifying service (two years where the EDT is before 1 January 2027; six months where it is on or after that date); claimant proves the inadmissible reason if they lack it (*Smith v Hayle*). If the claim fails as automatically unfair, consider ordinary unfair dismissal (if qualified) or discrimination alternatives.
4. Remedies: basic and compensatory awards; the compensatory cap does not apply to dismissals under s 100 or s 103A (or selection for redundancy for those reasons) (s 124(1A)), and from 1 January 2027 there is no cap for any unfair dismissal with an EDT on or after that date; a possible Acas uplift; interim relief if sought within seven days in the listed cases.
5. Parallel claims: detriment (ss 44, 47B, 47C, 48), discrimination, and wrongful dismissal.

Common traps: missing the seven-day deadline for interim relief; assuming the employer must prove the reason when the claimant has no service; not separating "principal reason" from "material factor" in detriment cases; using a "workers" claim for a dismissal (only employees can claim dismissal under s 103A).

Everyday question: "I was dismissed a week after I complained about unsafe equipment; I've worked here a year." Answer: possible s 100(1)(c) dismissal (no service needed); begin early conciliation promptly. With one year's service and an EDT before 1 January 2027 the employee lacks qualifying service, so must prove that the health and safety complaint was the reason (*Smith v Hayle*); with an EDT on or after 1 January 2027 the six-month period would be met and the employer would have to show its reason. Interim relief is not available for a s 100(1)(c) complaint (only s 100(1)(a)-(b)), but compensation is uncapped.

## Debates and reform
- Critics say that the burden on claimants without service is hard to discharge because the employer's reasons are inside its own knowledge; supporters reply that the employer should not have to justify dismissals outside the qualifying period.
- Interim relief is rarely granted in practice because of the "likely to succeed" threshold; there have been calls to lower it for whistleblowing.
- The 2025 Act's day-one protection against dismissal for refusal of restricted variations and its extension of protection to dismissal for industrial action broaden the automatic categories.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 18 February 2026: protection against dismissal for taking part in industrial action strengthened (ERA 2025 s 77; SI 2026/3).
- 1 January 2027: ordinary unfair dismissal qualifying period reduces to six months; the compensation cap is removed (SI 2026/559, EDT on or after that date).
- New ERA ss 104I and 104K (restricted variations, "fire and rehire"; replacement by non-employees): not in force; government timeline gives March 2027, and no commencement regulations had been made by November 2026.
- Pregnancy and family-leave dismissal protection (ERA 2025 ss 26-27): the enabling powers are in force but the protective regulations had not been made; the government timeline gives October 2027. See the pregnancy and maternity note.

## Common errors to avoid

- In a redundancy dismissal of a pregnant employee or one on family leave, check MPL Regs 1999 reg 10 first: failure to offer a suitable available vacancy makes the dismissal automatically unfair (reg 20(1)(b)) without her proving the reason. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q15]
