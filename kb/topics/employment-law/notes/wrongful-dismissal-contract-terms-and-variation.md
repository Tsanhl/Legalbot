# Wrongful dismissal, the contract of employment, variation of terms and deductions from wages

## Overview
Wrongful dismissal is a common-law claim for breach of contract (usually failure to give proper notice), distinct from statutory unfair dismissal and with no qualifying service. The contract contains express terms and implied terms, the most important being the duty of mutual trust and confidence, and an employer cannot ordinarily change those terms without the employee's agreement. Where it does, the employee may sue, resign and claim constructive dismissal, or accept the change. Unlawful deductions from wages are a separate statutory claim under ERA 1996 Part II. The Employment Rights Act 2025 targets "fire and rehire" practices by making dismissal over certain contract variations automatically unfair, once commenced.

## The law
**Written statement.** ERA 1996 s 1 requires a written statement of particulars on or before the first day of employment (a day-one right since 6 April 2020, when it was also extended from employees to workers), with changes notified under s 4. The statement is evidence of, not conclusive of, the contract terms.

**Notice.** The contract may specify notice; the statutory minimum under s 86 is one week for service from one month to two years, then one week per complete year to a maximum of twelve weeks; the employee must give at least one week after one month's service. A longer contractual period prevails. A payment in lieu of notice (PILON) clause lets the employer terminate immediately with a payment; without a PILON clause, a payment in lieu is damages for breach. Summary dismissal without notice is justified only where the employee is guilty of conduct amounting to a repudiatory breach (gross misconduct); the employer can rely on misconduct discovered after dismissal to justify it.

**Wrongful dismissal remedy.** Damages for breach of contract: what the employee would have earned during the proper notice period (salary, benefits and pension contributions), less mitigation and earnings in the period, plus tax adjustments. There is no compensation for the manner of dismissal (the "Johnson exclusion zone"), nor for loss of reputation or injured feelings arising from the dismissal itself. Claims arising or outstanding on termination can be heard in the employment tribunal up to £25,000 (Employment Tribunals Extension of Jurisdiction (England and Wales) Order 1994) and otherwise in the county court or High Court. The tribunal time limit (art 7 of the 1994 Order) is three months from the EDT, increased to six months where the EDT (or, if none, the last day worked) is on or after 1 October 2026 (SI 2026/759); in the courts the limitation period is six years (Limitation Act 1980 s 5).

**Implied terms.** (a) The mutual duty of trust and confidence: an employer must not, without reasonable and proper cause, conduct itself in a manner calculated or likely to destroy or seriously damage the relationship of confidence and trust between the parties. (b) Duty to provide a safe system of work and to take reasonable care for health; (c) duty to pay and not to act irrationally or arbitrarily in exercising a contractual discretion (*Braganza v BP Shipping Ltd* [2015] UKSC 17 for the general principle); (d) the employee's duties of fidelity, obedience and reasonable care; (e) in some contexts an obligation to provide work and a duty to exercise discretion in good faith.

**Variation.** The contract can only be varied by agreement (express or implied), by a contractual power of variation, or by a collective agreement incorporated into it. Where the employer imposes new terms:
1. Agreement may be express, or inferred from conduct (continuing to work after a change that has immediate, noticeable effect, with no protest). Where the change takes effect over time and the employee continues to work, conduct alone may not be acceptance: *Rigby v Ferodo Ltd* held that the employee who carries on working under protest or without accepting does not lose the right to sue for damages.
2. Employees may "work under protest" and claim damages for breach, or sue for the unauthorised deduction from wages (below), while remaining employed.
3. Alternatively, they may resign, treating the change as a repudiatory breach (constructive dismissal; *Western Excavating*), provided they do not delay too long and so affirm. The last straw doctrine allows a series of acts to amount to a breach (*Omilaju*; *Kaur*).
4. An employer who dismisses and offers re-engagement on new terms ("fire and rehire") dismisses, and the dismissal may still be fair if there is a sound business reason (SOSR: *Hollister*), but a tribunal will examine consultation and alternatives. Since 18 July 2024, the statutory Code of Practice on Dismissal and Re-engagement applies, and a failure to follow it can lead to an uplift of up to 25% on compensation (TULRCA s 207A).
5. Collective consultation duties apply if 20 or more dismissals for re-engagement are proposed at one establishment (TULRCA s 195 widens "redundancy" to dismissals for reasons not related to the individual).

**Employment Rights Act 2025: restricted variations.** Section 28 adds provisions to ERA 1996 (the principal one is new s 104I) under which dismissal is automatically unfair where the reason or principal reason is that the employer sought to make a "restricted variation" which the employee refused, or that the employer wants to re-engage the employee (or hire someone else) on varied terms for substantially the same duties. A "restricted variation" covers: a cut in pay or removal of a pay entitlement (with regulation-making power to exclude expenses and benefits in kind), a change in the way pay is measured by work done, a variation of pension terms, hours, shift timing or duration (as specified), a reduction in time off, other variations specified by regulations, and the insertion of a term allowing the employer to make such a variation unilaterally. An exemption removes automatic unfairness (so that ordinary s 98 fairness applies instead) where a non-local-authority employer shows that the reason for the variation was to eliminate, prevent or significantly reduce, or mitigate, financial difficulties affecting (or likely in the immediate future to affect) its ability to carry on as a going concern (or, for public sector employers, the financial sustainability of its statutory functions), and that in all the circumstances it could not reasonably have avoided the variation. A narrower exemption applies to local authorities under a financial-intervention direction. Where the exemption is made out, the tribunal considers matters such as consultation with the employee and any recognised union and anything offered in return for agreement. No qualifying service is required (s 108(3) as amended). A related new s 104K makes it automatically unfair to dismiss in order to replace the employee with a non-employee doing the same work. Neither provision is in force (s 28 has been commenced only for regulation-making purposes); the government timeline (updated 5 November 2026) gives March 2027, and no commencement regulations had been made by November 2026.

**Deductions from wages (ERA Part II).** Section 13(1): an employer must not make a deduction from a worker's wages unless authorised by statute, by a relevant provision of the contract (notified in writing to the worker before the deduction) or by the worker's prior written consent. "Wages" (s 27) covers any sum payable in connection with employment (fee, bonus, commission, holiday pay, statutory sick pay), but not a payment in lieu of notice, whether or not the contract provides for it (*Delaney v Staples* [1992] 1 AC 687 (HL); garden-leave pay while employment continues is wages), and not expenses. A deduction occurs when the total paid is less than the total properly payable (s 13(3)); there are exceptions for overpayments, strike deductions and others (s 14). A complaint must be presented within three months of the deduction (or the last in a series of deductions) (s 23(2)-(4)), extended where not reasonably practicable; the tribunal orders repayment (s 24) and may award additional losses. Under the Employment Rights Act 2025 (s 152, Sch 12) the period is six months where the deduction (or the last in the series) is on or after 1 October 2026 (SI 2026/954 reg 4). A two-year backstop on the period of deductions that can be claimed applies (s 23(4A)). Workers (not just employees) can bring the claim. Cases about whether a series continues past a three-month gap: *Chief Constable of the Police Service of Northern Ireland v Agnew* [2023] UKSC 33 held that a gap of more than three months between deductions does not automatically break a series; the question is whether there is a sufficient link.

## Key authorities
- *Malik v Bank of Credit and Commerce International SA* [1998] AC 20 (HL): the implied term of trust and confidence; employees of a bank run dishonestly could claim "stigma" damages for harm to their employment prospects; the term was applied to conduct during employment, with the "calculated or likely" test as above.
- *Johnson v Unisys Ltd* [2001] UKHL 13, [2003] 1 AC 518 (HL): no damages at common law for the manner of dismissal (including loss from psychiatric injury caused by dismissal itself); Parliament had given the statutory unfair dismissal regime, and the common law should not interfere.
- *Eastwood v Magnox Electric plc* [2004] UKHL 35, [2005] 1 AC 503 (HL): the Johnson exclusion zone applies only to events at or after dismissal; losses from pre-dismissal acts of breach (for instance, the manner in which a disciplinary process was started) are recoverable.
- *Edwards v Chesterfield Royal Hospital NHS Foundation Trust* [2011] UKSC 58 (UK Supreme Court): the exclusion zone also bars damages for breaches of a contractual disciplinary procedure leading to dismissal.
- *Geys v Société Générale, London Branch* [2012] UKSC 63, [2013] 1 AC 523 (UK Supreme Court): the "elective" theory applies to employment contracts, so an employer's wrongful repudiation does not end the contract until the employee accepts it (Lord Sumption dissenting on this point). Where the employer relies on a PILON clause, the contract ends only when the employee is clearly told that the employer is exercising that right; payment into the employee's account without clear notification was not enough.
- *Rigby v Ferodo Ltd* [1988] ICR 29 (HL): an employee who works under protest or without agreeing the unilateral wage cut did not accept the change, and could recover the difference as damages for breach.
- *Western Excavating (ECC) Ltd v Sharp* [1978] QB 761 (CA): contractual test for constructive dismissal.
- *Omilaju v Waltham Forest LBC* [2004] EWCA Civ 1493 (CA), and *Kaur v Leeds Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust* [2018] EWCA Civ 978 (CA): the last straw need not itself be a breach, but must contribute to the breach of the implied term; *Kaur* sets out the questions: what was the most recent act (or omission) said to have caused resignation; whether the employee affirmed after it; whether that act was itself a breach or part of a series amounting to a breach of trust and confidence; and whether the employee resigned in response to it.
- *Hollister v National Farmers' Union* [1979] ICR 542 (CA): re-organisation requiring terms change can be SOSR for dismissal.
- *Delaney v Staples* [1992] 1 AC 687 (HL): a payment in lieu of notice (as damages, wholly in lieu) was not "wages" for the purposes of the deductions provisions.
- *Tillman v Egon Zehnder Ltd* [2019] UKSC 32 (UK Supreme Court): post-termination restrictive covenants are enforceable only if protecting a legitimate business interest and no wider than necessary; the court may sever offending words (here, "interested in") if that does not require rewriting the covenant or change its overall effect.

## Applying it
1. Wrongful dismissal: identify the notice due (contract, then s 86), whether the employer had a right to dismiss summarily (conduct amounting to repudiation), whether a PILON clause exists, damages (notice period pay, benefits, mitigation) and forum (tribunal cap £25,000; courts above it; limitation).
2. Implied terms: ask whether a breach of trust and confidence is made out (reasonable and proper cause, serious effect), and whether it supports constructive dismissal.
3. Variation: Was there a contractual right to vary? Did the employee agree (expressly or by conduct)? Was there protest? Choose remedies: damages for breach; unlawful deduction claim; constructive dismissal; or unfair dismissal if dismissed. Apply the Code and, when in force, s 104I.
4. Deductions: wages? deduction? authority (statute, contract notified in advance, prior written consent)? time limit and series. Distinguish a deduction claim from a contractual claim for damages.
5. Everyday question: "My employer has cut my hours and pay; can they do that?" Answer: not unilaterally unless the contract allows it; the employee can object in writing and continue to work under protest, claim unlawful deductions, or resign and claim constructive dismissal if they have the qualifying service; negotiate or take Acas advice early.

## Debates and reform
- Whether the *Johnson* exclusion zone leaves employees without a remedy for the abuse of power in dismissal, and whether the statutory cap on unfair dismissal compensation compounds the problem (the cap is due to be removed for EDTs from 1 January 2027).
- Whether the proposed automatic-unfairness approach to fire and rehire is more effective than a statutory code uplift.
- Whether trust and confidence should be a fully fledged implied duty of good faith.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 18 July 2024: Code of Practice on Dismissal and Re-engagement in force.
- ERA 2025 s 28 (restricted variations, new ss 104I and 104K): not in force; government timeline gives March 2027.
- 1 October 2026: six-month tribunal time limits for deductions claims (relevant date on or after that day) and for tribunal contract claims (EDT on or after that day).
- Flexible working "reasonableness" changes under ERA 2025 s 9: government timeline gives October 2027.

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: treating a contractual PILON as 'wages' recoverable under ERA Part II. Under Delaney v Staples [1992] 1 AC 687 (HL) no PILON is wages (garden-leave pay apart), contractual or not; an unpaid contractual PILON is claimed as a contract debt. [2026-11-24 · ref 2026-11-24-1510/q15#3]
