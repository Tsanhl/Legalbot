# Whistleblowing - protected disclosures under ERA 1996 Part IVA

## Overview
A worker who makes a "protected disclosure" is protected from dismissal (employees: automatically unfair, no qualifying period) and from any detriment (all workers). A disclosure qualifies if it discloses information that the worker reasonably believes is in the public interest and tends to show one of six types of wrongdoing, and it is made to the right person by the right route. The route becomes more demanding the further outside the organisation the disclosure goes. Compensation for whistleblowing dismissal or detriment is not subject to the statutory cap.

## The law
**Qualifying disclosure (s 43B(1)).** Any disclosure of information which, in the reasonable belief of the worker making it, is made in the public interest and tends to show one or more of the following: (a) a criminal offence has been committed, is being committed or is likely to be committed; (b) a person has failed, is failing or is likely to fail to comply with any legal obligation to which they are subject; (c) a miscarriage of justice has occurred, is occurring or is likely to occur; (d) the health or safety of any individual has been, is being or is likely to be endangered; (e) the environment has been, is being or is likely to be damaged; (f) information tending to show any of those matters has been, is being or is likely to be deliberately concealed. The Employment Rights Act 2025 s 23 (in force from 6 April 2026) adds a further category, new s 43B(1)(da): that sexual harassment (unwanted conduct of a sexual nature within EqA s 26(2)) has occurred, is occurring or is likely to occur. A disclosure is not protected if the person making it commits an offence by making it, and a legally privileged disclosure is excluded (s 43B(3), (4)).

**Elements to analyse.**
1. *Disclosure of information.* The worker must convey information, not merely make an allegation. The two are not exclusive: a statement can be both, and the question is whether it has sufficient factual content and specificity to tend to show the relevant failure.
2. *Reasonable belief.* The worker must genuinely believe (subjective) and the belief must be reasonable (objective, judged by the worker's position and knowledge). It can be reasonable and wrong. Belief about the category of wrongdoing can be reasonable even if the worker misjudges the precise legal obligation.
3. *Public interest.* Inserted by the Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013 (from 25 June 2013). The worker need not have been motivated by it (the good faith requirement was removed from the qualification test, with a power to reduce compensation by up to 25% under s 49(6A) where the disclosure was not made in good faith).
4. *Tends to show* one of the six categories.
5. *The right recipient (ss 43C-43H).* Tier 1: the employer or the person the worker reasonably believes responsible (s 43C), a legal adviser (s 43D), a Minister of the Crown for certain bodies (s 43E). Tier 2: a prescribed regulator (s 43F), where the worker reasonably believes the information and allegations are substantially true and the matter falls within the regulator's remit. Tier 3 (wider disclosure, for instance to the media or an MP) (s 43G): requires reasonable belief in substantial truth, no personal gain, one of the preconditions (reasonable belief of detriment if disclosed internally, likelihood of evidence concealment, or previous disclosure), and that it is reasonable to make the disclosure. Section 43H: exceptionally serious failures, where wider disclosure may be protected without the s 43G preconditions. A disclosure made in accordance with a procedure authorised by the employer is treated as made to the employer (s 43C(2)).

**Who is protected.** "Worker" in s 43K is wider than s 230(3): it includes agency workers, some NHS practitioners and trainees. Only employees can claim automatically unfair dismissal; workers can claim detriment.

**Remedies and enforcement.**
- Dismissal (s 103A): automatically unfair if the reason or principal reason for the dismissal is the protected disclosure; no qualifying service; no cap on compensation (s 124(1A)); interim relief is available (ss 128-132: apply within seven days; the claimant must show a "likely" success).
- Detriment (s 47B): a worker has the right not to be subjected to any detriment by their employer on the ground that they made a protected disclosure. Protection extends to detriment by co-workers and agents, for which the employer is vicariously liable (s 47B(1A), (1B)) unless it took all reasonable steps. Claim to the employment tribunal within three months of the act (or last in a series) (s 48), or six months where that act is on or after 1 October 2026 (ERA 2025 Sch 12; SI 2026/954). On a detriment complaint it is for the employer to show the ground on which it acted (s 48(2)).
- A tribunal may reduce compensation by up to 25% where the disclosure was not made in good faith (s 49(6A)), and in a dismissal case a reduction applies similarly (s 123(6A)).
- Contractual gagging clauses are void so far as they preclude a protected disclosure (s 43J). The Employment Rights Act 2025 s 24 (new ERA s 202A) will make contractual confidentiality terms void so far as they prevent workers from making allegations or disclosures about harassment or discrimination (subject to "excepted agreements" defined by regulations); it is not yet in force, and the government timeline gives September 2027.

## Key authorities
- *Chesterton Global Ltd v Nurmohamed* [2017] EWCA Civ 979, [2018] ICR 731 (Court of Appeal, Underhill LJ): a senior manager disclosed that the employer was manipulating accounts to reduce commission paid to about 100 senior managers. Held: the public interest requirement can be satisfied by a disclosure that affects a limited group (here about 100 employees), even if the worker's own interest is also served; the tribunal must ask whether the worker believed the disclosure to be in the public interest and whether that belief was reasonable, and may consider the number of people affected, the nature of the interests, the nature and extent of the wrongdoing, and the identity of the alleged wrongdoer (larger and more prominent employers raising stronger public interest). The Court did not lay down a rigid test.
- *Babula v Waltham Forest College* [2007] EWCA Civ 174, [2007] ICR 1026 (CA): a worker's reasonable belief that a criminal offence or breach of legal obligation has occurred is enough even if mistaken as a matter of law or fact.
- *Cavendish Munro Professional Risks Management Ltd v Geduld* [2010] ICR 325 (EAT): a disclosure of "information" is something more than an allegation; a bare allegation without facts will not qualify.
- *Kilraine v London Borough of Wandsworth* [2018] EWCA Civ 1436, [2018] ICR 1850 (CA): the information/allegation distinction is not a rigid dichotomy; the statement must have sufficient factual content and specificity to be capable of tending to show one of the listed matters.
- *Fecitt v NHS Manchester* [2012] EWCA Civ 1190 (CA): detriment is made out where the protected disclosure materially influenced the employer's treatment (more than trivially); in dismissal under s 103A, the disclosure must be the reason or principal reason.
- *Royal Mail Group Ltd v Jhuti* [2019] UKSC 55 (UK Supreme Court): where the person who decides to dismiss is manipulated by a manager who has a hidden reason connected to a protected disclosure, the tribunal must attribute the manager's real reason to the employer.
- *Kuzel v Roche Products Ltd* [2008] EWCA Civ 380, [2008] ICR 799 (CA): burden of proof in s 103A dismissals where the employee has qualifying service: the employer must show its reason; the employee need only produce some evidence of the protected-disclosure reason. An employee without qualifying service must prove the s 103A reason (*Smith v Hayle Town Council* [1978] ICR 996). See the automatically unfair dismissal note.
- *Blackbay Ventures Ltd (t/a Chemistree) v Gahir* [2014] ICR 747 (EAT): structured approach to analysing whistleblowing claims: identify each disclosure, the failure it tends to show, and the worker's belief, in separate stages.

## Applying it
1. Status: employee (for dismissal) or worker (for detriment), using the wider s 43K definition where relevant.
2. For each alleged disclosure: date, recipient, content. Is it information with enough factual content (Kilraine)? Does it tend to show one of s 43B(1)(a)-(f)? Did the worker reasonably believe it did? Did they reasonably believe it was in the public interest (Chesterton factors)?
3. Route: employer (easy), regulator (more conditions), wider disclosure (hardest). Did the worker follow an internal procedure, and is there a reasonable excuse for going outside?
4. Causation: for dismissal, was it the sole or principal reason? For detriment, was it a material influence? Use timing, who knew, the quality of the employer's explanation, manipulation by managers (Jhuti), and the employer's process.
5. Remedies: unlimited compensation; interim relief (seven days); possible 25% reduction for bad faith; uplift if the Acas Code applied; claims against individual colleagues.
6. Common traps: calling something a disclosure when it was only a grievance about the worker's own contract (the public interest test may fail); missing the seven-day interim relief limit; forgetting that an employee without qualifying service can still claim; treating the dismissal as ordinary unfair dismissal and missing the uncapped remedy.
7. Everyday question: "I told my manager our firm was misreporting safety checks, and now I'm being managed out; what can I do?" Answer: this may be a protected disclosure about health and safety and a detriment claim; document dates, raise the matter formally, contact Acas promptly (six months from the act for acts on or after 1 October 2026; three months for earlier acts), and consider a prescribed regulator.

## Debates and reform
- The regime has been criticised as complex and focused on after-the-event remedies; calls for an independent Office of the Whistleblower, improved protection for job applicants and a duty on employers to have whistleblowing procedures, and for protection for volunteers and others. The Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 has been amended in 2013 and since.
- The relation to other confidentiality duties, including NDAs. The 2025 Act's treatment of harassment-related NDAs is a step in this direction.
- The effect of the cap removal under the 2025 Act on the cost of ordinary unfair dismissal claims (whistleblowing claims have always been uncapped).

## Recent changes and points to check
- 25 June 2013: public interest requirement added (responding to *Parkins v Sodexho Ltd* [2002] IRLR 109, where a complaint about the worker's own contract qualified) and good faith moved from qualification to remedy.
- 6 April 2026: sexual harassment disclosures are protected (ERA 2025 s 23).
- 1 October 2026: six-month time limit for detriment and dismissal claims with a relevant date on or after that day.
- Pending: ERA 2025 s 24 (confidentiality terms; government timeline September 2027).

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: requiring a disclosure to be 'made in the public interest'. ERA s 43B(1) requires only that the worker reasonably believes the disclosure is in the public interest; it need not objectively be so (Chesterton Global Ltd v Nurmohamed [2017] EWCA Civ 979).
