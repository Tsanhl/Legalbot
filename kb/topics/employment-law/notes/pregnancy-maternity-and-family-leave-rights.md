# Pregnancy, maternity and family leave rights

## Overview
Pregnant workers and new parents are protected by several overlapping regimes: discrimination law (Equality Act 2010 s 18), the statutory right to maternity and other family leave and pay, automatic unfair dismissal, and enhanced redundancy protection. Maternity discrimination needs no qualifying service, has uncapped compensation and does not require a comparator. The Employment Rights Act 2025 is extending these protections, in part from April 2026 (day-one paternity and parental leave) and in part by regulations still to be made (extended dismissal protection, expected October 2027).

## The law
**Equality Act 2010 s 18.** In the work context a person (A) discriminates against a woman if, in the protected period in relation to a pregnancy of hers, A treats her unfavourably because of the pregnancy (s 18(2)(a)) or because of illness suffered by her as a result of it (s 18(2)(b)). A also discriminates if A treats her unfavourably because she is on compulsory maternity leave (s 18(3)) or on ordinary or additional maternity leave (s 18(4)), or because she is exercising or seeking to exercise or has exercised the right to those leave periods (s 18(4)). The protected period (s 18(6)) begins when the pregnancy begins and, if she has the right to ordinary and additional maternity leave, ends at the end of the additional maternity leave period or when she returns to work, if earlier; if she has no right to maternity leave, it ends two weeks after the end of the pregnancy. Further points:
- The test is "unfavourable" treatment, not less favourable treatment, so no comparator is needed. It is distinct from sex discrimination (s 18(7) provides that s 13 does not apply to treatment that is covered by s 18), and the claim is not available to a man; a man's claim for less favourable treatment relating to family leave is under s 13.
- Unfavourable treatment after the protected period that implements a decision taken during it is treated as occurring in the protected period (s 18(5)); other treatment after the period linked to maternity may be sex discrimination.
- The employer's motive and whether it knew of the pregnancy: the treatment must be because of the pregnancy; knowledge is generally needed (the decision-maker's state of mind), but the employer need not have known the detail.
- Sex discrimination (s 13) applies to a woman who is treated less favourably in the later period for reasons linked to maternity or childcare; indirect sex discrimination can arise from a refusal of flexible working to a woman with childcare responsibilities (s 19).
- The same facts will often give rise to automatically unfair dismissal under ERA 1996 s 99 and the 1999 Regulations (reg 20), and detriment claims under s 47C.

**Statutory maternity leave.** Maternity and Parental Leave etc Regulations 1999 (SI 1999/3312): every employee, whatever her length of service, is entitled to 26 weeks' ordinary and 26 weeks' additional maternity leave (52 weeks) (regs 4, 5, 6 and 7). She must tell the employer by the end of the 15th week before the expected week of childbirth that she is pregnant, the expected week of childbirth and when she wants the leave to start. Compulsory maternity leave is two weeks after birth (four weeks for factory work) (reg 8). During leave the contract continues except for remuneration (reg 9: benefits such as holiday accrual, pension rights and notice rights continue). A woman who returns from ordinary maternity leave is entitled to return to the same job; on return from additional maternity leave she may return to the same job or, if not reasonably practicable, a suitable and appropriate alternative job on terms not less favourable (reg 18). An employee may use up to 10 "keeping in touch" days and, under the shared parental leave scheme, 20 "split" days without ending leave.
- Redundancy during leave: see the redundancy note. Since 6 April 2024 a woman has priority for suitable alternative vacancies from the date she tells her employer of the pregnancy until 18 months after the birth (SI 2024/264, amending reg 10 of the 1999 Regulations).

**Statutory maternity pay (SMP) and Maternity Allowance.** An employee with 26 weeks' continuous service by the end of the qualifying week (the 15th week before the expected week of childbirth) and average weekly earnings at or above the lower earnings limit receives SMP for up to 39 weeks: 90% of average weekly earnings for the first six weeks, then the lower of the standard weekly rate and 90% of average earnings for 33 weeks. The standard weekly rate is £194.32 from April 2026 (previously £187.18); the same flat rate applies to statutory paternity, adoption, shared parental, neonatal care and parental bereavement pay. Those who do not qualify for SMP may qualify for Maternity Allowance (up to 39 weeks), paid at the same flat rate or 90% of earnings if lower. The Social Security Contributions and Benefits Act 1992 and the Statutory Maternity Pay (General) Regulations 1986 contain the detail.

**Maternity equality clause.** The EqA (ss 73-76) implies a maternity equality clause into a woman's contract: any pay increase or bonus relating to the period of leave must be applied to her during and after leave (a rise she would have received but for the leave) and pay on return must reflect it.

**Other family leave.** Paternity leave: one or two weeks' leave within 52 weeks of the birth. Shared parental leave and pay: the mother or primary adopter can curtail her maternity leave and share up to 50 weeks' leave and 37 weeks' pay with her partner, in a limited number of blocks. Adoption leave mirrors maternity leave. Neonatal care leave and pay (from 6 April 2025) gives up to 12 weeks for parents whose baby is in neonatal care. Parental leave (unpaid): up to 18 weeks per child before the child's 18th birthday. Parental bereavement leave (Parental Bereavement (Leave and Pay) Act 2018, "Jack's Law"): two weeks. Time off for dependants (ERA 1996 s 57A): reasonable unpaid time off to deal with emergencies. The right to request flexible working is a day-one right (from April 2024) with two requests a year, a duty to consult before refusal and a two-month decision period.

**Employment Rights Act 2025 changes.** The Act removes the qualifying period for paternity leave and unpaid parental leave (ss 15, 16) and allows paternity leave to be taken after shared parental leave (s 17), all in force from 6 April 2026 (SI 2026/3); from the same date a bereaved partner can take extended paternity leave (up to 52 weeks) where the mother or primary adopter dies. Sections 26-27 are enabling powers: s 26 allows regulations about dismissal (other than redundancy) during, or after, a protected period of pregnancy, and s 27 allows the existing regulation-making powers on redundancy and dismissal around maternity, adoption, shared parental, paternity and neonatal leave to cover a period after leave. The powers are in force, but the regulations that would give the new protection had not been made by November 2026; the government timeline gives October 2027, and their content is not yet settled. Section 18 (a new right to bereavement leave, including for pregnancy loss) is expected in April 2027.

## Key authorities
- *Webb v EMO Air Cargo (UK) Ltd (No 2)* [1995] 1 WLR 1454 (HL), following the CJEU's ruling on the reference (Case C-32/93, 1994): dismissal of a woman because of her pregnancy is direct sex discrimination without any comparison with a sick man; a woman recruited for an indefinite period, initially to cover another employee's maternity leave, could not lawfully be dismissed because her own pregnancy made her unavailable for that period.
- *Dekker v Stichting Vormingscentrum voor Jong Volwassenen* (Case C-177/88), CJEU, 1990: refusal to hire a woman because of pregnancy is direct discrimination, and the employer's financial loss is no defence.
- *Brown v Rentokil Ltd* (Case C-394/96), CJEU, 1998: dismissal at any time during pregnancy for absences caused by a pregnancy-related illness is direct sex discrimination, whatever the length of absence and despite a contractual rule dismissing anyone absent for a set period; absences after maternity leave may be treated under the ordinary rules.
- *Handels- og Kontorfunktionaerernes Forbund i Danmark (Hertz) v Dansk Arbejdsgiverforening* (Case C-179/88), CJEU, 1990: illness that appears after maternity leave has ended may be treated like any other illness, even if it originated in the pregnancy or childbirth, provided a sick man would be treated the same way. The domestic protected period in s 18(6) reflects this by ending with the maternity leave period.

These are pre-2021 CJEU judgments; under the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 s 6 they still bind tribunals unless the Court of Appeal or Supreme Court departs from them, and they inform the reading of s 18, which is the primary source.

## Applying it
1. Establish the stage: pregnancy (protected period starts), maternity leave, or after return; the length of employment does not matter for s 18 or the leave right itself (only for statutory pay).
2. Identify the treatment complained of (dismissal, redundancy selection, failure to promote, denial of bonus, refusal of flexible working, treatment on return) and ask whether it was "because of" the pregnancy, an associated illness or the leave. The pregnancy need not be the only reason, but must be a substantial influence.
3. Choose the claims: EqA s 18 (and s 13/s 19 for later treatment); automatic unfair dismissal (s 99/reg 20); detriment (s 47C); redundancy priority (reg 10); maternity equality clause; right to return (reg 18); statutory pay claims; written statement or breach of contract claims.
4. Check notices and procedure: has the woman given the statutory notice, and did the employer respond properly? Check the 15-week notice for leave and the notice for early return (eight weeks).
5. Remedies: uncapped compensation including injury to feelings (Vento bands), reinstatement or recommendation, loss of earnings and pension, interest.
6. Time limit: three months less one day, or six months less one day for acts on or after 1 October 2026 (SI 2026/954), plus any early conciliation extension.
7. Everyday question: "I told my manager I'm pregnant and now I've been moved to a worse role; what are my rights?" Answer: this is probably unfavourable treatment under s 18; raise a written grievance, ask for a risk assessment, contact Acas early, and note the time limit.

Traps: using the sex-discrimination comparator test for s 18 claims; forgetting that protection applies without qualifying service; overlooking that maternity pay has its own qualifying rules; ignoring the 18-month redundancy priority; assuming s 26-27 of the 2025 Act are in force.

## Debates and reform
- Research reported by the Equality and Human Rights Commission and the then business department in 2016 estimated that a significant share of mothers experienced disadvantage or loss of work after pregnancy, which prompted the 2023 Act.
- Debate about whether paid family leave and pay rates are generous enough compared with other countries (the flat rate of statutory pay is low relative to earnings), and about shared parental leave's low take-up.
- Whether dismissal protection should extend for six months after return or longer.

## Recent changes and points to check
- 6 April 2024: extended redundancy priority (SI 2024/264); carer's leave and flexible working changes.
- 6 April 2025: neonatal care leave and pay.
- 6 April 2026: day-one paternity and unpaid parental leave; paternity leave after shared parental leave; SMP flat rate £194.32.
- Awaiting regulations: dismissal protection under ERA 2025 ss 26-27 (government timeline: October 2027) and bereavement leave under s 18 (April 2027).

## Common errors to avoid

- Common error: saying pregnancy must be a 'substantial' or main influence for s 18 EqA. 'Because of' requires only a significant, i.e. more than trivial, influence on the decision (Nagarajan v London Regional Transport [1999] UKHL 36; Igen v Wong [2005] EWCA Civ 142).
